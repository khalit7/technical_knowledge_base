**[new] A narrated explainer video is attached to **[Topic: llm-training-and-post-training](../topics/llm-training-and-post-training/summary.md)**.** Six minutes thirty-seven seconds, 1080p30, one narrator with a second voice carrying the viewer's assumption once. It is a topic overview, so it names the whole board before explaining any of it, and the board here is not a field of competitors. This page is one subject with structure rather than a comparison, so the inventory is the column of stages a model goes through, drawn top to bottom because the order is the argument: pretraining (the next-token objective, the data, the scaling laws), mid-training (the anneal, long context, domain adaptation), post-training (SFT, preference optimisation, RLVR), compression (distillation, pruning, quantization) and serving (sampling and decoding). The column is parked as the home frame and every later beat lights the part of it being discussed.

The organising question is what each stage is actually buying, which is the distinction that only exists once the whole column is visible: each stage buys a different capability, nothing further down the column puts back what an earlier one did not buy, and reaching for the wrong one is the expensive mistake. Then the tour, one stage at a time. Pretraining buys what the model knows, which is why it takes almost all of the compute; the rule for dividing that compute has been Chinchilla since 2022, fixing the split between parameters and tokens for a given budget, and everybody now trains far past it, and the decomposition of 2019--2025 progress puts far more of the compute-efficiency gain on the data side than on the model side, so the rule survives as advice on dividing a budget while its implicit claim that one token is worth as much as another does not. Then the machinery that runs under every stage and is a composition problem rather than a choice: FSDP2 and HSDP for sharded data parallelism, tensor parallelism splitting one matrix multiply across the cards inside a node, pipeline parallelism splitting the layers across nodes because that traffic has to cross the network, expert parallelism for a mixture of experts, context parallelism for the sequence dimension that makes a 128k-token run fit at all, precision moving from bf16 to fp8 with 4-bit formats now arriving for training rather than only for storage, and the Ultra-Scale Playbook's 4000+ benchmarked runs as the reference for picking among them. Then post-training, which buys behaviour in three steps that each buy what the one before could not: SFT buys the format, so the model answers your instruction instead of continuing your text; preference optimisation buys taste, which of two acceptable answers a person prefers, as RLHF or as the DPO family that skips the reward model entirely; and RLVR buys correct answers against a checker, on anything a program can verify, with GRPO as the workhorse and the algorithms themselves left to the RL topic, whose job they are. All three share one failure mode, reward hacking, the policy climbing the proxy you wrote down while the thing you actually wanted stalls or gets worse, which is why KL penalties, reward ensembles and hidden test splits exist. Then the part practitioners almost never publish, drawn as a grid: two organisations priced the two main paths this year, Thomson Reuters mid-training on top of Qwen3.5 on 200B curated tokens selected from a 19T pool for a reported $40M in three months, and Mercor post-training with SkyRL over 1,928 expert knowledge-work tasks for a 70% relative improvement on APEX-Agents, with a fourth row of that same grid saying where each figure came from, one reported rather than audited and the other the lab's own write-up. Then the same shape three orders of magnitude down the budget: a Postgres query planner specialised from 420 trajectories distilled from GPT-6 Astra into LoRA adapters of about 21M parameters on consumer cards, then agentic reinforcement learning with a custom reward and an anchored GRPO variant over about 13.6k queries, producing a 4B model that beat a hand-tuned production planner on its own task for hundreds of dollars, with Periodic's Neon the same shape again at a laboratory budget. It closes on the column turned into a diagnosis rather than a budget: does not know it, that is pretraining or continued pretraining, the expensive answer and the one people reach for first; knows it but will not say it in the shape you need, that is SFT and the cheap one; right in general, wrong on your task, and a program can check the answer, that is reinforcement learning against a verifier. And the precondition both ends of the price list turned on: without a verifier there is no cheap win.

**The finding worth carrying forward is that a parked map's reserve is the one that cannot be fitted the way every other beat's can**. Fitting is least squares: reveal k of n lands at (k-1)/(n-1) x (L - R) and segment k starts at its share of the beat's words, so solve for the R that puts each drawing a second or two after the line that names it. On eight beats here that gave a clean answer and every landing is within about two seconds of its words. On the map it gave 2.1 seconds, which is less than the two-second settle plus the 0.7-second morph that come out of the FRONT of a parked beat's reserve, so the fitted value leaves the finished column no motionless time at all and the format's own premise, that the viewer sees the whole field before any part of it means anything, goes unmet. The general form: fit every beat, then override the parked one upward, and prefer a picture that is a little early to a narrator pointing at something not yet drawn.

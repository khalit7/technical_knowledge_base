# 2026-09-22: explainer video for databases

A single addition: the topic overview video for [Topic: databases](../topics/databases/summary.md), plus the two things the page gained while it was being made.

**[new] A narrated explainer video is attached to **[Topic: databases](../topics/databases/summary.md)**.** Six minutes forty-seven seconds, 1080p30, one narrator with a second voice asking the viewer's questions four times. It is a topic overview, so it names the whole board before explaining any of it. Unlike the last two overviews this topic really is a field of competitors, so the board is families grouped by what the workload is: operational (Postgres and MySQL, MongoDB, Cassandra, Redis and DynamoDB), analytical (ClickHouse, BigQuery, Snowflake, Parquet on S3), specialised index (pgvector and Qdrant, Elasticsearch, Prometheus, Neo4j), and embedded (SQLite, DuckDB, RocksDB). The map is parked as the home frame and every later beat lights the part of it being discussed.

The organising question is the page's own first paragraph, and it is what stops the episode being a catalogue: a database is three stacked choices, a data model, a storage engine and a distribution story, and most categories are the top layer plus one specialised index. So the question is not which product, it is which physical trade each family took. Then the tour. The master trade as a grid, row store against column store on four properties: what is kept together, a point lookup, a big scan, and updates, with one to two orders of magnitude on aggregate scans bought at the price of cheap point updates, which is why analytics belongs on a replica rather than on the production primary. Then the specialised-index column, where vector search is an index type and not a database category: pgvector inside Postgres below roughly ten million vectors, one transaction and one backup and no dual-write skew between a chunk and its embedding, against a dedicated store past a hundred million, or when the predicate has to be evaluated during graph traversal, or when a hard p99 under 10 ms is the requirement, which are index and operations arguments and never data-model ones. Then the convergence, which is the page's real thesis and the most valuable thirty seconds in the episode: Postgres absorbing its neighbours through extensions while the specialists move onto the same substrate from the other side. Then the page's one measured result, late and attributed, 1.81x geometric mean speedup over the Postgres planner from a 4B distillation, best-of-three, 44.7% off join-heavy latency, zero regressions, and why the economics only close where the same analytic query runs again and again. It closes on the five questions in the order the page asks them, and on the two choices you cannot walk back.

The page held every piece of the evidence, Postgres absorbing documents and embeddings and time-series and geometry through extensions, InfluxDB v3 being a Parquet and DataFusion engine, ClickHouse beating a dedicated time-series store at its own job, Elasticsearch scoring dense vectors, a lakehouse table letting five engines read the same bytes, and a feature store turning out to be two ordinary databases with a name, and never once stated the pattern, which is the thing a practitioner can act on: a category boundary is a weak reason to add a system and the axes are a strong one. And How to actually choose gained a closing paragraph on what you cannot walk back: the page says the shard key cannot be cheaply reversed in one place and that the partition key is a one-way door in another, and never adds them up, so it now says that those two are the only irreversible choices here and everything else is a migration you are allowed to regret.

#### Production notes

The inventory axis came from the cut of this episode that was cleared when the method changed, which found it independently and recorded it: families rather than vendors, and the question is which physical trade each family took. That is the one thing worth keeping from it. What was not kept is its second half. Four of its eleven beats were caching, and every figure in them (320 KB of KV cache per token, the 0.99 and 0.75 similarity thresholds, cost-weighted hit ratio) comes from the deep dive Caching: types, policies, and semantic caching rather than from this page. An overview may not out-claim the page it derives from, and the inventory's source is the page, so that is a second episode with its own page to be made from.

A 4,984-word page does not fit in six minutes and a great deal was cut rather than compressed; the script's docstring records what. The omission that hurts most is transactions and isolation, read committed against snapshot against serialisable, write skew, and naming the anomaly before picking the level. It is the most useful section on the page for somebody already running Postgres, and it is an anomaly-by-anomaly walk, which is a deep dive rather than five seconds of a map tour. Also cut: distributed SQL as a family, because it answers a question almost nobody watching has yet earned the right to ask and the page says so itself; CAP and PACELC, which the page already hands to swe-and-system-design; replication against partitioning as separate ideas, surviving only as the shard key in the take; the twelve-row comparison table and the fourteen-line quick chooser, both reference material that reads off a page far better than it plays and whose recitation is the undifferentiated-list failure; the ML data path paragraph, with feature stores surviving as one clause in the convergence because 'a naming convention over two databases' is an argument while the rest is an assignment table; and the DuckLabs acquisition, which is a good story about foundations and intellectual property and not a database story.

**The finding worth carrying forward is about panel choice, and it is arithmetic.** spread now puts the last reveal at the end of the budget, so a beat's motionless tail is the reserve and nothing else, and check_timing caps that at six seconds. The consequence nobody had written down is that a beat's length has to be matched to its number of reveals. A compare panel has exactly two, so on a fifty-second beat the second side cannot be drawn until the narration has been talking about it for twenty seconds, unless the reserve is set to twenty, which is precisely a twenty-second dead frame. The first draft of this episode used compare for both the row-against-column trade and the vector-store threshold, and the preview came back with 19.3 and 22.3 second still frames. The trade became a table, whose header plus four rows is five reveals walking a fifty-five second beat at eleven seconds each, and the threshold became a six-item points walk. Every still frame is now between 2.4 and 5.5 seconds. The shipped data-curation episode has 11.3, 16.4, 21.4 and 22.3 second stills for this exact reason and would fail check_timing today.

**Two silent defects were fixed in the toolchain rather than worked around.** A comparison table's corner cell, above the row labels, is naturally blank, and panel_table placed its cells at absolute x and then arranged the rows with aligned_edge=LEFT, which realigns each row on its own left edge. A row whose first cell is empty has no left edge there, so the entire header slid one column left and sat over the wrong data. Nothing catches it: no overlap, nothing off frame, type well above the legibility floor, layout audit clean. It was visible in the frame and nowhere else. Rows are now stacked by moving them down only. The two existing tables in this series both happen to have a non-empty corner cell, which is why it never surfaced. And the voice gate's invented-word detector rejected the map beat on four seeds in a row at a character error of 0.008: the script spells names out for the voice model, 'S Q Lite, Duck D B, Rocks D B', and the transcriber writes them back joined, 'SQLite DuckDB RocksDB', so three correctly read product names in a row are three words appearing nowhere in the script. The detector already forgave the mirror case, a script compound heard split apart; it now forgives a run of consecutive script words heard joined, and still catches both hallucinations it was built for.

**One defect was caught only by reading the transcripts as text.** The planner beat said 'PostgreSQL query plans' and came back as 'postgres cool'. The character gate passed the beat at 0.011, because one mangled word inside a six-hundred-character take is far under the floor, and no other check looks at meaning. The page says PostgreSQL; the narrator now says Postgres, which is the same claim and is sayable. That is the rule about spelling names the way they should be said, and it cost a re-render to learn again.

**One thing the vocabulary cannot currently do, for whoever changes it next.** beat() runs spread(), then the park morph, then hold(), so the park begins the instant the last column is drawn. On a four-column map that means the complete board exists only for the 0.7 seconds of the morph, and the reserve cannot buy time for it because the reserve is the tail after the park, not before it. It is legible in that window and the narration lands correctly over it, so this was left alone rather than changed mid-batch: altering shared furniture now would make episode six behave differently from the five already published. But 'name everything before explaining anything' is the whole premise of the overview format, and the viewer currently sees the finished map for under a second.

**On length, which is a deviation worth stating.** The skill says plan at about 150 words a minute and aim to land under six. The script is 986 words and lands at 6:47 and 4.32 MiB, which is the third and last 1080p rung of the rate-factor ladder, so it delivers at full resolution with no margin left. The first draft ran to 1,150 words and about eight and a quarter minutes. Two beats read at 164 words a minute, one under the limit: the take, and the planner beat after its re-render came back faster than the take it replaced. Both were left alone rather than rewritten into a reroll with an unknown pace, which is the same call the cuda episode made. Overall the episode runs at 148 words a minute.

**The video was not watched.** Nothing here can play a file, so the proxies were used and are worth naming: a frame pulled per beat and read by eye, including the completed state of every panel; every transcript read as text against its line, which is what caught the PostgreSQL defect; and the per-beat words-per-minute table that check_timing prints. Pace, whether the convergence lands, and whether the take is worth hearing are not covered by any of that.

# 2026-09-22: explainer video for inference-and-serving

A single addition: the topic overview video for [Topic: inference-and-serving](../topics/inference-and-serving/summary.md), plus the one thing the page gained while it was being made.

**[new] A narrated explainer video is attached to **[Topic: inference-and-serving](../topics/inference-and-serving/summary.md)**.** Seven minutes two seconds, 1080p30, one narrator with a second voice asking the viewer's questions three times. It is a topic overview, so it names the whole board before explaining any of it, and the board is the page's own opening sentence rather than a product line: the engines that own a card (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, llama.cpp, MLX), the orchestration above them that owns none (NVIDIA Dynamo, llm-d, Triton Inference Server, Ray Serve, KServe), and the techniques both layers implement (a paged KV cache, continuous batching, prefix caching, speculative decoding, the prefill/decode split). The map is parked as the home frame and every later beat lights the part of it being discussed.

The organising question is the page's own arithmetic, turned into something a viewer can carry: a serving stack is fast for exactly one reason at a time, so where is the request actually stuck? The answer is a three-row table and it is the spine of the episode. Prefill is compute bound, decode is bandwidth bound, and batch size one is bound by neither, where what is left is the cost of launching kernels. Then the tour, one regime at a time. Prefill: chunked prefill co-scheduling pieces of a long prompt so a hundred thousand token prompt stops stalling the batch, prefix caching as the larger win because sixty to ninety percent of agent input is shared, and the prefill/decode split onto separate pools at datacentre scale. Decode: paged attention recovering the sixty to eighty percent that preallocation wasted, continuous batching worth roughly ten times the throughput, MLA at ten to thirty times smaller per token, and speculation that is lossless but fades with batch size. Batch size one: Cohere's fused decode kernel at 292 tokens a second against Cerebras at 1,500 to 1,850, drawn as two bars because the ratio is the argument. Then the objection, which is the sharpest paragraph on the page: a published cache saving that evicts from a cache it already had to build has not saved the prefill, and one that masks entries rather than shortening the sequence has not saved the memory either. The engines are not a beat of their own, deliberately. SGLang arrives as one clause at the end of the prefill beat, where prefix caching has just been explained, and vLLM as one clause inside decode, where paged attention has. That is the thesis of the episode in two sentences instead of a section, and it is why the close says the engine is the last decision rather than the first.

#### Production notes

**The inventory axis came from the cut of this episode cleared when the method changed, which found it independently, and it is the page's own: **the page says outright that decode speed is roughly HBM bandwidth divided by bytes touched per token, and that every technique on it is either "move fewer bytes" or "amortise the same bytes over more tokens". A tour of vLLM against SGLang against TensorRT-LLM is the alternative axis and it is the page's comparison table read aloud, which also makes the engine the first decision when the page treats it as the last. Five things from that cut are not kept. It ran twelve beats, which at this vocabulary's reveal pacing is over eight minutes, one rung below the length where the render fails outright. Not one of its beats carried a reserve. Four of them were two-reveal panels over forty-second lines, a stat, a two-bar chart and two compare panels, which is the still-frame defect four times in one episode. Its prefill beat lit the orchestration column while explaining chunked prefill and prefix caching, which are technique-layer things. And it spent ninety seconds on BITCOS, which is the weights half of bytes per token and cost the beat the objection needed.

**A 4,557-word page does not fit in one episode and a good deal was cut rather than compressed; the script's docstring records what. **The omission that hurts most is cache economics as a product decision, which is the obvious second episode: GLM-5.3-Flash's IndexPool averaging every four lookup vectors to cut the KV cache below a quarter at a million tokens, DeepSeek V4.1-Flash at 890 bytes of cache per token with its causal encoder-decoder split and FP4 cache storage, tiered offload reaching SSD, and the prices each of them buys. That 890 bytes figure was in three drafts of the decode beat and is out of all of them, because the page credits it to a different stack of mechanisms than MLA and a sentence holding both makes a causal claim the page does not. Also cut: BITCOS in full, ternary weights at an information-theoretic 1.585 bits against five-trit packing's 1.625 and BITCOS's 1.485 on the sparsest of 29 models; AgentX and the Vera Rubin evaluation, whose methodology argument is squarely about this page but is hardware and already has its own episode; the vLLM v0.28 and v0.29 and SGLang v0.5.18 release notes, including Model Runner V2 becoming the default and a 2.38x faster startup, because release notes date badly; DFlash2 and block-diffusion drafting at up to 3.43x, which is a deep dive because the argument is about what diffusion is for; and REFRAG at 30.75x with LCLM's architecture search.

**The finding worth carrying forward is that the reveal-pacing rule has a corollary nobody had written down, and it is about the FIRST segment. **spread divides a beat into n equal slices, so a panel with n reveals wants n roughly equal narration segments. What that actually bites on is the opening line, because a beat naturally opens with a short orienting sentence of nine or fourteen words and then runs three thirty-word segments. The head reveal takes a quarter or a fifth of the beat regardless, so every item after it is drawn five to eight seconds after the narration has named it. Measured on the first render of this episode: decode named paged attention at 3.4 seconds and drew it at 11.2, prefill named chunked prefill at 5.2 and drew it at 10.5, overhead named Cohere at 10.9 and drew the bar at 17.1. No check sees any of it. The layout audit was clean, check_timing passed with every still frame under six seconds, and the narrator was pointing at nothing four times. No reserve fixes it either, because the reserve moves every reveal together. Writing the opening segment as long as the others fixed all four and cost one voice pass.

**Two facts about the voice renderer that the method states the other way round. **The first is that an inventory beat is slower than 115 words a minute, not equal to it: this map beat came back at 95, twice, on two different seeds, 110 words in 69.5 seconds, against 50.8, 55.3 and 62.0 for the three map beats before it. Budget a map beat at nearer 100. The second is that the renderer does not search for pace in both directions. It breaks out of its attempt loop on the first take that passes and is at or below 165 words a minute, and only collects several takes to choose the best-paced one when the early takes are too FAST. A slow take is accepted on the first seed, so a beat that comes back at 95 or 140 words a minute cannot be rerolled faster. The only lever on a slow beat is the text.

**Five defects were caught only by reading the transcripts as text, and the classes are not the ones the error rate is built for. **"Paged attention" came back as "page detention" and "continuous batching" as "notice batching", both of them the first words of their sentence, at a character error of 0.026; neither name opens a sentence now, and paged attention still transcribes that way, which is a near-homophone the panel has on screen at that moment. "Baseten's" arrived as "BASINS'" and is written "Base Ten's", which is how it is said. "It scales out too: route to the replica" was heard as "it scales out to root to the replica", which is not a sentence in any language. And the closing clause of the prefill beat came back as "Cache Shipped Over RDMX Cache Shipped Over": a repetition burst and a mangled acronym in the most exposed position a clip has, its last words, at a character error of 0.047 and a no-speech probability of 0.21. R D M A is no longer the last thing said in that beat. The rule about not opening a sentence with a fragile name needs a second half: do not close one with it either.

**One beat ships reported BAD by the voice gate, deliberately, and the reason is the measurement rather than the audio. **The overhead beat scores a character error of 0.073 against a limit of 0.07 and its transcript is correct end to end. The whole excess is the normaliser: the script says "fifteen hundred to eighteen hundred and fifty", the transcriber writes "1500 to 1850", and num2words expands those as "one thousand, five hundred" and "one thousand, eight hundred and fifty", so a correctly read figure scores as six wrong words. Colloquial hundreds are the gap, and it will fire on any beat that says a four-digit number the way a person says it. It was kept rather than rerolled, because rerolling cannot fix a measurement, and because the same beat's real defect was found by reading instead: it originally said "reaches fifteen hundred to eighteen hundred and fifty. Weights on chip", with no unit on the number at all and a fragment the model welded onto it, so the transcript read "1850 weights on chip". The figure carries "tokens a second" now and the fragment has a verb.

**On length, which is a deviation worth stating. **The method says to aim between six and seven minutes and never past seven. This lands at 7:02 and 4.42 MiB, the third and last 1080p rung of the rate-factor ladder, with about a quarter of a megabyte of margin against the 4.7 target, so it delivers at full resolution. Two seconds over is not a rounding error and it is worth being honest about why it could not be trimmed. The pace lever and the length rule pull against each other here exactly as the method warns, and the renderer makes it worse: the map beat is 69.5 seconds for 110 words and cannot be rerolled faster, which is 13 seconds more than the same beat would cost at the budgeted rate, and that 13 seconds is the overrun. Cutting it out means cutting a beat rather than trimming every beat, and the only two candidates were batch size one, which is the third row of the table the whole episode is built on, and the objection, which is the best content on the page. An episode at 7:02 with both is better than one at 6:15 without either.

The idea in one screen

Machines that share data can only talk by messages, and a missing reply never tells you what happened; every technique on this page (replicas, quorums, consensus, fencing, idempotency) is a way to stay correct anyway, paid for in latency or availability.

One chat product, four jobs: split the data, copy each piece, choose what a read may see, agree on who is in charge

  1. Partitioning: users are split over shards, so no machine holds everything.
  2. Replication: each shard has a leader that takes writes and followers that replay its log, a little behind.
  3. Consistency: a read that lands on a lagging follower can miss Ana's own "hello"; a consistency model says whether that is allowed.
  4. Consensus: a small Raft group (etcd) decides which node leads each shard; leases and fencing tokens stop an old leader that has not noticed.

The running example is the parent page's LLM chat product (Topic: swe-and-system-design): users, their chats, the messages in each chat, and each user's paid credit balance. Ana sends "hello" and her page refreshes. The question the whole page answers: which copy does that refresh read, and is "hello" in it?

You need: nothing beyond a database and an HTTP request. The parent page's Numbers to know tab holds the round-trip times used here (0.5 ms in a data centre, 69 ms across the US).

1. Why it is hard: messages, silence, pauses and clocks

One database server has one copy of the truth, and the parent page's advice stands: "a bigger machine and one Postgres go a long way". You leave it for four reasons, each pointing at a different technique: it dies (fix: replication), too many reads (replication again, or a cache), too many writes or too much data (copies do not help, every copy takes every write; fix: partitioning), and users far away (light in fibre needs about 59 ms to cross the Atlantic and back, and Azure measures 78 ms East US to UK South, Numbers to know; fix: replicas near users, which puts the copies far from each other). Every fix puts data on machines that can only talk by message.

Partial failure: silence has four causes

A program on one computer mostly works or crashes. A distributed system has a third state, partial failure: some machines or links work and others do not. The app server sends "save hello" to the database and hears nothing. Predict before you click: in how many of these four cases did the write happen?

The app server's view is identical in all four; the database's is not

The only tool the app server has is a timeout: wait so long, then assume failure. It cannot say what failed, and in two of the four cases (slow, reply lost) the write did happen, in a third (crashed) it may have. That single fact drives most of this page: it is why retries need idempotency, why a "dead" leader may still be alive (leases and fencing), and why Kafka's documentation says of a producer that sees a network error: "it cannot be sure if this error happened before or after the message was committed" (Kafka 4.2 design docs).

Choosing the timeout has no free answer. Too short, and a slow but healthy node is declared dead, a replacement takes over, and two think they are in charge. Too long, and every real failure means a long wait. etcd defaults to a 100 ms heartbeat and a 1,000 ms election timeout, and its docs say the election timeout "must be at least 10 times the round-trip time" (etcd v3.6 tuning).

Partitions, pauses and clocks

  • Network partitions are real. A network partition is a split: two groups of machines each up but unable to reach each other. GitHub, 21 October 2018: maintenance cut the US East Coast data centre off for 43 seconds; automation promoted West Coast replicas to leaders, both coasts held writes the other lacked, and repair took 24 hours and 11 minutes (GitHub post-incident analysis; "gh-2018-10" in the parent's Case files).
  • A process can stop without knowing it stopped. A garbage collector's "stop the world" phase, a virtual machine being live-migrated, swapping, a laptop lid closing: when the program resumes, its code continues from the next line as if no time passed. If it held a lock or a lease (permission that expires), that permission may have been given to someone else. This is the scenario behind fencing tokens.
  • Clocks lie. Every machine's quartz clock drifts. Spanner budgets a worst-case drift of "200 microseconds/second" between synchronisations every 30 seconds (Corbett et al., OSDI 2012), so even Google's machines can be 200 µs/s × 30 s = 6 ms apart before correction. NTP (Network Time Protocol) corrects clocks and can step them backwards. So a computer has two clocks: the time-of-day clock (wall clock; comparable across machines only roughly, and it can jump) and the monotonic clock (only moves forward, meaningless across machines, the right tool for a duration or a timeout). The rule: measure durations with the monotonic clock; never order events across machines by their wall clocks unless the system bounds the error (next section).

The models people reason in. Papers and docs state which failures they tolerate. Crash-stop: a node works correctly until it halts for good. Crash-recovery: it may halt and come back with what it saved to disk (most databases assume this). Byzantine: a node may lie or behave arbitrarily (blockchains; rarely inside one company). On timing: synchronous (delays have a known bound), asynchronous (no bound at all), and the realistic middle, partially synchronous (usually bounded, sometimes not). The consensus algorithms below are safe in the asynchronous model and make progress only when the network behaves for a while.

2. Time and order without a shared clock

Last-write-wins, leases and "which happened first?" all need an order of events across machines, and wall clocks cannot give it reliably. Two ways out: give up physical time and track cause and effect (logical clocks), or keep physical time but bound its error (hybrid and bounded clocks).

Leslie Lamport (1978) defined happened-before (→): a → b if a came before b on the same machine, or a is sending a message and b is receiving it, or there is a chain of these. If neither a → b nor b → a, the events are concurrent: neither could have influenced the other.

  • A Lamport clock is one counter per machine: add one at every event; put the counter on every message; on receiving, set yours to max(yours, received) + 1. It satisfies Lamport's "Clock Condition. For any events a, b: if a→b then C(a) < C(b)", and he points out the converse fails: "we cannot expect the converse condition to hold as well" (Lamport, CACM 1978). A smaller number does not mean "happened before"; it may be concurrent.
  • A vector clock keeps one counter per machine: add one to your own entry at every event; on receiving, take the element-wise maximum, then add one to your own. Now a → b exactly when a's vector is ≤ b's in every entry (and differs somewhere); if each is larger somewhere, they are concurrent. The cost is one number per participant on every message. Dynamo used vector clocks to keep concurrent versions of an item side by side for the application to merge (the shopping cart that keeps both additions); Cassandra chose last-write-wins timestamps instead.

  • Hybrid logical clocks (Kulkarni et al., 2014) pair the wall-clock time with a small logical counter: they capture causality like a Lamport clock while staying close to real time, and fit in a 64-bit timestamp ("HLC captures the causality relationship like logical clocks ...   • TrueTime (Google Spanner) returns not a time but an interval, [earliest, latest], guaranteed to contain the true time, from GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every data centre. In 2012 the uncertainty ε was "typically a sawtooth function of time, varying from about 1 to 7 ms over each poll interval" (the 6 ms climb is the drift budget above). Spanner then waits out the uncertainty before making a commit visible (commit wait), so any transaction that starts after another finished gets a larger timestamp. That is how it offers external consistency, under which "the system behaves as if all transactions run sequentially" (Spanner docs, TrueTime and external consistency). Better clocks mean shorter waits.
Clock bounds in CockroachDB and on AWS (optional)

CockroachDB still needs synchronised clocks: when a node's clock is off from at least half the others "by 80% of the maximum offset allowed, it spontaneously shuts down. This offset defaults to 500ms", with 250 ms recommended for new clusters (CockroachDB operational FAQs).

In November 2023 AWS announced "microsecond-level clock accuracy on supported Amazon EC2 instances" and ClockBound, an open-source daemon that reports the time with an error bound, TrueTime-style (AWS Compute Blog, 16 Nov 2023).

3. Replication: keeping copies

Replication means keeping a copy of the same data on several machines, each copy a replica. It answers a dead machine, too many reads and far-away users. Copying data that never changes is trivial; all the difficulty is in changes. There are three ways to organise who accepts changes.

Single leader (leader and followers)

One replica is the leader (also called primary or master): every write goes to it. It records each change in an ordered log (Postgres calls it the write-ahead log, WAL: every change is written there before the tables are touched, which is also how it survives a crash). The other replicas, followers (secondaries, standbys, read replicas), receive that log and replay it in the same order, so they pass through the same states. Reads may go to any replica. This is Postgres streaming replication, MySQL replication, MongoDB replica sets and Kafka partitions; it is what the parent page called a read replica.

Synchronous or asynchronous: when does the leader say "saved"?

The leader can confirm a write to the client before or after followers have it:

  • Asynchronous: confirm after the leader's own disk has it. Fast, and a slow follower never slows writes. Cost: if the leader dies, writes its followers had not received are lost, even though the client was told "saved".
  • Synchronous: confirm only after a follower has it too. A confirmed write survives the leader's death. Cost: every commit waits a round trip to the follower, and if that follower is down, writes stop (so real setups wait for "any one of several").

Postgres makes this one setting, synchronous_commit, and its documentation is exact about what each value waits for (PostgreSQL 18 docs, synchronous_commit). With no synchronous standby named, only the leader's flush counts. With one named in synchronous_standby_names: on waits until the standby has "flushed it to durable storage"; remote_apply waits until it has "applied it, so that it has become visible to queries on the standby(s)". Those are different moments, and the difference is exactly the stale-read question. So it was measured.

What lag does to readers: the before and after

Asynchronous followers are behind by some amount, the replication lag: usually milliseconds, sometimes seconds or minutes when a follower is overloaded or recovering. Three anomalies follow, and each has a standard fix. Step through each mode; nothing moves until you press play or step.

The same write, four ways of reading it back: two failures and their fixes

Illustrative positions and timing; the stale-read rate and the wait are the measured ones above.

  • Read-your-writes (read-after-write): a user always sees their own writes. Fixes: read data the user may have just changed from the leader (for example, for one minute after their last write), or remember the log position of their last write and have the replica wait until it has replayed that far. The cost is a short wait on the reads that need it, and the session must carry a position.

  • Monotonic reads: once a user has seen some state, they never see an older one. Fix: send each user's reads to the same replica (hash the user id), as in the animation.
  • Consistent prefix reads: if writes happened in an order, readers see them in that order. In the chat, Ben asks "lunch?" and Ana answers "yes". If the two messages are stored on different machines with different lag, a third user can see "yes" before "lunch?". Fix: keep things that are causally related (one chat's messages) on the same partition, so one log orders them; the general tool is causal ordering.

Failover: when the leader dies

If the leader dies, a follower must be promoted to leader and clients must switch to it: failover. Done by hand it takes minutes; done automatically it is fast and dangerous, for three reasons:

  1. Is it really dead? Only a timeout says so (the four silences above). A leader that was merely slow or cut off comes back still believing it is the leader: two leaders accepting writes, split brain. Preventing this is what consensus and fencing are for.
  2. Lost writes. With asynchronous replication, the promoted follower may lack the last writes the old leader confirmed. Those confirmed writes are gone, and if the old leader returns its extra writes conflict with the new history.
  3. Across regions. GitHub's 2018 outage is all three in one: 43 seconds of partition, automatic promotion of the other coast's replicas, writes on both sides, and a 24-hour repair because GitHub "chose data integrity" over a fast fail-back (GitHub). Afterwards it stopped automatic promotion across regions.
Who actually promotes a Postgres follower (optional)

In practice, Postgres itself never promotes anything; tools such as Patroni do it. Patroni "supports a variety of distributed configuration stores like ZooKeeper, etcd, Consul or Kubernetes" (Patroni 4.1 documentation): consensus-backed stores that decide which single node holds the leader key at any moment. Managed services (Amazon RDS Multi-AZ, Cloud SQL) do the same internally.

Multi-leader: writes in several places

With users on several continents, you may want a leader in each region so writes do not cross an ocean. Each leader accepts writes and replicates them to the others. The price is write conflicts: Ana renames a chat to "Trip" on her phone (Europe leader) while her laptop renames it to "Holiday" (US leader). Both succeed locally; when the changes meet, something must choose. Options: last write wins (keep the one with the later timestamp; simple, silently discards the other, and clocks can lie), merge both values, ask the user, or use data types designed to merge automatically (CRDTs, conflict-free replicated data types: counters, sets and text that merge the same way whatever the order). Avoid multi-leader unless you need it; the usual trick is to route all writes for one item (one user, one chat) to one "home" leader, which avoids most conflicts.

Leaderless: any replica takes writes

Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007) made the third style famous: no leader at all. The client (or a coordinator acting for it) sends each write to several replicas and each read to several replicas, and counts answers. Cassandra, Riak and ScyllaDB work this way. Its central idea, the quorum, is the next section.

4. Quorums: N, R and W

In a leaderless system each piece of data is stored on N replicas. A write is sent to all N and counts as successful once W of them confirm. A read asks replicas and waits for R answers, then takes the newest version among them (every value carries a version). The rule that makes this work is one line:

R + W > N
N
replicas that hold each item
W
replicas that must confirm a write
R
replicas a read must hear from

If R + W > N, every read set and every write set share at least R + W − N replicas, so at least one replica the read hears from has the latest successful write. This overlapping set is a quorum. Amazon's Dynamo paper: "The common (N,R,W) configuration used by several instances of Dynamo is (3,2,2)" (DeCandia et al., SOSP 2007). Predict: with N = 3, can W = 1 and R = 2 ever miss the latest write? Set it and see; the read is drawn picking the worst replicas it could.

Overlap is guaranteed only when R + W > N

The knobs trade against each other: lower W makes writes faster and keeps them working with more replicas down (N − W may be down), lower R does the same for reads (N − R).

Cassandra's consistency levels, read repair and anti-entropy (optional)

Cassandra exposes this per query as consistency levels: "QUORUM: A majority (n/2 + 1) of the replicas must respond", ONE, ALL, LOCAL_QUORUM (a majority in the local data centre) and others, and its docs call them "a version of Dynamo's R + W > N consistency mechanism" (Apache Cassandra docs, Dynamo architecture). Replicas that missed a write catch up by read repair (a read that sees an old version writes the new one back) and by anti-entropy: background comparison of Merkle trees, hash trees that let two replicas find differing ranges without sending all the data (Dynamo, Table 1).

R + W > N is not the same as strong consistency
It guarantees overlap, not linearisability (defined below). Abadi: by increasing R + W such systems "gain more consistency at the expense of latency (although they cannot achieve full consistency as defined by Gilbert and Lynch, even if R + W > N)" (Abadi, IEEE Computer, Feb 2012). The gaps: two concurrent writes, settled by timestamps (Cassandra uses "a simpler last-write-wins model where every mutation is timestamped", so a skewed clock picks the winner); a write that reached fewer than W replicas reports failure but is not rolled back where it landed; and a sloppy quorum, Dynamo's choice under failures, accepts writes on replicas outside the usual N ("hinted handoff") so the overlap no longer holds.

Discord, which read and wrote "with quorum consistency level" on Cassandra, also found that one slow replica in a busy partition slowed everyone ("discord-2023" in the parent's Case files).

5. Consistency models: the ladder from linearisable to eventual

"Consistent" is used loosely. A consistency model is a precise promise: Jepsen defines it as "a safety property which declares what a system can do. Formally, a consistency model defines a set of histories that a system can legally execute" (Jepsen, Consistency). A history is what clients observed: who called which operation, when it started, when it returned, and what it returned. To make each model concrete, take one value x (say, the title of Ana's chat, encoded as a number), initially 0. How the verdicts are computed (optional)

Linearisable and sequentially consistent are checked by brute force in your browser: try every ordering of the operations, keep those that respect each client's own order (and, for linearisability, real time: if one operation ended before another began, it must come first), and accept if in one of them every read returns the latest preceding write. With at most six operations that is at most 720 orderings. src/recompute.py runs the same check in Python for every preset. Causal, read-your-writes and eventual verdicts are written by hand for the presets, with the reasoning shown; once you edit a read they show "?" rather than guess.

Now the models, strongest first. Each one is a rule for which histories are allowed:

  • Linearisability (Herlihy and Wing, 1990): "every operation appears to take place atomically, in some order, consistent with the real-time ordering of those operations: e.g., if operation A completes before operation B begins, then B should logically take effect after A" (Jepsen, Linearizability). Picture each operation taking effect at one instant somewhere inside its bar. History 2 fails (the write ended before the read began, so the read must see 1). History 4 fails too, and it is the subtle one: both reads overlap the write, so either answer alone would be fine, but once client 2 has seen 1 and returned, nobody may see 0 afterwards. Linearisability is what makes a system behave like one copy; it is what you need for a lock, a leader, a unique username or "never spend the same credit twice". It is the "strong consistency" of the parent page.
  • Sequential consistency (Lamport, 1979): all operations appear in one total order that every client agrees on, and each client's own operations are in that order in the order it issued them, but the order need not match real time. Histories 2 and 4 both pass: put the read of 0 before the write. A client may be behind, but everyone sees the same story.
  • Causal consistency: operations that are causally related must be seen by everyone in that order; unrelated (concurrent) ones may be seen in different orders by different clients. Jepsen's example is a chat: Attiya asks "shall we have lunch?", Barbarella says "yes" and Cyrus "no"; "no participant ever observes 'yes' or 'no' prior to the question 'lunch?'", but they may disagree on the order of "yes" and "no" (Jepsen, Causal). Its great property: it can stay available during a partition, as long as each client sticks to the same server.
  • Eventual consistency: if writes stop, all copies eventually return the same value. It promises nothing about what you read meanwhile, not even your own write. DynamoDB's default read is this: "the responses might not reflect the results of a recently completed write operation" (DynamoDB read consistency).

Between causal and eventual sit the session guarantees from the replication section: read-your-writes, monotonic reads, and two more (monotonic writes: one client's writes are applied in order; writes follow reads: a write made after reading something is ordered after it). They are cheap and they fix most of what users notice.

Linearisable is not serialisable

Two words sound alike and are different. Linearisability is about single operations on one object and respects real time. Serialisability is about transactions (groups of reads and writes on many objects): the result equals some one-at-a-time order of the transactions, but not necessarily the real-time order. Strict serialisability is both: Jepsen describes it as "serializability's total order of transactional multi-object operations, plus linearizability's real-time constraints" (Jepsen, Strong Serializability). Spanner calls its version external consistency (below). Isolation levels inside one database are taught in Transactions and concurrency; transactions across machines get a short section here.

Correction to the old page
The old page grouped "Sequential / causal: causally related ops are ordered everywhere; concurrent ops may interleave differently". That sentence describes causal consistency only. Sequential consistency is stronger: everyone sees the same single order, concurrent operations included. History 6, "Two observers disagree", separates them: allowed under causal, forbidden under sequential.
The sources behind the correction, and causal+ (optional)

Jepsen: under sequential consistency "operations appear to take place in some total order, and that that order is consistent with the order of operations on each individual process" (Jepsen, Sequential). The old page's "Causal+ is the sweet spot for geo-replicated data" is an opinion; it is the argument of the COPS paper that introduced causal+ (causal plus convergence) for wide-area stores (Lloyd et al., SOSP 2011), and Jepsen notes "Most 'causally consistent' systems actually provide these stronger properties, such as RTC or causal+".

6. CAP read correctly, and PACELC

CAP is the most quoted and most misquoted result in the field. Eric Brewer conjectured it in 2000; Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch proved it in 2002 (Gilbert and Lynch, 2002). Their theorem, in their terms: in an asynchronous network where messages can be lost, it is impossible to implement a read/write object that guarantees both

  • Consistency, meaning precisely linearisability ("atomic" consistency), and
  • Availability, meaning every request received by a non-failing node gets a response (not "the system is mostly up"),

in all executions, including those where the network Partitions. The proof is the picture below: split the replicas into two groups that cannot talk; a write lands in one group; a read arrives in the other. To be available the second group must answer, and it cannot know the new value, so it answers with the old one and breaks linearisability. To stay linearisable it must refuse, and so it is not available.

During a partition the far replica can answer stale or refuse; without one, it can answer fast or wait

Predict: with no partition at all, is there still a trade-off? Try the two "no partition" modes.

Illustrative balances; the 69 ms is the parent page's US cross-country round trip (Numbers to know).

What CAP does not say
  • Not "pick two of three". You cannot opt out of partitions; they happen. The real statement: during a partition, a system that keeps linearisability must refuse some requests. Brewer himself: "you only need forfeit something during an actual partition" (Brewer, 2017).
  • Its C and A are narrow. A system that is neither linearisable nor CAP-available (most real databases in some configuration) is not "breaking" CAP; CAP simply says nothing about it. Causal consistency can be available under partition; linearisability cannot.
  • "CA" is a label for "we assume partitions are rare". Brewer on Spanner: "It is technically a CP system", yet "effectively CA" because Google's network makes partitions so rare that users do not write code for them; he cites Chubby at "an average availability of 99.99958%" and Spanner incidents with the network category "under 8%".
  • It says nothing about latency, which is what you trade every day. That is PACELC.

PACELC (Daniel Abadi, 2012): if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, in normal running, choose Latency or Consistency, because keeping copies in step means waiting for them (Abadi, IEEE Computer, Feb 2012). Abadi's own classification (2012, of the default settings):

In our product: Ana's chat list is PA/EL territory (serve from a nearby replica, slightly stale is fine). Her credit balance is PC/EC (the spend must go through one linearisable place, and if that place is unreachable the spend is refused). One product, two choices, one per invariant.

7. Consensus: Raft step by step

Why it is needed

Several jobs above quietly assumed that the machines agree on something: which node is the leader (or failover makes two), which machine owns which shard, the order of writes in the log. Consensus is the problem of getting several machines to agree on one value, or one sequence of values, such that: everyone who decides decides the same thing; the decided value was actually proposed by someone; and the group keeps deciding as long as a majority is up and can talk.

Why a majority? Two majorities of the same group always share at least one member, so two conflicting decisions can never both collect one. With 2f + 1 machines you survive f failures: 3 machines survive 1, 5 survive 2. An even size buys nothing (4 machines still survive only 1, because the majority is 3). This is the quorum idea again, used for agreement rather than for reading values.

It is harder than it sounds. In 1985 Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved that in a fully asynchronous network no deterministic algorithm can guarantee to reach agreement if even one process may crash (FLP, JACM 1985): you cannot tell a crashed process from a slow one. Practical algorithms therefore promise safety always (never two different decisions) and progress only when the network behaves for long enough, using timeouts to suspect failures. Paxos (Lamport) was the first widely used one; Raft (2014) was designed to give the same result while being easier to understand and build: "It produces a result equivalent to (multi-)Paxos, and it is as efficient as Paxos", and "Results from a user study demonstrate that Raft is easier for students to learn than Paxos" (Ongaro and Ousterhout, USENIX ATC 2014).

Raft, step by step

Raft keeps a replicated log: an ordered list of commands that every machine applies in the same order, so every machine computes the same state (a key-value map, the cluster's configuration). Everything below quotes or follows the paper.

  1. Roles. Each server is a follower, a candidate or the leader. Normally there is one leader and the rest are followers. Clients talk to the leader.
  2. Terms. "Time is divided into terms of arbitrary length, numbered with consecutive integers. Each term begins with an election". A term is a logical clock: every message carries the sender's term, and a server that sees a higher term immediately adopts it and steps down to follower. A leader from an old term is therefore ignored as soon as it talks to anyone.
  3. Heartbeats and the election timeout. The leader sends periodic heartbeats (empty AppendEntries messages). A follower that hears nothing for its election timeout assumes the leader is gone, adds one to its term, becomes a candidate, votes for itself and asks the others for votes (RequestVote). The timeout is random, "chosen randomly from a fixed interval (e.g., 150–300ms)", so usually one follower times out first and wins before others start.
  4. Voting. Each server votes for at most one candidate per term, first come first served. A candidate with votes from a majority becomes leader. This gives the first guarantee: "Election Safety: at most one leader can be elected in a given term." If the vote splits, nobody wins, the term ends, and new random timeouts break the tie.
  5. The election restriction. A server refuses its vote to a candidate whose log is less up to date than its own (compare the term of the last entry first; if equal, the longer log wins). Since a committed entry is on a majority, and a winner needs a majority of votes, every new leader already has every committed entry ("Leader Completeness").
  6. Log replication. A client command becomes a new entry (index, term, command) appended to the leader's log and sent to followers with AppendEntries. Each AppendEntries also carries the index and term of the entry just before the new ones; "If the follower does not find an entry in its log with the same index and term, then it refuses the new entries." The leader then backs up and resends earlier entries until the follower's log matches, overwriting a follower's conflicting uncommitted entries. Result: "if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are identical in all entries up through the given index."
  7. Commit. Once an entry from the leader's current term is stored on a majority, it is committed: it will never be lost or changed. The leader applies it, answers the client, and tells followers the new commit index in later messages. One subtle rule: a leader never counts replicas to commit an entry from an older term (the paper's Figure 8 shows such an entry can still be overwritten); it commits its own term's entry, which commits everything before it.
  8. Reads. A read served by a leader that has silently been replaced returns stale data. Raft's fix: a new leader first commits a blank entry, and "the leader exchange[s] heartbeat messages with a majority of the cluster before responding to read-only requests" (or relies on a time-based lease, if clocks are trusted). Getting this wrong is a classic bug (etcd 0.4.1, 2014; details below).

How the simulation is checked, and what it leaves out (optional)

The model is checked by src/check_raft.mjs: 300 random runs of crashes, restarts, splits and writes, asserting after every event that no term has two leaders and that no committed entry ever differs between servers or changes, and that a write commits once the network heals (0 failures). Simplifications, stated plainly: no log compaction or snapshots, no membership changes, a client write goes to the newest reachable leader, and a restarted server forgets its commit index until the leader tells it (as in the paper, where it is volatile state).

How fast elections are, and a real stale-read bug (optional)

The paper crashed the leader of a five-server cluster repeatedly (broadcast time about 15 ms). With timeouts of 150 to 150 ms (no randomness), elections "consistently took longer than 10 seconds" because of split votes; "Adding just 5ms of randomness helps significantly, resulting in a median downtime of 287ms"; with 12 to 24 ms timeouts "it takes only 35ms on average to elect a leader (the longest trial took 152ms)". The timing rule they give is broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF (mean time between failures), with broadcast "0.5ms to 20ms, depending on storage technology", so "the election timeout is likely to be somewhere between 10ms and 500ms". Production systems are more conservative: etcd's defaults are a 100 ms heartbeat and a 1,000 ms election timeout.

Stale reads in practice. Jepsen found etcd 0.4.1 got this wrong in 2014: it "exhibited stale reads by default" because it "performed reads on any leader, locally"; etcd 3.0 "made linearizability the default for all operations except for watches" (Jepsen, etcd 3.4.3, 2020-01-30).

What consensus costs
Every committed write waits for a round trip to a majority plus a disk flush on each (a durable log entry). Throughput is limited by one leader. A minority side of a partition cannot write at all (that is CAP's "C over A"). So you put small, critical state through consensus (who is leader, which shard lives where, configuration, locks), and keep bulk data in systems that use consensus only to pick a leader. etcd stores "a small volume of infrequently-updated state (by default, up to 8 GB)" (Jepsen, 2020). Never write your own consensus; use etcd, ZooKeeper or Consul, or a database that embeds Raft or Paxos.

8. Leases and fencing tokens

Most systems do not run consensus on every write. They use it to elect one leader, then let that leader work alone. To make "one leader" survive crashes, leadership is a lease: permission that expires after a fixed time (the TTL, time to live) unless renewed. If the leader dies, the lease runs out and someone else takes it. DynamoDB works this way per partition: its replication group "uses Multi-Paxos for leader election and consensus", and "Once elected leader, a replica can maintain leadership as long as it periodically renews its leadership lease" (Elhemali et al., USENIX ATC 2022).

The trap is the pause from section 1. A lease holder that freezes does not know its lease ran out. When it wakes, it carries on writing as if it were still the leader. Martin Kleppmann's 2016 analysis of the Redlock algorithm walks through this with a garbage-collection pause and gives the fix (Kleppmann, How to do distributed locking, 2016-02-08): every time the lock is granted, the lock service hands out a number that only grows, a fencing token, and the storage rejects any write carrying a token lower than one it has already seen: "the storage server remembers that it has already processed a write with a higher token number (34), and so it rejects the request with token 33." Real evidence that this matters:

  • Jepsen, etcd 3.4.3 (2020-01-30): "etcd locks are fundamentally unsafe". With "two-second leases TTLs, five concurrent processes, and process pauses every five seconds, we could reliably induce the loss of ~18% of acknowledged updates", and short TTLs of 1 to 3 seconds "generally failed to provide mutual exclusion after only a few minutes of testing, even in healthy clusters". The fix Jepsen names: "users can use the revision of their lock key as a globally ordered fencing token" (Google's Chubby calls these sequencers; in ZooKeeper, Kleppmann suggests the zxid).
  • AWS, 19 October 2025: two DynamoDB DNS automation workers raced; a slow one had checked its plan was newer than the live one, but "The check that was made at the start of the plan application process ... was stale by this time", and it overwrote a newer plan, leaving the regional endpoint with an empty DNS record for hours (AWS summary; "aws-2025-dns" in the parent's Case files). A check made on old information is not a check; the store itself must reject the older write, which is exactly what a fencing token does.
Correction to the old page
The old page advised taking a lease from etcd or ZooKeeper and to "keep the lease TTL well above GC pauses". A long TTL makes the failure rarer, not impossible: a pause can always outlast it, and Jepsen showed mutual exclusion failing even "in healthy clusters". The old page's next sentence is the real fix and is kept: fence every write with a monotonically increasing token, checked by the storage. Jepsen adds that this gives isolation but "does not guarantee atomicity": a holder that dies halfway through a multi-step update still leaves a half-done state.

Kleppmann's other useful distinction: a lock for efficiency (stop two workers doing the same job twice; an occasional double is harmless) can be simple, even a single Redis key; a lock for correctness (two writers would corrupt data) needs consensus plus fencing.

9. Idempotency and exactly-once: an end-to-end property

Go back to the fourth silence: the database saved the charge and the reply was lost. The client cannot tell this from "request lost", so it has two choices. Never retry, and some charges silently never happen (at-most-once); or retry until it hears "done", and some charges happen twice (at-least-once). No protocol on the wire removes that choice, because the sender never learns which silence it got. What can happen exactly once is the effect: deliver at least once, and make the receiver recognise a repeat. An operation is idempotent when doing it twice has the same effect as doing it once ("set the title to Trip" is; "add 10 credits" is not).

Where the repeat is recognised matters, and that is the end-to-end argument (Saltzer, Reed and Clark, ACM Transactions on Computer Systems, 1984): "The function in question can completely and correctly be implemented only with the knowledge and help of the application standing at the end points of the communication system." On duplicates specifically: "even if the network suppresses duplicates, the application itself may accidentally originate duplicate requests, in its own failure/retry procedures. These application level duplications look like different messages to the communication system, so it cannot suppress them" (Saltzer, Reed and Clark).

One charge, one lost reply, one retry: only a key checked at the end stops the double charge

The recipe, wherever the end is (a payment service, a database, a job handler):

  • The originator names the operation. The client generates a unique idempotency key once, before the first attempt, and sends the same key on every retry. A key minted by a middle hop is a new key per attempt, which is the middle mode above.
  • The end records the key with the effect, atomically. Insert the key and apply the charge in one transaction (a unique constraint on the key does the checking). If they are separate steps, a crash between them leaves a charge with no key, or a key with no charge. On a repeat, return the stored result instead of running again.
  • Or make the operation idempotent by shape: "set to X" instead of "add X", a conditional write on a version number (compare-and-set), or a fencing token, which is the same idea applied to who may write.

Read "exactly-once" claims with this in mind. Kafka's transactions give exactly-once only while input and output are both Kafka topics; any external side effect needs its own deduplication, which Queues, streams and async work shows in detail. Retries done for you are the usual source of the duplicate: Jepsen found that the official etcd Java client "incorrectly retries non-idempotent requests which may have actually succeeded" (Jepsen, jetcd 0.8.2, 2024-08-08). How many times to retry, with what backoff, is Reliability engineering; the HTTP shape of an idempotency key (header, expiry, the stored response) is Service APIs (section 4).

10. Partitioning: splitting the data

Replication copies everything everywhere, so it cannot help when the data or the write rate outgrow one machine. Partitioning (sharding; each piece is a partition or shard) gives each machine a slice. Each slice is usually also replicated, so a real cluster is partitioned and replicated: DynamoDB keeps every partition as three replicas in three availability zones (Elhemali et al., USENIX ATC 2022). The design question is which machine holds which key.

By key range or by hash

  • Key-range partitioning: shard 1 holds users A to F, shard 2 G to M, and so on, like volumes of an encyclopedia. Range queries ("all messages in this chat between Monday and Friday") hit one shard. Danger: keys that arrive in order all land on the same shard; partitioning messages by timestamp sends every new message to the last shard.
  • Hash partitioning: run the key through a hash function and use the hash to pick the shard. Keys spread evenly whatever their pattern. Cost: range queries must ask every shard.

The obvious way to turn a hash into a shard is hash(key) mod N. It is a trap: add one machine (N to N + 1) and almost every key's remainder changes, so almost all data must move. Consistent hashing fixes this. Place each machine at several random points on a circle of hash values; a key belongs to the first machine point clockwise from its own hash. Adding a machine only takes over the arcs just before its points, about 1/(N + 1) of the keys. Each machine gets many points (virtual nodes, Dynamo's "tokens") so its share is even. More is not free: the Cassandra docs warn that every token adds ring neighbours, so "The more tokens you have, the higher the probability of an outage" when several machines fail at once (Cassandra docs). Hot keys, rebalancing, and finding the right shard

  • Hot keys. Hashing spreads keys, not load. One celebrity user, or one busy Discord channel, is one key on one shard. Discord's fix was to stop sending the same query a thousand times (request coalescing) before buying a bigger database ("discord-2023" in Case files). Other fixes: add a random suffix to a hot key so it spreads over several shards (reads must then gather all of them), or cache it.
  • Rebalancing. Rather than consistent hashing, many databases create many more partitions than machines from day one and move whole partitions when machines are added: Instagram created thousands of logical shards on a few Postgres servers in 2012, and Notion split its Postgres into 480 logical shards on 32 physical databases in 2021, choosing 480 because it divides evenly over 40 or 48 hosts later ("insta-2012" and "notion-2021" in the parent's Case files). Moving data is slow and loads the network, so it is throttled and usually triggered by a person or a careful controller.
Secondary indexes and routing across shards (optional)
  • Secondary indexes. Looking a chat up by id is one shard; "all chats mentioning Lisbon" is all of them. A local index (one per shard) makes writes cheap and reads ask every shard; a global index is itself partitioned and kept up to date asynchronously. In DynamoDB, "All reads from GSIs and streams are eventually consistent" (DynamoDB read consistency).
  • Routing. Someone must know the map from key to shard: the client library (Cassandra drivers), a routing tier (Vitess, MongoDB's mongos), or any node that forwards. The map itself must be agreed, which is a job for consensus.

Choose the shard key so the common queries hit one shard and related data lands together: for the chat product, by user (or by workspace, as Notion did). It is among the most expensive decisions to change later.

11. Transactions across machines

A transaction groups several reads and writes so they succeed or fail together (atomicity) and so concurrent transactions do not see each other's half-done work (isolation). Inside one database, the isolation levels (read committed, snapshot isolation, serialisable), write skew and what Postgres and MySQL really give are taught in Transactions and concurrency. What is distributed is the commit itself.

When one transaction touches two partitions, or a database and a message queue, no single machine can commit it. Two-phase commit (2PC) uses a coordinator: phase 1 asks every participant "can you commit?" (each makes the change durable but invisible and promises to obey); phase 2 tells all to commit, or all to abort. Its weakness: a participant that has said yes must wait, holding locks, until it hears the decision, so a dead coordinator blocks everyone. Spanner's answer is to make each participant and the coordinator a Paxos group, so none of them is a single machine: "Running two-phase commit over Paxos mitigates the availability problems" (Corbett et al., OSDI 2012). The everyday version of this problem is the dual write: save a row in Postgres and publish an event to Kafka; a crash between the two leaves them disagreeing. The standard fix, the transactional outbox, is taught in Queues, streams and async work.

Go deeper. 2PC inside sharded Postgres (Citus) and cross-shard queries: Scaling a database. Jepsen's consistency map links every model and anomaly to its definition.

Primary sources cited on this page

Ongaro and Ousterhout: In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm

USENIX ATC 2014 (extended version). Figure 2 is the whole algorithm on one page. About 1.5 h.

Kleppmann: How to do distributed locking

2016. Leases, GC pauses, fencing tokens, and why Redlock depends on timing. About 25 min.

Jepsen: etcd 3.4.3

2020. A model Jepsen report: what was strict serializable, why locks are unsafe, and the fencing fix. About 40 min.

Gilbert and Lynch: Brewer's Conjecture and the Feasibility of Consistent, Available, Partition-Tolerant Web Services

2002. The CAP proof; the definitions are the point. About 45 min.

Brewer: Spanner, TrueTime and the CAP Theorem

2017. CAP from its author, with Google's incident data. About 20 min.

Abadi: Consistency Tradeoffs in Modern Distributed Database System Design

IEEE Computer, 2012. PACELC and the classification table. About 20 min.

DeCandia et al.: Dynamo and Elhemali et al.: Amazon DynamoDB

SOSP 2007 (leaderless, consistent hashing, vector clocks, sloppy quorums) and USENIX ATC 2022 (what DynamoDB actually became: Multi-Paxos per partition). Read them as a pair. About 1 h each.

Corbett et al.: Spanner

OSDI 2012. Paxos groups, TrueTime, commit wait, 2PC over Paxos. About 1.5 h.

Lamport: Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System

CACM 1978. Happened-before and logical clocks. About 40 min.

Bailis and Kingsbury: The Network is Reliable

ACM Queue, 2014. A catalogue of real partitions and what they did (not re-read for this page; the site refuses automated fetches). About 30 min.

Jepsen: all analyses

Every report since 2013; read the one for the database you run. 30 to 60 min each.

1. Why it is hard: messages, silence, pauses and clocks

One database server has one copy of the truth, and the parent page's advice stands: "a bigger machine and one Postgres go a long way". You leave it for four reasons, each pointing at a different technique: it dies (fix: replication), too many reads (replication again, or a cache), too many writes or too much data (copies do not help, every copy takes every write; fix: partitioning), and users far away (light in fibre needs about 59 ms to cross the Atlantic and back, and Azure measures 78 ms East US to UK South, {{Numbers to know|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}}; fix: replicas near users, which puts the copies far from each other). Every fix puts data on machines that can only talk by message.

Partial failure: silence has four causes

A program on one computer mostly works or crashes. A distributed system has a third state, partial failure: some machines or links work and others do not. The app server sends "save hello" to the database and hears nothing. Predict before you click: in how many of these four cases did the write happen?

The app server's view is identical in all four; the database's is not

The only tool the app server has is a timeout: wait so long, then assume failure. It cannot say what failed, and in two of the four cases (slow, reply lost) the write did happen, in a third (crashed) it may have. That single fact drives most of this page: it is why retries need idempotency, why a "dead" leader may still be alive (leases and fencing), and why Kafka's documentation says of a producer that sees a network error: "it cannot be sure if this error happened before or after the message was committed" ({{Kafka 4.2 design docs|https://kafka.apache.org/42/design/design/}}).

Choosing the timeout has no free answer. Too short, and a slow but healthy node is declared dead, a replacement takes over, and two think they are in charge. Too long, and every real failure means a long wait. etcd defaults to a 100 ms heartbeat and a 1,000 ms election timeout, and its docs say the election timeout "must be at least 10 times the round-trip time" ({{etcd v3.6 tuning|https://etcd.io/docs/v3.6/tuning/}}).

Partitions, pauses and clocks

  • Network partitions are real. A network partition is a split: two groups of machines each up but unable to reach each other. GitHub, 21 October 2018: maintenance cut the US East Coast data centre off for 43 seconds; automation promoted West Coast replicas to leaders, both coasts held writes the other lacked, and repair took 24 hours and 11 minutes ({{GitHub post-incident analysis|https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/oct21-post-incident-analysis/}}; "gh-2018-10" in the parent's {{Case files|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}}).
  • A process can stop without knowing it stopped. A garbage collector's "stop the world" phase, a virtual machine being live-migrated, swapping, a laptop lid closing: when the program resumes, its code continues from the next line as if no time passed. If it held a lock or a lease (permission that expires), that permission may have been given to someone else. This is the scenario behind fencing tokens.
  • Clocks lie. Every machine's quartz clock drifts. Spanner budgets a worst-case drift of "200 microseconds/second" between synchronisations every 30 seconds ({{Corbett et al., OSDI 2012|https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/spanner-osdi2012.pdf}}), so even Google's machines can be 200 µs/s × 30 s = 6 ms apart before correction. NTP (Network Time Protocol) corrects clocks and can step them backwards. So a computer has two clocks: the time-of-day clock (wall clock; comparable across machines only roughly, and it can jump) and the monotonic clock (only moves forward, meaningless across machines, the right tool for a duration or a timeout). The rule: measure durations with the monotonic clock; never order events across machines by their wall clocks unless the system bounds the error (next section).
The eight fallacies, failure models, and two real records (optional)

In the 1990s L. Peter Deutsch and others at Sun Microsystems listed the false assumptions that programmers new to distributed systems "invariably make" ({{Wikipedia, with the original list|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fallacies_of_distributed_computing}}):

The false beliefWhat actually happens, and what you do about it
1. The network is reliablePackets are dropped, links are cut, switches are misconfigured. Every call needs a timeout and a plan for "unknown outcome".
2. Latency is zeroA round trip is about 0.5 ms inside a data centre and 69 ms across the US. Ten sequential calls across regions is most of a second: batch, parallelise, keep chatty calls local.
3. Bandwidth is infiniteCopying a big table to a new replica, or rebalancing shards, can saturate links and slow live traffic. Throttle background copies.
4. The network is secureAnything on the wire can be read or forged unless encrypted and authenticated (TLS between services, not only at the edge).
5. Topology does not changeMachines come and go, IP addresses move, autoscaling adds nodes. Discover peers dynamically; never hard-code addresses.
6. There is one administratorYour system spans a cloud provider, a DNS provider, other teams' services. Their changes are your outages (the parent's Case files hold several).
7. Transport cost is zeroSerialising data costs CPU, and cross-zone and cross-region traffic is billed per GB (egress prices in Numbers to know).
8. The network is homogeneousDifferent hardware, kernels, MTUs and client versions behave differently. Test across the mix you really run.

The models people reason in. Papers and docs state which failures they tolerate. Crash-stop: a node works correctly until it halts for good. Crash-recovery: it may halt and come back with what it saved to disk (most databases assume this). Byzantine: a node may lie or behave arbitrarily (blockchains; rarely inside one company). On timing: synchronous (delays have a known bound), asynchronous (no bound at all), and the realistic middle, partially synchronous (usually bounded, sometimes not). The consensus algorithms below are safe in the asynchronous model and make progress only when the network behaves for a while.

Eric Brewer's analysis of Spanner incidents put the network category "under 8%", and "Nor was a Spanner quorum ever on the minority side of a partition", but "We did see individual data centers or regions get cut off from the rest of the network" ({{Brewer, Spanner, TrueTime and the CAP Theorem, 14 Feb 2017|https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/pdf/45855.pdf}}).

Consistency models: what a reader is allowed to see

"Consistent" is used loosely. A consistency model is a precise promise: Jepsen defines it as "a safety property which declares what a system can do. Formally, a consistency model defines a set of histories that a system can legally execute" ({{Jepsen, Consistency|https://jepsen.io/consistency}}). A history is what clients observed: who called which operation, when it started, when it returned, and what it returned. To make each model concrete, take one value x (say, the title of Ana's chat, encoded as a number), initially 0, and look at histories drawn as bars on a time line: each bar is one operation, from the moment the client sent it to the moment the answer came back.

History A: a stale read after the write finished

Client 1 writes 1 and gets "done". Afterwards client 2 reads 0, and later 1. This is the replica lag from the animation.

History B: a new value, then an old one

A slow write of 1 is in progress. Client 2 reads 1; after that read returns, client 3 reads 0.

Now the models, strongest first. Each one is a rule for which histories are allowed:

  • Linearizability (Herlihy and Wing, 1990): "every operation appears to take place atomically, in some order, consistent with the real-time ordering of those operations: e.g., if operation A completes before operation B begins, then B should logically take effect after A" ({{Jepsen, Linearizability|https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/linearizable}}). Picture each operation taking effect at one instant somewhere inside its bar. History A fails (the write ended before the read began, so the read must see 1). History B fails too, and it is the subtle one: both reads overlap the write, so either answer alone would be fine, but once client 2 has seen 1 and returned, nobody may see 0 afterwards. Linearizability is what makes a system behave like one copy; it is what you need for a lock, a leader, a unique username or "never spend the same credit twice". It is the "strong consistency" of the parent page.
  • Sequential consistency (Lamport, 1979): all operations appear in one total order that every client agrees on, and each client's own operations are in that order in the order it issued them, but the order need not match real time. Histories A and B both pass: put client 2's read of 0 before the write. A client may be behind, but everyone sees the same story.
  • Causal consistency: operations that are causally related must be seen by everyone in that order; unrelated (concurrent) ones may be seen in different orders by different clients. Jepsen's example is a chat: Attiya asks "shall we have lunch?", Barbarella says "yes" and Cyrus "no"; "no participant ever observes 'yes' or 'no' prior to the question 'lunch?'", but they may disagree on the order of "yes" and "no" ({{Jepsen, Causal|https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/causal}}). Its great property: it can stay available during a partition, as long as each client sticks to the same server.
  • Eventual consistency: if writes stop, all copies eventually return the same value. It promises nothing about what you read meanwhile, not even your own write. DynamoDB's default read is this: "the responses might not reflect the results of a recently completed write operation" ({{DynamoDB read consistency|https://docs.aws.amazon.com/amazondynamodb/latest/developerguide/HowItWorks.ReadConsistency.html}}).

Between causal and eventual sit the session guarantees from the replication section: read-your-writes, monotonic reads, and two more (monotonic writes: one client's writes are applied in order; writes follow reads: a write made after reading something is ordered after it). They are cheap and they fix most of what users notice.

Try it. The {{Consistency lab|#t-hist}} tab has seven histories (these two and five more) with every model's verdict, checks linearizability and sequential consistency by brute force, and lets you change what each read returned to see the verdicts flip.

Linearizable is not serializable

Two words sound alike and are different. Linearizability is about single operations on one object and respects real time. Serializability is about transactions (groups of reads and writes on many objects): the result equals some one-at-a-time order of the transactions, but not necessarily the real-time order. Strict serializability is both: Jepsen describes it as "serializability's total order of transactional multi-object operations, plus linearizability's real-time constraints" ({{Jepsen, Strong Serializability|https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/strong-serializable}}). Spanner calls its version external consistency (below). Transactions get their own short section.

Correction to the old page
The old page grouped "Sequential / causal: causally related ops are ordered everywhere; concurrent ops may interleave differently". That sentence describes causal consistency only. Sequential consistency is stronger: everyone sees the same single order, concurrent operations included ("operations appear to take place in some total order, and that that order is consistent with the order of operations on each individual process", {{Jepsen, Sequential|https://jepsen.io/consistency/models/sequential}}). The lab's history "Two observers disagree" separates them: allowed under causal, forbidden under sequential. The old page's "Causal+ is the sweet spot for geo-replicated data" is an opinion; it is the argument of the COPS paper that introduced causal+ (causal plus convergence) for wide-area stores ({{Lloyd et al., SOSP 2011|https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dga/papers/cops-sosp2011.pdf}}), and Jepsen notes "Most 'causally consistent' systems actually provide these stronger properties, such as RTC or causal+".

CAP, stated correctly, and PACELC

CAP is the most quoted and most misquoted result in the field. Eric Brewer conjectured it in 2000; Seth Gilbert and Nancy Lynch proved it in 2002 ({{Gilbert and Lynch, 2002|https://users.ece.cmu.edu/~adrian/731-sp04/readings/GL-cap.pdf}}). Their theorem, in their terms: in an asynchronous network where messages can be lost, it is impossible to implement a read/write object that guarantees both

  • Consistency, meaning precisely linearizability ("atomic" consistency), and
  • Availability, meaning every request received by a non-failing node gets a response (not "the system is mostly up"),

in all executions, including those where the network Partitions. The proof is short: split the replicas into two groups that cannot talk; a write lands in one group; a read arrives in the other. To be available the second group must answer, and it cannot know the new value, so it answers with the old one and breaks linearizability. To stay linearizable it must refuse, and so it is not available.

What CAP does not say
  • Not "pick two of three". You cannot opt out of partitions; they happen. The real statement: during a partition, a system that keeps linearizability must refuse some requests. Brewer himself: "you only need forfeit something during an actual partition" ({{Brewer, 2017|https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/pdf/45855.pdf}}).
  • Its C and A are narrow. A system that is neither linearizable nor CAP-available (most real databases in some configuration) is not "breaking" CAP; CAP simply says nothing about it. Causal consistency can be available under partition; linearizability cannot.
  • "CA" is a label for "we assume partitions are rare". Brewer on Spanner: "It is technically a CP system", yet "effectively CA" because Google's network makes partitions so rare that users do not write code for them; he cites Chubby at "an average availability of 99.99958%" and Spanner incidents with the network category "under 8%".
  • It says nothing about latency, which is what you trade every day. That is PACELC.

PACELC (Daniel Abadi, 2012): if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, in normal running, choose Latency or Consistency, because keeping copies in step means waiting for them ({{Abadi, IEEE Computer, Feb 2012|https://www.cs.umd.edu/~abadi/papers/abadi-pacelc.pdf}}). Abadi's own classification (2012, of the default settings):

ClassMeaningAbadi's examples (2012)
PA/ELGive up consistency both under partition and for speed"The default versions of Dynamo, Cassandra, and Riak"
PC/ECNever give up consistency; pay in availability and latency"Fully ACID systems such as VoltDB/H-Store and Megastore", BigTable and HBase
PA/ECConsistent normally, available under partitionMongoDB (as of 2012)
PC/ELFast normally; under partition it does not reduce consistency furtherYahoo's PNUTS

In our product: Ana's chat list is PA/EL territory (serve from a nearby replica, slightly stale is fine). Her credit balance is PC/EC (the spend must go through one linearizable place, and if that place is unreachable the spend is refused). One product, two choices, one per invariant.

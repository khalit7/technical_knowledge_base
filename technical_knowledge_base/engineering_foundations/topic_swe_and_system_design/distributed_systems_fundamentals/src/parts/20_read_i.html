8. Leases and fencing tokens

Most systems do not run consensus on every write. They use it to elect one leader, then let that leader work alone. To make "one leader" survive crashes, leadership is a lease: permission that expires after a fixed time (the TTL, time to live) unless renewed. If the leader dies, the lease runs out and someone else takes it. DynamoDB works this way per partition: its replication group "uses Multi-Paxos for leader election and consensus", and "Once elected leader, a replica can maintain leadership as long as it periodically renews its leadership lease" ({{Elhemali et al., USENIX ATC 2022|https://www.usenix.org/system/files/atc22-elhemali.pdf}}).

The trap is the pause from section 1. A lease holder that freezes does not know its lease ran out. When it wakes, it carries on writing as if it were still the leader. Martin Kleppmann's 2016 analysis of the Redlock algorithm walks through this with a garbage-collection pause and gives the fix ({{Kleppmann, How to do distributed locking, 2016-02-08|https://martin.kleppmann.com/2016/02/08/how-to-do-distributed-locking.html}}): every time the lock is granted, the lock service hands out a number that only grows, a fencing token, and the storage rejects any write carrying a token lower than one it has already seen: "the storage server remembers that it has already processed a write with a higher token number (34), and so it rejects the request with token 33." Real evidence that this matters:

  • Jepsen, etcd 3.4.3 (2020-01-30): "etcd locks are fundamentally unsafe". With "two-second leases TTLs, five concurrent processes, and process pauses every five seconds, we could reliably induce the loss of ~18% of acknowledged updates", and short TTLs of 1 to 3 seconds "generally failed to provide mutual exclusion after only a few minutes of testing, even in healthy clusters". The fix Jepsen names: "users can use the revision of their lock key as a globally ordered fencing token" (Google's Chubby calls these sequencers; in ZooKeeper, Kleppmann suggests the zxid).
  • AWS, 19 October 2025: two DynamoDB DNS automation workers raced; a slow one had checked its plan was newer than the live one, but "The check that was made at the start of the plan application process ... was stale by this time", and it overwrote a newer plan, leaving the regional endpoint with an empty DNS record for hours ({{AWS summary|https://aws.amazon.com/message/101925/}}; "aws-2025-dns" in the parent's Case files). A check made on old information is not a check; the store itself must reject the older write, which is exactly what a fencing token does.
Correction to the old page
The old page advised taking a lease from etcd or ZooKeeper and to "keep the lease TTL well above GC pauses". A long TTL makes the failure rarer, not impossible: a pause can always outlast it, and Jepsen showed mutual exclusion failing even "in healthy clusters". The old page's next sentence is the real fix and is kept: fence every write with a monotonically increasing token, checked by the storage. Jepsen adds that this gives isolation but "does not guarantee atomicity": a holder that dies halfway through a multi-step update still leaves a half-done state.

Kleppmann's other useful distinction: a lock for efficiency (stop two workers doing the same job twice; an occasional double is harmless) can be simple, even a single Redis key; a lock for correctness (two writers would corrupt data) needs consensus plus fencing.

7. Consensus: Raft step by step

Why it is needed

Several jobs above quietly assumed that the machines agree on something: which node is the leader (or failover makes two), which machine owns which shard, the order of writes in the log. Consensus is the problem of getting several machines to agree on one value, or one sequence of values, such that: everyone who decides decides the same thing; the decided value was actually proposed by someone; and the group keeps deciding as long as a majority is up and can talk.

Why a majority? Two majorities of the same group always share at least one member, so two conflicting decisions can never both collect one. With 2f + 1 machines you survive f failures: 3 machines survive 1, 5 survive 2. An even size buys nothing (4 machines still survive only 1, because the majority is 3). This is the quorum idea again, used for agreement rather than for reading values.

It is harder than it sounds. In 1985 Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved that in a fully asynchronous network no deterministic algorithm can guarantee to reach agreement if even one process may crash ({{FLP, JACM 1985|https://groups.csail.mit.edu/tds/papers/Lynch/jacm85.pdf}}): you cannot tell a crashed process from a slow one. Practical algorithms therefore promise safety always (never two different decisions) and progress only when the network behaves for long enough, using timeouts to suspect failures. Paxos (Lamport) was the first widely used one; Raft (2014) was designed to give the same result while being easier to understand and build: "It produces a result equivalent to (multi-)Paxos, and it is as efficient as Paxos", and "Results from a user study demonstrate that Raft is easier for students to learn than Paxos" ({{Ongaro and Ousterhout, USENIX ATC 2014|https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf}}).

Raft, step by step

Raft keeps a replicated log: an ordered list of commands that every machine applies in the same order, so every machine computes the same state (a key-value map, the cluster's configuration). Everything below quotes or follows the paper.

  1. Roles. Each server is a follower, a candidate or the leader. Normally there is one leader and the rest are followers. Clients talk to the leader.
  2. Terms. "Time is divided into terms of arbitrary length, numbered with consecutive integers. Each term begins with an election". A term is a logical clock: every message carries the sender's term, and a server that sees a higher term immediately adopts it and steps down to follower. A leader from an old term is therefore ignored as soon as it talks to anyone.
  3. Heartbeats and the election timeout. The leader sends periodic heartbeats (empty AppendEntries messages). A follower that hears nothing for its election timeout assumes the leader is gone, adds one to its term, becomes a candidate, votes for itself and asks the others for votes (RequestVote). The timeout is random, "chosen randomly from a fixed interval (e.g., 150–300ms)", so usually one follower times out first and wins before others start.
  4. Voting. Each server votes for at most one candidate per term, first come first served. A candidate with votes from a majority becomes leader. This gives the first guarantee: "Election Safety: at most one leader can be elected in a given term." If the vote splits, nobody wins, the term ends, and new random timeouts break the tie.
  5. The election restriction. A server refuses its vote to a candidate whose log is less up to date than its own (compare the term of the last entry first; if equal, the longer log wins). Since a committed entry is on a majority, and a winner needs a majority of votes, every new leader already has every committed entry ("Leader Completeness").
  6. Log replication. A client command becomes a new entry (index, term, command) appended to the leader's log and sent to followers with AppendEntries. Each AppendEntries also carries the index and term of the entry just before the new ones; "If the follower does not find an entry in its log with the same index and term, then it refuses the new entries." The leader then backs up and resends earlier entries until the follower's log matches, overwriting a follower's conflicting uncommitted entries. Result: "if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are identical in all entries up through the given index."
  7. Commit. Once an entry from the leader's current term is stored on a majority, it is committed: it will never be lost or changed. The leader applies it, answers the client, and tells followers the new commit index in later messages. One subtle rule: a leader never counts replicas to commit an entry from an older term (the paper's Figure 8 shows such an entry can still be overwritten); it commits its own term's entry, which commits everything before it.
  8. Reads. A read served by a leader that has silently been replaced returns stale data. Raft's fix: a new leader first commits a blank entry, and "the leader exchange[s] heartbeat messages with a majority of the cluster before responding to read-only requests" (or relies on a time-based lease, if clocks are trusted). Getting this wrong is a classic bug (etcd 0.4.1, 2014; details below).

Five servers, one log: crash the leader, split the network, and the committed entries never change

A real Raft implementation (following the paper's Figure 2) runs in your browser: five servers, random election timeouts of 150 to 300 ms, heartbeats every 50 ms, message delays of 12 to 24 ms, all in simulated time and slowed down so you can watch. What just happened

How the simulation is checked, and what it leaves out (optional)

The model is checked by src/check_raft.mjs: 300 random runs of crashes, restarts, splits and writes, asserting after every event that no term has two leaders and that no committed entry ever differs between servers or changes, and that a write commits once the network heals (0 failures). Simplifications, stated plainly: no log compaction or snapshots, no membership changes, a client write goes to the newest reachable leader, and a restarted server forgets its commit index until the leader tells it (as in the paper, where it is volatile state).

How fast elections are, and a real stale-read bug (optional)

The paper crashed the leader of a five-server cluster repeatedly (broadcast time about 15 ms). With timeouts of 150 to 150 ms (no randomness), elections "consistently took longer than 10 seconds" because of split votes; "Adding just 5ms of randomness helps significantly, resulting in a median downtime of 287ms"; with 12 to 24 ms timeouts "it takes only 35ms on average to elect a leader (the longest trial took 152ms)". The timing rule they give is broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF (mean time between failures), with broadcast "0.5ms to 20ms, depending on storage technology", so "the election timeout is likely to be somewhere between 10ms and 500ms". Production systems are more conservative: etcd's defaults are a 100 ms heartbeat and a 1,000 ms election timeout.

Stale reads in practice. Jepsen found etcd 0.4.1 got this wrong in 2014: it "exhibited stale reads by default" because it "performed reads on any leader, locally"; etcd 3.0 "made linearizability the default for all operations except for watches" ({{Jepsen, etcd 3.4.3, 2020-01-30|https://jepsen.io/analyses/etcd-3.4.3}}).

What consensus costs
Every committed write waits for a round trip to a majority plus a disk flush on each (a durable log entry). Throughput is limited by one leader. A minority side of a partition cannot write at all (that is CAP's "C over A"). So you put small, critical state through consensus (who is leader, which shard lives where, configuration, locks), and keep bulk data in systems that use consensus only to pick a leader. etcd stores "a small volume of infrequently-updated state (by default, up to 8 GB)" (Jepsen, 2020). Never write your own consensus; use etcd, ZooKeeper or Consul, or a database that embeds Raft or Paxos.

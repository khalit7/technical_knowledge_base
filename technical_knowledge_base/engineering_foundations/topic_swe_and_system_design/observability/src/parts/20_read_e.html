13. One incident, debugged two ways

Same incident, same data, two toolkits: an average-latency graph and a log search, against percentiles from a histogram, exemplars and traces. Every line, number, log entry and span is from the real run; only the engineer's reasoning in the steps is written for the page. Step through one toolkit, then switch.

  1. Normal: one graph, the average. The service dashboard shows average latency: about 323 ms. Your toolkit is this graph and a log search.
  2. The incident: the average rises. Average latency climbs to about 441 ms (+37%). Is every request a bit slower, or are some much slower? The average cannot say.
  3. Open the logs. Fourteen consecutive chat-api lines from the incident (lines over 800 ms highlighted for you; nothing highlights them for the engineer). Every successful request says "request served" with its total duration; nothing says where the time went.
  4. Search for errors. level = ERROR finds 12 lines in the 90-second incident, all "request failed: retrieval timed out". A lead, but errors are 1.7% of requests, while 91 successful requests also took over 800 ms.
  5. The slow successes have no story. Sorting the successful requests by duration finds them, but each is one number. Retrieval? The model? The database? A log line written at the end of a request cannot split its time.
  6. It recovers; the cause stays unknown. The cold replica warms and latency returns. The write-up can only say "retrieval sometimes timed out and things were slower". Next step: add logging, redeploy, and wait for it to happen again.
  1. Normal: p50 and p99 from the histogram. Same service, same moment. p50 about 356 ms, p99 about 621 ms (histogram estimates).
  2. The incident: only the tail moves. p50 barely moves (382 ms); p99 jumps to 1,462 ms. Not everyone is slower: a minority of requests is much slower. That narrows the question to "what do the slow ones have in common?"
  3. Follow an exemplar to a trace. The dot on the p99 line is an exemplar: a real request's trace ID stored with the bucket it landed in. It opens this trace: 1,497 ms in total.
  4. The waterfall points at one span. Of 1,497 ms, the call to retrieval took 912 ms, nearly all of it inside "vector index lookup" (910 ms) on replica r3-cold. The model call took a normal 579 ms.
  5. An error trace from the same minute. chat-api timed out at 1,015 ms and answered 503; retrieval kept working until 1,517 ms on a request nobody was waiting for. Two findings: the same slow replica, and a missing deadline.
  6. Confirm across all requests, then act. Group every retrieval lookup in the incident by its replica attribute: r1 and r2 answered in a median of about 19 ms; all 103 lookups on r3-cold took over 500 ms (median 910 ms). Action: drain r3-cold. Tail sampling kept every one of the 90 slow or failed traces to check against.

The difference is not effort or talent. With averages and logs, the slow-but-successful requests are invisible: they log "request served" like everyone else, and the average blends them into a mild rise. With a p99 the tail shows as its own line; an exemplar links the line to a request; the trace shows where its time went across a process boundary; grouping spans by an attribute confirms it. Real numbers from the incident phase: replicas r1 and r2 answered in a median of about 18 ms, while every one of the lookups on r3-cold took over 500 ms (median ms). In this demo the slow replica is labelled by construction; in production the attribute would carry the real host or pod name, and the query would be the same.

The error trace also shows a second problem: chat-api gave up after its 1-second timeout and answered 503, but retrieval kept working for another half second on a request nobody was waiting for. Passing the deadline down with the call fixes that; deadline propagation is taught on {{Reliability engineering|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8152b8dececf34196174}}.

14. Dashboards that help

A dashboard is a saved page of graphs. The SRE book's test: dashboards "should answer basic questions about your service, and normally include some form of the four golden signals", and teams "carefully avoid any situation that requires someone to 'stare at a screen to watch for problems'" ({{SRE book, ch. 6|https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/}}): alerts watch, dashboards explain. The first row of chat-api's dashboard is on the right, from the real Prometheus queries over the demo run (shaded: the incident).

A layout that works, top to bottom: (1) is it healthy for users: RED for the service, with the SLO drawn as a line; (2) its dependencies, the same three graphs per dependency as the client sees them (the bottom-right panel found the incident); (3) its resources, USE for CPU, memory, connection pools, GPUs; (4) deploy and config-change markers on every graph, since "roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system" ({{SRE book, ch. 1|https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/}}). Show p50 and p99 together, never only the mean; keep one dashboard per service in the same layout; and prune: "Signals that are collected, but not exposed in any prebaked dashboard nor used by any alert, are candidates for removal."

The error-ratio panel starts only at the first failure: the 503 series did not exist until a 503 happened (client libraries create a labelled series on first use), so before that the query returns nothing rather than 0, and dashboards and alerts read "nothing" as "no data", not "fine". Pre-create the label values you expect (in the Python client, calling .labels(route, "503") once at start-up creates the series at 0) or write the query to treat a missing series as zero. Likewise a rate() window needs at least two samples, so a window as short as the scrape interval returns nothing: use windows several times the scrape interval.

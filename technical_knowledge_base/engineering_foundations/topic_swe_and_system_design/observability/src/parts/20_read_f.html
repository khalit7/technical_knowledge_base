15. Alerting: when to wake a human

An alert is a rule evaluated over and over (every minute, say) that notifies someone when it becomes true. A page interrupts a person now, a ticket waits for working hours, and anything less urgent belongs on a dashboard ({{SRE book, ch. 6|https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/}}). The philosophy every on-call team rediscovers:

"Every time the pager goes off, I should be able to react with a sense of urgency. I can only react with a sense of urgency a few times a day before I become fatigued. Every page should be actionable." ... "Pages with rote, algorithmic responses should be a red flag."

Symptoms, not causes

"Your monitoring system should address two questions: what's broken, and why?" The symptom is what users experience; the cause is why:

Symptom (page on these)Possible cause (dashboards, tickets)
Replies failing (5xx ratio burning the error budget)A retrieval replica is cold; the model provider is down; a bad deploy
Replies slow (latency SLO burning)Database connection pool exhausted; GPU queue long; one tenant sending huge prompts
Wrong answers with status 200A model or prompt change; a serving bug (the Anthropic 2025 case)

Cause alerts ("CPU above 80%", "retrieval p99 above 500 ms") wake people for things users may not feel and miss the failures nobody predicted; symptom alerts catch everything that hurts users, whatever the cause. So spend "much more effort on catching symptoms than causes; when it comes to causes, only worry about very definite, very imminent causes", such as a disk full in four hours. "One person's symptom is another person's cause": retrieval's own team pages on retrieval's latency SLO.

Alerting on an SLO: burn rate

The symptom to page on is "we are spending the error budget too fast" (SLOs and budgets as policy: {{Reliability engineering|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8152b8dececf34196174}}). An SLO is a target such as "99.9% of messages succeed over 30 days"; the error budget is 1 − SLO = 0.1% of requests; the burn rate is "how fast, relative to the SLO, the service consumes the error budget". With a 99.9% SLO, a steady 0.1% error ratio is a burn rate of 1 and uses exactly the whole budget in 30 days; 1% is a burn rate of 10 and uses it in 3 days ({{SRE workbook, ch. 5|https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/}}).

burn rate = error ratio ÷ (1 − SLO)     budget spent = burn rate × window ÷ period

The workbook builds its alert in six steps, each fixing the previous one's flaw, judged on precision (share of alerts that were significant), recall (share of significant events that alerted), detection time and reset time (how long it keeps firing after the problem ends):

#Rule (99.9% SLO)What goes wrong, in the workbook's numbers
1Error ratio over 10 min ≥ 0.1%Fast (0.6 s for a total outage) but noisy: a 10-minute blip spending 0.02% of the budget pages; "up to 144 alerts per day"
2Over 36 h > 0.1%Pages only after 5% of the budget, but after a total outage keeps firing for 36 hours
3Over 1 min > 0.1%, held for: 1hA total outage pages after an hour, having spent 140% of the budget; flapping errors reset the timer and never page
4Over 1 h > 36 × 0.1%Good precision; a 35x burn never pages yet empties the budget in 20.5 hours; resets after 58 minutes
5Page: 1 h > 14.4x or 6 h > 6x; ticket: 3 d > 1xGood recall; reset times of hours to days; one incident sends several notifications
6Rule 5 with a short window as well (1/12 of the long one): 1 h and 5 min > 14.4x, or 6 h and 30 min > 6x; tickets on 24 h and 2 h > 3x, or 3 d and 6 h > 1xThe recommended one: pages only while the budget is still burning, and stops about 5 minutes after the errors do

The thresholds come from choosing how much budget is "significant": 2% of a 30-day budget in 1 hour is a burn rate of 0.02 × 720 h / 1 h = 14.4; 5% in 6 hours is 6; 10% in 3 days is 1.

Correction to the workbook

In its low-traffic section the workbook says that at 10 requests per hour "a single failed request results in an hourly error rate of 10%. For a 99.9% SLO, this request constitutes a 1,000x burn rate and would page immediately, as it consumed 13.9% of the 30-day error budget." A 10% error ratio against a 0.1% budget is a burn rate of 10% ÷ 0.1% = 100, not 1,000. The 13.9% is right: 30 days at 10 requests an hour is 7,200 requests, the budget is 7.2 failures, and one failure is 1 ÷ 7.2 = 13.9% (checked in src/recompute.py).

Real rules, tested. The {{Real telemetry|#t-real}} tab holds the approach-6 rules written for chat-api as a Prometheus rules file, with recording rules for each window and a runbook_url on every alert, plus a unit test. promtool test rules (Prometheus 3.15.0) feeds it three clean days and then 10 minutes at 15% errors (the workbook's figure 5-6): the page fires at minute 6 of the incident and stops at minute 14, against the workbook's "after 5 minutes" and "5 minutes after the errors stop"; the minute of difference comes from evaluating once a minute and from how rate() extrapolates at window edges. A typo in a label matcher makes an alert silently never fire, so test rules like code, in CI.

Low traffic breaks this. At 10 requests an hour one failure is 13.9% of the month's budget. The workbook's options: synthetic traffic (probers), combine small services into one SLO, make clients retry so one failure matters less, or accept a lower SLO. At very high targets the arithmetic stops helping: at 99.999% a total outage empties a 30-day budget in 26 seconds, faster than most alert pipelines; there the defence is design (canaries that expose 1% of users first), not alerting.

From alert to the right person

Prometheus evaluates rules; Alertmanager decides who hears about them. It "takes care of deduplicating, grouping, and routing them to the correct receiver integration such as email, PagerDuty, or OpsGenie", plus inhibition, "suppressing notifications for certain alerts if certain other alerts are already firing" (a cluster-down alert silences the hundred per-service alerts it causes), and silences, "a straightforward way to simply mute alerts for a given time" during planned work ({{Alertmanager|https://prometheus.io/docs/alerting/latest/alertmanager/}}). Grouping and inhibition are the main technical cures for alert storms.

A runbook (the SRE book says playbook) is the page the on-call engineer opens from the alert; recording best practices in one "produces roughly a 3x improvement in MTTR as compared to the strategy of 'winging it'" (MTTR: mean time to repair, {{SRE book, ch. 1|https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/}}). Keep it short: what the alert means and what users see; links to the dashboard and a pre-filtered trace search; the first three checks; the safe mitigations (roll back, drain a replica, fail over, switch to the fallback model); whom to escalate to. Link it from the alert (our rules carry runbook_url); if a runbook is purely mechanical, automate it.

Alert fatigue: the Bigtable story (under 1 min)

Too many pages and people "second-guess, skim, or even ignore incoming alerts, sometimes even ignoring a 'real' page that's masked by the noise". In the SRE book's Bigtable story, alerts on a latency SLO driven by a slow tail fired "voluminously"; the team "temporarily dialed back" the SLO to the 75th percentile and disabled email alerts, which gave them room to fix the underlying storage problems. Fewer, better alerts made the service more reliable, not less. A practical habit: review every page weekly, ask whether it was actionable, and delete or demote the ones that were not.

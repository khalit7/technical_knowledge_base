Design 4News feed and timeline: fan-out on write against on read, and celebrities

A news feed (home timeline) shows each user the recent posts of the accounts they follow, newest first. The classic case is Twitter around 2012: its engineers published the numbers, and one decision, when to do the merging work, dominates everything else.

Requirements
Functional
  • Post a short message; follow and unfollow.
  • Home timeline: newest posts from followed accounts, paginated.
  • Out of scope at first: ranking, search, replies.
Non-functional
  • Twitter's published scale (2012): "150M world wide active users, handles 300K QPS to generate timelines"; "5K/sec daily average; 7K/sec daily peak; >12K/sec during large events" for posts; "30b deliveries / day" ({{High Scalability, 2013-07-08|http://highscalability.com/blog/2013/7/8/the-architecture-twitter-uses-to-deal-with-150m-active-users.html}}, notes on Raffi Krikorian's QCon 2012 talk {{Timelines at Scale|https://www.infoq.com/presentations/Twitter-Timeline-Scalability/}}; the blogger's notes, not a transcript).
  • Timeline reads in hundreds of ms end to end; a new post visible to followers within seconds.

What the numbers say. Reads outnumber posts 60 to 1. Assembling each timeline when it is read (fan-out on read: look up everyone you follow, fetch their recent posts, merge) is about 22 million index lookups a second. Copying each post into every follower's timeline when it is written (fan-out on write) makes a read one lookup and moves the work to 375,000 deliveries a second, about 1.1 million inserts counting three copies. Twitter chose write. The bill: 2.9 TB of RAM to hold 800 entries for every user, and one post by an account with 31 million followers is 93 million inserts.

The gap widens with the number of accounts followed (on-read work grows with it; a precomputed read stays one index range). The price: every post stored once per follower (8 million rows, 766 MB for this toy), and at 31 million followers one post takes minutes to fan out.

The decision that matters: celebrities, and the hybrid

The talk notes: "it can take up to 5 minutes for a tweet to flow from Lady Gaga's fingers to her 31 million followers", and the plan was "Not fanning out the high value users anymore. For people like Taylor Swift don't bother with fanout anymore, instead merge in her timeline at read time. Balances read and write paths" (High Scalability, 2013). This hybrid is the expected answer: accounts under a follower threshold fan out on write; accounts over it are stored once and merged into each reader's timeline at read time (a handful of extra lookups, not hundreds); inactive users get no maintained timeline and have it rebuilt on login, which cuts the 2.9 TB to the active users' share.

The lesson that generalises: on write or on read is where to spend work

Spend it when something is produced if reads vastly outnumber writes and the result is reused; spend it when it is consumed if writes fan out enormously, or the result is personal and changes per read. Caches (design 1), materialised views and search indexes are the same decision under other names. Ranking moved Twitter itself back to read time (closed block below).

Deep dives: deletes and unfollows, and how ranking changes the question

Deleting a post must remove it from millions of timelines (store ids, not copies of the text, and filter deleted ids at read time); unfollowing leaves old entries (filter at read time or accept it); the fan-out runs on a queue of delivery jobs, so a burst of posts delays delivery rather than failing posts (backpressure: {{Queues, streams and async work, Backpressure and Little's law|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81aebf91df927f215b6e}}).

A chronological timeline is a merge; a ranked one ("For You") is a search problem. Twitter's 2023 description: the system must "distill the roughly 500 million Tweets posted daily down to a handful", first by candidate sourcing ("we attempt to extract the best 1500 Tweets from a pool of hundreds of millions"), then a "~48M parameter neural network" ranks them; the timeline is "50% In-Network Tweets and 50% Out-of-Network Tweets on average"; the pipeline "runs approximately 5 billion times per day and completes in under 1.5 seconds on average", and "A single pipeline execution requires 220 seconds of CPU time". Most strikingly: "We recently stopped using Fanout Service, a 12-year old service that was previously used to provide In-Network Tweets from a cache of Tweets for each user" ({{Twitter engineering, 2023-03-31, Wayback copy|http://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://blog.x.com/engineering/en_us/topics/open-source/2023/twitter-recommendation-algorithm}}). Once most of a feed comes from accounts you do not follow, precomputing per-follower lists stops paying, and the work moves back to read time, with heavy caching and machine learning. Facebook had made the read-time choice from the start: an aggregator "fanned out the request to all the leaves to fetch data, rank and filter data", where leaves keep recent actions in memory ({{Zhu, Facebook Multifeed, 2015-03-10|https://engineering.fb.com/2015/03/10/production-engineering/serving-facebook-multifeed-efficiency-performance-gains-through-redesign/}}).

What interviewers listen for
  • You computed the read-to-write ratio first and let it choose fan-out on write.
  • You raised the celebrity problem yourself and proposed the hybrid, with a threshold.
  • You estimated the timeline cache memory and trimmed it (cap at N entries, active users only).
  • Deletes, unfollows and ranking were at least mentioned.

12. SLIs, SLOs and error budgets: how reliable is enough

From the SRE book ({{ch. 4|https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/}}): an SLI is "a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service that is provided", best written as "the number of good events divided by the total number of events" ({{SRE workbook, ch. 2|https://sre.google/workbook/implementing-slos/}}); an SLO is "a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI", by default over "a four-week rolling window" (whole weeks, "so it always contains the same number of weekends"); an SLA is a contract "that includes consequences of meeting (or missing) the SLOs" ("if there is no explicit consequence, then you are almost certainly looking at an SLO"). S3 keeps the gap on purpose: designed for 99.99% availability, with "an availability SLA of 99.9%" ({{S3 storage classes|https://aws.amazon.com/s3/storage-classes/}}).

The workbook's menu for request-driven services is availability, latency and quality (responses "served in an undegraded state"); for pipelines freshness, correctness and coverage; for storage durability; "five or fewer" SLI types. A first set for the chat assistant, measured at the load balancer or in the app:

Serving-level SLOs for an LLM endpoint (time to first token, time per output token, goodput) are {{LLM serving in production|n:3f35c17b0d0d818aa406da1f7a784c62}}'s.

The error budget is "100% minus the SLO" (workbook). At 10 million messages a day and 99.9% it is 10,000 failed messages a day, 300,000 per 30 days (the SRE book's own: "a system that serves 2.5M requests in a day with a daily availability target of 99.99% can serve up to 250 errors"). It turns arguments into arithmetic: while budget remains, "new releases can be pushed"; when it is spent, "releases are temporarily halted" ({{ch. 3|https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/}}). Google's example policy: "halt all changes and releases other than P0 issues or security fixes until the service is back within its SLO", and any incident using more than 20% of the budget gets a postmortem with at least one top-priority action item; "This policy is not intended to serve as a punishment for missing SLOs" ({{Example error budget policy, 2018|https://sre.google/workbook/error-budget-policy/}}).

Alerting on the budget: burn rate

"Error rate above 0.1%" pages for blips and misses slow leaks. The burn rate is "how fast, relative to the SLO, the service consumes the error budget"; at 99.9% over 30 days "a constant 0.1% error rate uses exactly all of the error budget: a burn rate of 1" ({{SRE workbook, ch. 5|https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/}}). Window: 28 days, the workbook's "four-week rolling window" (its thresholds are quoted for 30 days; over 28 days they spend 7% more budget, shown rather than hidden). An alert fires when the error ratio over both its windows exceeds threshold × (1 − SLO), minute by minute; requests are spread evenly over the day (a night-time incident spends less). Low traffic breaks burn-rate alerts: at 10 requests an hour one failure is a 10% error ratio and 1 ÷ 7.2 = 13.9% of a 30-day budget at 99.9%. A slip to know: the workbook's low-traffic example calls that one failure "a 1,000x burn rate", but 10% ÷ 0.1% = 100x (its 13.9% is right). 13. Incidents and postmortems

An incident is an unplanned event that hurts or threatens users and needs coordinated work. Declare one if any holds: "Do you need to involve a second team in fixing the problem? Is the outage visible to customers? Is the issue unsolved even after an hour's concentrated analysis?"; "It is better to declare an incident early and then find a simple fix and close out the incident than to have to spin up the incident management framework hours into a burgeoning problem" ({{SRE book, ch. 14|https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/}}).

Mitigate before you understand: if a deploy went out an hour ago, roll it back now. Name the roles at once: an incident commander who coordinates and does not fix, an operations lead who alone changes the system, a communications lead, and a scribe (details below). An update can be one line: "Some replies are failing. We have rolled back a recent change and are watching recovery. Next update 14:30 UTC." PagerDuty: "Never hesitate to page the IC" ({{During an incident|https://response.pagerduty.com/during/during_an_incident/}}).

Incident roles, in their sources' words

Roles, from the Incident Command System used by fire services (the workbook's "three Cs": coordinate, communicate, maintain control, {{ch. 9|https://sre.google/workbook/incident-response/}}): the incident commander "holds the high-level state about the incident" and is "NOT a resolver" ({{PagerDuty roles|https://response.pagerduty.com/before/different_roles/}}); the operations lead: "The operations team should be the only group modifying the system during an incident"; the communications lead issues "periodic updates"; the scribe "documents the timeline", since the IC's "most important responsibility is to keep a living incident document" (SRE book).

A postmortem is the written account: what happened, why, what will change. Google writes one for user-visible downtime beyond a threshold, any data loss, any on-call intervention such as a rollback, a long resolution or a monitoring failure ({{SRE book, ch. 15|https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/}}). Blameless: "identifying the contributing causes of the incident without indicting any individual or team", because "You can't 'fix' people, but you can fix systems and processes"; Allspaw (Etsy, 2012): without accounts given "without fear of punishment or retribution" the organisation never learns how it happened, which "all but guarantees that it will repeat" ({{Code as Craft, archived|https://web.archive.org/web/20231230191519/https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/blameless-postmortems/}}). Headings from the book's {{example postmortem|https://sre.google/sre-book/example-postmortem/}} (Shakespeare search, "down for 66 minutes", ending "Freeze production until 2015-11-20 due to error budget exhaustion"): Summary, Impact, Root causes (the sustaining effect, not just the trigger), Trigger, Resolution, Detection, Action items (type, owner, bug), Lessons learned (what went well, wrong, where we got lucky), Timeline.

A short example on the running product illustrative incident

Summary. For 23 minutes, 31% of chat replies failed. Impact. About 50,000 failed messages: 17% of the 30-day error budget at 99.9% and 10 million messages a day. Trigger. The provider's latency rose from 1 s to 20 s in one region for 4 minutes. Root causes. (1) a 60 s provider timeout, so workers filled; (2) the app, the API and the gateway each retried 3 times (27 attempts per message), keeping the provider overloaded after its incident ended, a metastable failure; (3) history shared the worker pool and failed too. Detection. The 14.4x burn-rate page fired 5 minutes in. Resolution. Kill switch to the fallback model; retries drained. Action items. 5 s time-to-first-token timeout; retries only in the API, with a 10% budget; a separate pool for history; a breaker with the fallback as its open state; a monthly game day on the fallback. Where we got lucky. The fallback provider had spare capacity.

Postmortem mistakes: "human error" as the root cause (it is where the investigation starts); stopping at the trigger; action items nobody owns; Five Whys chains that pick one linear story out of several causes; skipping near misses, which teach the same lessons for free.

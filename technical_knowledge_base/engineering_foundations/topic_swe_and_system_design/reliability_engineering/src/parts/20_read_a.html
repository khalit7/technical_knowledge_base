Our running example is the parent topic's chat assistant: a phone app sends a message to our chat API (the program we run on our servers), which saves the message in a database and calls a model provider (a separate company's service that generates the reply). Every arrow in that sentence is a network call to something we do not control, and at the parent root's scale of 10 million messages a day, "rarely" means "every few minutes". Reliability engineering is the craft of keeping the product working anyway, and of deciding how much "working" is enough.

Three ideas carry most of this page

1. Slow is worse than down. A dependency that is down fails fast; one that is slow quietly holds your workers, queues and memory until you fall over too. Timeouts, breakers and bulkheads exist to turn "slow" back into "fast failure".

2. Every defence adds load or removes work. Retries add load; shedding, breakers and deadlines remove work. A system recovers only when the work removed beats the work added, which is why the measured retry storm below never recovered with backoff and jitter alone, and recovered at once with a retry budget or a bounded queue.

3. Reliability is a number you choose and then spend. 100% is impossible and each extra nine costs roughly ten times the last; pick a target per user journey, measure it, and spend the remainder on shipping.

What "reliable" means, and what it costs

"Reliable" bundles three different promises, and a product can keep one while breaking another:

  • Availability: the service answers when asked. Google's SRE book gives two ways to measure it: by time, uptime / (uptime + downtime), or by requests, successful requests / total requests; for a service spread over many machines, which is never fully up or fully down, it uses the request form, "availability in terms of the request success rate" ({{SRE book, ch. 3|https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/}}).
  • Durability: data you were told is saved is still there later. It is a separate number: Amazon S3 is "designed to provide 99.999999999% (11 nines) data durability" while S3 Standard is designed for "99.99% availability" ({{S3 FAQ|https://aws.amazon.com/s3/faqs/}}). A chat history that is temporarily unreachable is an availability problem; one that is gone is a durability problem, and far worse.
  • Correctness: the answer is right. A model service can return 200 OK with a worse answer; the root's Case files hold Anthropic's 2025 account of three serving bugs that quietly degraded Claude's answers, which no error counter saw (the root's {{Case files|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}} tab). For LLM products, quality is measured with evaluations ({{Production eval engineering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b39ad9e96193d21adc}}).

The nines, and what each one costs

Availability targets are spoken in nines: 99.9% is "three nines". The table converts each into allowed downtime (computed as (1 − target) × period; the SRE book's {{availability table|https://sre.google/sre-book/availability-table/}} gives the same values with a 30-day month).

Each step down the table is ten times less downtime, and the cost grows faster than that: "an incremental improvement in reliability may cost 100x more than the previous increment" (SRE book, ch. 3). The book's own worked example: moving a service with $1M of revenue from 99.9% to 99.99% is worth at most $900 ($1M × 0.09%), so a nine that costs more is not worth buying. Users also cannot tell the difference past a point, because their phone network and Wi-Fi fail more often than that.

Dependencies multiply. If our chat API needs the load balancer, our servers, the database, the cache and the model provider all to work, its availability is at most the product of theirs. With the parent's example figures (99.99%, 99.9%, 99.95%, 99.9%, 99.5%) that is 99.24%, below the weakest link. Treynor and colleagues put it as a rule: "A service cannot be more available than the intersection of all its critical dependencies", so critical dependencies "must offer one additional 9 relative to your service", the "rule of the extra 9" ({{Treynor, Dahlin, Rau, Beyer, ACM Queue 2017 (archived copy)|https://web.archive.org/web/20231218074029/https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3096459}}). The ways out are to need fewer things (make a dependency optional, with a fallback), or to have copies of them (two providers, three replicas). The parent's {{Numbers to know|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}} tab has an availability calculator for chains like this.

A second rule from the same article: "A service cannot be more available than its incident frequency multiplied by its detection and recovery time." Three 20-minute outages a year are 60 minutes, already more than the 52.56 minutes that 99.99% allows. Past three nines, reliability is mostly about how fast you notice and undo a problem, not about how rarely it happens.

What the big services actually show (2026-10-04, 90-day uptime on their own status pages): OpenAI APIs 99.96%, ChatGPT 99.65% ({{status.openai.com|https://status.openai.com/}}); Claude API 99.52%, claude.ai 99.44% ({{status.claude.com|https://status.claude.com/}}). These are each company's own aggregate definitions, not comparable SLOs, but they show that LLM products live around two to three nines today.

How things fail

Design against the failure you will actually get. From easiest to hardest to handle:

Crash (fail-stop)

The process dies or the machine disappears; connections are refused at once. Easy to detect, easy to route around: the load balancer stops sending traffic to it.

Slow

It answers, but in 3 seconds instead of 30 ms. Nothing errors, so nothing alarms, while every caller holds a worker, a connection and memory for 100 times longer. Most cascades start here.

Partial

One shard, one zone, one in ten requests, or one kind of request fails. Averages hide it: 1% of users completely broken looks like a 99% healthy service.

Gray failure

The system's own health checks say "fine" while its users see failures. Huang and colleagues name the key feature "differential observability: that the system's failure detectors may not notice problems even when applications are afflicted by them" ({{HotOS 2017|https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~ryanph/paper/grayfailure-hotos17.pdf}}). The defence is to measure from the user's side (SLIs) as well as the server's.

Metastable failure: when the system stays broken after the cause is gone

The most dangerous pattern has a name. Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu: "Metastable failures occur in open systems with an uncontrolled source of load where a trigger causes the system to enter a bad state that persists even when the trigger is removed" ({{HotOS 2021|https://sigops.org/s/conferences/hotos/2021/papers/hotos21-s11-bronson.pdf}}). An open system is one where new work keeps arriving whatever the system does (users keep sending messages); its opposite, a closed system, has a fixed set of clients that each wait for an answer before sending more. The paper describes three states:

StableLoad is low enough that a hiccup is absorbed.
→
VulnerableLoad is above a hidden threshold. Still healthy, and efficient, so many systems run here all the time.
→
MetastableA trigger (a slowdown, a deploy, a spike) tips it over, and a sustaining effect keeps it there.

"One of the most common failure-sustaining mechanisms is request retries" (same paper). A follow-up study of 22 metastable failures at 11 organisations found retry policy "by far, the most common sustaining effect ... affecting more than 50% of the studied incidents", and that at least 4 of 15 major AWS outages in a decade were metastable ({{Huang et al., OSDI 2022|https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi22-huang-lexiang.pdf}}). The practical lesson from Bronson: "It is common for an outage that involves a metastable failure to be initially blamed on the trigger, but the true root cause is the sustaining effect." The root's Case files hold real ones: AWS's October 2025 EC2 outage (a queue that never drained), Google Cloud's June 2025 restart stampede and Roblox's 73-hour outage. You will watch one happen, on a real service, in A retry storm, measured.

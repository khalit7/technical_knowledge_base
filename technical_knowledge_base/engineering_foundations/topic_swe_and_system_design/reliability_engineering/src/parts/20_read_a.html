The idea in one screen

A service survives dependencies that are slow, overloaded or down when it bounds every wait (timeouts, deadlines), caps the extra work failures create (one retry layer with a budget) and refuses or cheapens work it cannot finish (shedding, breakers, fallbacks); an SLO then says how much failure is allowed, and the rest is spent on shipping.

Where each defence acts on one chat request

Running example, from the parent topic: a phone app sends a message to our chat API, which saves it and calls a model provider (another company's service). At the parent root's 10 million messages a day, "rarely" means "every few minutes". Every highlighted box either bounds a wait, caps added load, or removes work.

The rule under every section: each defence adds load or removes work. Retries add load; shedding, breakers and deadlines remove it. A system in trouble recovers only when the work removed beats the work added, which is why the measured retry storm never recovered with backoff and jitter alone and recovered at once with a retry budget or a bounded queue. And slow is worse than down: a dependency that is down fails fast, one that is slow quietly holds your workers until you fall over too.

SLI, SLO, error budget
the measure (good events / all events), the target for it, and 100% minus the target: the failures you may spend
Timeout, deadline
the longest one call waits; the absolute time after which the whole request is worthless
Retry budget
retries capped at a share of normal traffic, so they switch themselves off when most calls fail
Circuit breaker
stops calling a failing dependency for a while and fails fast instead
Bulkhead, load shedding
separate pools so one dependency cannot take every worker; rejecting excess work early and cheaply
Metastable failure
a bad state that persists after its trigger is gone, kept alive by a sustaining effect (usually retries)
Blast radius
how much of the product one failure or one change can hurt; canaries, waves, cells and bulkheads shrink it

1. What "reliable" means, and what each nine costs

Three promises that break separately: availability (it answers; for a service spread over many machines Google measures it by requests, "availability in terms of the request success rate", {{SRE book, ch. 3|https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/}}), durability (saved data stays saved: Amazon S3 is "designed to provide 99.999999999% (11 nines) data durability" but "99.99% availability", {{S3 FAQ|https://aws.amazon.com/s3/faqs/}}) and correctness (the answer is right: a model can return 200 OK with a worse answer, as in Anthropic's 2025 account of three serving bugs that no error counter saw, in the root's {{Case files|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}}; LLM quality is measured with evaluations, {{Production eval engineering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b39ad9e96193d21adc}}).

Allowed downtime = (1 − target) × period; the SRE book's {{availability table|https://sre.google/sre-book/availability-table/}} gives the same values with a 30-day month.

100% is impossible, and each row is ten times less downtime while the cost grows faster: "an incremental improvement in reliability may cost 100x more than the previous increment" (SRE book, ch. 3). Its worked example: moving a service with $1M of revenue from 99.9% to 99.99% is worth at most $900 ($1M × 0.09%). Past a point users cannot tell, because their phone network fails more often.

Required dependencies multiply: the chain is weaker than its weakest link

The parent's example figures; product 99.24%. The parent's {{Numbers to know|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}} tab has a calculator for chains like this.

Treynor and colleagues: "A service cannot be more available than the intersection of all its critical dependencies", so critical dependencies "must offer one additional 9 relative to your service", the "rule of the extra 9" ({{ACM Queue 2017, archived|https://web.archive.org/web/20231218074029/https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3096459}}). The ways out are to need fewer things (an optional dependency with a fallback) or to have copies (two providers, three replicas). Their second rule: availability is bounded by incident frequency times detection and recovery time. Three 20-minute outages a year are 60 minutes, already more than the 52.56 minutes that 99.99% allows: past three nines, reliability is mostly how fast you notice and undo a problem.

What the big LLM services showed (dated reading, 2026-10-04)

90-day uptime on their own status pages: OpenAI APIs 99.96%, ChatGPT 99.65% ({{status.openai.com|https://status.openai.com/}}); Claude API 99.52%, claude.ai 99.44% ({{status.claude.com|https://status.claude.com/}}). Each company's own aggregate definition, not comparable SLOs, but LLM products live around two to three nines.

2. How things fail

Crash (fail-stop)

The process dies; connections are refused at once. Easy to detect and route around.

Slow

It answers in 3 s instead of 30 ms. Nothing errors, so nothing alarms, while every caller holds a worker, a connection and memory 100 times longer. Most cascades start here.

Partial

One shard, zone or kind of request fails. Averages hide it: 1% of users completely broken looks like a 99% healthy service.

Gray failure

Health checks say "fine" while users see failures: "differential observability" (Huang et al., {{HotOS 2017|https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~ryanph/paper/grayfailure-hotos17.pdf}}). Defence: measure from the user's side (SLIs).

Metastable failure: broken after the cause is gone

Bronson and colleagues: "Metastable failures occur in open systems with an uncontrolled source of load where a trigger causes the system to enter a bad state that persists even when the trigger is removed" ({{HotOS 2021|https://sigops.org/s/conferences/hotos/2021/papers/hotos21-s11-bronson.pdf}}). An open system keeps receiving new work whatever it does (users keep sending messages). A system runs stable, then vulnerable (above a hidden load threshold, still healthy and efficient), until a trigger tips it over and a sustaining effect keeps it there.

"One of the most common failure-sustaining mechanisms is request retries" (same paper). A study of 22 metastable failures at 11 organisations found retry policy "by far, the most common sustaining effect ... affecting more than 50% of the studied incidents", and at least 4 of 15 major AWS outages in a decade were metastable ({{Huang et al., OSDI 2022|https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi22-huang-lexiang.pdf}}). Bronson's lesson for postmortems: outages are "initially blamed on the trigger, but the true root cause is the sustaining effect." The root's Case files hold real ones: AWS's October 2025 EC2 outage (a queue that never drained), Google Cloud's June 2025 restart stampede, Roblox's 73-hour outage.

Watch one outage, step by step: AWS, 20 October 2025

On 20 October 2025 EC2 could not start new virtual machines for hours after its database came back (the card). Three words first. A lease is a claim on a server that expires unless it is renewed. A queue is a waiting line of work. A timeout is how long work may take before it is abandoned. Below, every square in the top row is one physical server (AWS calls them droplets); each needs a lease before it can host new machines. Before replays the mechanism AWS describes; After replays the fix it shipped that morning.

  1. 2:25 AM, DynamoDB is back: 48 attempts join the line. While it was down the servers’ leases timed out, so all 48 now ask the lease manager (DWFM) at once. The worker finishes 4 per tick; an attempt that has waited 3 ticks or more when it reaches the front has timed out. Red squares in the line are already doomed.
  2. Ticks 1 to 3: the front of the line succeeds. The 4 attempts at the front each tick waited less than 3 ticks, so they succeed: 12 of 48 servers now have a lease.
  3. Tick 4: the first timeouts. The next 4 attempts waited 3 ticks: timed out. The work was done but wasted, and each timeout puts a new attempt at the back of the line. AWS: “Additional work was queued to reattempt establishing the droplet lease.”
  4. Ticks 5 to 12: doomed on arrival. Every attempt behind 12th place was doomed the moment it joined: 12 places at 4 per tick is 3 ticks of waiting. Each one times out and rejoins, so the line stays at 36.
  5. Ticks 13 to 15: congestive collapse. The retries reach the front, but they have waited 9 to 11 ticks. All time out and rejoin. The worker is fully busy and makes no progress, which AWS calls “congestive collapse”.
  6. After 16 ticks: 64 attempts finished, 52 of them wasted. Still only 12 of 48 servers are leased; EC2 told customers “insufficient capacity errors”. Waiting longer would not help, and more retries only add to the line: the line itself is the problem. Switch to After.
  1. 4:14 AM: where Before ended. 12 servers leased and 36 attempts stuck in a line that never shrinks.
  2. Restart. AWS: “Restarting the DWFM hosts cleared out the DWFM queues”. The 36 servers still need leases, but now they wait outside the line, where no timeout is running (grey).
  3. Ticks 1 to 8 after the restart: the throttle. Work is let in only while fewer than 12 attempts are waiting, the most that can finish before the timeout. Every attempt the worker finishes succeeds: 4 more leases a tick, from 16 to 44 of 48.
  4. All 48 leased, 9 ticks after the restart. In the real event, 5:28 AM: “DWFM had established leases with all droplets”. Same worker, same timeout: capping the line turned zero progress into 4 leases per tick. AWS will now rate limit incoming work by the size of the waiting queue.

Illustrative model, not AWS's numbers: 48 servers, a worker that finishes 4 lease attempts per tick, and a timeout of 3 ticks, so at most 12 attempts can be waiting and still finish in time. AWS publishes the mechanism and the times, not its internal sizes. What the model shares with the postmortem: attempts that wait too long time out, each timeout queues a new attempt, the line stops shrinking, and the fix is to let in only as much work as can finish (AWS's planned fix rate-limits incoming work "based on the size of the waiting queue").

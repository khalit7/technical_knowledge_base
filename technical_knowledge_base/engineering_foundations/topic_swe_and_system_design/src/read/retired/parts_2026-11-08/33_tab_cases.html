The Reading tab builds an imaginary chat product from one server to ten million users. This tab is the evidence that the same problems hit real companies: 33 published architectures and outages, each read from the company's own write-up (engineering blog, official postmortem, regulator's order), each mapped to the building block it teaches. A postmortem is the report a team writes after an outage: what broke, why, and what it changed; good ones are blameless, about systems rather than people.

How to read a card: the system (what it was), what happened in plain words, what they changed, the lesson in one line, the numbers the source gives (each tied to a quote, or derived with its formula shown), and the source's own words under "Quotes from the source". Where a source prints an em-dash, this page shows "--". Sources read on 4 October 2026.

Watch one outage, step by step: AWS, 20 October 2025

On 20 October 2025 EC2 could not start new virtual machines for hours after its database came back (the card). Three words first. A lease is a claim on a server that expires unless it is renewed. A queue is a waiting line of work. A timeout is how long work may take before it is abandoned. Below, every square in the top row is one physical server (AWS calls them droplets); each needs a lease before it can host new machines. Before replays the mechanism AWS describes; After replays the fix it shipped that morning.

has a leaseattempt waiting in linein line, will time out before servedwaits outside the lineattempt just timed out

Illustrative model, not AWS's numbers: 48 servers, a worker that finishes 4 lease attempts per tick, and a timeout of 3 ticks, so at most 12 attempts can be waiting and still finish in time. AWS publishes the mechanism and the times, not its internal sizes. What the model shares with the postmortem: attempts that wait too long time out, each timeout queues a new attempt, the line stops shrinking, and the fix is to let in only as much work as can finish (AWS's planned fix rate-limits incoming work "based on the size of the waiting queue").

Backpressure and Little's law on a queue

A queue absorbs bursts: if uploads arrive at 3 times the usual rate for a minute, the queue grows and then drains. But it absorbs everything, including overload that never ends, and it does so silently. David Yanacek of Amazon describes the two modes: "When there is no backlog in the queue, the system's latency is low, and the system is in fast mode. But if a failure or unexpected load pattern causes the arrival rate to exceed the processing rate, it quickly flips into a more sinister operating mode. In this mode, the end-to-end latency grows higher and higher" ({{Yanacek, Avoiding insurmountable queue backlogs, Amazon Builders' Library|https://builder.aws.com/content/3EuRcgkTP1MI0c7zM8W6HL3WIqA/avoiding-insurmountable-queue-backlogs}}; quotes checked against the 2024 archived copy).

The arithmetic. Call the arrival rate λ (messages per second), the time one worker needs per message S, and the number of workers C. The workers can finish at most C / S messages per second, so the utilisation is ρ = λS / C.

  • If ρ < 1 the queue stays short on average, but waiting time rises steeply as ρ approaches 1 (with random arrivals, a queue at 90% utilisation waits several times longer than at 50%; the shape and the formulas are on {{Capacity planning and performance|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81458291d55d7443700c}}).
  • If ρ > 1 the backlog grows by λ − C/S messages every second, forever. Adding queue space does not help; only more workers, less work per message, or fewer messages do.
  • Worse, a backlog can keep itself alive after the cause is gone: old messages time out at their callers, who retry and add more. Bronson et al. call this a metastable failure, "a bad state that persists even when the trigger is removed" ({{Metastable Failures in Distributed Systems, HotOS 2021|https://sigops.org/s/conferences/hotos/2021/papers/hotos21-s11-bronson.pdf}}). Hence the old page's rule: bound the queue, and prefer rejecting at admission over timing out deep in the stack.
  • Draining a backlog of B messages takes B / (C/S − λ): only the spare capacity drains it. Yanacek: after an hour-long outage, "recovering from the outage requires double the system's capacity for another hour after the recovery", and a backlog "10x the capacity that the consumer side was scaled to" took "300 minutes" to work through.

Little's law ties the averages together: "the average number of items in a queuing system, denoted L, equals the average arrival rate of items to the system, λ, multiplied by the average waiting time of an item in the system, W. Thus, L = λW" ({{Little, Operations Research 59(3), 2011|https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.1110.0940}}). It holds for long-run averages of a stable system, whatever the distributions; a backlog that keeps growing has no stable average, so the law does not describe it. Two uses: size workers (embedding 5 documents a second at 20 s each keeps 5 × 20 = 100 jobs in progress, so at least 100 worker slots: the parent root's example), and turn a backlog into a wait (500 messages in the queue at 50 per second means about 10 s of waiting). Yanacek uses it to show how latency spikes eat thread pools: 100 messages a second at 100 ms is 10 busy threads; at 10 s it is 1,000. The {{Queue lab|#t-lab}} checks L against λW on every run.

Backpressure is how "we cannot keep up" travels back to whoever produces the work, so the system degrades at the edge instead of everywhere:

  • Bound the queue and reject at admission: when the queue is over its limit, the API answers 429 Too Many Requests (RFC 6585 says it "MAY include a Retry-After header", {{RFC 6585 §4|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6585#section-4}}) or 503, and the client backs off. Fail fast at the door rather than time out deep inside.
  • Block the producer: Kafka's producer buffers up to buffer.memory and then blocks send() for up to max.block.ms ({{producer configs|https://kafka.apache.org/43/configuration/producer-configs/}}); a bounded asyncio.Queue(maxsize=...) makes await q.put() wait.
  • Limit what each consumer holds: RabbitMQ's prefetch "defines the max number of unacknowledged deliveries that are permitted on a channel" ({{RabbitMQ confirms|https://www.rabbitmq.com/docs/confirms}}); Celery prefetches 4 messages per process by default (worker_prefetch_multiplier), which is why long tasks are usually run with 1.
  • Credit-based flow control in protocols: HTTP/2 and gRPC use per-stream windows (WINDOW_UPDATE, {{RFC 9113 §5.2|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9113#section-5.2}}); depth on {{Topic: protocols|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ec9355f4eecd6eed02}}.
  • Shed or sideline old work: when a backlog has formed, the newest work is often the most valuable. Yanacek: real-time systems "often are implemented with FIFO-ish queues, but prefer LIFO-ish behavior"; Amazon moves messages older than a threshold "into a backlog queue that we work through only after we're caught up on the live queue", drops messages a periodic full sync will redo anyway, and isolates noisy customers by hashing each to a few of many queues (shuffle sharding). Load shedding by priority is on {{Reliability engineering|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8152b8dececf34196174}}.

What to watch: the age of the oldest message, not just the queue length. Yanacek: "we focus more on measuring the age of messages that are in the queue. This quickly catches cases where the systems are behind, or are frequently erroring out and causing retries." In SQS that is ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage; in Kafka, lag converted to time. The parent root's Case files hold a real example of a backlog that never drained ("EC2 could not hand out servers: a queue that never drained", the root's {{Case files|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}} tab).

The dual-write problem and the transactional outbox

When an order is placed, the order service must save the order in its database and publish "order placed" for the e-mail service, the warehouse and analytics. That is a dual write: two writes to two systems that share no transaction. Gunnar Morling, then Debezium's lead: "we cannot have one shared transaction that would span the service's database as well as Apache Kafka, as the latter doesn't support to be enlisted in distributed (XA) transactions" ({{Debezium blog, 2019-02-19|https://debezium.io/blog/2019/02/19/reliable-microservices-data-exchange-with-the-outbox-pattern/}}). Whichever write goes first, a crash between them leaves the two systems disagreeing. Watch the same order go through three designs:

Crash points are the ones the measured lab injects; counts in the captions are from that lab (500 orders, 5% crash chance, three runs).

The transactional outbox (Chris Richardson's name for it): "the service that sends the message to first store the message in the database as part of the transaction that updates the business entities. A separate process then sends the messages to the message broker" ({{microservices.io, Transactional outbox|https://microservices.io/patterns/data/transactional-outbox.html}}). Its cost is stated on the same page: the relay "might, for example, crash after publishing a message but before recording the fact that it has done so. When it restarts, it will then publish the message again. As a result, a message consumer must be idempotent". So the outbox converts "sometimes lost" into "sometimes duplicated", and the idempotent consumer converts that into "once".

Measured on the same laptop Postgres (the "broker" is a second database, so there is genuinely no shared transaction; 500 orders, 5% crash chance at the dangerous line, three runs):

With a batch of 10 events per relay transaction, a crash mid-batch rolls back every "sent" mark in the batch, so all of them are published again: about 4 to 5 duplicates per crash. With one event per transaction, duplicates equal crashes. Batching is faster; it multiplies duplicates.

Two ways to run the relay

  • Polling publisher: "Publish messages by polling the database's outbox table" ({{microservices.io|https://microservices.io/patterns/data/polling-publisher.html}}). A loop runs SELECT ... WHERE sent_at IS NULL ORDER BY seq LIMIT 100 FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, publishes, marks sent. Simple, works on any database, adds polling load and a little latency; several relays can run thanks to SKIP LOCKED, but then strict ordering across them is lost.
  • Transaction log tailing, or change data capture (CDC): "Tail the database transaction log and publish each message/event inserted into the outbox to the message broker" ({{microservices.io|https://microservices.io/patterns/data/transaction-log-tailing.html}}). In Postgres this uses logical decoding, "the process of extracting all persistent changes to a database's tables into a coherent, easy to understand format" ({{PostgreSQL docs|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/logicaldecoding-explanation.html}}), which reads the write-ahead log (WAL). Debezium is the standard tool: its Postgres connector reads the WAL, and its Outbox Event Router expects an outbox table with columns id, aggregatetype, aggregateid, type and payload, routing each row by default to a topic named outbox.event.<aggregatetype> with aggregateid as the message key ({{Debezium outbox event router|https://debezium.io/documentation/reference/stable/transformations/outbox-event-router.html}}). No polling, ordered by commit; costs a Kafka Connect deployment and a replication slot to monitor (an abandoned slot makes Postgres keep WAL forever). Delivery is still at-least-once: "while it is recovering from the fault, it's possible that the connector might emit some duplicate change events" ({{Debezium Postgres connector|https://debezium.io/documentation/reference/stable/connectors/postgresql.html}}).

The receiving side has a mirror pattern, the inbox: store each incoming message's id (and often the message) in the consumer's database in the same transaction as its effects, which is the idempotent consumer by another name. MassTransit: "The inbox is used to keep track of received messages to guarantee exactly-once consumer behavior" ({{MassTransit|https://masstransit.io/documentation/patterns/transactional-outbox}}); NServiceBus "uses the message identity (MessageId) to deduplicate messages" ({{NServiceBus outbox|https://docs.particular.net/nservicebus/outbox/}}).

When you do not need an outbox: when the queue lives in the same database. Postgres-backed job queues let you enqueue in the same transaction as your data: River "encourages the use of the same database for application data and job queue. By enqueueing jobs transactionally along with other database changes, whole classes of distributed systems problems are avoided" ({{River docs|https://github.com/riverqueue/river/blob/master/docs/README.md}}); Oban lists "Transactional Control: Enqueue a job along with other database changes, ensuring that everything is committed or rolled back atomically" ({{Oban|https://github.com/oban-bg/oban/blob/main/README.md}}). The outbox is such a queue, with a relay that forwards to Kafka for the consumers that live elsewhere.

Webhooks are the same problem facing outwards. Sending a webhook to a customer after a database change is a dual write to their server. The sending side uses an outbox (write "deliver event X to customer Y" in the transaction, deliver from a worker with retries); the receiving side answers 2xx at once and queues the work. Stripe: "Your endpoint must quickly return a successful status code (2xx) before any complex logic that could cause a timeout", and it retries "for up to three days with an exponential back off in live mode" ({{Stripe webhooks|https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks}}). GitHub expects a 2XX "within 10 seconds" and "does not automatically redeliver failed deliveries" ({{GitHub best practices|https://docs.github.com/en/webhooks/using-webhooks/best-practices-for-using-webhooks}}, {{failed deliveries|https://docs.github.com/en/webhooks/using-webhooks/handling-failed-webhook-deliveries}}), so a receiver that is down simply misses them. Webhook API design (signatures, payloads) is on {{Building a backend API|n:3c65c17b0d0d8190ade6c34e8f653e79}}.

What the outbox costs
A table that grows (delete or partition sent rows), a relay process or a CDC pipeline to run and monitor, events that arrive a little later than the commit, and duplicates every consumer must absorb. When not to use it: when the event is not critical (a lost analytics ping is fine), or when the consumers can read your database's job queue directly.

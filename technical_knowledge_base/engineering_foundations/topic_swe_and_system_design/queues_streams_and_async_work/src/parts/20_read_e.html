Backpressure, Little's law and draining a backlog

A queue absorbs bursts, and it absorbs overload that never ends just as quietly. David Yanacek of Amazon: "if a failure or unexpected load pattern causes the arrival rate to exceed the processing rate, it quickly flips into a more sinister operating mode. In this mode, the end-to-end latency grows higher and higher" ({{Yanacek, Avoiding insurmountable queue backlogs, Amazon Builders' Library|https://builder.aws.com/content/3EuRcgkTP1MI0c7zM8W6HL3WIqA/avoiding-insurmountable-queue-backlogs}}; quotes checked against the 2024 archived copy).

The arithmetic. Arrivals λ per second, S seconds of work per message, C workers: they finish at most C/S per second, so utilisation is ρ = λS / C. Below 1 the queue stays short on average, though waiting climbs steeply as ρ nears 1 (the curve is on {{Capacity planning and performance|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81458291d55d7443700c}}). Above 1 the backlog grows by λ − C/S every second, forever; more queue space does not help, only more workers, less work per message or fewer messages. And once a backlog exists, only the spare capacity drains it: B messages take B / (C/S − λ).

Only spare capacity drains a backlog: at twice the arrival rate, a one-hour outage takes another hour

Predict: consumers are down for an hour while messages keep arriving. With 50% spare capacity (1.5× the arrival rate), how long until the queue is empty again? Then drag.

Drain time = outage × λ / (C/S − λ) = 60 min / (k − 1) for capacity k×λ derived. The default lands on Yanacek's "recovering from the outage requires double the system's capacity for another hour after the recovery"; he also reports a backlog "10x the capacity that the consumer side was scaled to" taking "300 minutes" to work through. For a first-in, first-out queue the line is also the age of the oldest message, in minutes, because the backlog divided by λ is exactly how long the head has waited.

Little's law ties the averages together: "the average number of items in a queuing system, denoted L, equals the average arrival rate of items to the system, λ, multiplied by the average waiting time of an item in the system, W. Thus, L = λW" ({{Little, Operations Research 59(3), 2011|https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.1110.0940}}), for the long-run averages of a stable system, whatever the distributions (a backlog that keeps growing has no stable average). Two uses: size workers (embedding 5 documents a second at 20 s each keeps 5 × 20 = 100 jobs in progress, so at least 100 worker slots: the parent root's example), and turn a backlog into a wait (500 messages at 50 a second is about 10 s). Yanacek's version: 100 messages a second at 100 ms is 10 busy threads; at 10 s it is 1,000. The {{Queue lab|#t-lab}} checks L against λW on every run.

Backpressure carries "we cannot keep up" back to whoever produces the work, so the system degrades at the edge instead of everywhere:

  • Bound the queue and reject at admission: over the limit, answer 429 Too Many Requests (which "MAY include a Retry-After header", {{RFC 6585 §4|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6585#section-4}}) or 503. Fail fast at the door rather than time out deep inside.
  • Block the producer: Kafka's producer buffers up to buffer.memory, then blocks send() for up to max.block.ms ({{producer configs|https://kafka.apache.org/43/configuration/producer-configs/}}); a bounded asyncio.Queue(maxsize=...) makes await q.put() wait.
  • Limit what each consumer holds: RabbitMQ's prefetch "defines the max number of unacknowledged deliveries that are permitted on a channel" ({{RabbitMQ confirms|https://www.rabbitmq.com/docs/confirms}}); Celery prefetches 4 messages per process by default (worker_prefetch_multiplier), which is why long tasks run with 1.
  • Shed or sideline old work: in a backlog the newest work is often the most valuable; real-time systems "often are implemented with FIFO-ish queues, but prefer LIFO-ish behavior" (Yanacek).

What to watch: the age of the oldest message, not the length. "we focus more on measuring the age of messages that are in the queue. This quickly catches cases where the systems are behind, or are frequently erroring out and causing retries" (Yanacek). A queue of 10,000 may be healthy (fast consumers) and a queue of 50 stuck (one poison message retried forever); age tells which. In SQS that is ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage; in Kafka, lag converted to time.

Metastable failures, Amazon's backlog tactics, credit-based flow control (depth)

A backlog can keep itself alive after its cause is gone: old messages time out at their callers, who retry and add more. Bronson et al. call this a metastable failure, "a bad state that persists even when the trigger is removed" ({{Metastable Failures in Distributed Systems, HotOS 2021|https://sigops.org/s/conferences/hotos/2021/papers/hotos21-s11-bronson.pdf}}). Hence the old page's rule: bound the queue, and prefer rejecting at admission over timing out deep in the stack.

Yanacek's other mode: "When there is no backlog in the queue, the system's latency is low, and the system is in fast mode." Amazon moves messages older than a threshold "into a backlog queue that we work through only after we're caught up on the live queue", drops messages a periodic full sync will redo anyway, and isolates noisy customers by hashing each to a few of many queues (shuffle sharding). Load shedding by priority is on {{Reliability engineering|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8152b8dececf34196174}}. The parent root's {{Case files|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}} tab holds a real backlog that never drained ("EC2 could not hand out servers: a queue that never drained").

Protocols push back too: HTTP/2 and gRPC use per-stream credit windows (WINDOW_UPDATE, {{RFC 9113 §5.2|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9113#section-5.2}}); depth on {{Topic: protocols|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ec9355f4eecd6eed02}}.

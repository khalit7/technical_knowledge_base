Queueing theory you actually use

Every resource in our chat API is a queue: a CPU core, a database connection, a GPU batch slot, a worker in a pool. Requests arrive, wait if it is busy, get served, leave. Four results cover nearly every capacity question, and none needs more than arithmetic.

1. The hockey stick: waiting grows like 1 / (1 − ρ)

Take one server and requests that arrive at random, independently, the way users do (a Poisson process), each needing on average S = 10 ms of work, exponentially distributed. This is the M/M/1 queue (Markov arrivals, Markov service, 1 server):

W = S / (1 − ρ)
W
mean time from arrival to answer (waiting plus service)
S
mean service time (the work itself)
ρ
utilisation = arrival rate × S (for one server)

At 50% busy W = 2S; at 80%, 5S; at 90%, 10S; at 95%, 20S; at 99%, 100S; at 100% the queue grows without limit (the root's Step 1). The time in the system is exponential, so the p99 is ln(100) × W = 4.6 × W: at 90% busy, 10 ms of work has a p99 of 460 ms.

Exact M/M/c values (Erlang C, the same code as the root's Scale simulator), in multiples of S; c servers share one queue. Checked in src/recompute.py.

Measured: the hockey stick on a real service

Experiment 1: one real HTTP server, one request at a time, a random 10 ms of work, driven open-loop at nine rates from 10% to 106% busy (the setup is in the block below). Predict first: at 80% busy, how many times the work will a request take at the median?

Measured: p99 54 ms at 11% busy, 114 ms at 57%, 190 ms at 79%, 321 ms at 89%, 556 ms at 95%, then seconds (median 1.54 s at 101%, 3.21 s at 106%).

Reading the curve against M/M/1 theory (depth)

The lesson the experiment adds

The two runs aimed at 90% and 95% ran at 101% and 106% because the real service time was 12% longer than the nominal one. Nobody would see that from a dashboard showing "90% CPU". A capacity estimate is only as good as the service time it uses, so measure the service time under load, not on an idle machine.

How Experiment 1 was run (provenance)
Experiment 1: one server, open-loop load from 10% to 106% busy

A tiny HTTP server (Python asyncio, one request at a time, each holding the server for a random time with a mean of 10 ms drawn from an exponential distribution: a timed wait standing in for a database or model call), driven by an open-loop load generator that sends requests at random times at a fixed average rate whether or not earlier ones have returned. Nine rates, 40 to 75 seconds each, first 2 seconds dropped, latency measured from each request's scheduled send time. The server logged every service time it actually gave: the sleep overshoots by about 1 ms on this laptop, so the real mean service time was 11.1 to 11.5 ms and the utilisation is computed from that, not from the nominal 10 ms. Code: src/lab/server.py, loadgen.py, run_all.py (Apple M1 Pro, Python 3.12, 2026-10-04).

Against theory: up to about 80% the measured means sit within 15% of M/M/1 (at 79%: 49.6 ms measured, 55.2 ms predicted). Near 95% the measurement falls below it (p99 556 ms against 962 ms) for two honest reasons: every sleep adds about 1 ms of fixed overshoot, so the service time is less variable than exponential (Kingman, below, says that lowers waiting), and a 75-second run is short for so busy a queue to reach its long-run average.

2. Little's law: how many requests are inside

Averaged over time, the number of requests in a system equals the rate they arrive times the time each stays: L = λW ({{Little, 2011|https://people.cs.umass.edu/~emery/classes/cmpsci691st/readings/OS/Littles-Law-50-Years-Later.pdf}}, the root's Step 3). It holds for any arrival pattern and any service time, which is why it is the most used formula in capacity work: it turns "requests a second" into "things held at once", and every limit (cores, connections, threads, streams) is a "held at once" limit.

The same law sizes three very different things in our chat API

Bars on a log scale. Cores: 1,157 requests a second × 25 ms of CPU each (the Scale simulator's figure before background jobs move to a queue) = 29 cores busy on average, more than 7 of the root's 4-core m7i.xlarge servers before any headroom; at 5.2 ms (jobs on a queue) it drops to 6. Connections: 3,472 database reads a second × 1 ms = 3.5 connections. Model streams: 231 model calls a second × 10 s of streaming = 2,315 calls open at once (the root's estimate), a number that the HTTP client pool, the worker limits and the provider must all allow.

Little's law also checks a load test: in Experiment 1 at 89% busy, 80 requests a second × 87 ms mean = 7 requests in the server on average. If a tool claims a throughput and a latency that do not multiply out to its concurrency, one of the numbers is wrong.

3. One queue for many servers beats a queue per server

A bank with one line feeding 8 tellers, against a supermarket with a line per till. With one shared line (M/M/c, c servers), no teller idles while someone waits in another line. At 90% busy, in multiples of S: one shared queue for 8 servers, mean 1.9S, p99 7.1S; 8 separate queues with requests sprinkled at random, each its own M/M/1, mean 10S, p99 46S. Same servers, same load, a p99 6.5 times worse (the table above). So big pools run hotter safely (8 servers sharing a queue at 90% wait less than 1 server at 70%, and splitting one pool into many small dedicated ones costs capacity), and the load balancer decides which world you live in: random assignment builds the supermarket, least outstanding requests approximates the bank (measured below).

Why aim for 60 to 80% utilisation, not 100%

  1. The curve. From 50% to 80% busy, one server's mean wait rises from 1S to 4S; from 80% to 95%, to 19S.
  2. Your measurement window hides peaks. An hourly average of 70% contains minutes at 90% (peak factors below; Twitter's busiest second in 2013 was 25 times its average, root {{Case files|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}}: "A peak 25 times the average").
  3. Servers fail. If one of four dies, the other three take its share: 60% becomes 80% (N+1).
  4. Capacity takes time to add. A VM takes a minute or two, a GPU replica several minutes, a database resize longer.

The root's Scale simulator flags a component as hot at 70% and its fixes stop at 60% (src/sim/model.py: HOT, TARGET = 0.7, 0.6); this page keeps those numbers. Work nobody waits for (overnight batch, training) is the exception: run it near 100%.

4. Variability multiplies waiting: Kingman's formula

The coefficient of variation (cv, standard deviation over mean) measures how variable the work is: constant service has cv 0, exponential 1, and a mix of 5 ms cache hits and 500 ms misses, or short prompts and 100,000-token ones, easily 2 or more. John Kingman's 1961 heavy-traffic result ({{Proc. Cambridge Phil. Soc. 57, 902|https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305004100036094}}) gives a single queue's mean wait, approximately:

Wq ≈ [ρ / (1 − ρ)] × [(ca2 + cs2) / 2] × S
Wq
mean time waiting in the queue (before service starts)
ca
cv of the gaps between arrivals (1 for random arrivals; above 1 for bursty traffic)
cs
cv of the service times

Utilisation times variability times the work. At 80% busy with random arrivals: constant service waits 2S, exponential 4S, cv 2 waits 10S. Variability is a lever as strong as utilisation and often cheaper: put the slow kind of request in its own pool (the root's Step 3 moved PDF processing to a queue for this reason), cap request sizes, or batch small requests. Move the variability slider and watch the 60 to 80% band.

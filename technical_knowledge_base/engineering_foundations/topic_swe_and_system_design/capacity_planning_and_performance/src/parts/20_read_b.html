Queueing theory you actually use

Every resource in our chat API is a queue: requests arrive, wait if the resource is busy, get served, leave. A CPU core, a database connection, a GPU batch slot, a worker in a pool: same shape. Four results cover nearly every capacity question you will meet, and none needs more than arithmetic.

1. The hockey stick: waiting grows like 1 / (1 − ρ)

Take one server and requests that arrive at random, independently of each other, the way users do (a Poisson process: the gap between arrivals is unpredictable, with a known average). Say each request needs, on average, S = 10 ms of work, with the amount varying from request to request (exponentially distributed, the textbook assumption). This is the M/M/1 queue (Markov arrivals, Markov service, 1 server), and its mean time in the system is

W = S / (1 − ρ)
W
mean time from arrival to answer (waiting plus service)
S
mean service time (the work itself)
ρ
utilisation = arrival rate × S (for one server)

At ρ = 50%, W = 2S; at 80%, 5S; at 90%, 10S; at 95%, 20S; at 99%, 100S. Each step closer to 100% costs more than the last, and at 100% the queue grows without limit (the root's Step 1). For M/M/1 the whole distribution is known: the time in the system is exponential with mean W, so the p99 is ln(100) × W = 4.6 × W. At 90% busy, a request needing 10 ms of work has a p99 of 460 ms.

Measured: the hockey stick on a real service

Experiment 1: one server, open-loop load from 10% to 106% busy

A tiny HTTP server (Python asyncio, one request at a time, each holding the server for a random time with a mean of 10 ms drawn from an exponential distribution: a timed wait standing in for a database or model call), driven by an open-loop load generator that sends requests at random times at a fixed average rate whether or not earlier ones have returned. Nine rates, 40 to 75 seconds each, first 2 seconds dropped, latency measured from each request's scheduled send time. The server logged every service time it actually gave: the sleep overshoots by about 1 ms on this laptop, so the real mean service time was 11.1 to 11.5 ms and the utilisation is computed from that, not from the nominal 10 ms. Code: src/lab/server.py, loadgen.py, run_all.py (Apple M1 Pro, Python 3.12, 2026-10-04).

The lesson the experiment adds

The two runs aimed at 90% and 95% ran at 101% and 106% because the real service time was 12% longer than the nominal one. Nobody would see that from a dashboard showing "90% CPU". A capacity estimate is only as good as the service time it uses, so measure the service time under load, not on an idle machine.

2. Little's law: how many requests are inside

Little's law (taught on the root's Step 3) says that, averaged over time, the number of requests in a system equals the rate they arrive times the time each stays: L = λW ({{Little, 2011|https://people.cs.umass.edu/~emery/classes/cmpsci691st/readings/OS/Littles-Law-50-Years-Later.pdf}}). It holds for any arrival pattern and any service time, which is why it is the most used formula in capacity work. Three uses on this page:

  • Concurrency needed. Our chat API at the busy hour: 1,157 requests a second, each holding a CPU core for 25 ms (the Scale simulator's figure before background jobs move to a queue): on average 1,157 × 0.025 = 29 cores are busy, so with the root's 4-core m7i.xlarge servers you need more than 7 of them before counting any headroom. Move the jobs to a queue (5.2 ms each) and it drops to 6 cores.
  • Connection pools. 3,472 database reads a second, each holding a connection for 1 ms: 3.5 connections busy on average (pools).
  • Sanity-check a load test. In Experiment 1 at 89% busy: 80 requests a second × 87 ms mean = 7 requests in the server on average. If a tool claims a throughput and a latency that do not multiply out to its concurrency, one of the numbers is wrong.

3. One queue for many servers beats a queue per server

A bank with one line feeding 8 tellers, against a supermarket with a line per till. With one shared line (M/M/c, c servers), a teller never sits idle while someone waits in another line. The numbers at 90% busy, in multiples of S: one shared queue for 8 servers, mean 1.9S, p99 7.1S; 8 separate queues with requests sprinkled at random, each is its own M/M/1, so mean 10S, p99 46S. Same servers, same load, a p99 6.5 times worse. Two consequences:

  • Big pools run hotter safely. 8 servers sharing a queue at 90% wait less than 1 server at 70%. This is why a large fleet can target a higher utilisation than a small one, and why splitting one pool into many small dedicated ones costs capacity.
  • A load balancer decides which world you live in. Random assignment builds the supermarket; least-outstanding-requests approximates the bank. The load balancing section measures the difference.

Why aim for 60 to 80% utilisation, not 100%

Four reasons stack up, each cheap to see from the formulas above:

  1. The curve. From 50% to 80% busy, the mean wait for one server rises from 1S to 4S; from 80% to 95% it rises to 19S.
  2. Your measurement window hides peaks. An hourly average of 70% contains minutes at 90% (see the peak factors below; Twitter's busiest second in 2013 was 25 times its average, in the root's {{Case files|n:3c65c17b0d0d81048ae0dd14ac399b4d}}: "A peak 25 times the average").
  3. Servers fail. If one of four dies, the other three take its share: 60% becomes 80%. That is N+1 arithmetic.
  4. Capacity takes time to add. A new VM takes a minute or two, a GPU replica several minutes, a database resize longer: the headroom must cover the load growth while you wait.

The root's Scale simulator flags a component as hot at 70% and its suggested fixes stop at 60% (src/sim/model.py: HOT, TARGET = 0.7, 0.6); this page keeps those numbers. Work nobody is waiting for (overnight batch jobs, training) is the exception: run it near 100%, since only throughput matters there.

4. Variability multiplies waiting: Kingman's formula

The M/M/1 formula assumed exponential service times. Real ones are less or more variable. The coefficient of variation (cv) measures it: standard deviation divided by mean. Constant service has cv 0, exponential has cv 1, and a service where most requests take 5 ms and a few take 500 ms (a cache hit against a cache miss, a short prompt against a 100,000-token one) easily has cv 2 or more. John Kingman's 1961 heavy-traffic result ({{Proc. Cambridge Phil. Soc. 57, 902|https://doi.org/10.1017/S0305004100036094}}) gives the mean wait of a single queue, approximately:

Wq ≈ [ρ / (1 − ρ)] × [(ca2 + cs2) / 2] × S
Wq
mean time waiting in the queue (before service starts)
ca
cv of the gaps between arrivals (1 for random, Poisson arrivals; above 1 for bursty traffic)
cs
cv of the service times

Three factors: utilisation, variability, and the service time itself. At 80% busy with random arrivals: constant service waits 2S, exponential 4S, cv 2 waits 10S. Variability is a lever as strong as utilisation, and often cheaper to pull: put the slow kind of request in its own pool (the root's Step 3 moved PDF processing to a queue for exactly this reason), cap request sizes, or batch small requests together. For several servers the same correction applied to the exact M/M/c wait (the Allen-Cunneen approximation) is what the explorer below uses.

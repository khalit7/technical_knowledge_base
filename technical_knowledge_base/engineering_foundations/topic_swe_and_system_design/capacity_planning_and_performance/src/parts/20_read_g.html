Capacity planning

Capacity planning decides how much hardware to have, and when, so the peak is served with the required reliability at the lowest cost. Google's SRE book lists what it needs: "An accurate organic demand forecast, which extends beyond the lead time required for acquiring capacity", "An accurate incorporation of inorganic demand sources into the demand forecast", and "Regular load testing of the system to correlate raw capacity (servers, disks, and so on) to service capacity" ({{SRE book, ch. 1|https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/}}). Organic demand is steady growth; inorganic is a launch or a viral post. The load test gives per-server capacity; the rest turns the forecast into servers.

Peak factors: the root's 2× explained

Capacity is sized for the peak: the peak factor is the busiest period's rate over the average. The root assumed 2× (busy hour over daily average) and labelled it illustrative. Real hourly traffic says it is sensible for one region and too high for a global product: Wikipedia's hourly page views by human users, September 2026:

Busy hour over its day's average: German 1.92, Japanese 1.63, English 1.24; busiest hour of the month: Japanese 2.85. German's quietest hour is 0.21 of its average: one region sleeps at once, while worldwide English is flat. What the three Wikipedias show (depth)

The window matters as much as the product. Per second, peaks are far sharper: Twitter's busiest second in August 2013 was 143,199 Tweets against an average of about 5,700, a factor of 25 (root Case files: "A peak 25 times the average"). Servers absorb second-scale bursts with queues and headroom; hour-scale peaks need capacity; known events need a separate inorganic forecast.

Headroom, N+1 and N+2

Let N be the servers needed to carry the peak at the target utilisation. N+1 adds one spare so losing any single server still serves the peak; N+2 adds two, so one can be down for maintenance while another fails. Google's capacity tool states requirements this way: "I want to meet service Foo's demand in each geographic region, and have N + 2 redundancy" ({{SRE book, ch. 18|https://sre.google/sre-book/software-engineering-in-sre/}}). The same logic covers bigger failure units: across Z availability zones, losing one loses 1/Z of capacity, so the fleet must be Z/(Z − 1) times the peak need: 1.5× with 3 zones, 2× with 2.

Autoscaling (AWS target tracking, Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler) does not remove the need to plan: it needs a floor that survives a failure, a ceiling the database behind it can take, a signal that tracks real load (Slack's autoscaler removed servers that looked idle because they were waiting on a saturated network: root Case files, "The first Monday of the year: autoscaling made it worse"), and it is too slow for the first minutes of a spike. GPU replicas take minutes to start: {{LLM serving in production, sections 2 and 3|n:3f35c17b0d0d818aa406da1f7a784c62}}.

The plan for the chat API, and cost per request

Set the lead time to 6 months and growth to 10% before you look: how many servers?

One server: an m7i.xlarge (4 vCPU, $0.2016 an hour on demand in us-east-1, 730 hours a month, the root's price) carrying 769 requests a second at 100% (4 cores / 5.2 ms of CPU per request, the Scale simulator's figure with background jobs on a queue). Defaults: 1 million daily users × 10 messages × 5 requests per message / 86,400 s = 579 requests a second on average. Servers = ceil(peak / (769 × target)) + spares. Checked in src/recompute.py.

With the defaults: peak 1,157 requests a second, 769 × 0.6 = 462 per server, so 3 servers carry the peak at 50% each, and N+1 makes 4 ($589 a month). With all 4 up the peak runs at 38%; with one down, 50%, the same as the root's Scale simulator after its fixes (3 app servers at 50%). Growing 10% a month for 6 months multiplies the load by 1.77, which needs 6 servers.

Cost per request = the fleet's cost per hour / requests served per hour. The app tier: 4 × $0.2016 = $0.81 an hour over 2.08 million requests an hour on average = $0.39 per million requests, about $0.0000019 per message (5 requests). The model is a different scale: the root's estimate sizes 56 GPUs for the busy hour (400 output tokens per reply, 2,000 tokens a second per GPU, 20% headroom); at $3.99 per GPU-hour (Lambda on demand, the root's figure) running all day, that is $223 an hour over 416,667 replies an hour, $0.00054 per reply, about 280 times the app tier's cost per message (derived; src/README.md has the arithmetic). So spend the planning effort, and the autoscaling, where the money is; the app tier's servers autoscale in minutes, so plan their floor and limits rather than a year of growth, and forecast where lead times are long (GPUs, database instances, reserved capacity).

Capacity for an LLM-backed service

When the model is called through a provider, the capacity you plan is not servers but tokens per minute, requests per minute and open streams, and the provider's limits are the ceiling. The same three steps as above (demand at the peak, Little's law for what is held at once, headroom against the limit) apply, in the provider's units. Inside a self-hosted serving engine the capacity question becomes KV-cache memory and batching, which {{Batching, KV cache and scheduling|n:3c65c17b0d0d819db53bd2c48909e5b2}} and {{LLM serving in production|n:3f35c17b0d0d818aa406da1f7a784c62}} own.

The chat assistant's busy hour against one provider tier's limits

Demand (derived from the root's estimate inputs: 231 model calls a second at the busy hour, 1,000 input and 400 output tokens each): 13,889 requests a minute (RPM), 13.9 million input tokens a minute (ITPM), 5.56 million output tokens a minute (OTPM). Limit: Anthropic's Scale tier for one model class such as Claude Sonnet 5.5, 10,000 RPM, 10,000,000 ITPM and 2,000,000 OTPM ({{Anthropic rate limits|https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/rate-limits}}, read 2026-10-24; tiers change, so treat the limits as a dated example). Output tokens are 2.8 times over, the binding limit.

  • Output tokens bind first. Every axis is over at the busy hour, output most of all; the fixes are a custom tier, spreading traffic over providers or model classes (each row has its own limits), shorter replies, or self-hosting. A gateway that spreads and budgets the traffic is {{Gateways and routing|n:3f15c17b0d0d81e4a964c129a3e520ae}}.
  • Cached input is free capacity. For most Claude models "only uncached input tokens count toward your ITPM rate limits"; if 80% of each request's input is cached conversation history (illustrative), uncached input falls to 2.8 million a minute, under the limit. Prompt caching is on {{Caching, section 11|n:3cd5c17b0d0d817ebd6ccadd4750c23a}}.
  • Per minute is really per second. "a rate of 60 requests per minute (RPM) might be enforced as 1 request per second. Short bursts of requests can exceed the limit", and the limiter is a token bucket ("continuously replenished up to your maximum limit, rather than being reset at fixed intervals"). Size against the busiest seconds, not the busy hour's average; a burst returns 429 with a retry-after header, and naive retries add load (retry budgets on {{Reliability engineering|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8152b8dececf34196174}}).
  • Open streams are a concurrency limit. By Little's law, 231 calls a second streaming for 10 s means 2,315 calls open at once. httpx, the client under the Anthropic and OpenAI Python SDKs, defaults to max_connections 100 per client ({{httpx resource limits|https://www.python-httpx.org/advanced/resource-limits/}}), and a sync FastAPI endpoint holds one of 40 threads for the whole call; spread the streams over enough processes, use async endpoints, and set the client pool explicitly.

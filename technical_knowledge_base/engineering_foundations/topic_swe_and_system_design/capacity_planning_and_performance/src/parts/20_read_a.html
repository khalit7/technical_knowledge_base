The idea in one screen

A request's latency is its work plus its waiting, and waiting grows like 1 / (1 − utilisation): gently at first, then violently. Capacity planning keeps every resource left of that knee at the peak, with spares for failure, and checks it with load tests that send traffic the way users do.

The same work, more and more waiting: one server, mean time in the system at four utilisations

Blue: the work itself (service time S). Orange: waiting in the queue. M/M/1 (random arrivals, exponential work): W = S / (1 − ρ), so 2S at 50% busy, 5S at 80%, 10S at 90%, 20S at 95%; the p99 is 4.6 times the mean. Measured on a real service below: the same 11 ms of work took a median of 12 ms at 11% busy and 142 ms at 95% busy.

Our running example is the parent topic's chat assistant: a phone app sends a message to our chat API, which reads the conversation from a database, calls a model, and streams the reply back. At the root's scale (1 million people a day, 10 messages each, 5 requests per message) that is about 579 requests a second on average and 1,157 in the busy hour; 1 in 5 of those requests (the message itself) calls the model, about 231 a second at the busy hour. Three questions organise the page: how fast is it (latency, and especially the tail), how much can it carry (throughput, and the queueing maths that decides where "falls over" begins), and how much hardware does it need (capacity planning).

What performance means: percentiles and the tail

Latency is how long one request takes; throughput is how many requests the whole system finishes per second; utilisation (ρ) is the share of time a resource is busy; saturation is work waiting because the resource is busy. Latency is service time plus waiting time. Throughput rises with load until a resource saturates; past that, only latency grows. That bend is the knee. Brendan Gregg's {{USE method|https://www.brendangregg.com/usemethod.html}} checks every resource for Utilisation, Saturation and Errors, in that order: the fastest way to find a bottleneck you did not expect.

Latency is a distribution, so read it with percentiles: the p50 (median) is the time half the requests beat, the p99 the time 99 in 100 beat, the p99.9 1 in 1,000. The slow end is the tail. Google's SRE book: "Using percentiles for indicators allows you to consider the shape of the distribution" ({{SRE book, ch. 4|https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/}}). The mean hides the tail: in our hockey-stick measurement at 89% busy, the mean was 87 ms while 1 request in 100 took more than 321 ms. Two rules: you cannot average percentiles (merge the raw data or histograms, HdrHistogram or Prometheus and OpenTelemetry histograms, then read the percentile), and you need enough samples (a p99 from 200 requests rests on the 2 slowest; a p99.9 needs tens of thousands).

The tail at scale: fan-out turns rare into common

Our chat API calls the database, the cache, the user service, the rate limiter and the model; if any one is slow, the user waits. Dean and Barroso ({{"The Tail at Scale", CACM 2013|https://www.barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf}}): with servers that are slow 1 time in 100, "If a user request must collect responses from 100 such servers in parallel, then 63% of user requests will take more than one second." Predict before you drag: with 1 slow call in 10,000, how many of 2,000 parallel calls make one user request in five slow?

P(user request is slow) = 1 − (1 − p)n, assuming the calls are slow independently: 1 − 0.99100 = 63.4%; 1 − 0.99992000 = 18.1%, Dean and Barroso's "almost one in five". Defaults reproduce their 63% independently; checked in src/recompute.py.

So the more calls a request fans out to, the further into the tail each dependency's target must reach: if a screen makes 100 calls and you want 99 in 100 screens fast, each call needs roughly a p99.99 target. This is the rule for a latency budget: split the end-to-end target across the calls on the critical path, each share stated at the percentile the fan-out demands.

Google's measured fan-out, hedged requests, and when percentiles mislead (depth)

Their measured Google service: "The 99th-percentile latency for a single random request to finish, measured at the root, is 10ms. However, the 99th-percentile latency for all requests to finish is 140ms, and the 99th-percentile latency for 95% of the requests finishing is 70ms, meaning that waiting for the slowest 5% of the requests to complete is responsible for half of the total 99%-percentile latency." Two cures from the same paper: reduce the variability itself (most of this page), or send a backup copy of a slow call (a hedged request): "sending a hedging request after a 10ms delay reduces the 99.9th-percentile latency for retrieving all 1,000 values from 1,800ms to 74ms while sending just 2% more requests." Hedging is load added by the client, so it belongs with retries on {{Reliability engineering|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8152b8dececf34196174}} (section 4).

When percentiles mislead: the p99 of a service that sheds load looks great because the rejected requests are not in it; always read latency next to the error and rejection rate. And a percentile computed by a closed-loop load tester can be wrong by a factor of 16 (measured below).

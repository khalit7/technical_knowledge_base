Three ideas carry most of this page

1. Waiting, not working, is where latency goes. A request that needs 10 ms of work can take 200 ms because it waited behind others. Waiting grows like 1 / (1 − utilisation): gently at first, then violently. Measured below on a real service: the same 11 ms of work took a median of 12 ms at 11% busy and 142 ms at 95% busy.

2. The tail decides the experience. A page that makes 100 calls is slow if any one of them is slow, so the 1-in-100 slow call becomes the common case (the tail at scale).

3. Measure the way users arrive. Users do not wait for each other. A load test that sends the next request only after the previous answer (a closed loop) quietly stops sending during a stall and reports a p99 sixteen times too good (measured below).

What performance means

Four words, which people mix up constantly:

  • Latency: how long one request takes, from the moment it is sent to the moment the answer is back. Measured per request, in milliseconds.
  • Throughput: how many requests the system finishes per second. A property of the whole system, not of one request.
  • Utilisation: the share of time a resource (a CPU core, a database connection, a GPU) is busy. A server that works 0.6 seconds of every second is 60% utilised. Written ρ (rho) in queueing maths.
  • Saturation: work that is waiting because the resource is busy (a queue). Brendan Gregg's USE method checks every resource for Utilisation, Saturation and Errors, in that order; it is the fastest way to find a bottleneck you did not expect.

They are linked. Latency is service time (the work itself) plus waiting time (time in a queue before the work starts). Throughput rises with load until a resource saturates; past that point throughput stops rising and only latency grows. That bend is the knee, and most of this page is about finding it, staying left of it and moving it right.

Percentiles: describe the whole distribution

Requests do not all take the same time, so "the latency" is a distribution. A percentile reads it: the p50 (median) is the time half the requests beat; the p99 is the time 99 in 100 beat, so 1 in 100 is slower; the p99.9 is 1 in 1,000. The slow end is the tail. Google's SRE book: "Using percentiles for indicators allows you to consider the shape of the distribution" and treat metrics "as distributions rather than averages" (SRE book, ch. 4). The mean hides the tail: in our hockey-stick measurement at 89% busy, the mean was 87 ms while 1 request in 100 took more than 321 ms.

Two rules for working with percentiles. You cannot average them: the p99 of a service is not the average of each server's p99; merge the raw data or a histogram (HdrHistogram, or the histogram types in Prometheus and OpenTelemetry) and read the percentile from the merged one. You need enough samples: a p99 from 200 requests rests on the 2 slowest; a p99.9 needs tens of thousands.

Why the tail matters more than it seems: the tail at scale

Our chat API, to show one screen, calls the database, the cache, the user service, the rate limiter and the model. If any one is slow, the user waits. Jeff Dean and Luiz Barroso put numbers on this in "The Tail at Scale" (CACM, February 2013): "consider a system where each server typically responds in 10ms but with a 99th-percentile latency of one second ... If a user request must collect responses from 100 such servers in parallel, then 63% of user requests will take more than one second." The arithmetic: each call is fast with probability 0.99, all 100 are fast with probability 0.99100 = 0.366, so 1 − 0.366 = 63.4% hit at least one slow call. Their second example: one-in-10,000 slow calls across 2,000 servers gives 1 − 0.99992000 = 18.1%, "almost one in five".

Curve: share of user requests that are slow, against fan-out, for the chosen p.

Formula: P(user request is slow) = 1 − (1 − p)n, assuming the calls are slow independently. Defaults reproduce Dean and Barroso's 63% (1% slow, 100 calls) independently; checked in src/recompute.py.

Their measured Google service says the same from real data: "The 99th-percentile latency for a single random request to finish, measured at the root, is 10ms. However, the 99th-percentile latency for all requests to finish is 140ms, and the 99th-percentile latency for 95% of the requests finishing is 70ms, meaning that waiting for the slowest 5% of the requests to complete is responsible for half of the total 99%-percentile latency." The lesson for design: the more calls a request fans out to, the further into the tail each dependency's target must reach. If a screen makes 100 calls and you want 99 in 100 screens fast, each call needs roughly a p99.99 target. Two cures from the same paper: reduce the variability itself (most of this page), or send a backup copy of a slow call (a hedged request): "sending a hedging request after a 10ms delay reduces the 99.9th-percentile latency for retrieving all 1,000 values from 1,800ms to 74ms while sending just 2% more requests." Hedging is load added by the client, so it belongs with retries on Reliability engineering, which covers it.

When percentiles mislead: a p99 of a service that sheds load looks great because the rejected requests are not in it; always read latency next to the error and rejection rate. And a percentile computed by your load tester can be wrong by a factor of 16 if the tester is closed-loop (measured below).

Queueing theory you actually use

Every resource in our chat API is a queue: requests arrive, wait if the resource is busy, get served, leave. A CPU core, a database connection, a GPU batch slot, a worker in a pool: same shape. Four results cover nearly every capacity question you will meet, and none needs more than arithmetic.

1. The hockey stick: waiting grows like 1 / (1 − ρ)

Take one server and requests that arrive at random, independently of each other, the way users do (a Poisson process: the gap between arrivals is unpredictable, with a known average). Say each request needs, on average, S = 10 ms of work, with the amount varying from request to request (exponentially distributed, the textbook assumption). This is the M/M/1 queue (Markov arrivals, Markov service, 1 server), and its mean time in the system is

W = S / (1 − ρ)
W
mean time from arrival to answer (waiting plus service)
S
mean service time (the work itself)
ρ
utilisation = arrival rate × S (for one server)

At ρ = 50%, W = 2S; at 80%, 5S; at 90%, 10S; at 95%, 20S; at 99%, 100S. Each step closer to 100% costs more than the last, and at 100% the queue grows without limit (the root's Step 1). For M/M/1 the whole distribution is known: the time in the system is exponential with mean W, so the p99 is ln(100) × W = 4.6 × W. At 90% busy, a request needing 10 ms of work has a p99 of 460 ms.

Exact M/M/c values (Erlang C, the same code as the root's Scale simulator), in multiples of the mean service time S; c is the number of servers sharing one queue. Checked in src/recompute.py.

The lesson the experiment adds

The two runs aimed at 90% and 95% ran at 101% and 106% because the real service time was 12% longer than the nominal one. Nobody would see that from a dashboard showing "90% CPU". A capacity estimate is only as good as the service time it uses, so measure the service time under load, not on an idle machine.

2. Little's law: how many requests are inside

Little's law (taught on the root's Step 3) says that, averaged over time, the number of requests in a system equals the rate they arrive times the time each stays: L = λW (Little, 2011). It holds for any arrival pattern and any service time, which is why it is the most used formula in capacity work. Three uses on this page:

  • Concurrency needed. Our chat API at the busy hour: 1,157 requests a second, each holding a CPU core for 25 ms (the Scale simulator's figure before background jobs move to a queue): on average 1,157 × 0.025 = 29 cores are busy, so with the root's 4-core m7i.xlarge servers you need more than 7 of them before counting any headroom. Move the jobs to a queue (5.2 ms each) and it drops to 6 cores.
  • Connection pools. 3,472 database reads a second, each holding a connection for 1 ms: 3.5 connections busy on average (pools).
  • Sanity-check a load test. In Experiment 1 at 89% busy: 80 requests a second × 87 ms mean = 7 requests in the server on average. If a tool claims a throughput and a latency that do not multiply out to its concurrency, one of the numbers is wrong.

3. One queue for many servers beats a queue per server

A bank with one line feeding 8 tellers, against a supermarket with a line per till. With one shared line (M/M/c, c servers), a teller never sits idle while someone waits in another line. The numbers at 90% busy, in multiples of S: one shared queue for 8 servers, mean 1.9S, p99 7.1S; 8 separate queues with requests sprinkled at random, each is its own M/M/1, so mean 10S, p99 46S. Same servers, same load, a p99 6.5 times worse. Two consequences:

  • Big pools run hotter safely. 8 servers sharing a queue at 90% wait less than 1 server at 70%. This is why a large fleet can target a higher utilisation than a small one, and why splitting one pool into many small dedicated ones costs capacity.
  • A load balancer decides which world you live in. Random assignment builds the supermarket; least-outstanding-requests approximates the bank. The load balancing section measures the difference.

Why aim for 60 to 80% utilisation, not 100%

Four reasons stack up, each cheap to see from the formulas above:

  1. The curve. From 50% to 80% busy, the mean wait for one server rises from 1S to 4S; from 80% to 95% it rises to 19S.
  2. Your measurement window hides peaks. An hourly average of 70% contains minutes at 90% (see the peak factors below; Twitter's busiest second in 2013 was 25 times its average, in the root's Case files: "A peak 25 times the average").
  3. Servers fail. If one of four dies, the other three take its share: 60% becomes 80%. That is N+1 arithmetic.
  4. Capacity takes time to add. A new VM takes a minute or two, a GPU replica several minutes, a database resize longer: the headroom must cover the load growth while you wait.

The root's Scale simulator flags a component as hot at 70% and its suggested fixes stop at 60% (src/sim/model.py: HOT, TARGET = 0.7, 0.6); this page keeps those numbers. Work nobody is waiting for (overnight batch jobs, training) is the exception: run it near 100%, since only throughput matters there.

4. Variability multiplies waiting: Kingman's formula

The M/M/1 formula assumed exponential service times. Real ones are less or more variable. The coefficient of variation (cv) measures it: standard deviation divided by mean. Constant service has cv 0, exponential has cv 1, and a service where most requests take 5 ms and a few take 500 ms (a cache hit against a cache miss, a short prompt against a 100,000-token one) easily has cv 2 or more. John Kingman's 1961 heavy-traffic result (Proc. Cambridge Phil. Soc. 57, 902) gives the mean wait of a single queue, approximately:

Wq ≈ [ρ / (1 − ρ)] × [(ca2 + cs2) / 2] × S
Wq
mean time waiting in the queue (before service starts)
ca
cv of the gaps between arrivals (1 for random, Poisson arrivals; above 1 for bursty traffic)
cs
cv of the service times

Three factors: utilisation, variability, and the service time itself. At 80% busy with random arrivals: constant service waits 2S, exponential 4S, cv 2 waits 10S. Variability is a lever as strong as utilisation, and often cheaper to pull: put the slow kind of request in its own pool (the root's Step 3 moved PDF processing to a queue for exactly this reason), cap request sizes, or batch small requests together. For several servers the same correction applied to the exact M/M/c wait (the Allen-Cunneen approximation) is what the explorer below uses.

Load balancing

A load balancer (the root's Step 1) sits in front of a pool of identical servers and picks one for each piece of work. The previous section showed why the choice matters: random assignment turns 8 servers into 8 separate queues, while a policy that sends work to an idle server approaches one shared queue, with a p99 several times lower at the same cost. This section covers where balancers sit, the algorithms, how to keep affinity when you need it, and how servers enter and leave the pool safely.

Layer 4 and layer 7

The layer names come from the networking stack. A layer 4 (L4) balancer works at the transport layer: it sees TCP connections (addresses and ports), picks a server per connection and copies bytes; it never reads the HTTP inside. gRPC's engineers describe it: "The application data (HTTP/2 and gRPC frames) are simply copied between the client connection to the backend connection" (gRPC blog, 2017). Examples: AWS Network Load Balancer, Linux IPVS, Google's Maglev. A layer 7 (L7) balancer terminates HTTP: it reads each request, so it can balance per request, route by path or header, retry, and count requests in flight per server. Examples: nginx, Envoy, HAProxy, AWS Application Load Balancer.

The difference bites with long-lived connections. HTTP keep-alive and HTTP/2 send many requests over one connection, so an L4 balancer that spread connections evenly can still leave one server with the busiest client. For per-request balancing of HTTP/2 or gRPC you want L7, or client-side balancing, where the client itself knows the server list and picks per call (the gRPC post contrasts "Proxy Load Balancing" and "Client-Side Load Balancing"; a service-mesh sidecar such as Envoy next to each app is the common middle ground). Typical production shape: an L4 or cloud balancer at the edge, L7 inside.

Power of two choices deserves its name. Michael Mitzenmacher analysed it as the "supermarket model": requests arrive at n servers, "each customer chooses some constant d servers independently and uniformly at random ... and waits for service at the one with the fewest customers", and found that "having d = 2 choices leads to exponential improvements in the expected time a customer spends in the system over d = 1, whereas having d = 3 choices is only a constant factor better than d = 2" (IEEE TPDS 12(10), 2001). For many servers his limit gives the mean time in the system, in units of the service time, as the sum over i ≥ 1 of λ(di − d)/(d − 1). At 90% load: random (d = 1) 10, two choices 2.61, three choices 2.03. At 99%: 100 against 5.4. The second sample removes most of the damage and more samples add little, which is why Envoy ("Defaults to 2 so that we perform two-choice selection") and HAProxy ("The default value is 2") both sample two servers.

Mitzenmacher's limit as n grows (exponential service), computed from the formula above in src/recompute.py; his paper reports simulations within a few percent of it for 100 queues up to 95% load. The lab and the animation below use 8 servers, where two choices helps a little less.

Affinity: consistent hashing

Sometimes the same key must keep reaching the same server: a per-server in-memory cache, a chat whose model context (the LLM's KV cache) is warm on one GPU replica, or Discord's data services, where routing every request for a channel to one instance is what makes request coalescing work (root Case files: "Trillions of messages: hot partitions, request coalescing, a new database"). The naive way, server = hash(key) mod N, moves almost every key when N changes; nginx's documentation warns of exactly that: "adding or removing a server from the group may result in remapping most of the keys to different servers" (nginx upstream module). Consistent hashing places servers and keys on a circle and sends each key to the next server clockwise, so adding a server moves only the keys between it and its neighbour, about 1/N of them ("only a few keys will be remapped", nginx's hash ... consistent, ketama). Envoy offers a ring hash and Maglev, which "uses the algorithm described in section 3.4 of [the Maglev paper] with a fixed table size of 65537" and looks up faster (Envoy load balancers).

Affinity's cost is hot keys: one viral chat lands on one server. Bounded loads fixes it by refusing a server that already carries more than a set multiple of the average and moving on round the ring; HAProxy's hash-balance-factor: "if <factor> is 150, then no server will be allowed to have a load more than 1.5 times the average ... Reasonable values are from 125 to 200" (HAProxy 3.2 manual). Do not use affinity for stateless requests: it throws away the balancing you just paid for.

Health checks, draining and slow start

  • Active health checks: the balancer probes each server (an HTTP GET to a health endpoint every few seconds) and removes it after several failures. In open-source nginx they are not available: "periodic health checks is available as part of our commercial subscription"; the open-source version only counts real failures (max_fails, default 1, within fail_timeout, default 10 seconds). What a health endpoint should check (and why "the database is down" must not mark every server unhealthy) is on Reliability engineering, Health checks done right.
  • Outlier detection (passive): eject a server whose real responses go bad. Envoy's defaults: eject after 5 consecutive 5xx responses, checked every 10 s, for a base 30 s, never more than 10% of the pool at once (Envoy outlier detection). The 10% cap matters: a balancer that ejects every server has built an outage.
  • Connection draining: to remove a server (a deploy, a scale-in), stop sending it new requests but let the ones in flight finish. AWS ALB waits 300 seconds by default ("deregistration delay") and warns: "If a deregistering target terminates the connection before the deregistration delay elapses, the client receives a 500-level error response" (AWS ALB target group attributes). On Kubernetes a deleted pod gets a 30-second grace period by default before it is killed (pod lifecycle); the server must keep serving after SIGTERM until the balancer has stopped sending to it.
  • Slow start: a new server with a cold cache gets a ramp instead of its full share at once. HAProxy's slowstart grows a returning server's weight "linearly from 0 to 100%"; ALB's slow start mode does the same, but "You can't enable slow start mode when using the least outstanding requests or weighted random routing algorithms".

Caching strategy

A cache keeps a copy of an answer somewhere faster than where it came from, so the next request for it skips the slow path (the root's Step 2). It is the cheapest capacity you will ever buy: in the root's Numbers to know, a Redis read takes about 0.14 ms while the query it replaces can take milliseconds and, more importantly, uses up the database, the one component that is hardest to scale. It also brings three new problems: stale answers, a dependency that can empty itself, and stampedes. This section takes them in order.

Further left is faster and takes more load off everything behind it, but is harder to invalidate: you can delete a Redis key, you cannot reach into a million phones. So cache immutable or slowly changing things far out (the app's code and images on a CDN, with long Cache-Control: max-age and a new file name per version), and changing data close in, where you control it. An in-process cache (Python's functools.lru_cache, or cachetools 7.2 with a TTL) costs nothing per read but is per server: 8 servers mean 8 copies, 8 times the misses, and 8 places to invalidate. A shared cache is one network hop away and holds one copy for everyone.

Cache-aside for our chat API's "load a conversation", with redis-py 8.1 (current on 2026-10-04):

import json, redis r = redis.Redis(host="cache", port=6379) def get_chat(chat_id): key = f"chat:{chat_id}:v1" # version the key so a format change cannot read old entries hit = r.get(key) if hit is not None: return json.loads(hit) chat = db_load_chat(chat_id) # the slow path: a Postgres query r.set(key, json.dumps(chat), ex=300) # TTL 300 s bounds how stale it can ever be return chat def rename_chat(chat_id, title): db_rename_chat(chat_id, title) # 1. write the source of truth (commit) r.delete(f"chat:{chat_id}:v1") # 2. then delete, never update, the cached copy

Two details carry most of the correctness. Delete, do not update, on a write: two concurrent writers updating the cache can land in the wrong order and leave the older value cached forever, while a delete just forces a fresh read. Keep the TTL even with invalidation: some code path will forget to delete (a migration script, an admin tool), and the TTL is the upper bound on how long that bug lasts. Facebook's memcache paper names the remaining race, a reader putting back a value that a writer has just invalidated (a "stale set"), and closes it with leases: on a miss the cache hands out a token, and a later fill is accepted only if the token is still valid (Nishtala et al., NSDI 2013).

Hit-rate maths: what the cache does to the database

The hit rate h is the share of reads the cache answers. With λ reads a second, the database sees (1 − h) × λ. The scale is not linear in the way intuition says: going from 90% to 99% hits looks like a 9-point gain but divides the database's read load by ten (1,000 reads a second become 100, then 10). And the mean latency is h × tcache + (1 − h) × (tcache + tdb).

Cache read 0.143 ms (Redis GET, root's Numbers to know); a miss adds 5 ms for the database query (illustrative: an indexed primary-key lookup measured 0.067 ms there, a chat history with joins and sorting is slower). Default database capacity 600 reads a second and 1,000 reads a second of demand are the root's Step 2 example. Formulas checked in src/recompute.py.

Three consequences. The tail is the miss path: at a 95% hit rate, 5% of reads take the slow path, so the p99 is a database read however fast the cache is; the cache improves the median far more than the p99. The database is now sized for the misses, so the cache has become part of your capacity, and when it empties (a restart, a failover, a deploy that changes every key) the full load lands on a database sized for a tenth of it: the root's Scale simulator shows this as "cold cache", and Facebook keeps a small pool of spare "Gutter" servers (about 1% of the memcached fleet) to take over a failed server's keys rather than let its misses reach the databases. Warm up before taking traffic: fill a new cache from the old one, or ramp traffic with slow start. The root's Case files carry the good side of this dependency too: "DNS failed in one data center; caches kept most of GitHub up".

What sets the hit rate: how often the same key is read again within its TTL (a user's conversation is re-read on every message; a search with a unique query never is), how skewed popularity is (a few hot keys get most reads, which is why small caches work at all), and whether the hot set fits in memory.

Eviction: what to throw out when memory is full

  • LRU (least recently used): evict what was read longest ago. Cheap, good for most workloads. Redis approximates it by sampling: it "samples a small number of keys at random and then evicts the ones with the longest time since last access" (maxmemory-samples 5 by default).
  • LFU (least frequently used): evict what is read least often, with counts that decay so old popularity fades. Better when a few keys are steadily popular and a scan of one-off keys would flush them out of an LRU. Redis has it since 4.0, with a "Morris counter" of a few bits per key.
  • TinyLFU: an admission policy: a new entry only gets in if it is estimated to be read more often than the entry it would evict, using a compact frequency sketch (Einziger, Friedman and Manes, ACM ToS 2017). Caffeine, the standard Java cache, uses W-TinyLFU: "a small admission LRU that evicts to a large Segmented LRU if accepted by the TinyLfu admission policy" (Caffeine wiki).
Redis does not evict by default

The shipped redis.conf says "The default is: maxmemory-policy noeviction" (redis.conf, 8.2): when memory is full, writes fail. For a cache, set maxmemory and a policy explicitly; the Redis docs call allkeys-lru "a good default option if you have no reason to prefer any others" (Redis key eviction). Redis 8.6 added LRM (least recently modified) policies.

Thundering herds and cache stampedes

A cache stampede (also dog-piling, or a thundering herd): a popular entry expires, and every request that arrives before someone has rebuilt it misses and rebuilds it too. If the rebuild takes 300 ms and the key is read 200 times a second, 60 requests run the same expensive query at once, which makes the query slower, which widens the window. Facebook measured it on keys prone to it: "Without leases, all of the cache misses resulted in a peak database query rate of 17K/s. With leases, the peak database query rate was 1.3K/s" (Nishtala et al.; root Case files: "Leases cut a cache stampede from 17,000 to 1,300 queries a second"). Four defences:

  1. Request coalescing (single-flight): the first miss rebuilds; the others wait for its answer. Go ships it as singleflight: "Do executes and returns the results of the given function, making sure that only one execution is in-flight for a given key at a time. If a duplicate comes in, the duplicate caller waits for the original to complete and receives the same results" (golang.org/x/sync v0.23.0). nginx has proxy_cache_lock on: "only one request at a time will be allowed to populate a new cache element" (nginx proxy module). Facebook's leases do it across servers: one token per key every 10 seconds, other clients told to "wait a short amount of time". Cost: the waiters still wait for the rebuild.
  2. Serve stale while revalidating: keep the old value a little past its TTL and refresh in the background, so nobody waits. HTTP has a header for it (Cache-Control: stale-while-revalidate=30, RFC 5861); nginx does it with proxy_cache_use_stale updating plus proxy_cache_background_update on. Cost: a few seconds of staleness.
  3. Probabilistic early expiry (XFetch): each reader, before the entry expires, recomputes early with a probability that rises as expiry approaches and as the rebuild gets more expensive. Vattani, Chierichetti and Lowenstein proved this optimal (VLDB 2015); the whole algorithm is one line: recompute if now − Δ · β · ln(rand()) ≥ expiry, where Δ is how long the last rebuild took and β = 1 "already provides effective prevention against cache stampedes". Cost: one early rebuild per expiry, no waiting, no locks.
  4. TTL jitter: entries written together (after a deploy, a cache flush) with the same TTL expire together. Add a random 0 to 10% to each TTL so they spread out. (Advice, not from a single source.)

Before and after: one hot key expiring

The page's model (src/model.py, stampede): one key read 200 times a second, expiring at t = 1 s, rebuilt by a 300 ms database query; XFetch with β = 1 and Δ = 300 ms, seeded random draws. Illustrative rates; the counts (60, 1, 1 database queries) are checked in src/recompute.py.

A minimal single-flight for an asyncio service: the first caller for a key creates a future and does the work; others await the same future.

import asyncio _inflight: dict[str, asyncio.Future] = {} async def get_coalesced(key, load): if (fut := _inflight.get(key)) is not None: return await fut # someone is already loading this key: share their answer fut = asyncio.get_running_loop().create_future() _inflight[key] = fut try: value = await load(key) # the one real database call fut.set_result(value) return value except Exception as e: fut.set_exception(e) # waiters get the error too, not a hang raise finally: del _inflight[key]

Per process only: 8 servers can still send 8 queries. Route each key to one server (consistent hashing, as Discord did) or coalesce in the shared cache (a short lock key with SET key NX PX 3000) to go further.

CDNs

A CDN is a cache run for you in hundreds of cities. It serves static files (the app bundle, images) and any public response you mark cacheable with Cache-Control: public, max-age=... (s-maxage for shared caches only). Its hit ratio is what you pay for: every miss crosses the world to your servers. CDNs collapse concurrent misses for the same URL into one request to you (the same coalescing idea), and support stale-while-revalidate and stale-if-error. For our chat assistant the CDN carries the app and static assets; the conversations themselves are personal and uncacheable at the edge. LLM-specific caches (provider prompt caching, KV prefix reuse, semantic caches) are on ML system design, section 9.

When not to cache: data that must be exactly current (a credit balance, a permission just revoked), data read once (a unique search), or anything you cannot invalidate and would be harmed by serving late. And do not add a cache to hide a missing index: fix the query first (root Step 2).

Connection pooling and database load

Every query travels over a database connection: a TCP connection, often TLS, plus a login, plus memory on the database server. In Postgres it is a whole operating-system process: "PostgreSQL implements a "process per user" client/server model. In this model, every client process connects to exactly one backend process" (Postgres docs, How Connections Are Established). Opening one costs several network round trips (the root's Numbers to know: a new TCP plus TLS 1.3 connection costs 16 ms nearby and 156 ms across the world), and the server allows few: max_connections "is typically 100" by default (Postgres 18 docs). So production code never opens a connection per request. A connection pool keeps a few open and lends them out: a request borrows one, runs its queries, gives it back.

How big: Little's law again

Connections in use on average = queries per second × time each query holds a connection. The chat API at the busy hour sends about 3,472 database reads a second (1,157 requests, 3 reads each, the Scale simulator's defaults); at 1 ms each that is 3.5 connections busy on average. Pools need headroom for bursts, and the M/M/c maths says how much (the share of queries that find every connection busy, Erlang C, for 3.47 connections' worth of load):

Six to eight connections per database is enough for this whole product's reads, which surprises everyone the first time. The HikariCP authors (Java's most used pool) make the same point with the Oracle Real-World Performance group's demonstration, where cutting a pool from 2,048 connections to 96 took response times from about 100 ms to about 2 ms, and a starting formula, "connections = ((core_count * 2) + effective_spindle_count)", summed up as "You want a small pool, saturated with threads waiting for connections" (HikariCP, About Pool Sizing). A database with 8 cores cannot run more than 8 queries at once anyway; extra connections only add contention.

The two traps that take databases down

1. Pools multiply. SQLAlchemy 2.1's default pool is pool_size=5 plus max_overflow=10 per process (create_engine docs). 20 app servers × 4 worker processes × 15 = 1,200 possible connections against a database allowing 100. Size pools from the database's side: total across every process ≤ what the database can run.

2. Hold time is what counts. Holding a connection (or worse, an open transaction) while calling the model turns 1 ms into 10 seconds: 1,157 requests a second × 10 s = 11,570 connections. Read what you need, release the connection, call the model, then borrow one again to save the reply.

PgBouncer: a pool in front of the database

When many processes each hold their own pool, put one pooler in front of Postgres. PgBouncer (1.26.0, 23 September 2026) accepts thousands of client connections and maps them onto a few real ones. Its pool_mode decides when a real connection goes back (PgBouncer config):

  • session (the default): "Server is released back to pool after client disconnects." Safe, saves little.
  • transaction: "Server is released back to pool after transaction finishes." The mode that gives the big win, at a price: "clients must not use any session-based features, since each transaction ends up in a different connection and thus gets a different session state" (session variables, advisory locks, LISTEN; prepared statements are tracked, up to max_prepared_statements = 200 per connection by default).
  • statement: released after every query; multi-statement transactions are refused.

Defaults to know: default_pool_size = 20 real connections per user and database pair, max_client_conn = 100 client connections. Managed poolers exist too (Amazon RDS Proxy is one). For the chat API: each app process keeps a small pool (psycopg_pool 3.3 defaults to min_size=4, fixed size unless you set max_size), PgBouncer in transaction mode in front, and the database's max_connections sized for PgBouncer's real connections plus admin headroom.

When not to: a single small app with one process does not need PgBouncer; its own pool is enough. And a pool does not create database capacity: if the database is at 90% CPU, a bigger pool only makes more queries wait inside it.

Profiling: find where the time goes

When something is slow, the most expensive mistake is guessing. A profiler measures where a running program spends its time. Two kinds: tracing (deterministic) profilers record every function call (Python's cProfile), exact but slow enough to distort the program; sampling profilers look at the call stack many times a second and count what they see, which is cheap enough to run on a production server.

Reading a flame graph

Brendan Gregg's flame graph (December 2011) draws the sampled stacks: "The x-axis shows the stack profile population, sorted alphabetically (it is not the passage of time), and the y-axis shows stack depth, counting from zero at the bottom ... The wider a frame is, the more often it was present in the stacks" (Gregg, Flame Graphs; the CACM 2016 article). So you look for wide plateaus at the top: functions that are themselves on the CPU in many samples. A wide frame lower down only says that a lot happens beneath it.

Illustrative, not a measured profile: the shape to look for. Here re-tokenising the whole conversation on every message is the plateau; caching the token count per message would remove most of it.

The trap: a CPU profile shows only time on the CPU. A request that spends 400 ms waiting for the database or the model is invisible in it, because waiting uses no CPU. For waits you need off-CPU or wall-clock profiling (py-spy's --idle includes idle threads), or better, distributed tracing, which shows each call's duration across services (Observability).

py-spy, and Python's new built-in sampler

py-spy (0.4.2, April 2026) is the usual tool: a sampling profiler written in Rust that "doesn't run in the same process as the profiled Python program", so it attaches to a live server without a restart, with "extremely low overhead"; it supports CPython 2.3 to 2.7 and 3.3 to 3.14 (py-spy).

py-spy top --pid 12345 # live view: which functions are on the CPU now py-spy record -o profile.svg --pid 12345 # flame graph of a running server (Ctrl-C to stop) py-spy dump --pid 12345 # every thread's stack, once: "where is it stuck?" py-spy record --gil --idle -o p.svg --pid 12345 # only threads holding the GIL; include idle ones

Python 3.15 (final release due 9 October 2026, PEP 790) ships a sampling profiler in the standard library, profiling.sampling (the "tachyon" profiler), with cProfile moved to profiling.tracing (PEP 799, Final).

Async I/O, threads and processes in Python, and the GIL

Standard CPython has a global interpreter lock (GIL): only one thread runs Python code at a time in a process. A thread waiting on the network or a disk releases it. So the right tool depends on what the work waits for:

  • Waiting on I/O (database, Redis, the model API: almost everything our chat API does): asyncio (one thread, thousands of waits interleaved; FastAPI's async def endpoints) or threads. FastAPI runs plain def endpoints in AnyIO worker threads, and "The default AnyIO worker thread limiter has a value of 40" (AnyIO docs), a hidden concurrency cap worth knowing.
  • CPU work in Python (tokenising, JSON on large payloads, image resizing): threads do not help under the GIL; use processes (several Uvicorn or Gunicorn workers, roughly one per core, or a ProcessPoolExecutor), or native code that releases the GIL (NumPy, most tokenizers written in Rust or C).

Python 3.14 (October 2025) made it "officially supported but still optional" (phase II of PEP 779), whose acceptance criteria capped the single-thread slowdown at 15%; the 3.14 docs measure about 1% on macOS on Apple silicon and 8% on x86-64 Linux (Python 3.14 free-threading howto). Making it the default (phase III) is "left for a future PEP". In practice: the default python3 still has a GIL, C extensions must be rebuilt and declare support, so for a web service in 2026 processes for CPU work and asyncio for I/O remain the safe default; try the free-threaded build for CPU-heavy, thread-friendly code with its dependencies checked.

Load testing without fooling yourself

A load test sends synthetic traffic at a system to learn what it does under load before real users find out. Its kinds differ by the question: a load test (can it carry the expected peak?), a stress or breakpoint test (where and how does it break?), a soak test (hours at normal load: leaks, slow growth), a spike test (a sudden jump: does autoscaling keep up?). All of them share two traps that make a test report numbers far better than reality.

Trap 1: the workload model

A closed workload has a fixed number of virtual users; each sends a request, waits for the answer, maybe "thinks", and only then sends the next. An open workload has requests arriving at a rate, whatever the system is doing. Bianca Schroeder, Adam Wierman and Mor Harchol-Balter showed how different the answers are ("Open Versus Closed: A Cautionary Tale", NSDI 2006): "for a fixed load, the mean response time for an open system model can exceed that for a closed system model by an order of magnitude or more", and variability in request sizes "has a huge impact on response times in open systems" but "much less of an effect in closed models". A closed test is self-limiting: when the server slows down, the users slow down with it, so the queue never builds the way it would in production. Users on the internet do not wait for each other, so a public API is close to open (strictly, "partly open": sessions arrive independently, and inside a session a user waits for each reply).

Trap 2: coordinated omission

Gil Tene named the measurement version of the same problem ("How NOT to Measure Latency", Strange Loop 2015). A closed-loop tool measures each request from when it was actually sent. During a stall it sends nothing, so the requests that real users would have sent during the stall, and that would have waited, are never measured: the tool has coordinated with the server to omit the bad moments. That is coordinated omission. wrk2's README: wrk "exhibits a strong Coordinated Omission effect, through which most of the high latency artifacts exhibited by the measured server will be ignored"; wrk2 instead "measures response latency from the time the transmission should have occurred according to the constant throughput configured for the run" (wrk2). Its example with a 1.4 second server pause: corrected p99 1.27 s, uncorrected p99 6.04 ms.

Experiment 2: the same stalling server, measured closed-loop and open-loop

Experiment 1's server (one request at a time, exponential service, mean 10 ms nominal), set to freeze for 1.5 s every 15 s, as a stop-the-world garbage-collection pause or a slow disk flush would. 30 requests a second for 60 s, twice: a closed-loop generator (3 users, each sending at most 10 a second, waiting for each answer, catching up immediately when late: what wrk, Locust users and k6's constant-vus do), and an open-loop one (evenly spaced sends at the same rate, never waiting). The closed run also logged when each request should have been sent, which gives the corrected reading.

Designing a realistic test

  1. Model the traffic from production: the mix of endpoints (for every message, our app also loads history, lists chats, refreshes auth: 1 + 4 requests), the request sizes, the spread of keys. A test that reads one chat a million times measures the cache, not the system.
  2. Model the slow dependencies honestly. If the model provider is stubbed out, stub it with its real latency distribution, streaming for about 10 seconds, or the test will never find the connection pool and worker limits that a 10-second call exposes.
  3. Ramp in steps and hold each step long enough for queues to settle and for the p99 to rest on enough samples (thousands of requests per step).
  4. Record offered against achieved throughput, errors and every resource's utilisation (USE). If achieved stops following offered, you are past the knee; if errors rise, the latency numbers are flattered by the failures.
  5. Check the load generator. If its own CPU is saturated, its latency numbers are its own queueing. Our Experiment 1 logged how late each send was against its schedule for this reason (its p99 lateness was 3 to 7 ms, small next to latencies of 50 ms and more, and counted in the measurement because latency runs from the scheduled time).
  6. Test in an environment shaped like production (same instance types, same database size, same cache warmth) and say what differs.

Finding the knee

Plot the p99 (y) against achieved throughput (x) across the steps: flat, then bending up, then vertical. The knee is where it bends; the usable capacity is the highest throughput whose p99 still meets your target with headroom left. From Experiment 1 (one server, about 11 ms of work): p99 54 ms at 11% busy, 114 ms at 57%, 190 ms at 79%, 321 ms at 89%, 556 ms at 95%, then seconds. With a p99 target of 200 ms this server's usable capacity is about 80% of its maximum; with 100 ms, about 50%. The target, not the hardware, sets the usable fraction. Little's law checks the result: achieved throughput × mean latency must equal the concurrency the generator held in flight.

Capacity planning

Capacity planning decides how much hardware to have, and when, so that the peak is served with the required reliability at the lowest cost. Google's SRE book lists what it needs: "An accurate organic demand forecast, which extends beyond the lead time required for acquiring capacity", "An accurate incorporation of inorganic demand sources into the demand forecast", and "Regular load testing of the system to correlate raw capacity (servers, disks, and so on) to service capacity" (SRE book, ch. 1). Organic demand is steady growth; inorganic is a launch, a marketing push, a viral post. The load test supplies the per-server capacity; the rest of this section turns the forecast into a number of servers.

Peak factors: the root's 2× explained

Capacity is sized for the peak, not the average: the peak factor is the busiest period's rate divided by the average. The root assumed 2× (busy hour over daily average) and labelled it illustrative. Real hourly traffic says it is a sensible number for one region and too high for a global product. Wikipedia's hourly page views by human users for September 2026 (Wikimedia's public API):

Source: Wikimedia REST API, hourly aggregate page views, agent "user", 2026-09-01 to 2026-09-30 UTC (fetched 2026-10-04 by src/fetch_peaks.py; 720 hours per project). Curves: the average September day, each hour's mean divided by the month's mean. Table: busy hour over its day's average (median day), quietest hour of the average day over the mean, and the busiest hour of the month over the month's mean.

The window matters as much as the product. Per second, peaks are far sharper: Twitter's busiest second in August 2013 was 143,199 Tweets against an average of about 5,700, a factor of 25 (root Case files: "A peak 25 times the average"). Servers absorb second-scale bursts with queues and headroom; hours-scale peaks need capacity; known events (a launch, a match final) need a separate inorganic forecast.

Headroom, N+1 and N+2

Let N be the number of servers needed to carry the peak at the target utilisation. N+1 adds one spare so that losing any single server still leaves the peak served. N+2 adds two, so that one server can be down for maintenance or a deploy while another fails. Google's capacity planning tool expresses requirements this way: "I want to meet service Foo's demand in each geographic region, and have N + 2 redundancy" (SRE book, ch. 18). The same logic applies to bigger failure units. Spread across Z availability zones (separate data centres in one region), losing a zone loses 1/Z of capacity, so the fleet must be Z/(Z − 1) times the peak need: 1.5× with 3 zones, 2× with 2.

Autoscaling adds and removes servers as load moves (AWS target tracking on CPU or requests per target, Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler). It does not remove the need to plan: it needs a floor that survives a failure, a ceiling that the database behind it can take, a signal that tracks real load (Slack's autoscaler removed servers that looked idle because they were waiting on a saturated network: root Case files, "The first Monday of the year: autoscaling made it worse"), and it is too slow for the first minutes of a spike. GPU replicas take minutes to start, which changes the whole approach: ML system design, section 8.

The plan for the chat API

One server: an m7i.xlarge (4 vCPU, $0.2016 an hour on demand in us-east-1, 730 hours a month, the root's price) carrying 769 requests a second at 100% (4 cores / 5.2 ms of CPU per request, the Scale simulator's figure with background jobs on a queue). Defaults: the root's chat assistant, 1 million daily users × 10 messages × 5 requests per message / 86,400 s = 579 requests a second on average. Servers = ceil(peak / (769 × target)) + spares. Checked in src/recompute.py.

With the defaults the arithmetic is: peak 1,157 requests a second, 769 × 0.6 = 462 per server, so 3 servers carry the peak at 50% each, and N+1 makes 4 ($589 a month). With all 4 up, the peak runs at 38%; with one down, 50%, the same as the root's Scale simulator after its suggested fixes (3 app servers at 50%). Growing 10% a month for 6 months nearly doubles the load (1.77×), which needs 6 servers. The app tier is the cheap part: the root's Step 6 shows the GPU fleet dominating the bill, and the same method sizes it with a per-GPU throughput instead of a per-server one.

When not to over-plan: stateless app servers in a cloud autoscale in minutes, so plan their floor and limits rather than a year of growth; spend the forecasting effort where lead times are long (GPUs, database instances, reserved capacity contracts).

Common mistakes

Reporting the average latency
It hides the tail that users feel and cannot be combined across servers. Report p50, p99 and p99.9 from merged histograms.
Planning to run at 90% to "use what we pay for"
At 90% a single queue's wait is 9 service times and the p99 is 46; one failure or a 10% burst pushes it past 100%. Experiment 1's runs aimed at 90% actually ran at 101% because the real service time was 12% longer than assumed.
Load testing closed-loop and quoting the p99
The tool stops sending when the server stalls, so the stall is missing from the data: Experiment 2's closed-loop p99 was 89 ms against 1,400 ms for the same server measured open-loop. Use arrival-rate executors and measure from the scheduled time.
Load testing one URL with one key
100% cache hits measures the cache. Use production's key spread and endpoint mix.
Random or round-robin balancing in front of uneven work
Both keep sending a slow server its full share. In Experiment 3, with one server three times slower, random and round robin had p99s of 28 seconds (the slow server's queue grew all run); two choices kept the p99 at 280 ms.
Treating the cache as optional when the database is sized for the misses
A cold cache sends ten times the reads to a database sized for 10% of them. Warm caches before traffic, keep spare cache capacity (Facebook's Gutter), and protect hot keys from stampedes.
Updating the cache on write instead of deleting it
Concurrent writers can leave the older value cached for the full TTL. Delete after the database commit; keep a TTL as the backstop.
Every process opening its own big pool
Pools multiply across processes and servers; the database's 100 connections run out. Size from the database's side; add PgBouncer.
Holding a database connection across a model call
The hold time becomes seconds and Little's law asks for thousands of connections. Release before calling out.
Adding threads to speed up CPU-bound Python
Under the GIL four threads took as long as running the jobs one after another (Experiment 4). Use processes, native code, or the free-threaded build with its dependencies checked.
Profiling only the CPU
A request waiting 400 ms on the network shows nothing in a CPU flame graph. Use wall-clock profiling or tracing for waits.
Sizing for the daily average, or for a per-hour peak when per-second bursts matter
Size for the busy hour with headroom for bursts; plan known events separately. Real peak factors range from 1.2 (global Wikipedia, hourly) to 25 (Twitter, per second).

Glossary

Cache: a faster copy of an answer; hit rate is the share of reads it serves.
Capacity planning: forecast, peak factor, headroom and spares turned into hardware and dates.
CDN: a cache run in many cities near users.
Closed workload: a fixed set of users, each waiting for an answer before sending again.
Coordinated omission: a load tester missing the requests it failed to send during a stall.
Coalescing: many identical requests in flight served by one backend call.
Consistent hashing: key-to-server mapping that moves about 1/N of keys when a server changes.
Connection and pool: an open, logged-in channel to a database; a set kept open and lent out.
Coefficient of variation: standard deviation divided by mean; 0 constant, 1 exponential.
Flame graph: sampled call stacks; width is time share, not a timeline.
GIL: CPython's lock letting one thread run Python code at a time.
Hedged request: a backup copy sent after a delay; the first answer wins.
Hit rate: share of reads answered by the cache; backend load is (1 − h) × reads.
Knee: where latency starts to rise steeply with load.
L4 / L7 balancer: picks per connection without reading HTTP / reads each HTTP request.
Latency: time for one request, sent to answered.
Load balancer: picks a server for each piece of work.
Load test: synthetic traffic to learn behaviour under load.
M/M/1, M/M/c: random arrivals, random (exponential) service, 1 or c servers sharing one queue.
Open workload: requests arrive at a rate regardless of answers.
Power of two choices: sample two servers, use the less loaded.
Peak factor: busiest period's rate over the average.
Percentile: the value a given share of requests beat (p50, p99).
Poisson process: independent random arrivals at a steady average rate.
Profiler: measures where a program spends its time; sampling or tracing.
Queue: work waiting for a busy resource.
Saturation: work queued because a resource is fully busy.
Stampede: many requests rebuilding the same expired cache entry at once.
Tail: the slow end of the latency distribution.
Throughput: requests finished per second.
Utilisation (ρ): share of time a resource is busy.

Load-test lab

A simulated load test you can run a hundred times a minute. Requests arrive at random (an open workload, as real users do) at the utilisation you choose; a balancer sends each one to one of several servers, each serving one request at a time, first come first served. For every policy the lab sweeps the load from 10% to 98% and draws the p50 or p99 latency, so you see where each one's knee is. Every policy gets identical arrivals and identical work per request; only the choice of server differs. The code is the same as the Reading's animation (src/model.py and parts/21_js_model.js, checked against each other).

Try: start with "Measured setup" and compare the dots (Experiment 3, eight real server processes) with the curves. Then "One slow server": random and round robin go vertical early, two choices barely moves. Then "Heavy-tailed requests": every curve rises (Kingman's variability term), and the gap between policies widens. Then 32 servers: one shared queue and least outstanding can run far hotter.

How the lab works, and how it was checked

  • Model. Poisson arrivals at rate ρ × total capacity; each server serves one request at a time in arrival order; a request's latency is its wait plus its own service time. Policies: random; round robin; power of two choices (two distinct servers at random, the one with fewer requests in it, ties to the first drawn); least outstanding (fewest in it, ties to the lowest number); one shared queue (each request goes to whichever server frees up first: exactly M/M/c when service is exponential). The first 10% of each run is dropped as warm-up.
  • Checked against theory. With exponential service, random assignment makes every server an M/M/1 queue, so its p99 should be ln(100) / (1 − ρ) service times; one shared queue should match M/M/c's exact distribution (Erlang C). Tick "Theory lines" to see both. At 90% with 8 servers, the default run gives a random p99 of 40.4 against 46.1 in theory, and shared 7.6 against 7.1 (src/recompute_out.json); the gap is sampling noise of 18,000 requests, which the 60,000 option narrows.
  • Checked against measurement. The dots are Experiment 3 on the Reading tab: eight real server processes and a client-side balancer, at the real utilisations of 75% and 96%, latencies divided by the measured service time (21.6 ms) and multiplied by the one you set. They show when the settings match the experiment (8 servers, exponential, no slow server; or the slow-server run at 75%).
  • Same code in two languages. src/model.py and parts/21_js_model.js are line-for-line twins; src/check_model.mjs runs both on 110 cases (1,525 numbers) and finds no difference above 1.2 × 10−15.
  • Not modelled: network time, servers with several worker slots, retries and timeouts (the Resilience lab on Reliability engineering has those), stale load information at the balancer.

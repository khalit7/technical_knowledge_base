4. Writing: dirty pages, writeback and throttling

If every write() waited for the device, a checkpoint written in 4 KiB pieces would pay a device round trip for each. So write() copies the bytes into the cached page, marks it dirty (changed in memory, not yet on the device) and returns. Per-device flusher threads (kernel workers named kworker/u*:*-flush-*) write dirty pages back for one of three reasons ({{vm sysctl docs|https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/sysctl/vm.html}}; mm/page-writeback.c lines 74 to 109):

  1. Age: every dirty_writeback_centisecs (500, so 5 s) the flusher wakes and writes data that has been dirty longer than dirty_expire_centisecs (3000, so 30 s).
  2. Background threshold: when dirty data passes dirty_background_ratio (10%) of dirtyable memory (free memory plus file pages that could be reclaimed, not total RAM: global_dirtyable_memory, line 362), the flusher writes everything, whatever its age.
  3. Hard threshold: near dirty_ratio (20%) the writing process itself is paused in balance_dirty_pages (line 1555, the pause at line 1795) so it cannot dirty memory faster than the device drains it. This is dirty throttling: write() becomes slow without any error.

With cgroup v2 each memory cgroup is also its own writeback domain, with thresholds scaled to its memory, so a container with a 1.5 GB limit is throttled long before the machine is ({{cgroup v2 docs, Writeback|https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/cgroup-v2.html#writeback}}). measured Each run in Fig. 3 and the chart below it wrote a file into a fresh container (limit 1,500 MB) and sampled its memory.stat (dirty and under-writeback page cache) and io.stat (bytes the device received) every 100 ms.

  1. The write returns; all of it is dirty. Each lane is one run in its own fresh container: 16 MiB written in 0.01 or 0.02 s, 256 MiB in 0.29 s. Nothing has reached the device yet.
  2. fsync forces the 256 MiB out: 110 ms. The right lane calls fsync after its write: the dirty pages go under writeback and the device receives all 256 MiB before fsync returns.
  3. A fresh container's 16 MiB leaves 4.5 s after the write, at the flusher's next 5-second wake-up, long before it is 30 s old. Likely because a brand-new writeback domain has almost no share of the background threshold, so even 16 MiB counts as over it (our reading of wb_over_bg_thresh, not traced; see the question below).
  4. A primed container's 16 MiB stays dirty. This container had just written and synced 1 GiB, so it has a share and 16 MiB is under its threshold: the flusher wakes every 5 s and leaves the data alone.
  5. 30.2 s after the write, it leaves. That is when the age rule (dirty longer than dirty_expire_centisecs, 30 s, found at a 5-second wake-up) would send it. The other three primed runs left after 7.7, 18.0 and 0.4 s: when is not a promise.

Fig. 3: each lane's 16 tiles are sixteenths of what that run wrote: orange dirty in the page cache, blue received by the device (memory.stat and io.stat every 100 ms) measured. Time is seconds after the first sample that saw the write.

Six runs wrote 16 MiB (or 256 MiB) and then waited, with nothing calling fsync. The textbook rule is "written back once it is 30 s old". How long did the 16 MiB actually stay dirty?
Measured, from the first 100 ms sample that saw the write: in fresh containers the data stayed dirty 4.5 s (16 MiB) and 5.3 s (256 MiB); in the four primed runs 7.7, 30.2, 18.0 and 0.4 s. In a fresh container the data left at the flusher's next 5-second wake-up, long before it was 30 s old. Our reading of the source: wb_over_bg_thresh (mm/page-writeback.c line 1960) also compares this writeback domain's dirty pages with its share of the background threshold, a share proportional to how much it recently wrote back. A brand-new container has almost no share, so even 16 MiB counts as "over its threshold" and is written in the background. After the container had just written and synced 1 GiB (the "primed" runs), it had a share, and the same 16 MiB waited between 0.4 and 30 s as that share decayed. We could not trace the flusher here (that needs privileges), so treat the explanation as the likely one, not a proven one. The lesson does not depend on it: when dirty data reaches the device is not a promise you can use. Only fsync is.
When it bites in ML
  • A crash in the window loses data that every call said was written. The 256 MiB checkpoint of section 10 left 256 MiB dirty when torch.save returned. Killing the process does not lose it (the dirty pages belong to the kernel); only a kernel crash, a power loss or a VM or node being killed does.
  • Dirty throttling stalls training. In the "1.5 GiB, as fast as possible" run the writer was caught sleeping in balance_dirty_pages while the container's dirty data hovered at the domain's limits. A loop that saves a large checkpoint synchronously stalls the same way; a background writer thread moves the stall off the training thread but does not remove it.
  • Dirty pages count toward your container's memory and cannot be dropped until written, so a memory-limited container that writes faster than its device can be pushed toward the OOM killer by its own page cache ({{Virtual memory|n:3f05c17b0d0d81a48892cea154ce950b}}).

The write path in the source, this VM's thresholds, commands and fixes (depth)

source write() on ext4 runs generic_perform_write (mm/filemap.c line 3288, v5.10): for each page, ext4_da_write_begin (fs/ext4/inode.c line 2934) finds or creates the page in the cache and reserves space without choosing disk blocks (delayed allocation, section 7), the bytes are copied in, the page is marked dirty, and balance_dirty_pages_ratelimited (line 3365) checks whether this writer has dirtied too much. Then write() returns. Nothing has touched the device.

On this VM the kernel computed a background threshold of MiB and a hard threshold of MiB VM-wide (/proc/vmstat) measured.

Commands and fixes
  • See it: grep -E "^(Dirty|Writeback):" /proc/meminfo (machine), grep -E "file_(dirty|writeback)" /sys/fs/cgroup/memory.stat (container), cat /proc/PID/wchan (a writer in balance_dirty_pages is being throttled), grep -E "nr_dirty(_background)?_threshold" /proc/vmstat.
  • Tune (hosts you own): vm.dirty_background_bytes and vm.dirty_bytes set absolute limits instead of ratios, which is saner on machines with hundreds of GB of RAM where 20% is far more than a device can drain in seconds.
  • In code: fsync what must survive (section 5); for a long streaming write, start writeback early with sync_file_range(SYNC_FILE_RANGE_WRITE) on each finished chunk and fdatasync once at the end, which keeps dirty data and the final fsync small.

This also explains the root's Debug lab "fsync" case, where 256 MiB was clean 5 s later: a fresh container's writeback domain is over its share at once.

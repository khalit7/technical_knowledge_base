5. EEVDF: the same fairness, with deadlines (Linux 6.6 and later) not run here

ch. 9 extended; {{EEVDF doc|https://docs.kernel.org/scheduler/sched-eevdf.html}}; {{LWN, 2023-03-09|https://lwn.net/Articles/925371/}}; {{LWN, Completing the EEVDF scheduler, 2024|https://lwn.net/Articles/969062/}}

The problem with CFS. Its latency comes from heuristics (the sleeper credit, the wake-up granularity, "buddies"), and a thread cannot say "short bursts, but soon" except by raising its weight, which also gives it more CPU. EEVDF (Stoica and Abdel-Wahab, 1995, {{Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First|https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~istoica/papers/eevdf-tr-95.pdf}}), merged in Linux 6.6 to replace CFS's pick, keeps virtual runtime and adds a request size per thread (its slice) and a virtual deadline:

V, the virtual clockThe weighted mean of the queued vruntimes (avg_vruntime, {{fair.c line 653, v6.12|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.12/kernel/sched/fair.c#L653}}): where an ideal fair CPU would have everyone now.
lagw × (V - v): service owed (positive) or overdrawn (negative); lags sum to zero.
eligiblelag ≥ 0, that is v ≤ V (line 726): only threads owed service may run.
virtual deadlined = v + slice × 1024 / w, renewed when a slice is used up (line 1007).
the pickThe eligible thread with the earliest deadline (pick_eevdf, line 907): a short slice means an early deadline, so latency is requested, not earned.
run to parityA picked thread keeps the CPU until its slice ends or it turns ineligible (RUN_TO_PARITY, {{features.h line 20|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.12/kernel/sched/features.h#L20}}).

The slice is 0.75 ms (sysctl_sched_base_slice) times the CPU factor: 2.25 ms on this VM's 5 CPUs, 3 ms on machines with 8 or more. Since 6.12 an ordinary thread can ask for its own with sched_setattr() and a sched_runtime, clamped to 0.1 to 100 ms ({{kernel/sched/syscalls.c line 306, v6.12|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.12/kernel/sched/syscalls.c#L306}}); a woken thread with a shorter slice may then cut the current one's slice short (PREEMPT_SHORT). CFS's sched_latency_ns, sched_min_granularity_ns and sched_wakeup_granularity_ns are gone.

One CPU, three threads, two schedulers model

A trainer and a preproc thread are busy loops at nice 0; a loader wakes every 4 ms, needs 0.4 ms of CPU and sleeps (a thread that receives a batch and queues the next prefetch). One CPU, 1 ms tick, this VM's tunables, under 5.10's CFS rules and 6.12's EEVDF rules as this page's simulator implements them (the {{Fair-share stepper|#t-fair}} tab lists each rule's source line and what it leaves out). Predict: under which scheduler does the loader wait longest after waking?

EEVDF makes latency a request: the loader waits until it asks for a short slice

Under CFS the loader wakes 9 ms behind min_vruntime, more than the 3 ms wake-up granularity, and preempts at once: zero wait. Under EEVDF it wakes with the lag it left with (about zero), eligible but with a deadline 2.25 ms ahead, and the running loop keeps its slice (run to parity): it waits up to the end of that slice. With a 0.1 ms slice its deadline comes first and PREEMPT_SHORT lets it in: the wait nearly vanishes, its CPU share unchanged. Total CPU per thread is the same in all three; EEVDF switches more often because its slices are shorter.

How far to trust this
A one-CPU model checked line by line against the source, not against a 6.6+ kernel (this VM runs 5.10). With an idle CPU the loader would simply be woken there (section 7); the difference matters when every CPU is busy, as on an oversubscribed training node. Custom slices (6.12) were added as "a new feature intended to help latency-sensitive tasks" ({{LWN, Completing the EEVDF scheduler|https://lwn.net/Articles/969062/}}).

On a 6.12 kernel, a serving process's request thread wakes, runs 200 us and sleeps, next to batch jobs on every core. Which change cuts its wake-up delay without giving it more CPU?

A custom slice moves its deadline earlier, which EEVDF uses to pick, and lets it preempt a thread with a longer slice; its share is unchanged because weight is unchanged. nice -10 also helps, by taking about 9 times the weight, which is more CPU, not lower latency. SCHED_BATCH does the opposite: batch threads never preempt on wake-up (section 6 measures 2 ms of delay).
Sleeping and waking under EEVDF: lag kept, delayed dequeue (depth)

CFS forgot a sleeper's history (max(own, min_vruntime - 9 ms)). EEVDF remembers its lag (PLACE_LAG, place_entity, v6.12 line 5266): a thread that slept while owed service comes back owed it, and one that slept after running ahead comes back behind. A thread that blocks while ineligible is not removed at all until it becomes eligible (DELAY_DEQUEUE, features.h line 49), so sleeping cannot be used to shed negative lag. The base slice is set at line 76 and scaled by the CPU factor in update_sysctl (line 212).

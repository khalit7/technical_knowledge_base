3. Policies, from OSTEP to proportional share

ch. 7 Scheduling: Introduction; ch. 8 Multi-level Feedback Queue; ch. 9 Proportional Share

Two yardsticks. Turnaround time (finish minus arrival) measures how long batch work takes; response time (first run minus arrival) measures how long an interactive thread waits before anything happens. OSTEP builds policies one flaw at a time: FIFO (a long job delays everyone behind it), shortest-job-first and shortest-time-to-completion-first (best turnaround, but they need to know job lengths), round robin (best response, worst turnaround), and the multi-level feedback queue, which learns lengths from behaviour. The root's {{OS simulators|n:3f05c17b0d0d81c5a97ce991644af140}} tab runs all of them on the same jobs, reproducing OSTEP's figures; this page does not repeat them.

Proportional share asks a different question: not "who finishes first" but "does each job get its share". OSTEP chapter 9 gives three answers, and the third is Linux's:

Lottery (1994)Each job holds tickets; every slice the scheduler draws a random ticket. Shares are right on average, noisy over short windows ({{Waldspurger and Weihl, OSDI 1994|https://www.usenix.org/legacy/publications/library/proceedings/osdi/full_papers/waldspurger.pdf}}).
StrideDeterministic: each job has a stride = a big number / its tickets and a pass counter; run the job with the smallest pass, then add its stride to its pass. Exact shares every round.
CFS (2007)Stride scheduling in units of time: the pass is virtual runtime, which grows by the CPU time used divided by the weight. Run the smallest. Section 4.

OSTEP's stride example: A, B and C hold 100, 50 and 250 tickets, so with a big number of 10,000 their strides are . Starting from zero (ties to A, then B), the first eight picks are : C runs five times in eight, A twice, B once, and over any long window the shares are exactly 250 : 100 : 50. Replace "tickets" by "weight" and "stride" by "1024 / weight per unit of CPU time" and you have the core of CFS.

What a real scheduler must add
Jobs that sleep (should a thread that slept for an hour get an hour of catch-up? CFS: no, at most half a latency period, section 4), new jobs (where to start their counter), many CPUs (one queue or many, section 7), and groups of jobs (containers, section 9). Most of the kernel code is about these, not about the pick.

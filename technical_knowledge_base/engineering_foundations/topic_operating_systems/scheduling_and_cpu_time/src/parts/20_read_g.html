5. EEVDF: the same fairness, with deadlines (Linux 6.6 and later) not run here

ch. 9 extended; {{EEVDF doc|https://docs.kernel.org/scheduler/sched-eevdf.html}}; {{LWN, 2023-03-09|https://lwn.net/Articles/925371/}}; {{LWN, Completing the EEVDF scheduler, 2024|https://lwn.net/Articles/969062/}}

The problem with CFS. CFS's fairness is clean, but its latency comes from heuristics bolted on: the sleeper credit, the wake-up granularity, the "buddies" that bias the pick, each with a tunable. A thread had no way to say "I only need short bursts, but I need them soon" except raising its weight, which also gives it more CPU. The idea, from Stoica and Abdel-Wahab's 1995 paper ({{Earliest Eligible Virtual Deadline First|https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~istoica/papers/eevdf-tr-95.pdf}}), merged into Linux 6.6 to replace CFS's pick: keep virtual runtime, and add a request size per thread (its slice) and a virtual deadline.

V, the virtual clockThe weighted average of the queued threads' vruntimes (avg_vruntime, {{fair.c line 653, v6.12|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.12/kernel/sched/fair.c#L653}}): where an ideal fair CPU would have everyone now.
lagw × (V - v): service a thread is owed (positive) or has had too much of (negative). The lags sum to zero.
eligiblelag ≥ 0, that is v ≤ V (vruntime_eligible, line 726): only threads that are owed service may run.
virtual deadlined = v + slice × 1024 / w, renewed each time a slice is used up (update_deadline, line 1007).
the pickAmong eligible threads, the earliest virtual deadline (pick_eevdf, line 907). A short slice means an early deadline, so latency is requested, not earned.
run to parityOnce picked, a thread keeps the CPU until its slice is used up or it becomes ineligible (RUN_TO_PARITY, {{features.h line 20|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.12/kernel/sched/features.h#L20}}): no CFS-style "wake-up granularity" race.

Sleeping and waking. CFS forgot a sleeper's history (max(own, min_vruntime - 9 ms)). EEVDF remembers its lag (PLACE_LAG, place_entity, line 5266): a thread that slept while owed service comes back owed it, and one that slept after running ahead comes back behind. A thread that blocks while ineligible is not removed at all until it becomes eligible (DELAY_DEQUEUE, features.h line 49), so sleeping cannot be used to shed negative lag. The slice is sysctl_sched_base_slice = 0.75 ms (line 76) times the same CPU factor as CFS's tunables (update_sysctl, line 212): 2.25 ms on this VM's 5 CPUs, 3 ms on machines with 8 or more. Since 6.12 a thread can ask for its own slice with sched_setattr() and a sched_runtime while staying an ordinary thread, clamped to 0.1 to 100 ms ({{kernel/sched/syscalls.c line 306, v6.12|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/v6.12/kernel/sched/syscalls.c#L306}}); a woken thread with a shorter slice may then cut the current one's slice short (PREEMPT_SHORT). CFS's sched_latency_ns, sched_min_granularity_ns and sched_wakeup_granularity_ns are gone.

One CPU, three threads, two schedulers model

The same input under Linux 5.10's CFS rules and Linux 6.12's EEVDF rules, as this page's simulator implements them (each rule cites its source line; the {{Fair-share stepper|#t-fair}} tab lists what it leaves out): a trainer thread and a preproc thread are busy loops at nice 0; a loader thread wakes every 4 ms, needs 0.4 ms of CPU, and sleeps again, like a thread that receives a batch and queues the next prefetch. All on one CPU with a 1 ms tick and this VM's tunables (factor 3). Watch the loader's wake-up delay.

What it shows. Under CFS the loader is placed 9 ms behind min_vruntime at every wake-up, is always more than the 3 ms wake-up granularity behind the running thread, and preempts at once: zero wait. Under EEVDF it wakes with the lag it left with (about zero), is eligible with a deadline 2.25 ms ahead, but the running busy loop keeps its slice (run to parity): the loader waits up to the end of that slice. Give the loader a 0.1 ms slice and its deadline comes first and PREEMPT_SHORT lets it in: the wait nearly vanishes, with no change in its CPU share. The busy loops get the same total CPU under all three; EEVDF switches more often because its slices are shorter than CFS's 6 ms.

How far to trust this
A one-CPU model of the pick, placement and preemption rules, checked line by line against the source but not against a 6.6+ kernel (this VM runs 5.10). On a real machine with an idle CPU the loader would simply be woken there (section 7), so the difference matters when every CPU is busy, which is exactly an oversubscribed training node. The custom-slice interface (6.12) was added as "a new feature intended to help latency-sensitive tasks" ({{LWN, Completing the EEVDF scheduler|https://lwn.net/Articles/969062/}}).

On a 6.12 kernel, a serving process's request thread wakes, runs 200 us and sleeps, next to batch jobs on every core. Which change cuts its wake-up delay without giving it more CPU?

A custom slice moves its deadline earlier, which EEVDF uses to pick, and lets it preempt a thread with a longer slice; its share is unchanged because weight is unchanged. nice -10 also helps, by taking about 9 times the weight, which is more CPU, not lower latency. SCHED_BATCH does the opposite: batch threads never preempt on wake-up (section 6 measures 2 ms of delay).

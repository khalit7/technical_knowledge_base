3. Inside glibc's malloc: chunks, bins, the heap's top, and two thresholds

ch. 17 (real allocators), beyond OSTEP

glibc's allocator (called ptmalloc, descended from Doug Lea's dlmalloc) is what malloc means on Debian, Ubuntu and most Linux machines a training job runs on. Its source is one file, malloc/malloc.c, plus arena.c; line numbers below are {{glibc 2.36|https://sourceware.org/git/?p=glibc.git;a=blob;f=malloc/malloc.c;hb=refs/tags/glibc-2.36}}, the version in the lab image, and glibc's own {{MallocInternals|https://sourceware.org/glibc/wiki/MallocInternals}} page is the best tour.

A chunk. struct malloc_chunk (line 1148) starts with the previous chunk's size (used only while that one is free) and this chunk's size. Sizes are multiples of 16, so the three low bits of the size are free for flags: P (PREV_INUSE, line 1366: the chunk before me is in use, so do not merge with it), M (IS_MMAPPED, line 1373: I came from my own mmap) and A (NON_MAIN_ARENA, line 1382: I belong to a thread arena, section 4). A free chunk reuses its first user bytes for the list links fd and bk (large free chunks also fd_nextsize, bk_nextsize).

Freeing merges a chunk with free neighbours (except into tcache and fastbins, which keep chunks whole for speed) and, if the merged chunk touches the top, grows the top. That cost shows in a measurement: a malloc plus free of the same small size, repeated, is a tcache push and pop, 11.1 ns here; a thousand mallocs followed by a thousand frees overflow the 7-entry tcache and go through the bins, still cheap for 256 bytes (28.9 ns a pair) but 899.1 ns for 4 KiB. That jump is the second threshold below.

Where the bytes come from: brk, mmap, and the dynamic threshold

glibc gets memory from the kernel two ways. The main heap sits after the program's data and grows by moving its end, the "program break", with brk ({{brk(2)|https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/brk.2.html}}): cheap, but only the top can ever be given back. A request at or above M_MMAP_THRESHOLD gets its own anonymous mmap instead, given back with munmap the moment it is freed. The threshold starts at 128 KiB (DEFAULT_MMAP_THRESHOLD_MIN, line 961) and is dynamic: when you free an mmapped chunk larger than the current threshold, glibc raises the threshold to that chunk's size and the trim threshold to twice that (lines 3366 to 3371), up to 32 MiB on 64-bit (4 * 1024 * 1024 * sizeof(long), line 972). The reasoning, from {{mallopt(3)|https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/mallopt.3.html}}: a program that frees a big block probably allocates blocks that size again, and serving them from the heap avoids an mmap, a munmap and fresh page faults each time.

Predict: malloc 1 MiB, touch it, free it, then malloc 1 MiB again and free it. Which system calls does the second malloc make?
The first free raised the threshold to about 1 MiB, so the second request is below it and comes from the heap (one brk), and freeing it gives nothing back: the trim threshold is now about 2 MiB. Step through both traces below, the default and with the threshold pinned.

The trim threshold, and the 899 ns. When the top chunk grows past M_TRIM_THRESHOLD (128 KiB to start, line 919), free shrinks the heap with brk (systrim, line 2971). In the batch benchmark, a thousand 4 KiB blocks freed together merge into a 4 MiB top, glibc trims it, and the next round grows the heap again and takes a page fault on every new page: 899.1 ns a pair. With MALLOC_TRIM_THRESHOLD_=67108864 the heap is kept and the same loop costs 28.4 ns. The price moves elsewhere: the setting also pins the mmap threshold, so 1 MiB blocks now take an mmap and munmap every time (4,723.3 ns a pair, against 2,142.4 by default). Every knob here trades memory held against system calls and page faults.

The knobs and the views. Each tunable can be set three ways: mallopt() in C; an environment variable with a trailing underscore (MALLOC_ARENA_MAX, MALLOC_MMAP_THRESHOLD_, MALLOC_TRIM_THRESHOLD_, MALLOC_TOP_PAD_); or GLIBC_TUNABLES=glibc.malloc.trim_threshold=...:glibc.malloc.arena_max=2 ({{glibc manual, memory allocation tunables|https://www.gnu.org/software/libc/manual/html_node/Memory-Allocation-Tunables.html}}). To look inside a running process: malloc_stats() prints per-arena totals, malloc_info(0, stdout) prints XML with every bin, mallinfo2() returns the totals as a struct ({{mallinfo(3)|https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/mallinfo.3.html}}). From Python, ctypes.CDLL("libc.so.6").malloc_stats() works in any process; gdb -p <pid> -batch -ex 'call (void)malloc_stats()' works in one you did not write.

malloc_trim. malloc_trim(0) ({{malloc_trim(3)|https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/malloc_trim.3.html}}) does more than its name says: it consolidates every arena and then calls madvise(MADV_DONTNEED) on every whole free page inside free chunks anywhere in the heap, not just at the top (mtrim, lines 5035 to 5089). The kernel drops those pages from RSS while the addresses stay valid. Section 5 measures what that recovers, and what it cannot.

4. Arenas: one heap per group of threads

ch. 17, ch. 28 Locks (why a shared heap needs one)

The problem. One heap shared by every thread needs a lock around every malloc and free, and a training process has many threads (PyTorch's intra-op pool, the DataLoader's pin-memory thread, the main thread). glibc's answer is several arenas, each a separate heap with its own lock and bins. A thread sticks to its last arena, moves to a free one when that lock is taken, and creates a new one while there are fewer than the limit (arena_get2, arena.c line 924). The limit is 8 per CPU on 64-bit (NARENAS_FROM_NCORES, malloc.c line 1935): 40 on this 5-CPU VM, 1,024 on a 128-core training host, unless MALLOC_ARENA_MAX says otherwise. Every thread arena reserves a 64 MiB heap (HEAP_MAX_SIZE, arena.c line 31) and commits pages as it grows: the root page's recording of the running job's /proc/PID/maps (its src/read/code/out/maps) shows exactly these, 132 KiB used and 65,404 KiB reserved, appearing three times when the DataLoader started.

The cost is steps 4 and 5 of Fig. 4: freed memory stays in the arena it came from. That is the documented reason behind the common MALLOC_ARENA_MAX=2 advice for multi-threaded servers.

Fig. 4, step by step: eight producers, one consumer, one heap or ten

  1. One heap, one lock. With MALLOC_ARENA_MAX=1 all nine threads of the benchmark (eight producers, one consumer) share the main arena: every malloc and free waits for the same lock.
  2. An arena per thread. By default a thread that finds the lock taken gets an arena of its own, up to 8 per CPU: glibc used 10 here (main, eight producers, the consumer). Each thread arena reserves a 64 MiB heap and commits pages as it grows.
  3. Producers hand buffers to the consumer. Each producer allocates in its own arena, buffers of 64 B to 64 KiB, and passes them on, at most 4,096 in flight: the shape of a data pipeline.
  4. The consumer frees each one into the arena it came from. A chunk freed by another thread goes back to the arena it came from, not the freeing thread's (the A flag of section 3 marks a thread arena's chunk).
  5. Free space is stranded per arena. Memory free in one arena cannot serve a request in another, so with many threads each holding some free memory, the total held grows with the number of arenas: peak RSS 220.8 MiB with ten arenas against 136.4 MiB with one, which took 9.37 s instead of 5.88.

How full each arena is in Fig. 4 is illustrative; the thread and arena counts and the numbers in the steps are measured (the chart beside the next paragraph).

One arena held the least at peak but was the slowest (step 5): the lock became the bottleneck arenas remove. When the work ended glibc held 2.3 MiB, while jemalloc, tcmalloc and mimalloc still held about 125 to 235 MiB, because they return memory on a timer or a rate (section 6). This workload frees everything, so it cannot show arena bloat (long-lived objects mixed with short-lived ones in a long-running server); the lesson is the method: measure peak RSS, steady RSS and time for your own pattern before setting a knob.

5. Fragmentation, measured

ch. 17

Test 1: free 98% of a heap. 400,000 blocks of 256 bytes (272-byte chunks, 100,000 KiB of data) are allocated and touched; then all but every 64th are freed, leaving 1,562 KiB live, spread evenly over the whole heap.

Fig. 5, step by step: four gaps of glibc's heap, 17 pages, to scale

  1. Allocate and touch 400,000 blocks. 272-byte chunks packed end to end across 4 KiB pages; RSS 110,524 KiB. Fig. 5 shows a window of four gaps (17 pages) of a heap of about 27,600 (110,524 KiB / 4 KiB).
  2. Free all but every 64th. Each freed chunk merges with its free neighbours (except the 7 the tcache keeps), so every gap becomes one free chunk of 63 × 272 = 17,136 bytes, with a survivor every 17,408 bytes. 1,562 KiB live; RSS does not move: the freed pages stay mapped and resident.
  3. malloc_trim(0) drops the whole free pages. madvise(MADV_DONTNEED) on every page that lies entirely inside a free chunk; the partly used page at each end of a gap, holding a survivor, stays. RSS falls to 29,404 KiB.
  4. Free the rest. Every chunk merges into the top, which glibc trims at once: 4,436 KiB. The other three allocators keep about 105 to 110 MiB until they are told to purge (the table beside the next paragraph).

Page and chunk positions in Fig. 5 follow from the sizes (272-byte chunks laid end to end from the heap's start); the RSS figures are measured.

(1) Every allocator keeps about 105 to 110 MiB resident for 1.6 MB of live data. (2) malloc_trim(0) returns glibc's whole free pages, down to 29,404 KiB, and no further: the 6,250 survivors sit one every 17,408 bytes (64 × 272), so every gap keeps a partly used page at each end, about 24 MiB of pinned pages (29,404 minus the 4,436 KiB held once everything is freed). jemalloc keeps all 107 MiB even with purging on, because each of its 256-byte slabs still holds one survivor: purging returns only pages with nothing live on them. (3) With everything freed, glibc is back near its start at once (4,436 KiB: the top was trimmed); the others keep about 105 to 110 MiB by default and give it back only when told to: jemalloc with its background purging thread (9,624 KiB 3 s later), mimalloc with MIMALLOC_PAGE_RESET=1 (4,828 KiB); this tcmalloc kept everything even with TCMALLOC_RELEASE_RATE=10 in the 3 s window.

Predict: a process allocated 2 GB of small objects, then freed all but 1% of them, scattered. RSS stays near 2 GB. Will malloc_trim(0) bring it near 20 MB?
It returns every whole free page, anywhere in the heap (glibc's mtrim calls MADV_DONTNEED inside free chunks, not only at the top), but a page holding even one live chunk stays. With 1% of objects alive and spread out, many pages still hold one: here 1.5% live left RSS at 27% of its peak after the trim. The real fix is to not interleave long-lived and short-lived objects in the same memory (separate arenas or pools, or allocate the long-lived ones first), or to restart worker processes periodically.

Test 2: long-run growth in a data loader. Four threads "decode" 120,000 samples into NumPy arrays of 64 KiB to 8 MiB (log-uniform, fixed seed); a prefetch window keeps the last 8 arrays; and for every sample the loader also keeps a 600-byte record forever (metadata, a cache entry, a log line), so live memory grows by a known 600 bytes a sample, 68.7 MiB in total. The question is how much RSS each allocator holds on top of that.

glibc's default ends at about 211.1 MiB against 68.7 MiB live: the dynamic threshold soon moves the arrays onto the heap (section 3), and the small records made right after each array land between them and pin it. Pinning the threshold (MALLOC_MMAP_THRESHOLD_=131072) keeps every array in its own mmap and holds the least, 106.5 MiB, but every array then costs an mmap, a munmap and a page fault per page: 42.83 s against 10.43 s. jemalloc holds less than glibc here (148.4 MiB); tcmalloc and mimalloc hold more but finish fastest.

The idea in one screen

One training step of one model uses almost all of the mathematics of machine learning, each area for one move: linear algebra scores the input, probability turns the scores into a bet and prices it, information theory says what the price means, calculus finds the downhill direction, optimisation decides how far to step, and statistics asks, from outside the loop, whether the model really got better. Floating point sits under every move, and variational inference is where the areas meet.

The running example every child page uses

A language model with a three-word vocabulary, cat, dog and sat, has read "the cat"; the right next word is sat. The context is the vector x=(2,1)x = (2, 1) and the model is one weight matrix WW with one row per word: cat (1,0)(1, 0), dog (0,1)(0, 1), sat (1,−2)(1, -2). It scores the words z=Wx=(2,1,0)z = Wx = (2, 1, 0), turns the scores into the bet p=(0.665,0.245,0.090)p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090), and so bets on cat and gives the truth only 9%. A real language model does the same with a vocabulary of about 100,000 tokens and thousands of numbers per word. Every child page works its examples on this model (or on the two-layer version in the Gradient lab), so a number met on one page can be checked on another.

You need: nothing; this is the entry page, and each child page says what it assumes.

New terms

Logits: raw scores, one per word, before softmax turns them into probabilities.
Loss (negative log-likelihood): −ln⁡py-\ln p_y, minus the log of the probability given to what happened.
Nat and bit: units of the loss with ln⁡\ln and log2⁡\log_2; 1 nat = 1.443 bits. Training code prints nats.
Gradient: one slope of the loss per number in the model, the same shape as what it is the gradient of.
Curvature: how fast the slope changes; with many weights, the Hessian matrix.
Standard error: the uncertainty of an average, s/ns/\sqrt{n}.

3. The same object in different hats

Two objects turn up on several child pages under different names. Seeing that each is one thing is the payoff of learning the areas together, and no single child can show it.

Cross-entropy wears four hats

Same scores z=(2,1,0)z = (2, 1, 0) and bet p=(0.665,0.245,0.090)p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090), but suppose the right word had been cat, the model's favourite. One number, 0.408 nats, read four ways:

Probability: a likelihood

−ln⁡0.665=0.408-\ln 0.665 = 0.408
The negative log-likelihood of cat under a categorical model; minimising its average is maximum likelihood. Probability, section 7

Information: a code length

0.588 bits
The same number divided by ln⁡2\ln 2: cat's codeword length in a code built from the bet. Averaged over real data it is entropy plus KL. Information theory, section 3

Calculus: prediction minus target

(−0.335,0.245,0.090)(-0.335, 0.245, 0.090)
Its gradient on the scores, p−yp - y, adding up to 0. Calculus and optimisation, section 2

Contrastive learning: InfoNCE

ln⁡3−0.408=0.691\ln 3 - 0.408 = 0.691
Read cat as the positive and dog and sat as two negatives with similarities 2, 1 and 0: 0.408 is the InfoNCE loss with N=3N = 3, and it certifies at least 0.691 nats of mutual information by I≥ln⁡N−LI \ge \ln N - L (van den Oord et al. 2018). Information theory, section 9

With the real answer, sat, the loss is 2.408 and the bound ln⁡3−2.408\ln 3 - 2.408 is negative: it certifies nothing. The bound can never exceed ln⁡N\ln N, which is why contrastive models such as CLIP train with huge batches (32,768 pairs; Radford et al. 2021).

Curvature is read three ways

For a yes-or-no prediction with score zz and probability p=σ(z)p = \sigma(z), three quantities from three areas are the same number, p(1−p)p(1 - p): 0.25 at z=0z = 0 (its largest value) and 0.09 at z=ln⁡9=2.197z = \ln 9 = 2.197 (p=0.9p = 0.9). Drag zz in the Gradient lab, panel 4, which computes each one by its own route.

Optimisation: the Hessian

The second derivative of binary cross-entropy in zz is p(1−p)p(1 - p), whatever the label yy. Calculus and optimisation, section 3

Statistics: a variance

A Bernoulli label that is 1 with probability pp has variance p(1−p)p(1 - p). Probability, section 3

Probability: the Fisher information

The score (the slope of the log-likelihood in zz) is y−py - p; its variance, the Fisher information, is p(1−p)p(1 - p). Statistics, section 2

A confident prediction therefore sits in a flat part of the loss. On the tiny model the many-word version holds: the Hessian of the loss in the scores, diag⁡(p)−pp⊤\operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top, is exactly the covariance matrix of the one-hot draw, with per-word variances pi(1−pi)=(0.223,0.185,0.082)p_i(1 - p_i) = (0.223, 0.185, 0.082) (a simulation of 200,000 draws gives 0.222, 0.185, 0.081), and its largest eigenvalue, times ‖x‖2=5\lVert x \rVert^2 = 5, is section 2's λmax⁡=1.855\lambda_{\max} = 1.855.

Why this matters for natural gradients, and a correction to the old page

For the matched pairs (sigmoid with binary cross-entropy, softmax with cross-entropy, identity with squared error) the curvature in the scores does not depend on the label, so it equals its own average over labels, the Fisher. That is why natural-gradient methods and K-FAC precondition with the Fisher, and why the curvature of TRPO's KL constraint is the Fisher matrix. A correction to the old page: the equality is exact with respect to the scores only. With respect to the weights of a deep network the Hessian gains an extra term (the residual p−yp - y times the curvature of the network itself), so there the Fisher equals the Hessian only approximately; it is exactly the "generalised Gauss-Newton" part of it (Martens 2020, New insights and perspectives on the natural gradient method).

5. How to read an equation in a paper

An equation in a paper is a compressed paragraph, and every child page's notation meets in it. Expanding it takes five steps, always in the same order. Here they are on scaled dot-product attention, equation 1 of Attention Is All You Need (Vaswani et al. 2017):

Attention⁡(Q,K,V)=softmax⁡(QK⊤dk)V\operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
  1. Name every symbol. QQ holds the queries (one row per position, "what should I look at?"), KK the keys ("what I contain"), VV the values ("what I hand over"), dkd_k the length of each query and key row, ⊤\top the transpose, softmax applied to each row separately. If a symbol is not defined near the equation, search the paper for its first use; the Notation decoder lists the conventions papers assume without saying.
  2. Write the shape beside every symbol. With nn queries and mm keys: QQ is n×dkn \times d_k, KK is m×dkm \times d_k, so QK⊤QK^\top is n×mn \times m, one score for every query-key pair; VV is m×dvm \times d_v, so the result is n×dvn \times d_v, one output row per query. If the shapes do not fit, you have misread a symbol.
  3. Read the operations from the inside out, in words. Dot each query with each key (how well they match); divide by dk\sqrt{d_k}; turn each query's row of scores into weights that add up to 1; return, for each query, the weighted average of the value rows.
  4. Plug in tiny numbers. One query q=x=(2,1)q = x = (2, 1), the three keys and values are the rows of the tiny model's WW, dk=2d_k = 2. The raw scores are (2,1,0)(2, 1, 0); divided by 2\sqrt{2} they are (1.414,0.707,0)(1.414, 0.707, 0), and softmax turns them into weights (0.576,0.284,0.140)(0.576, 0.284, 0.140). The output is 0.576(1,0)+0.284(0,1)0.576\,(1, 0) + 0.284\,(0, 1) +0.140(1,−2)+\, 0.140\,(1, -2) =(0.716,0.004)= (0.716, 0.004): mostly cat's row. Without the division the weights would be (0.665,0.245,0.090)(0.665, 0.245, 0.090), sharper.
  5. Ask why each piece is there. Why dk\sqrt{d_k}? The paper's footnote: if a query's and a key's entries are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, their dot product has variance dkd_k. With dk=64d_k = 64 raw scores would typically be about 8 apart, softmax would put nearly all the weight on one key, and its gradients would vanish; dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k} brings the variance back to 1. A term you cannot justify is usually the thing the paper is really about.
The habit
Name, shape, read, plug, ask. Step 4 is also how you test your own derivations: if tiny numbers do not come out right, the algebra is wrong. Glossary: every term this page uses, each linked to the page that owns it (reference)

Terms

Vocabulary: the set of tokens a language model can output (3 words here, about 100,000 in real models).
Vector: an ordered list of numbers; its dimension is how many.
Matrix: a table of numbers; its shape is rows by columns.
Dot product: multiply two vectors entry by entry and add; large when they point the same way.
Matrix-vector product: one dot product per row of the matrix.
Weight matrix: the numbers a layer learns.
Logits: raw scores before softmax.
Length (norm): ‖x‖=∑ixi2\lVert x \rVert = \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}.
Cosine similarity: the dot product divided by both lengths; 1 aligned, 0 at right angles.
Transpose: rows and columns swapped, W⊤W^\top.
Batch: several inputs processed together as one matrix.
Outer product: a column times a row, giving a rank-1 matrix.
Rank: how many independent directions a matrix really uses.
Singular value decomposition (SVD): any matrix as a sum of rank-1 layers, largest first.
LoRA: fine-tuning by learning only a low-rank change BABA.
Probability distribution: non-negative numbers, one per outcome, adding up to 1.
Categorical distribution: one draw among several outcomes.
Softmax: exponentiate the scores, then divide by their total.
Exponential: eze^z, always positive; e≈2.718e \approx 2.718.
Temperature: divide the scores by TT before softmax; low sharpens, high flattens.
Likelihood: the probability the model gave to what actually happened.
Independent (i.i.d.): knowing one tells nothing about another; identically distributed means drawn from the same distribution.
Negative log-likelihood (NLL): −ln⁡py-\ln p_y, the loss.
Maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE): choose the weights that make the data most probable.
MAP estimation: MLE plus a prior; "loss plus regulariser".
Proper scoring rule: a loss whose expected value is lowest at the true probabilities.
Bernoulli: a yes-or-no outcome; its NLL is binary cross-entropy.
Gaussian: the bell curve; its NLL is squared error.
Surprise: −log⁡p-\log p of an outcome.
Bit and nat: units of surprise with log2⁡\log_2 and ln⁡\ln; 1 nat = 1.443 bits.
Entropy: average surprise; the shortest average code length.
Cross-entropy: average surprise when the data comes from qq and you bet pp; the training loss.
KL divergence: cross-entropy minus entropy; the removable part of the loss; not symmetric.
One-hot: a vector with 1 for the right answer and 0 elsewhere.
Perplexity: elosse^{\text{loss}}; an effective number of choices; tokenizer-dependent.
Bits per byte: total surprise in bits divided by text length in bytes; tokenizer-free.
Derivative: a slope, change in output per tiny change in input.
Partial derivative: the slope along one input with the others fixed.
Gradient: all partial derivatives together; points uphill.
Chain rule: the slope through a chain of steps is the product of their slopes.
Backpropagation: the chain rule run from the loss backwards through every layer.
Jacobian: the table of every output's slope with respect to every input.
Vector-Jacobian product: what each layer computes in backprop, without building its Jacobian.
Gradient descent: W←W−η∇WLW \leftarrow W - \eta \nabla_W L.
Learning rate: the step size η\eta.
Curvature or second derivative: how fast the slope changes.
Hessian: the matrix of second derivatives.
Eigenvalues: here, the curvatures along the Hessian's principal directions.
Condition number: largest over smallest curvature; how lopsided the bowl is.
Newton's method: divide the gradient by the curvature, −H−1g-H^{-1}g.
Adam: a per-weight step size from a moving average of squared gradients.
Convex: bowl-shaped everywhere; any minimum is the global one.
Mean, standard deviation, variance: the average, the typical distance from it, and that distance squared.
Standard error: the uncertainty of a mean, s/ns/\sqrt{n}.
Central limit theorem: averages of many independent measurements are approximately normal.
Paired comparison: compare two models example by example so shared difficulty cancels.
Minibatch gradient: the average gradient over BB examples; noise shrinks like 1/B1/\sqrt{B}.
InfoNCE: cross-entropy over one positive and N−1N - 1 negatives; contrastive learning's loss.
Mutual information: how much knowing one variable tells you about another.
Score (statistics): the slope of the log-likelihood.
Fisher information: the variance of the score; the expected curvature of the NLL.

Read it aloud

Attention of Q, K, V equals: take every query dotted with every key, divide by the square root of the key length, turn each row into weights with a softmax, and use those weights to average the rows of V.

Traps

  • The softmax runs along each row (over keys), never over the whole matrix and never down a column. In code: softmax(scores, dim=-1).
  • Rows are tokens. ML papers usually stack examples or positions as rows (n×dn \times d), so a layer is XWXW, not WxWx. Check which way round a paper writes it before multiplying.
  • dk\sqrt{d_k} is the square root of the key length, not of the sequence length. Without it, scores grow with dkd_k and the softmax saturates (Vaswani et al. footnote 4).
  • The decoder's mask is not in Eq. (1): §3.2.3 says it is applied by setting illegal positions to −∞-\infty before the softmax.

Conventions that trip people up

Habits that papers rarely state. Each comes with a tiny example; "Seen in" opens the equation above where it appears. When a paper defines its notation (often in a short section or a footnote), its definition wins over any of these.

Vectors are columns

𝐱∈ℝ3=[123]𝐱⊤=[123]𝐰⊤𝐱=∑iwixi\begin{gathered}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}\\ \mathbf{x}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\end{bmatrix}\\ \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\textstyle\sum_i w_i x_i\end{gathered}

A bold lower-case letter is a column vector unless the paper says otherwise, so 𝐰⊤𝐱\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} is a single number (a dot product) and 𝐱𝐰⊤\mathbf{x}\mathbf{w}^\top is a matrix (an outer product).

Data matrices: one row per example

X∈ℝn×dn rows (examples or tokens)d columns (features)XW∈ℝn×d′\begin{gathered}X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\\ n\ \text{rows (examples or tokens)}\\ d\ \text{columns (features)}\\ XW\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d'}\end{gathered}

Stacked data puts examples in rows, so a layer is applied as XWXW with WW of shape d×d′d\times d'. The same layer on one column vector is W⊤𝐱W^\top\mathbf{x} or, with WW stored the other way, W𝐱W\mathbf{x}. Check which a paper uses before multiplying.

Shapes multiply by the inner sizes

(a×b)(b×c)=(a×c)A∈ℝa×b\begin{gathered}(a\times b)(b\times c)=(a\times c)\\ A\in\mathbb{R}^{a\times b}\end{gathered}

ℝa×b\mathbb{R}^{a\times b} means aa rows and bb columns of real numbers. A product needs the inner sizes to match and keeps the outer ones. Writing the shape under every symbol catches most misreadings.

Transpose and Hermitian marks

AT=A⊤=A′AH=A∗=A†=A⊤\begin{gathered}A^{T}=A^{\top}=A'\\ A^{H}=A^{*}=A^{\dagger}=\overline{A}^{\top}\end{gathered}

TT, ⊤\top and a prime all mean transpose. HH, ∗* and †\dagger mean conjugate transpose, which matters only for complex numbers (Fourier features, the complex form of RoPE); for real matrices it equals the transpose. Careful: ∗* can also mean \"optimal\" (θ∗\theta^*) or convolution.

Numerator versus denominator layout

L=𝐮⊤W𝐱,  𝐮=[12], 𝐱=[345], W∈ℝ2×3:∂L∂W=𝐮𝐱⊤=[3456810]⏟denominator layout: 2×3, what autograd returns𝐱𝐮⊤=[3648510]⏟numerator layout: 3×2\begin{gathered}L=\mathbf{u}^{\top}W\mathbf{x},\ \ \mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\\5\end{bmatrix},\ W\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times3}:\\ \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}3&4&5\\6&8&10\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{denominator layout: }2\times3,\ \text{what autograd returns}}\\ \underbrace{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}3&6\\4&8\\5&10\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{numerator layout: }3\times2}\end{gathered}

Each entry is the same partial derivative, ∂L/∂Wij=uixj\partial L/\partial W_{ij}=u_i x_j (nudge W12W_{12} by 0.01 and LL moves by 1⋅4⋅0.011\cdot4\cdot0.01); the layouts only disagree on how to arrange them. Words used throughout: a gradient is the list of slopes of the loss, one per input number: how much the loss changes per unit nudge of that number. An analytic gradient comes from a formula derived by hand; a numerical gradient is measured by nudging each number a tiny step hh up and down and timing how the loss moves. If the two disagree, the formula (or the code) is wrong. The derivative, the gradient and the finite-difference check are taught on Calculus and optimisation, section 1 and Matrix calculus and backprop, section 11.

1. Loss explorer: three losses, one gradient

Taught on: Calculus and optimisation, section 2 (every loss's gradient, prediction minus target) and Probability, section 7 (each loss as a likelihood). This panel is practice.

Pick a loss, move the model's raw output with the sliders, and compare the gradient from the formula with the gradient measured by nudging. The striking fact to find: for the three standard pairings (a plain number with squared error, a sigmoid with binary cross-entropy, a softmax with cross-entropy) the gradient with respect to the raw output is the same expression, prediction minus target. The two extra losses show what happens when you change the recipe.

Mean squared error with an identity output. Use it when the target is a real number (a price, a temperature). The model's raw output zz is used directly as the prediction, yˆ=z\hat y = z (the "identity" output: nothing is applied). The loss is half the squared miss:

L=12(yˆ−y)2,yˆ=zL = \tfrac{1}{2}(\hat y - y)^2, \qquad \hat y = z

In words: square how far the prediction is from the target yy, then halve it (the half is a convention that makes the derivative tidy).

  1. Chain rule: the loss depends on zz through yˆ\hat y, so multiply the two slopes:
    ∂L∂z=∂L∂yˆ⋅∂yˆ∂z\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y}\cdot\frac{\partial \hat y}{\partial z}
  2. Slope of 12u2\tfrac12 u^2 is uu, so ∂L∂yˆ=yˆ−y\frac{\partial L}{\partial \hat y} = \hat y - y; and yˆ=z\hat y = z has slope 1.
  3. Multiply:
    ∂L∂z=(yˆ−y)⋅1=yˆ−y(prediction minus target)\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = (\hat y - y)\cdot 1 = \hat y - y \quad\text{(prediction minus target)}

Without the half, L=(yˆ−y)2L = (\hat y - y)^2 and the gradient is 2(yˆ−y)2(\hat y - y). Averaged over a batch of nn examples, L=1n∑i(yˆi−yi)2L = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i (\hat y_i - y_i)^2, each example's gradient is 2n(yˆi−yi)\frac{2}{n}(\hat y_i - y_i) (the old page's "times 2/n2/n" is this convention). Worked example: z=1.5z = 1.5, y=1y = 1: L=12(0.5)2=0.125L = \tfrac12 (0.5)^2 = 0.125, gradient 0.50.5.

Binary cross-entropy with a sigmoid. Use it for a yes/no target y∈{0,1}y \in \{0, 1\} (spam or not). The raw output zz is squashed into a probability by the sigmoid, and the loss is the negative log of the probability given to what actually happened:

p=σ(z)=11+e−z,L=−[yln⁡p+(1−y)ln⁡(1−p)]p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}, \qquad L = -\big[\,y \ln p + (1 - y)\ln(1 - p)\,\big]

In words: if y=1y = 1 the loss is −ln⁡p-\ln p; if y=0y = 0 it is −ln⁡(1−p)-\ln(1 - p). Only one of the two terms is ever switched on.

  1. Slope of the sigmoid. Write σ=(1+e−z)−1\sigma = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}; the chain rule gives
    σ′(z)=e−z(1+e−z)2=σ(z)(1−σ(z))=p(1−p)\sigma'(z) = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\,\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big) = p(1 - p)
  2. Slope of the loss in pp (slope of ln⁡u\ln u is 1/u1/u):
    ∂L∂p=−yp+1−y1−p\frac{\partial L}{\partial p} = -\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p}
  3. Multiply the two and simplify; the p(1−p)p(1-p) cancels the denominators:
    ∂L∂z=(−yp+1−y1−p)p(1−p)=−y(1−p)+(1−y)p=p−y\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \Big(-\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p}\Big)\,p(1 - p) = -y(1 - p) + (1 - y)\,p = p - y

Worked example: z=2z = 2, y=1y = 1: p=0.881p = 0.881, L=−ln⁡0.881=0.127L = -\ln 0.881 = 0.127, gradient 0.881−1=−0.1190.881 - 1 = -0.119 (negative: raising zz lowers the loss).

Softmax cross-entropy. The loss of the tiny model: three logits z=(z1,z2,z3)z = (z_1, z_2, z_3), one per token, turned into probabilities by the softmax; the loss is minus the log of the probability of the correct token tt (here cat):

pi=ezi∑jezj,L=−ln⁡pt=−zt+ln⁡∑jezjp_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}, \qquad L = -\ln p_t = -z_t + \ln \sum_j e^{z_j}

In words: exponentiate every score (all become positive), divide by the total (they now sum to 1), and pay −ln⁡-\ln of the share the right answer got. The second form comes from ln⁡(a/b)=ln⁡a−ln⁡b\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b and ln⁡ezt=zt\ln e^{z_t} = z_t.

  1. Differentiate the second form with respect to one logit ziz_i. The first term −zt-z_t has slope −1-1 if i=ti = t and 0 otherwise; write that as −yi-y_i, where yy is the one-hot target (1 for the correct token, 0 elsewhere).
  2. The second term: slope of ln⁡S\ln S is 1/S1/S, times the slope of S=∑jezjS = \sum_j e^{z_j} in ziz_i, which is ezie^{z_i}:
    ∂∂ziln⁡∑jezj=ezi∑jezj=pi\frac{\partial}{\partial z_i}\ln\sum_j e^{z_j} = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} = p_i
  3. Add:
    ∂L∂zi=pi−yi(prediction minus target, per token)\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = p_i - y_i \quad\text{(prediction minus target, per token)}

Worked example at the default: ez=(7.389,2.718,1)e^{z} = (7.389, 2.718, 1), sum 11.107, p=(0.665,0.245,0.090)p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090), L=−ln⁡0.090=2.408L = -\ln 0.090 = 2.408 nats (3.473 bits), gradient (0.665, 0.245, 0.090−1)=(0.665,0.245,−0.910)(0.665,\ 0.245,\ 0.090 - 1) = (0.665, 0.245, -0.910): push cat and dog down, sat up. The entries sum to zero, because both pp and yy sum to 1. The old page's "four hats" example uses the same logits with cat correct: L=−ln⁡0.665=0.408L = -\ln 0.665 = 0.408 nats (0.588 bits), gradient (−0.335,0.245,0.090)(-0.335, 0.245, 0.090); the button below the sliders loads it.

Label-smoothed cross-entropy (Szegedy et al. 2016, section 7). Replace the one-hot target by a softened one that gives a little probability ε\varepsilon to every token: with KK tokens,

qi=(1−ε)yi+εK,L=−∑iqiln⁡pi=ln⁡∑jezj−∑iqiziq_i = (1 - \varepsilon)\,y_i + \frac{\varepsilon}{K}, \qquad L = -\sum_i q_i \ln p_i = \ln\sum_j e^{z_j} - \sum_i q_i z_i

The second form uses ln⁡pi=zi−ln⁡∑jezj\ln p_i = z_i - \ln\sum_j e^{z_j} and ∑iqi=1\sum_i q_i = 1.

  1. Same steps as plain cross-entropy: the first term gives pip_i, the second gives −qi-q_i.
  2. ∂L∂zi=pi−qi\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = p_i - q_i

Still prediction minus target, only the target is the smoothed qq. The loss can no longer reach zero: its floor is the entropy of qq. Worked example (ε=0.1\varepsilon = 0.1, K=3K = 3): q=(0.033,0.033,0.933)q = (0.033, 0.033, 0.933), L=2.308L = 2.308, gradient (0.632,0.211,−0.843)(0.632, 0.211, -0.843).

Focal loss (Lin et al. 2017, written there for two classes; here the usual softmax form). It multiplies cross-entropy by (1−pt)γ(1 - p_t)^{\gamma} so that examples the model already gets right count less:

L=−(1−pt)γln⁡ptL = -(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}\,\ln p_t
  1. Slope in ptp_t (product rule on (1−pt)γ(1-p_t)^\gamma times ln⁡pt\ln p_t):
    ∂L∂pt=γ(1−pt)γ−1ln⁡pt−(1−pt)γpt\frac{\partial L}{\partial p_t} = \gamma(1 - p_t)^{\gamma - 1}\ln p_t - \frac{(1 - p_t)^{\gamma}}{p_t}
  2. Slope of the softmax output ptp_t in a logit ziz_i: ∂pt∂zi=pt(yi−pi)\frac{\partial p_t}{\partial z_i} = p_t\,(y_i - p_i).
  3. Multiply and collect:
    ∂L∂zi=w(pi−yi),w=(1−pt)γ−γpt(1−pt)γ−1ln⁡pt\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i} = w\,(p_i - y_i), \qquad w = (1 - p_t)^{\gamma} - \gamma\,p_t\,(1 - p_t)^{\gamma - 1}\ln p_t

The direction is still p−yp - y, but scaled by a weight ww that shrinks as the model gets confident (w=1w = 1 at γ=0\gamma = 0, which is plain cross-entropy). It is not one of the canonical pairs, so the tidy cancellation is gone. Worked example (γ=2\gamma = 2) at the default, where the correct token gets only pt=0.090p_t = 0.090: w=1.223w = 1.223, L=1.994L = 1.994, gradient (0.813,0.299,−1.112)(0.813, 0.299, -1.112). Why three different losses end at the same gradient

Each standard pair is a probability distribution with its "canonical" output function: a Gaussian with the identity, a Bernoulli with the sigmoid, a categorical with the softmax. For every such pair the slope of the output function cancels the slope of the log-likelihood exactly, leaving prediction minus target (Stanford CS229's generalised linear models chapter, main notes). Practical reading: a softmax followed by cross-entropy should never be coded as two separate steps, since the combined gradient p−yp - y is both simpler and safer (see panels 5 and 6).

2. Backprop, step by step: the chain rule run backwards

Taught on: Matrix calculus and backprop, section 5 derives the backward pass of this same two-layer network by hand, with every gradient checked against PyTorch autograd; its section 8 explains the factor of two in cost. This panel is practice.

Two versions of the model, both giving the same logits (2, 1, 0). The Reading's model is one matrix: z=Wxz = Wx, then softmax and the loss, with WW rows cat (1, 0), dog (0, 1), sat (1, −2-2). Its backward pass is z‾=p−y\bar z = p - y, W‾=z‾x⊤\bar W = \bar z\,x^{\top}, x‾=W⊤z‾\bar x = W^{\top}\bar z, and one step at learning rate 0.1 takes the loss from 2.408 to 1.773, as in the Reading. The two-layer network below adds a hidden layer with a relu, which shows what a nonlinearity does to the backward pass. Its chain has five simple steps. The forward pass computes each value from the one before. The backward pass then walks the chain in reverse, and at each step asks one question: "if this value moved a little, how much would the loss move?" That number is the value's gradient. Each backward step only needs the gradient of the step after it and the local slope of its own operation; this product is called a vector-Jacobian product (VJP), the Jacobian being the table of local slopes, which is never built in full.

a=W1x,h=relu⁡(a),z=W2h,p=softmax⁡(z),L=−ln⁡psat\begin{aligned} a &= W_1 x, & h &= \operatorname{relu}(a), & z &= W_2 h, \\ p &= \operatorname{softmax}(z), & L &= -\ln p_{\text{sat}} \end{aligned}

xx: the embedding of "the cat" (2 numbers). W1W_1: first weight matrix (3 rows, 2 columns), so aa has 3 numbers. relu⁡(a)=max⁡(0,a)\operatorname{relu}(a) = \max(0, a) element by element: negative values become 0. W2W_2: second weight matrix (3 by 3). Gradients are written v‾=∂L/∂v\bar v = \partial L / \partial v ("v-bar"), with the same shape as vv.

z‾=p−yW‾2=z‾h⊤,h‾=W2⊤z‾a‾=h‾⊙[a>0]W‾1=a‾x⊤,x‾=W1⊤a‾\begin{aligned} \bar z &= p - y \\ \bar W_2 &= \bar z\, h^{\top}, & \bar h &= W_2^{\top}\bar z \\ \bar a &= \bar h \odot [a > 0] \\ \bar W_1 &= \bar a\, x^{\top}, & \bar x &= W_1^{\top}\bar a \end{aligned}

⊤\top is the transpose (rows become columns), ⊙\odot multiplies element by element, [a>0][a > 0] is 1 where aa is positive and 0 elsewhere. A matrix's gradient is always (gradient coming in) times (input that went in), transposed into the matrix's shape; an input's gradient is the matrix, transposed, times the gradient coming in. Each matrix product in the forward pass therefore becomes two in the backward pass.


Multiply-add count: a matrix with rr rows and cc columns costs r×cr \times c multiply-adds to apply. The Reading's model: forward WxWx is 6; backward W‾\bar W 6 and x‾\bar x 6, total 12, twice forward (6 if the embedding is held fixed). Two layers: forward W1xW_1 x is 6, W2hW_2 h is 9, total 15. Backward: W‾2\bar W_2 9, h‾\bar h 9, W‾1\bar W_1 6, x‾\bar x 6, total 30, exactly twice; 24 if the input is not trained (the first layer then needs no input gradient). Softmax, relu and the loss are a handful of extra operations, not counted. This factor of two is why training compute is usually counted as 6N6N per token for a model with NN parameters: 2N2N forward and 4N4N backward (Kaplan et al. 2020, section 2.1). The backward pass also needs the forward values (xx, aa, hh, pp) kept in memory, which is why activations, not arithmetic, usually limit training memory.

3. Curvature and step size: why the learning rate has a ceiling

Taught on: Calculus and optimisation, sections 4 and 5 (the 2/λmax⁡2/\lambda_{\max} limit, the rate, momentum, Newton), with eight methods on four landscapes in its Optimisation lab. This panel is practice.

Close to a minimum, almost any loss looks like a bowl. With two parameters θ=(θ1,θ2)\theta = (\theta_1, \theta_2) the bowl is

L(θ)=12θ⊤Hθ,∇L=Hθ,gradient descent: θ←θ−ηHθL(\theta) = \tfrac12\,\theta^{\top} H\,\theta, \qquad \nabla L = H\theta, \qquad \text{gradient descent: } \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,H\theta

HH is the Hessian, the 2 by 2 table of second derivatives (how fast each slope changes): it measures curvature. Its eigenvalues λmax⁡\lambda_{\max} and λmin⁡\lambda_{\min} are the curvatures along the bowl's steepest and flattest directions (the axes of the ellipses below), and κ=λmax⁡/λmin⁡\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} is the condition number. η\eta is the learning rate. Along a direction of curvature λ\lambda, one step multiplies the distance to the minimum by 1−ηλ1 - \eta\lambda. So that direction shrinks smoothly if ηλ<1\eta\lambda < 1, overshoots and flips side each step if 1<ηλ<21 < \eta\lambda < 2, and grows (diverges) if ηλ>2\eta\lambda > 2: the learning rate must stay below 2/λmax⁡2/\lambda_{\max}. Meanwhile the flat direction shrinks only by 1−ηλmin⁡1 - \eta\lambda_{\min} per step, which is why a large κ\kappa means slow progress (Goh 2017, Why Momentum Really Works).

The same limit on the tiny model: at the Reading's starting point the steepest curvature of the loss along the weights WW is λmax⁡=1.855\lambda_{\max} = 1.855 (the largest eigenvalue of diag⁡(p)−pp⊤\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^{\top}, times ‖x‖2=5\lVert x\rVert^2 = 5), so the local limit is about 2/1.855=1.082/1.855 = 1.08. The learning rates 0.1, 0.3 and 1 in panel 2 all sit below it (one step each: loss 1.773, 0.778, 0.011). It is a local limit: the curvature changes as the weights move.

Momentum keeps a running velocity, v←βv−η∇Lv \leftarrow \beta v - \eta\nabla L, θ←θ+v\theta \leftarrow \theta + v (β=0.9\beta = 0.9 here): steps that agree add up along the flat direction, steps that flip sign cancel across the steep one. On a bowl it is stable up to ηλ<2(1+β)=3.8\eta\lambda < 2(1 + \beta) = 3.8 (Goh 2017). Adam (Kingma and Ba 2014, defaults β1=0.9\beta_1 = 0.9, β2=0.999\beta_2 = 0.999) divides each coordinate's averaged gradient by the root of its averaged square, so every coordinate moves by about η\eta per step whatever its curvature. Intuition only: that evens out a valley lined up with the axes, helps less when the valley is diagonal (try the rotation slider), and is a rough per-coordinate scale, not a curvature estimate. Newton's method uses the curvature itself: step −H−1∇L-H^{-1}\nabla L, which on an exact bowl lands on the minimum in one step whatever κ\kappa is (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.5); for a network with NN parameters, HH has N2N^2 entries, which is why it is not used directly.

4. Bernoulli curvature: one number, three meanings

Taught on: the Reading tab's section 3 (the same object in different hats), Calculus and optimisation, section 3 (the Hessian) and Statistics, section 2 (curvature as the error bar). This panel is practice.

Take the binary cross-entropy of panel 1 and ask three different-sounding questions about it. The answer to all three is p(1−p)p(1 - p), where p=σ(z)p = \sigma(z). The page computes each one by its own route (no shared formula), so the agreement below is a check, not a copy.

  • Curvature (Hessian): the second derivative of the loss in zz, measured by nudging zz both ways: L(z+h)−2L(z)+L(z−h)h2\frac{L(z+h) - 2L(z) + L(z-h)}{h^2}. Done separately for y=1y = 1 and y=0y = 0: the label does not matter.
  • Variance of the label: if the label really is a coin with P(y=1)=pP(y = 1) = p, its spread around its mean pp is p(1−p)2+(1−p)(0−p)2p\,(1-p)^2 + (1-p)\,(0-p)^2, summed over the two outcomes.
  • Fisher information: the average squared score, the score being the slope of the log-likelihood in zz (measured by nudging, for each outcome), averaged with weights pp and 1−p1 - p.

At z=0z = 0 all three are 0.25, the largest they get; at z=ln⁡9=2.197z = \ln 9 = 2.197 (p=0.9p = 0.9) all three are 0.09 (both values from the old page, recomputed). A confident prediction sits in a flat part of the loss: the gradient there barely changes as zz moves. That curvature equals Fisher information for every canonical pair (the label drops out of the second derivative) is why natural-gradient methods and K-FAC can use the Fisher as their curvature: for the whole network it equals the Gauss-Newton form of the Hessian (Martens 2020, section 9.2).

5. Overflow and the log-sum-exp trick

Taught on: Numerical computing, section 7 (the stable softmax, log-sum-exp and log-softmax, with the naive and stable versions animated side by side). This panel is practice.

Computers store numbers with a largest value. eze^{z} passes it at z=709.78z = 709.78 in 64-bit floats, z=88.72z = 88.72 in 32-bit floats and z=11.09z = 11.09 in 16-bit floats (each is ln⁡\ln of the format's largest number). Past it the computer returns infinity, and infinity divided by infinity is "not a number" (NaN). The fix uses a fact about the softmax: adding the same constant to every logit changes nothing, because ezi−m/∑jezj−m=ezi/∑jezje^{z_i - m} / \sum_j e^{z_j - m} = e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j} (the e−me^{-m} cancels). Subtract the largest logit mm first, so the biggest exponent is e0=1e^0 = 1:

ln⁡∑jezj=m+ln⁡∑jezj−m,m=maxj⁡zj\ln\sum_j e^{z_j} = m + \ln\sum_j e^{z_j - m}, \qquad m = \max_j z_j

32-bit results are emulated by rounding every intermediate to 32 bits (JavaScript's Math.fround). The libraries do this for you only if you call the fused function: PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss takes "unnormalized logits" (its docstring), and BCEWithLogitsLoss is "more numerically stable than using a plain Sigmoid followed by a BCELoss" because it uses "the log-sum-exp trick" (same file). Error analysis: Blanchard, Higham and Higham 2021.

Entropy, cross-entropy, KL and mutual information as pictures of codes. When: before or beside Information theory. About 45 min to 1 h.

A matrix is a map: it rotates, stretches along a few directions, squashes some directions to zero, and rotates again. Almost every linear-algebra question in ML is a question about that picture: which directions survive (rank), how much things stretch (norms, singular values), which directions are special (eigenvectors), how badly errors grow (conditioning), and how much is lost by keeping only the biggest stretches (low-rank approximation).

1. Vectors: arrows and lists, length, dot product, cosine, projection

The question. A model holds a word, an image patch or a whole sentence as a list of numbers. What can you do with such a list, and why does multiplying two of them pair by pair and adding tell you how similar they are?

Two pictures of the same thing

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. The running example from Topic: math is the input of a tiny three-word language model, x=(2,1)x = (2, 1). You can read it two ways, and you need both:

  • As a list: two numbers in a fixed order, x1=2x_1 = 2 and x2=1x_2 = 1. This is how code stores it (a tensor of shape (2,)). How many numbers it holds is its dimension, written x∈ℝ2x \in \mathbb{R}^2 ("x is a member of the set of all lists of 2 real numbers").
  • As an arrow from the origin: two steps right, one step up. This is how you reason about it. Adding vectors is placing arrows head to tail, (2,1)+(1,−2)=(3,−1)(2,1) + (1,-2) = (3,-1); multiplying by a number (a scalar) stretches the arrow, 3⋅(2,1)=(6,3)3 \cdot (2,1) = (6,3).

A GPT-2 small token is a list of 768 numbers. Nobody can draw 768 axes, but every rule on this page works the same in 768 dimensions as in 2, which is why the 2-D pictures are worth learning.

The tiny model's input x=(2,1)x = (2, 1) and its three word rows: cat (1,0)(1, 0), dog (0,1)(0, 1), sat (1,−2)(1, -2). The dashed line is the projection of xx onto the cat arrow. Every number comes from the root's model.

The dot product: two definitions that agree

The dot product (or inner product) of two vectors of the same dimension multiplies them entry by entry and adds:

a⋅b=a⊤b=∑i=1naibia \cdot b = a^\top b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i

The tiny model scores each word this way: x⋅wcat=2⋅1+1⋅0=2x \cdot w_{\text{cat}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 = 2, x⋅wdog=1x \cdot w_{\text{dog}} = 1, x⋅wsat=2⋅1+1⋅(−2)=0x \cdot w_{\text{sat}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-2) = 0. The a⊤ba^\top b notation reads the first vector as a row and the second as a column; it is the same number (the transpose ⊤\top is explained in section 3).

The second definition is geometric. If θ\theta is the angle between the arrows,

a⋅b=‖a‖‖b‖cos⁡θa \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta

Cosine similarity

Divide out the lengths and only the direction is left:

Projection: how much of one vector lies along another

The projection of aa onto the direction of bb is the shadow aa casts on the line through bb:

projb⁡(a)=a⋅bb⋅bb\operatorname{proj}_b(a) = \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b}\, b

The fraction is a number (how many copies of bb); multiplying by bb turns it back into an arrow. What is left over, a−projb⁡(a)a - \operatorname{proj}_b(a), is orthogonal to bb. Example: projecting x=(2,1)x = (2, 1) onto b=(1,1)b = (1, 1): x⋅b=3x \cdot b = 3, b⋅b=2b \cdot b = 2, so the projection is 1.5(1,1)=(1.5,1.5)1.5\,(1, 1) = (1.5, 1.5) and the leftover is (0.5,−0.5)(0.5, -0.5); check (0.5,−0.5)⋅(1,1)=0(0.5, -0.5) \cdot (1, 1) = 0. Projection is the idea behind least squares (section 9), PCA (section 12) and attention reading values (section 15).

What it is used for in ML
Embeddings are vectors and similar meanings point in similar directions; retrieval ranks documents by cosine similarity (usually after scaling every embedding to length 1, so the dot product is the cosine); attention scores are dot products of queries and keys; a logit is a dot product of the final hidden state with a row of the output matrix.

2. Matrices as maps: what a matrix does to the plane

The question. A matrix is a table of numbers. Why do people say it "rotates" or "stretches" things, and how can you see what a given table does without computing anything?

A matrix is a rectangular table of numbers; an m×nm \times n matrix has mm rows and nn columns, and AijA_{ij} is the entry in row ii, column jj (row first, always). Multiplying a matrix by a vector, AxAx, takes a vector with nn entries and returns one with mm entries. So a matrix is a map (a function) from ℝn\mathbb{R}^n to ℝm\mathbb{R}^m, and a very special kind: it is linear, meaning

A(x+y)=Ax+Ay,A(cx)=c(Ax)A(x + y) = Ax + Ay, \qquad A(c\,x) = c\,(Ax)

In plain words: the map of a sum is the sum of the maps, and stretching the input stretches the output by the same factor. Straight lines stay straight, the origin stays put, and evenly spaced grid lines stay evenly spaced.

The columns tell you everything

Write x=(x1,x2)=x1e1+x2e2x = (x_1, x_2) = x_1 e_1 + x_2 e_2, where e1=(1,0)e_1 = (1, 0) and e2=(0,1)e_2 = (0, 1) are the unit arrows along the axes (the standard basis). Linearity gives Ax=x1(Ae1)+x2(Ae2)Ax = x_1 (Ae_1) + x_2 (Ae_2), and Ae1Ae_1 is just the first column of AA, Ae2Ae_2 the second. So:

The one picture to keep
The columns of a matrix are where the axis arrows land. Know where e1e_1 and e2e_2 go, and you know where every point goes: the whole grid follows them.

The running 2x2 matrix on this page (it comes from the old page, every number checked) is

A=(3045)A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}

Its first column (3,4)(3, 4) is where e1e_1 lands and its second column (0,5)(0, 5) is where e2e_2 lands. The unit square becomes the parallelogram with sides (3,4)(3, 4) and (0,5)(0, 5), and the unit circle becomes an ellipse.

The determinant: how much area scales

The determinant of a 2x2 matrix is

det⁡(abcd)=ad−bc\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc

and it is the signed factor by which the map scales area: the unit square (area 1) becomes a parallelogram of area |det⁡A||\det A|. For AA: 3⋅5−0⋅4=153 \cdot 5 - 0 \cdot 4 = 15. A negative determinant means the map also flips orientation (a mirror image, like the reflection preset). A determinant of 0 means the plane is squashed onto a line or a point (the projection preset): area 0, and the map cannot be undone, because many inputs land on the same output. In nn dimensions the determinant scales volume the same way.

Non-square matrices change the dimension. The tiny model's WW is 3×23 \times 2: it takes the 2-number input to 3 scores, so it maps the plane into a flat plane sitting inside 3-D space. Its columns (1,0,1)(1, 0, 1) and (0,1,−2)(0, 1, -2) are where e1e_1 and e2e_2 land. A 2×32 \times 3 matrix goes the other way and must squash a whole direction of 3-D space to zero (section 6 says exactly which one).

What it is used for in ML
Every dense layer, every attention projection and every embedding lookup is a linear map; a network is linear maps alternating with simple non-linear functions (activations), because a stack of purely linear maps would collapse into one linear map (section 3). Thinking of a weight matrix as "what it does to directions" is what makes rank, conditioning and low-rank methods make sense.

3. Matrix multiplication, four ways

The question. Multiplying two matrices is taught as a rule about rows and columns. Why that rule, and why do papers keep rewriting the same product as a sum of columns, or a sum of outer products?

The product of an m×nm \times n matrix PP and an n×pn \times p matrix QQ is the m×pm \times p matrix PQPQ. The inner sizes must match (both nn); the outer sizes survive. There are four ways to read the same product, and each one is the natural reading in some part of ML. Take

P=(1234),Q=(5678),PQ=(19224350)P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad Q = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}, \quad PQ = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix}
  1. Row times column (dot products). Entry (i,j)(i, j) of PQPQ is the dot product of row ii of PP with column jj of QQ: (PQ)ij=∑kPikQkj(PQ)_{ij} = \sum_k P_{ik} Q_{kj}. So (PQ)11=1⋅5+2⋅7=19(PQ)_{11} = 1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 7 = 19. This is how attention scores are read: every query (a row) against every key (a column).
  2. Column picture. Column jj of PQPQ is PP times column jj of QQ, which is a combination of the columns of PP with weights from QQ: column 1 is 5(1,3)+7(2,4)=(19,43)5\,(1, 3) + 7\,(2, 4) = (19, 43). This is how you see that every output of a layer lies in the span of the weight matrix's columns (section 5).
  3. Sum of outer products. An outer product uv⊤u v^\top of a column and a row is a whole matrix with entries uivju_i v_j, and it has rank 1 (every row is a multiple of vv). PQPQ is the sum over kk of (column kk of PP) times (row kk of QQ):
    (1,3)(5,6)+(2,4)(7,8)=(561518)+(14162832)(1, 3)(5, 6) + (2, 4)(7, 8) = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 15 & 18 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 14 & 16 \\ 28 & 32 \end{pmatrix}
    . This is the SVD's reading (section 12), LoRA's BABA (a sum of rr rank-1 layers) and the gradient of a layer (∇WL=gx⊤\nabla_W L = g\,x^\top, the root's rank-1 gradient).
  4. Composition. (PQ)x=P(Qx)(PQ)x = P(Qx): applying PQPQ is applying QQ first, then PP. With x=e1x = e_1: Qx=(5,7)Qx = (5, 7), then P(5,7)=(19,43)P(5, 7) = (19, 43). Read right to left. This is why order matters: QP=(23343146)≠PQQP = \begin{pmatrix} 23 & 34 \\ 31 & 46 \end{pmatrix} \ne PQ, because "rotate then stretch" is not "stretch then rotate".

The same product computed in four orders. The counter shows that every order does the same 8 multiplications: the readings differ in grouping, not in work. Same input in every mode, drawn to the same layout.

The transpose, and why (PQ)⊤=Q⊤P⊤(PQ)^\top = Q^\top P^\top

The transpose P⊤P^\top swaps rows and columns: (P⊤)ij=Pji(P^\top)_{ij} = P_{ji}. A column vector becomes a row, which is why a⊤ba^\top b is a dot product (a 1×n1 \times n row times an n×1n \times 1 column gives a 1×11 \times 1 number) while ab⊤a b^\top is an outer product (n×1n \times 1 times 1×n1 \times n gives n×nn \times n). Transposing a product reverses the order: entry (i,j)(i, j) of (PQ)⊤(PQ)^\top is entry (j,i)(j, i) of PQPQ, ∑kPjkQki\sum_k P_{jk} Q_{ki}, which is row ii of Q⊤Q^\top dotted with column jj of P⊤P^\top. That reversal is what makes backpropagation multiply by W⊤W^\top on the way back.

Why stacked linear layers collapse

Composition gives a fact that shapes every network: two linear layers in a row, W2(W1x)W_2 (W_1 x), are one linear layer (W2W1)x(W_2 W_1) x. However many you stack, without a non-linear function between them the network can only ever compute one matrix. The activation function (ReLU, GELU) between layers is what stops the collapse.

What a product costs

An m×nm \times n times n×pn \times p product does m⋅n⋅pm \cdot n \cdot p multiplications and about as many additions, usually counted as 2mnp2mnp floating-point operations (FLOPs). For one GPT-2 small layer of 768 by 768 applied to 1,024 tokens that is 2×1024×768×768≈1.22 \times 1024 \times 768 \times 768 \approx 1.2 billion FLOPs. Counting FLOPs for whole networks and for the backward pass belongs to Matrix calculus and backprop.

4. Shapes, batches and broadcasting

The question. Papers write WxWx for one input. Code applies a layer to a batch of sequences of vectors at once, and a single misplaced axis silently gives wrong numbers. What are the rules?

Shapes: the check that catches most mistakes

Write the shape beside every symbol. WW is 3×23 \times 2, xx has 2 entries, so WxWx has 3: inner sizes match and vanish, outer sizes survive. A real model handles many inputs at once as a tensor (a table with more than two axes) of shape (B,T,d)(B, T, d): BB sequences in the batch, TT tokens each, dd numbers per token. A layer acts on the last axis only, the same matrix for every token.

Two conventions for the same layer. Papers usually treat one input as a column and write y=Wx+by = Wx + b with WW of shape (outputs by inputs). Code stacks inputs as rows, so it computes Y=XW⊤+bY = XW^\top + b: PyTorch's nn.Linear stores weight with shape (out_features, in_features) and computes y=xA⊤+by = xA^\top + b (PyTorch nn.Linear docs). GPT-2's original code uses a third layout: its Conv1D stores the weight as (in, out) and computes xW+bxW + b; Hugging Face's own docstring says it "works like a linear layer but the weights are transposed" (transformers pytorch_utils.py). The Low-rank lab tab uses this layout. The transpose between conventions is the same map; mixing them inside one derivation is the bug.

Broadcasting

Adding a bias bb of shape (d,)(d,) to activations of shape (B,T,d)(B, T, d) works because libraries broadcast: they line up shapes from the right, and an axis of size 1 (or a missing axis) is stretched to match. NumPy's rule, word for word: "two dimensions are compatible when they are equal, or one of them is 1" (NumPy broadcasting). So (3,1)+(1,2)(3, 1) + (1, 2) gives (3,2)(3, 2), and (B,T,d)+(d,)(B, T, d) + (d,) gives (B,T,d)(B, T, d).

The broadcasting bug, with numbers
Predictions of shape (3, 1), (1,2,3)(1, 2, 3) as a column, minus targets of shape (3,), also (1,2,3)(1, 2, 3): instead of three differences of 0 you get a 3×33 \times 3 table of every prediction minus every target, (0−1−210−1210)\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. Its mean square is 12/9=1.33312/9 = 1.333, though the predictions are perfect and the true mean squared error is 0. No error is raised. Fix: make the shapes equal (pred.squeeze(-1)) and assert shapes before every loss.
What it is used for in ML
Reading a model's code against its paper is mostly translating shapes and layouts: which axis is the batch, which is heads, whether the weight is stored (out, in) or (in, out). Einsum notation (section 16) makes every axis explicit and is the cure when a shape bug survives ten minutes of staring.

5. Independence, span, basis and rank

The question. A 768×768768 \times 768 weight matrix has 589,824 numbers. How many genuinely different directions does it use, and how would you tell?

Span and linear independence

A linear combination of vectors is a sum of scaled copies, c1v1+c2v2+…c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots. The span of a set of vectors is everything you can reach that way. The span of (1,0)(1, 0) and (0,1)(0, 1) is the whole plane; the span of (1,2)(1, 2) alone is a line through the origin; the span of (1,2)(1, 2) and (2,4)(2, 4) is still that same line, because (2,4)=2(1,2)(2, 4) = 2\,(1, 2) adds nothing new.

Vectors are linearly independent when none of them is a combination of the others; equivalently, the only way to combine them into the zero vector is with all coefficients 0. (1,2)(1, 2) and (2,4)(2, 4) are dependent: 2(1,2)−1(2,4)=(0,0)2\,(1, 2) - 1\,(2, 4) = (0, 0).

Basis, dimension and coordinates

A basis of a space is a set of independent vectors that spans it; every basis of the same space has the same number of vectors, the dimension. Once a basis is fixed, every vector has unique coordinates in it. The axis arrows are one basis of the plane; b1=(1,1)b_1 = (1, 1), b2=(1,−1)b_2 = (1, -1) is another, and in it the tiny model's input is x=1.5b1+0.5b2x = 1.5\, b_1 + 0.5\, b_2 (check: (1.5+0.5,1.5−0.5)=(2,1)(1.5 + 0.5, 1.5 - 0.5) = (2, 1)). Same arrow, different numbers: "the coordinates of a vector" always means "in some basis", and the eigenvector and SVD sections choose bases that make a matrix simple.

Rank

The rank of a matrix is the number of independent columns, which always equals the number of independent rows, and is the dimension of the set of outputs the map can produce. An m×nm \times n matrix has rank at most min⁡(m,n)\min(m, n); a square matrix of full rank is invertible (its map can be undone, A−1A=IA^{-1}A = I, where II is the identity matrix, ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere).

The numbers (old page, checked)

In plain words: the nn input dimensions are shared out exactly between the ones that reach the output (rank) and the ones squashed to zero (nullity). For (1224)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}: 1+1=21 + 1 = 2. For MM: 2+1=32 + 1 = 3.

Why rank-nullity holds (a proof in four steps)

1. Pick a basis u1,…,uku_1, \dots, u_k of the null space (kk is the nullity). 2. Extend it with v1,…,vn−kv_1, \dots, v_{n-k} to a basis of all of ℝn\mathbb{R}^n (always possible: keep adding vectors that are not yet in the span). 3. Every output AxAx is a combination of Av1,…,Avn−kAv_1, \dots, Av_{n-k}, because writing xx in the basis, the uu parts are sent to zero. 4. Those n−kn - k outputs are independent: if ∑ciAvi=0\sum c_i A v_i = 0 then ∑civi\sum c_i v_i is in the null space, so it is a combination of the uu's, and since the whole basis is independent all ci=0c_i = 0. So the output space has dimension exactly n−kn - k, which is the rank.

The tiny model
WW has columns (1,0,1)(1, 0, 1) and (0,1,−2)(0, 1, -2), independent, so its rank is 2 and its null space is just {0}\{0\}: no input direction is lost. Rank in floating point. In exact arithmetic rank is an integer you can read off. In floating point, rounding makes almost every matrix full rank, so the useful question is how many singular values (section 12) are large: the effective rank. The Low-rank lab tab measures it on real GPT-2 weights.

What it is used for in ML
Rank counts how many independent directions a map really uses. A low-rank update is cheap to store and train (LoRA); a low-rank bottleneck limits what can be expressed (the softmax bottleneck, next section); collapsed representations, where every embedding lies in a small subspace, show up as low effective rank.

The question. Which outputs can a layer never produce, whatever its input? Which inputs does it ignore? Gilbert Strang organises all of linear algebra around four sets that answer these questions.

The softmax bottleneck: the same fact at scale

A language model computes its logits as z=Ehz = E h, with hh the final hidden state (dd numbers) and EE the output matrix (V×dV \times d, one row per token). The logits therefore live in the column space of EE, of dimension at most dd. GPT-2 small has d=768d = 768 and V=50,257V = 50{,}257 and its output layer has no bias (it reuses the embedding table), so all its logit vectors lie in a 768-dimensional subspace of a 50,257-dimensional space. Their fix, a mixture of softmaxes, breaks the linearity.

What it is used for in ML
The column space says what a layer can output; the null space says what it ignores (an input change along it has no effect, and no gradient flows back along it); the left null space says what it can never produce. Low-rank adapters and probes are statements about these subspaces.

7. Norms: how big is a vector, a matrix, an update

The question. Weight decay, gradient clipping, spectral normalisation and "the update was too large" all measure size. Size in what sense? There are several, and the choice decides what a regulariser pushes towards.

Vector norms

A norm is a length: it is zero only for the zero vector, scales with the vector (‖cx‖=|c|‖x‖\lVert c x \rVert = |c| \lVert x \rVert) and obeys the triangle inequality (‖x+y‖≤‖x‖+‖y‖\lVert x + y \rVert \le \lVert x \rVert + \lVert y \rVert). Three are common:

‖x‖1=∑i|xi|,‖x‖2=∑ixi2,‖x‖∞=maxi⁡|xi|\lVert x \rVert_1 = \sum_i |x_i|, \qquad \lVert x \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}, \qquad \lVert x \rVert_\infty = \max_i |x_i|
The numbers
Tiny model input x=(2,1)x = (2, 1): ‖x‖1=3\lVert x \rVert_1 = 3, ‖x‖2=2.236\lVert x \rVert_2 = 2.236, ‖x‖∞=2\lVert x \rVert_\infty = 2. Old page, checked: (3,−4)(3, -4) gives 7, 5 and 4. Always ‖x‖∞≤‖x‖2≤‖x‖1\lVert x \rVert_\infty \le \lVert x \rVert_2 \le \lVert x \rVert_1.

The unit "circle" of each norm: every point at distance 1 from the origin. L1 is a diamond with corners on the axes, L2 the round circle, L-infinity a square. The dot is where a growing ball first touches a straight line of equal loss (the dashed line): for L1 at a corner, where one coordinate is exactly 0.

Why L1 gives sparsity. Minimising a loss subject to ‖w‖1≤c\lVert w \rVert_1 \le c means growing the diamond until it first touches a contour of the loss. A diamond is pointed at the axes, so the first contact is usually at a corner, where some coordinates are exactly zero. The round L2 ball has no corners: it shrinks every coordinate a little and zeroes none. That is lasso against ridge regression (L1 and L2 penalties on a linear model; Regularisation owns the full story).

Matrix norms

  • Frobenius: treat the matrix as one long vector, ‖A‖F=∑ijAij2\lVert A \rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{ij} A_{ij}^2}. Weight decay penalises ‖W‖F2\lVert W \rVert_F^2.
  • Spectral (operator 2-norm): the largest factor by which the map can stretch any vector, ‖A‖2=maxx≠0⁡‖Ax‖2/‖x‖2=σmax⁡(A)\lVert A \rVert_2 = \max_{x \ne 0} \lVert Ax \rVert_2 / \lVert x \rVert_2 = \sigma_{\max}(A), the largest singular value (section 12).
  • Nuclear: the sum of the singular values, ‖A‖∗=∑iσi\lVert A \rVert_* = \sum_i \sigma_i; the closest convex stand-in for rank, used when a method wants low rank but needs a smooth objective.

All three are functions of the singular values: Frobenius is ∑σi2\sqrt{\sum \sigma_i^2}, spectral is σ1\sigma_1, nuclear is ∑σi\sum \sigma_i.

Why the spectral norm is a Lipschitz constant. ‖Ax−Ay‖=‖A(x−y)‖≤σmax⁡‖x−y‖\lVert Ax - Ay \rVert = \lVert A(x - y) \rVert \le \sigma_{\max} \lVert x - y \rVert: no two inputs can end up further apart than σmax⁡\sigma_{\max} times their distance. Spectral normalisation divides a layer's weights by σmax⁡\sigma_{\max} to make that factor 1 (Miyato et al. 2018), and it is why the size of a layer's largest singular value controls how much it can amplify a perturbation.

What it is used for in ML

8. Orthogonality, projections and QR

The question. Why are rotations "nice" for numerics, and how do you find the point of a subspace closest to a vector, which is what every least-squares fit and every PCA does?

Orthonormal vectors and orthogonal matrices

Vectors are orthonormal when each has length 1 and every pair is orthogonal. A square matrix QQ whose columns are orthonormal is an orthogonal matrix, and that fact fits in one equation, Q⊤Q=IQ^\top Q = I (entry (i,j)(i, j) of Q⊤QQ^\top Q is column ii dotted with column jj: 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere). So its inverse is its transpose, free to compute. Orthogonal matrices are rotations and reflections: they keep every length and angle, ‖Qx‖2=x⊤Q⊤Qx=x⊤x\lVert Qx \rVert^2 = x^\top Q^\top Q x = x^\top x. Multiplying by them never amplifies rounding errors, which is why stable algorithms are built from them. The rotation by angle θ\theta is (cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ)\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}; RoPE, the position encoding of most current LLMs, rotates pairs of query and key coordinates by such matrices.

10. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors: the directions a map does not turn

The question. Apply the same matrix over and over (a recurrent network, a power iteration, a long product of layers). What happens to a vector, and why does the answer depend on one or two special numbers?

The picture first

Most arrows change direction when a matrix acts on them. A few special ones only get longer or shorter (or flip). These are the eigenvectors, and the factor is the eigenvalue:

Av=λv,v≠0A v = \lambda v, \qquad v \ne 0

In plain words: along an eigenvector, the matrix is just multiplication by the number λ\lambda. ("Eigen" is German for "own": the matrix's own directions.)

How to find them

Av=λvAv = \lambda v means (A−λI)v=0(A - \lambda I) v = 0 for a nonzero vv, so A−λIA - \lambda I has a nonzero null space, so it squashes area to zero, so its determinant is zero. That gives the characteristic equation:

det⁡(A−λI)=0\det(A - \lambda I) = 0
Worked: A=(3045)A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} (old page, checked)

det⁡(3−λ045−λ)=(3−λ)(5−λ)−0⋅4=0\det \begin{pmatrix} 3 - \lambda & 0 \\ 4 & 5 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (3 - \lambda)(5 - \lambda) - 0 \cdot 4 = 0, so λ=3\lambda = 3 or λ=5\lambda = 5. (A triangular matrix always has its diagonal entries as eigenvalues.)

For λ=3\lambda = 3: (A−3I)v=(0042)v=0(A - 3I)v = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} v = 0 needs 4v1+2v2=04v_1 + 2v_2 = 0, so v=(1,−2)v = (1, -2). Check: A(1,−2)=(3,4−10)=(3,−6)=3(1,−2)A(1, -2) = (3, 4 - 10) = (3, -6) = 3\,(1, -2). For λ=5\lambda = 5: v=(0,1)v = (0, 1); check A(0,1)=(0,5)A(0, 1) = (0, 5).

Two checks that always hold: the eigenvalues add up to the trace (sum of the diagonal), 3+5=8=3+53 + 5 = 8 = 3 + 5; they multiply to the determinant, 3×5=153 \times 5 = 15.

The two eigenvectors (1,−2)(1, -2) and (0,1)(0, 1) are not orthogonal: their dot product is −2-2. For a non-symmetric matrix that is normal; section 11 shows symmetric matrices are different.

Why eigenvalues rule repeated application

If v1,v2v_1, v_2 are eigenvectors that form a basis, write any input as x=c1v1+c2v2x = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2. Then Akx=c1λ1kv1+c2λ2kv2A^k x = c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^k v_2: each component is multiplied by its own eigenvalue kk times. The largest |λ||\lambda| wins, and the size of the result grows like |λmax⁡|k|\lambda_{\max}|^k when it is above 1 and shrinks to zero when every |λ|<1|\lambda| \lt 1. Written for the whole matrix: A=PΛP−1A = P \Lambda P^{-1}, with the eigenvectors as the columns of PP and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of Λ\Lambda, and then Ak=PΛkP−1A^k = P \Lambda^k P^{-1}: powers of a matrix are powers of numbers in the eigenvector basis.

Power iteration, on AA
Start from (1,1)(1, 1), multiply by AA and rescale to length 1, repeatedly. The angle to the dominant eigenvector (0,1)(0, 1) (eigenvalue 5) goes 18.4, 9.0, 4.8, 2.7, 1.6, 0.9, 0.5, 0.3 degrees: it shrinks by about the ratio 3/5=0.63/5 = 0.6 each step, because the other component is multiplied by 3 while the dominant one is multiplied by 5. This is how the largest singular value of a layer is estimated cheaply in spectral normalisation (one step per training step).

Exploding and vanishing gradients. A plain recurrent network applies the same matrix at every time step, and the gradient flowing back through TT steps is multiplied by (the transpose of) that matrix TT times. If its largest eigenvalue magnitude is above 1 the gradient can explode; if all are below 1 it vanishes. Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio state it as: "it is sufficient for λ1<1/γ\lambda_1 \lt 1/\gamma, where λ1\lambda_1 is the absolute value of the largest eigenvalue of the recurrent weight matrix", for the vanishing gradient problem to occur, with γ\gamma a bound on the activation's derivative; for linear units (γ=1\gamma = 1) that is the eigenvalue-below-1 picture above (Pascanu et al. 2013, section 2.1).

No real eigenvectors. A rotation by 30 degrees turns every arrow, so it has no real eigenvector: its characteristic equation λ2−2cos⁡(30°)λ+1=0\lambda^2 - 2\cos(30°)\lambda + 1 = 0 has complex roots cos⁡30°±isin⁡30°\cos 30° \pm i \sin 30°. Some matrices have too few eigenvectors to form a basis (the shear (1101)\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} has only the direction (1,0)(1, 0)). Both are reasons the SVD, which always exists, is usually the better tool for "what does this matrix do".

What it is used for in ML
The Hessian's eigenvalues are the curvatures of the loss along its eigenvectors, and the largest one sets the largest stable learning rate (2/λmax⁡2/\lambda_{\max}, on the root's Reading tab); PCA is the eigendecomposition of a covariance; PageRank and power iteration find a dominant eigenvector; stability of recurrent and state-space models is an eigenvalue condition.

The question. Covariance matrices and Hessians are symmetric. What does symmetry buy, and what does it mean for a matrix to be "positive"?

A matrix is symmetric when S=S⊤S = S^\top (mirror image across the diagonal). The tiny model gives one for free: S=W⊤WS = W^\top W, which is always symmetric because (W⊤W)⊤=W⊤W(W^\top W)^\top = W^\top W.

The spectral theorem

Spectral theorem
A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and a full set of orthonormal eigenvectors, so S=QΛQ⊤S = Q \Lambda Q^\top with QQ orthogonal: rotate into the eigenvector axes, stretch along each axis, rotate back.
Why eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal (two lines)

Take Sv1=λ1v1S v_1 = \lambda_1 v_1 and Sv2=λ2v2S v_2 = \lambda_2 v_2 with λ1≠λ2\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2. Compute v1⊤Sv2v_1^\top S v_2 two ways: as v1⊤(λ2v2)=λ2v1⋅v2v_1^\top (\lambda_2 v_2) = \lambda_2\, v_1 \cdot v_2, and, because S=S⊤S = S^\top, as (Sv1)⊤v2=λ1v1⋅v2(S v_1)^\top v_2 = \lambda_1\, v_1 \cdot v_2. Subtract: (λ1−λ2)v1⋅v2=0(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\, v_1 \cdot v_2 = 0, and since the eigenvalues differ, v1⋅v2=0v_1 \cdot v_2 = 0. A symmetric matrix defines a quadratic form, the number x⊤Sxx^\top S x. For SS above, x⊤Sx=2a2−4ab+5b2x^\top S x = 2a^2 - 4ab + 5b^2 with x=(a,b)x = (a, b).   1. every eigenvalue is positive (6 and 1: yes);

12. The SVD: every matrix is rotate, stretch, rotate

The question. Eigenvectors need a square matrix and may not exist. Is there a description of what any matrix does, including a 768×3072768 \times 3072 weight, that always works?

The picture: watch it happen

Take the unit circle and the grid of the plane, apply A=(3045)A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, and the circle becomes an ellipse. The singular value decomposition says this always happens in three simple moves: a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and another rotation. Step through the same circle under AA in one go, then under its three SVD factors. Drawn to scale: the circle has radius 1, the ellipse's long axis is 6.708.

The words, then the formula

A=UΣV⊤=∑i=1rσiuivi⊤A = U \Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^\top
VV
an n×nn \times n orthogonal matrix; its columns viv_i (the right singular vectors) are the input directions. V⊤V^\top rotates viv_i onto the ii-th axis.
Σ\Sigma
an m×nm \times n matrix, zero except for the singular values σ1≥σ2≥⋯≥0\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0 on its diagonal: the stretch along each axis.
UU
an m×mm \times m orthogonal matrix; its columns uiu_i (the left singular vectors) are the output directions.
rr
the rank: the number of nonzero singular values.

In plain words: there are orthonormal input directions viv_i that the matrix sends to orthogonal output directions, Avi=σiuiA v_i = \sigma_i u_i. The sum form reads the matrix as rr rank-1 layers, largest first. Every matrix, of any shape, has an SVD.

Computing it by hand

The SVD answers the earlier sections at once

  • Rank = number of nonzero σi\sigma_i; effective rank = number of σi\sigma_i above a tolerance.
  • Four subspaces: u1..uru_1..u_r span the column space, ur+1..umu_{r+1}..u_m the left null space, v1..vrv_1..v_r the row space, vr+1..vnv_{r+1}..v_n the null space.

13. The condition number: why some problems lose all their digits

The question. Two linear systems of the same size, solved by the same code. One gives an answer accurate to every digit, the other gives garbage. Why, and why does the same number decide how fast gradient descent converges?

The definition

κ(A)=σmax⁡(A)σmin⁡(A)\kappa(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}

In plain words: the ratio of the most a matrix stretches any direction to the least. A circle becomes an ellipse with this aspect ratio. κ=1\kappa = 1 for an orthogonal matrix (a circle stays a circle); κ=∞\kappa = \infty for a singular one (some direction is squashed flat). For the running examples: κ(A)=6.708/2.236=3\kappa(A) = 6.708/2.236 = 3; κ(W)=6/1=2.449\kappa(W) = \sqrt{6}/1 = 2.449.

What it does to a linear solve, derived

Solve Ax=bAx = b, but bb carries a small error δb\delta b (measurement noise or rounding). The computed answer is off by δx=A−1δb\delta x = A^{-1}\delta b. Two inequalities from the SVD: ‖δx‖=‖A−1δb‖≤‖δb‖/σmin⁡\lVert \delta x \rVert = \lVert A^{-1}\delta b \rVert \le \lVert \delta b \rVert / \sigma_{\min} (the inverse stretches by at most 1/σmin⁡1/\sigma_{\min}), and ‖b‖=‖Ax‖≤σmax⁡‖x‖\lVert b \rVert = \lVert Ax \rVert \le \sigma_{\max} \lVert x \rVert. Divide the first by ‖x‖≥‖b‖/σmax⁡\lVert x \rVert \ge \lVert b \rVert / \sigma_{\max}:

‖δx‖‖x‖≤κ(A)‖δb‖‖b‖\frac{\lVert \delta x \rVert}{\lVert x \rVert} \le \kappa(A)\, \frac{\lVert \delta b \rVert}{\lVert b \rVert}

In plain words: a relative error in the data can come out up to κ\kappa times bigger in the answer. Rule of thumb: you lose about log10⁡κ\log_{10}\kappa decimal digits. float32 carries about 7 digits, bfloat16 about 3, so a problem with κ=104\kappa = 10^4 is fine in float64, marginal in float32 and hopeless in bfloat16.

Worked: a nearly singular system
B=(1111.001)B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1.001 \end{pmatrix} has κ=4,002\kappa = 4{,}002. With b=(2,2.001)b = (2, 2.001) the solution is x=(1,1)x = (1, 1). Change bb to (2,2.002)(2, 2.002), a relative change of 0.035%, and the solution jumps to x=(0,2)x = (0, 2), a relative change of 100%: an amplification of 2,829, within the bound of 4,002.

The same thing happens inside a model fit. What it does to gradient descent

Near a minimum, a loss looks like a bowl 12w⊤Hw\tfrac{1}{2} w^\top H w whose curvature matrix is the Hessian HH (symmetric, PD at a strict minimum). Gradient descent's step size must stay below 2/λmax⁡2/\lambda_{\max} to be stable in the steepest direction, but then it crawls along the flattest direction, which has curvature λmin⁡\lambda_{\min}. With the best fixed step, η=2/(λmax⁡+λmin⁡)\eta = 2/(\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min}), the error shrinks by (κ−1)/(κ+1)(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1) per step, κ=λmax⁡/λmin⁡\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}. The old page stated this rate without the best-step condition; it holds only at that step size, and is slower at any other. The full derivation, with the root's diag(1, 10) bowl, is on Calculus and optimisation for ML; the root's Gradient lab tab animates it.

Fixing conditioning: preconditioning

  • Rescale the inputs. Standardising features (zero mean, unit variance) makes X⊤XX^\top X closer to a multiple of II; normalisation layers and careful initialisation do the same inside a network (Normalisation and initialisation).
  • Regularise. Adding λI\lambda I lifts the smallest eigenvalue (ridge, section 9; damping in second-order methods).
  • Precondition the update. Multiply the gradient by an approximation of H−1H^{-1}: Newton's method uses H−1H^{-1} itself; Adam divides each coordinate by the square root of an exponential moving average of its squared gradient, a diagonal preconditioner.
  • Orthogonalise the update. Muon takes the momentum update G=UΣV⊤G = U\Sigma V^\top of a 2-D weight and replaces it by UV⊤UV^\top: every singular value set to 1, so the update has κ=1\kappa = 1 and pushes equally in every direction it touches instead of being dominated by a few. A full SVD is too slow, so it runs five Newton-Schulz iterations X←aX+(bA+cA2)XX \leftarrow aX + (bA + cA^2)X with A=XX⊤A = XX^\top and (a,b,c)=(3.4445,−4.7750,2.0315)(a, b, c) = (3.4445, -4.7750, 2.0315), which "can be stably run in bfloat16"; it applies only to the 2-D weights of hidden layers, with embeddings, the output layer and scalar parameters left to AdamW (Keller Jordan, Muon, 8 Dec 2024). How it behaves in training is on Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers.

14. Low-rank structure in ML: what is really low rank, and what is not

The question. LoRA, MLA, GaLore and many compression papers all say "low rank". Low rank of what, exactly? Pretrained weights, the updates fine-tuning makes, the gradients, or the cached keys and values? The answers differ, and real numbers settle them.

Pretrained weights: concentrated, but not low rank

The old page said trained weight matrices "are often effectively low rank". Measured on GPT-2 small (the released weights, 2,048 tokens of WikiText-2 test text, script in this page's source folder), that does not hold in the sense that matters for compression:

k90: singular directions needed to keep 90% of the squared Frobenius norm, out of 768; "random" is a Gaussian matrix of the same shape and norm. Output error: relative error of the layer's output on the real inputs that reach it, ‖X(W−Wk)‖F/‖XW‖F\lVert X(W - W_k) \rVert_F / \lVert XW \rVert_F. Loss: GPT-2's next-token loss on the same text with only that one matrix replaced by its rank-64 truncation (unchanged model: nats).

Three readings. First, every real spectrum is more concentrated than random (k90 near 300 against 391 for the attention matrices), so there is structure. Second, it is nowhere near rank 64: keeping 64 of 768 directions leaves a relative output error of 42% for the MLP input matrix and 70% to 86% for the other five. Third, how much a matrix matters is not how compressible it is: cutting the MLP input matrix to rank 64 raises the loss by 0.72 nats, cutting the key matrix by 0.20. Truncating every attention and MLP matrix in all 12 layers to 90% of its rank, which saves no parameters at all, already raises the loss from 3.17 to 3.91; at 50% of the rank it is 8.50, close to the 10.8 of a uniform guess over 50,257 tokens (). Real weights carry information in their small singular values too. The Low-rank lab tab lets you pick the matrix and kk.

Fine-tuning updates: genuinely low rank

LoRA (Hu et al. 2021) does not compress the weights; it constrains the change. The layer computes W0x+αrBAxW_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A x with the pretrained W0W_0 frozen, BB of shape d×rd \times r, AA of shape r×kr \times k and r≪min⁡(d,k)r \ll \min(d, k). BABA is a sum of rr outer products, so its rank is at most rr. BB starts at zero, so training starts exactly at the pretrained model, and after training BABA can be merged into W0W_0 for free inference. For d=k=4096d = k = 4096, r=8r = 8: 2×4096×8=65,5362 \times 4096 \times 8 = 65{,}536 trainable numbers instead of 16,777,21616{,}777{,}216, 0.39%.

Correction carried from the root
The old page said "ranks as low as 1 to 8 were competitive on GPT-3". The paper's words: "a rank as small as one suffices for adapting both WqW_q and WvW_v" on GPT-3, with Table 6 testing r=1,2,4,8,64r = 1, 2, 4, 8, 64; and the low "intrinsic rank" of the update is the authors' hypothesis, which they then test (LoRA, arXiv 2106.09685, sections 4 and 7). The idea goes back to Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer and Gupta: "by optimizing only 200 trainable parameters randomly projected back into the full space, we can tune a RoBERTa model to achieve 90% of the full parameter performance levels on MRPC" (Intrinsic dimensionality, arXiv 2012.13255).

  • Exactly rr singular values are nonzero (the next is about 10−1410^{-14}, rounding), as the algebra says.

  • The paper's "amplification factor" (section 7.3 and appendix H.4: ‖ΔWq‖F/‖U⊤WqV⊤‖F\lVert \Delta W_q \rVert_F / \lVert U^\top W_q V^\top \rVert_F, with U,VU, V the top-rr singular directions of ΔWq\Delta W_q) was "21.5≈6.91/0.3221.5 \approx 6.91/0.32 for r=4r = 4" on GPT-3's layer 48, and "only around 2" at r=64r = 64. The LoRA paper page trains LoRA live on a toy where the true update is known, and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) covers the variants (DoRA, rsLoRA, PiSSA) and current practice.

Cached keys and values: MLA

Multi-head latent attention (DeepSeek-V2 and V3) caches, per token and layer, one compressed vector c=WDKVhc = W^{DKV} h of dc=512d_c = 512 numbers (plus a 64-number key for the rotary position part), and rebuilds every head's keys and values from it with up-projections k=WUKck = W^{UK} c, v=WUVcv = W^{UV} c. The map from hidden state to keys is the product WUKWDKVW^{UK} W^{DKV}, a factorisation through a 512-wide bottleneck: exactly the BABA shape. Because q⊤k=(WQh)⊤WUKc=h⊤((WQ)⊤WUK)cq^\top k = (W^Q h)^\top W^{UK} c = h^\top \big((W^Q)^\top W^{UK}\big) c, the up-projection can be absorbed into the query side and the full keys never built. With nh=128n_h = 128 heads of dh=128d_h = 128, the cache is 512+64=576512 + 64 = 576 numbers per token per layer against 2×128×128=32,7682 \times 128 \times 128 = 32{,}768 for standard multi-head attention. The animated version is on DeepSeek.

Gradients: GaLore

GaLore keeps full-rank weights but notices that the gradient of a weight matrix is approximately low rank (the root shows one example's gradient is exactly rank 1; a batch sums a few such outer products). Every TT steps it takes the SVD of the current gradient GG (m×nm \times n, m≤nm \le n), keeps the top rr left singular vectors as a projector PP (m×rm \times r), runs Adam on the small matrix P⊤GP^\top G (r×nr \times n), and maps the update back with PP. Adam's two moment matrices shrink from 2mn2mn numbers to 2nr2nr, plus mrmr for PP. The paper uses T=200T = 200 and scale α=0.25\alpha = 0.25 for its LLaMA pretraining runs and reports that it "reduces memory usage by up to 65.5% in optimizer states" (GaLore, Zhao et al. 2024, arXiv 2403.03507). For one 4096×40964096 \times 4096 matrix at r=128r = 128: 33,554,432 moment numbers become 4096×128+2×4096×128=1,572,8644096 \times 128 + 2 \times 4096 \times 128 = 1{,}572{,}864.

The pattern
Low rank is a fact about changes and products (fine-tuning updates, gradients, the query-key product of one head, the KV bottleneck a model is trained with), much more than about pretrained weight matrices themselves. Methods that impose low rank during training (LoRA, MLA, GaLore) work; compressing a finished model by truncated SVD costs accuracy fast.

15. Embeddings and attention, read as linear algebra

The question. The attention equation softmax⁡(QK⊤/dk)V\operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V is the most-read line in ML. What is each piece, as an operation on vectors and matrices?

An embedding lookup is a matrix product

A token with id ii can be written as a one-hot vector eie_i (all zeros except a 1 in position ii). With an embedding table EE of shape V×dV \times d, one row per token, ei⊤Ee_i^\top E picks out row ii. Libraries do the lookup by indexing because multiplying by a vector of zeros is wasteful, but the gradient is that of a matrix product: only the rows that were used get updated. GPT-2 small's table is 50,257×76850{,}257 \times 768, and it is reused as the output matrix (section 6).

Real embeddings are not centred. Two random GPT-2 token embeddings have cosine similarity on average, not 0: the vectors share a common direction (the mean embedding's length is of the average embedding's length). That is why cosine similarities like cat to dog (0.55) must be read against that baseline, and why many retrieval systems subtract the mean or whiten embeddings before comparing them.

Attention, piece by piece

For a sequence of TT token vectors stacked as the rows of HH (T×dT \times d), one attention head computes three projections and three products:

Q=HWQ,K=HWK,V=HWV,out=softmax⁡(QK⊤dk)VQ = H W_Q,\quad K = H W_K,\quad V = H W_V, \qquad \text{out} = \operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\, V
  1. Projections (section 2): each token's vector is mapped to a query, a key and a value of dkd_k numbers each (dk=64d_k = 64 in GPT-2 small).
  2. Scores: QK⊤QK^\top is T×TT \times T, entry (i,j)(i, j) the dot product of query ii with key jj (row times column, section 3): how well token jj matches what token ii is looking for. Dividing by dk\sqrt{d_k} keeps the scores' spread near 1 when the entries of qq and kk have variance 1 (the variance of a sum of dkd_k products is dkd_k).
  3. Weights: softmax along each row turns scores into positive weights summing to 1.
  4. Output: multiplying by VV makes each output row a weighted average of value vectors, a point inside their convex hull: the column picture of section 3, with the weights as coefficients.

The query-key product is a low-rank bilinear form

The score between hidden states hih_i and hjh_j is qi⋅kj=hi⊤(WQWK⊤)hjq_i \cdot k_j = h_i^\top (W_Q W_K^\top) h_j. The 768×768768 \times 768 matrix WQWK⊤W_Q W_K^\top is a product through a 64-wide middle, so its rank is at most 64 (rank of a product is at most the smallest inner size). Measured on GPT-2 small's layer 5, head 0: exactly nonzero singular values out of 768 (the 65th is 3×10−143 \times 10^{-14}, rounding). Each head compares tokens through a 64-dimensional "view" of the 768-dimensional residual stream; twelve heads give twelve such views. The same holds for WVWOW_V W_O, the head's output map (Elhage et al., A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits, 2021, call these the QK and OV circuits).

Multi-head attention as block matrices. The 768×2304768 \times 2304 matrix c_attn holds WQ,WK,WVW_Q, W_K, W_V side by side, and each is twelve 768×64768 \times 64 blocks, one per head; "splitting heads" in code is a reshape from (B,T,768)(B, T, 768) to (B,T,12,64)(B, T, 12, 64) and a transpose of two axes, no arithmetic.

18. Common mistakes, and the questions interviews ask

Mistakes

Treating eigenvalues as stretches
For a non-symmetric matrix they differ from the singular values (3 and 5 against 6.708 and 2.236 for AA), and the eigenvectors need not be orthogonal. Stretch, norms and conditioning are singular-value questions.
Forgetting to centre before PCA
1nX⊤X\frac{1}{n}X^\top X is the covariance only if every column of XX has mean zero; otherwise the first "principal component" mostly points at the mean. GPT-2's embeddings, with a mean cosine near 0.27 between random tokens, are a real case.
Mixing layouts and conventions
WxWx with WW as (out, in), XW⊤XW^\top in PyTorch, XWXW with GPT-2's Conv1D (in, out). Each is right; mixing two in one derivation puts transposes in the wrong places. Write the shape beside every symbol. (Derivative layouts are a separate convention, owned by Matrix calculus and backprop.)
Reading "low rank" as "small", or "small" as "unimportant"
A rank-1 matrix can have a huge norm and a full-rank matrix can be tiny: rank counts directions, norms measure size. And small singular values of a pretrained weight still matter: dropping the bottom 10% of every GPT-2 matrix raises the loss from 3.17 to 3.91 (section 14).
Inverting a matrix to solve a system
Computing A−1bA^{-1}b is slower and less accurate than a factorise-and-solve, and fails badly when κ\kappa is large. Likewise, forming X⊤XX^\top X squares κ\kappa: 77% coefficient error against 0.0018% for QR in the float32 run of section 13.
Taking the rank of a floating-point matrix literally
In floating point almost every matrix is full rank. Ask how many singular values exceed a tolerance (NumPy's matrix_rank uses σmax⁡⋅max⁡(m,n)⋅ε\sigma_{\max} \cdot \max(m, n) \cdot \varepsilon), or look at how fast the spectrum falls.
Silent broadcasting
(n,1)−(n,)(n, 1) - (n,) gives an n×nn \times n table, not nn differences: perfect predictions scored as mean squared error 1.333 in section 4. Assert shapes before every reduction.
Assuming AB=BAAB = BA
Order matters (PQ≠QPPQ \ne QP in section 3), and (AB)⊤=B⊤A⊤(AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top, (AB)−1=B−1A−1(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}: both reverse the order.

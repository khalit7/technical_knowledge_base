The idea in one screen

A matrix is a map: it rotates, stretches along a few orthogonal directions (squashing some to zero), and rotates again. That is the SVD, and its stretches, the singular values, answer almost every linear-algebra question in ML: which directions survive (rank), how big the map is (norms), how badly it amplifies errors (conditioning), and how much is lost by keeping only the biggest stretches (low-rank approximation).

The running matrix A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]] is a rotation, a stretch by 6.708 and 2.236, and another rotation

The unit circle and two input directions \(v_1, v_2\) at right angles. \(V^\top\) rotates them onto the axes (by \(-45°\)), \(\Sigma\) stretches the axes by the singular values \(\sigma_1 = 6.708\) and \(\sigma_2 = 2.236\), and \(U\) rotates the result onto the output directions (by \(71.57°\)). Read off the last panel: rank 2 (two nonzero stretches), spectral norm 6.708, condition number \(6.708 / 2.236 = 3\), and the best rank-1 approximation misses by exactly \(\sigma_2 = 2.236\). Section 10 steps through it; the {{Matrix playground|#t-play}} does it for any 2x2 or 3x3 matrix.

The one idea to keep
Ask of any matrix: which directions does it keep, how much does it stretch each, and how unequal are the stretches? The SVD answers all three, which is why it sits in the middle of this page.

1. Vectors and the dot product: the score behind every logit

A vector is a list of numbers and, equally, an arrow from the origin. A GPT-2 small token is a list of 768 numbers; nobody can draw 768 axes, but every rule on this page works the same in 768 dimensions as in 2, which is why the 2-D pictures are worth learning. The dot product multiplies two vectors entry by entry and adds, \(a \cdot b = a^\top b = \sum_i a_i b_i\), and that is how the tiny model scores each word: \(x \cdot w_{\text{cat}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 = 2\), \(x \cdot w_{\text{dog}} = 1\), \(x \cdot w_{\text{sat}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-2) = 0\).

The right answer, sat, is at right angles to the input, so its score is 0

The tiny model's input \(x = (2, 1)\) and its three word rows: cat \((1, 0)\), dog \((0, 1)\), sat \((1, -2)\). The dashed line is the projection of \(x\) onto the cat arrow. Every number comes from the root's model.

The same number has a geometric reading, \(a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta\), with the length \(\lVert x \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}\) (\(\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236\) for \(x\)). Divide out the lengths and only the direction is left, the cosine similarity \(\cos\theta = a \cdot b / (\lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert)\): 1 for the same direction, 0 at right angles (orthogonal), \(-1\) opposite. For the tiny model sat is exactly orthogonal to the input, which is why its score is 0 however long the arrows are.

The projection of \(a\) onto the direction of \(b\) is the shadow \(a\) casts on the line through \(b\), \(\operatorname{proj}_b(a) = \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b}\, b\), and what is left over is orthogonal to \(b\). Projection is the idea behind least squares (section 7), PCA (section 10) and attention reading values (section 13).

What it is used for in ML
Embeddings are vectors and similar meanings point in similar directions; retrieval ranks documents by cosine similarity (usually after scaling every embedding to length 1, so the dot product is the cosine); attention scores are dot products of queries and keys; a logit is a dot product of the final hidden state with a row of the output matrix.
From zero: lists and arrows, length, the cosines and projection worked, and why the two definitions of the dot product agree

Two pictures of the same thing. As a list, \(x = (2, 1)\) is two numbers in a fixed order, \(x_1 = 2\) and \(x_2 = 1\): how code stores it (a tensor of shape (2,)). How many numbers it holds is its dimension, written \(x \in \mathbb{R}^2\) ("x is a member of the set of all lists of 2 real numbers"). As an arrow from the origin it is two steps right, one step up: how you reason about it. Adding vectors is placing arrows head to tail, \((2,1) + (1,-2) = (3,-1)\); multiplying by a number (a scalar) stretches the arrow, \(3 \cdot (2,1) = (6,3)\).

Length. The L2 norm follows from Pythagoras: \(\lVert x \rVert_2 = \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236\); the subscript 2 says "squares, then square root" (other norms are in section 6). A unit vector has length 1; dividing any nonzero vector by its length gives the unit vector pointing the same way, \(x / \lVert x \rVert = (0.894, 0.447)\). The \(a^\top b\) notation reads the first vector as a row and the second as a column; it is the same number (the transpose is in section 3).

Why the two definitions agree. Draw \(a\), \(b\) and the third side of their triangle, \(a - b\). The law of cosines (Pythagoras for a triangle without a right angle) says \(\lVert a - b \rVert^2 = \lVert a \rVert^2 + \lVert b \rVert^2 - 2 \lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta\). Expanding with entries, \(\lVert a - b \rVert^2 = \sum_i (a_i - b_i)^2 = \lVert a \rVert^2 - 2\, a \cdot b + \lVert b \rVert^2\). The two expressions are equal; cancel \(\lVert a \rVert^2 + \lVert b \rVert^2\) and divide by \(-2\): \(a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta\). Check on \(x\) and \(w_{\text{cat}}\): \(2 = 2.236 \times 1 \times \cos\theta\), so \(\cos\theta = 0.894\), an angle of 26.6 degrees, which is the angle of the arrow \((2, 1)\) above the horizontal.

The numbers. \(\cos(x, w_{\text{cat}}) = 2/\sqrt{5} = 0.894\), \(\cos(x, w_{\text{dog}}) = 1/\sqrt{5} = 0.447\), \(\cos(x, w_{\text{sat}}) = 0\). Projecting \(x = (2, 1)\) onto \(b = (1, 1)\): \(x \cdot b = 3\), \(b \cdot b = 2\), so the projection is \(1.5\,(1, 1) = (1.5, 1.5)\) and the leftover \((0.5, -0.5)\) has \((0.5, -0.5) \cdot (1, 1) = 0\).

From the old page, checked: \(a = (1, 2, 2)\), \(b = (2, 1, 2)\) give \(a \cdot b = 8\), \(\lVert a \rVert = \lVert b \rVert = 3\), \(\cos\theta = 8/9 = 0.889\); and \((3, -4) \cdot (4, 3) = 12 - 12 = 0\), orthogonal.

2. Matrices as maps: the columns are where the axes land

An \(m \times n\) matrix times a vector with \(n\) entries gives one with \(m\) entries, so a matrix is a map from \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to \(\mathbb{R}^m\), and a linear one: \(A(x + y) = Ax + Ay\) and \(A(c\,x) = c\,(Ax)\). Straight lines stay straight, the origin stays put, and evenly spaced grid lines stay evenly spaced. Writing \(x = x_1 e_1 + x_2 e_2\) with the axis arrows \(e_1 = (1, 0)\), \(e_2 = (0, 1)\) (the standard basis), linearity gives \(Ax = x_1 (Ae_1) + x_2 (Ae_2)\), and \(Ae_1\) is just the first column of \(A\).

The one picture to keep
The columns of a matrix are where the axis arrows land. Know where \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) go, and you know where every point goes: the whole grid follows them.

The running matrix (from the old page, every number checked) is \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\): \(e_1\) lands on \((3, 4)\), \(e_2\) on \((0, 5)\), the unit square becomes the parallelogram with those sides and the unit circle becomes an ellipse. Switch the preset to see a rotation, shear, projection and reflection do the same.

Each preset moves the axis arrows; the grid, the square and the circle follow

The determinant, \(\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc\), is the signed factor by which the map scales area: for \(A\), \(3 \cdot 5 - 0 \cdot 4 = 15\). A negative determinant means the map also flips orientation (a mirror image, like the reflection preset). A determinant of 0 means the plane is squashed onto a line or a point (the projection preset): many inputs land on the same output, so the map cannot be undone. In \(n\) dimensions the determinant scales volume the same way.

Non-square matrices change the dimension. The tiny model's \(W\) is \(3 \times 2\): it takes the 2-number input to 3 scores, so it maps the plane into a flat plane sitting inside 3-D space, with \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) landing on its columns \((1, 0, 1)\) and \((0, 1, -2)\). A \(2 \times 3\) matrix goes the other way and must squash a whole direction of 3-D space to zero (section 5 says exactly which one).

What it is used for in ML
Every dense layer, every attention projection and every embedding lookup is a linear map; a network is linear maps alternating with simple non-linear functions (activations), because a stack of purely linear maps would collapse into one linear map (section 3). Thinking of a weight matrix as "what it does to directions" is what makes rank, conditioning and low-rank methods make sense.

Notation: \(A_{ij}\) is the entry in row \(i\), column \(j\) (row first, always); an \(m \times n\) matrix has \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns.

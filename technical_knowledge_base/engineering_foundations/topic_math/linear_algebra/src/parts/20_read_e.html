6. Norms: how big is a vector, a matrix, an update

Weight decay, gradient clipping, spectral normalisation and "the update was too large" all measure size, in different senses, and the choice decides what a regulariser pushes towards. For vectors three norms are common:

\[ \lVert x \rVert_1 = \sum_i |x_i|, \qquad \lVert x \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}, \qquad \lVert x \rVert_\infty = \max_i |x_i| \]

For \(x = (2, 1)\): \(\lVert x \rVert_1 = 3\), \(\lVert x \rVert_2 = 2.236\), \(\lVert x \rVert_\infty = 2\) (old page, checked: \((3, -4)\) gives 7, 5 and 4). Always \(\lVert x \rVert_\infty \le \lVert x \rVert_2 \le \lVert x \rVert_1\).

The L1 ball has corners on the axes, so a loss contour usually touches it where a coordinate is 0

The unit "circle" of each norm: every point at distance 1 from the origin. L1 is a diamond with corners on the axes, L2 the round circle, L-infinity a square. The dot is where a growing ball first touches a straight line of equal loss (the dashed line): for L1 at a corner, where one coordinate is exactly 0.

Why L1 gives sparsity. Minimising a loss subject to \(\lVert w \rVert_1 \le c\) grows the diamond until it first touches a contour of the loss, usually at a corner, where some coordinates are exactly zero; the round L2 ball shrinks every coordinate a little and zeroes none. That is lasso against ridge regression ({{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}} owns the full story).

Matrix norms are functions of the singular values.

  • Frobenius: the matrix as one long vector, \(\lVert A \rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{ij} A_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\sum \sigma_i^2}\). Weight decay penalises \(\lVert W \rVert_F^2\).
  • Spectral (operator 2-norm): the largest factor by which the map stretches any vector, \(\lVert A \rVert_2 = \max_{x \ne 0} \lVert Ax \rVert_2 / \lVert x \rVert_2 = \sigma_{\max}(A)\).
  • Nuclear: \(\lVert A \rVert_* = \sum_i \sigma_i\), the closest convex stand-in for rank, used when a method wants low rank but needs a smooth objective.

For \(A\) (singular values 6.708 and 2.236, section 10): Frobenius \(\sqrt{50} = 7.071\), spectral 6.708, nuclear 8.944.

The norms worked: A, the tiny model's W and its rank-1 gradient

For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) (singular values \(\sqrt{45} = 6.708\) and \(\sqrt{5} = 2.236\), derived in section 10): Frobenius \(\sqrt{9 + 0 + 16 + 25} = \sqrt{50} = 7.071 = \sqrt{45 + 5}\); spectral 6.708; nuclear \(6.708 + 2.236 = 8.944\).

For the tiny model's \(W\) (singular values \(\sqrt{6} = 2.449\) and 1): Frobenius \(\sqrt{7} = 2.646\), spectral 2.449, nuclear 3.449. For its rank-1 gradient \(g x^\top\) all three coincide: \(\lVert g \rVert \lVert x \rVert = 1.153 \times 2.236 = 2.579\), because a rank-1 matrix has a single nonzero singular value.

The spectral norm is a Lipschitz constant. \(\lVert Ax - Ay \rVert = \lVert A(x - y) \rVert \le \sigma_{\max} \lVert x - y \rVert\): no two inputs can end up further apart than \(\sigma_{\max}\) times their distance. Spectral normalisation divides a layer's weights by \(\sigma_{\max}\) to make that factor 1 ({{Miyato et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05957}}), which is why a layer's largest singular value controls how much it can amplify a perturbation.

What it is used for in ML
Gradient clipping rescales the whole gradient when its L2 norm (over all parameters together) exceeds a threshold; weight decay shrinks the Frobenius norm; adversarial robustness budgets are usually L-infinity balls; spectral norms bound how much a layer amplifies; optimisers such as Muon choose the update with the best decrease per unit of spectral norm (section 11).

What makes a norm a norm: it is zero only for the zero vector, scales with the vector (\(\lVert c x \rVert = |c| \lVert x \rVert\)) and obeys the triangle inequality (\(\lVert x + y \rVert \le \lVert x \rVert + \lVert y \rVert\)).

7. Orthogonality, projections, least squares and QR

Vectors are orthonormal when each has length 1 and every pair is orthogonal. A square matrix with orthonormal columns is an orthogonal matrix, and that fits in one equation, \(Q^\top Q = I\), so its inverse is its transpose, free to compute. Orthogonal matrices are rotations and reflections: they keep every length and angle, \(\lVert Qx \rVert^2 = x^\top Q^\top Q x = x^\top x\), so multiplying by them never amplifies rounding errors, which is why stable algorithms are built from them. The rotation by \(\theta\) is \(\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}\); RoPE, the position encoding of most current LLMs, rotates pairs of query and key coordinates by such matrices.

Least squares: the best fit is a projection

Fit \(\hat y = w_0 + w_1 t\) to the points \((t, y) = (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2)\) (the old page's example, checked). Three equations for two unknowns have no exact solution. Stack them, a column of ones for the intercept and a column of \(t\) values:

\[ X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad y = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \text{minimise } \lVert y - Xw \rVert_2^2 \]

The best line leaves residuals that are orthogonal to both columns of X

Left: the three points, the fitted line \(1.167 + 0.5\,t\) and the residuals \((-0.167, 0.333, -0.167)\). Right: the same fit in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), one axis per data point: \(y\) sits off the plane of all predictions \(Xw\) (the column space of \(X\)); the fit \(\hat y = (1.167, 1.667, 2.167)\) is its foot on the plane, and the residual \(y - \hat y\) stands at right angles to it. The 3-D panel is a schematic projection.

The predictions \(Xw\) range over the column space of \(X\), a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and \(y\) is not on it. The closest point leaves a leftover orthogonal to every column, \(X^\top (y - Xw) = 0\), which gives the normal equations:

\[ X^\top X\, w = X^\top y \;\Rightarrow\; \hat y = Xw = \underbrace{X (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top}_{P}\, y \]

No calculus is needed: the derivation is orthogonality. (The same equations come from setting the gradient \(2X^\top(Xw - y)\) to zero; deriving such gradients is on {{Matrix calculus and backprop|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}}.) \(P\) is the projection matrix: projecting twice changes nothing (\(P^2 = P\)) and \(P\) is symmetric.

Here \(X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(X^\top y = (5, 6)\) give \(w = (1.167, 0.5)\); the residuals \((-0.167, 0.333, -0.167)\) sum to 0 (orthogonal to the column of ones) and have zero dot product with \(t = (0, 1, 2)\), exactly as \(X^\top(y - Xw) = 0\) demands.

QR: the same answer without forming \(X^\top X\). Gram-Schmidt makes the columns orthonormal one at a time (subtract from each column its projections onto the ones already done, then scale to length 1); recording the coefficients gives \(X = QR\), with \(Q\) orthonormal columns and \(R\) upper triangular. The normal equations then become \(R w = Q^\top y\), solved from the bottom row up. Why bother: forming \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number (section 11), here \(\kappa(X) = 2.92\) against \(\kappa(X^\top X) = 8.55\), the square of 2.924. Harmless at this size, ruinous for an ill-conditioned problem in float32, as section 11 shows on a real run. Library routines (LAPACK, behind NumPy and PyTorch) use Householder reflections rather than textbook Gram-Schmidt, because classical Gram-Schmidt loses orthogonality in floating point when columns are nearly parallel (Trefethen and Bau, lectures 8 to 10).

Ridge and the pseudo-inverse. Ridge regression adds \(\lambda \lVert w \rVert^2\), which changes the equations to \((X^\top X + \lambda I) w = X^\top y\). Adding \(\lambda I\) adds \(\lambda\) to every eigenvalue of \(X^\top X\) (7.162 and 0.838 here), so the condition number drops from \(7.162 / 0.838 = 8.55\) to \(8.162 / 1.838 = 4.44\) at \(\lambda = 1\): regularisation is also a numerical fix. The pseudo-inverse \(X^{+}\) (section 10) gives the least-squares solution even when \(X\) is rank-deficient, choosing the shortest \(w\) among the many that fit equally well.

What it is used for in ML
Orthogonal initialisation keeps signal norms constant through deep linear stacks; RoPE is a rotation; linear regression, linear probes and the last layer of a network with squared loss are projections (training a linear readout is projecting targets onto the span of the features); the closed-form ridge solution sits behind kernel methods; QR is the stable way to solve least squares and to orthonormalise a basis (randomized SVD routines, such as PyTorch's torch.svd_lowrank, orthonormalise with QR at every pass).
Worked: the normal equations solved by hand, why the orthogonal leftover is the closest point, and the QR of this X

The solve by hand. \(X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) (3 points, sum of \(t\) is 3, sum of \(t^2\) is 5) and \(X^\top y = (5, 6)\). The 2x2 inverse is \(\frac{1}{\det}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}\) with \(\det = 15 - 9 = 6\), so \(w = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & -3 \\ -3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = (1.167, 0.5)\).

Predictions \(1.167, 1.667, 2.167\); residuals \(y - \hat y = (-0.167, 0.333, -0.167)\). They sum to 0 (orthogonal to the column of ones) and \(0 \cdot (-0.167) + 1 \cdot 0.333 + 2 \cdot (-0.167) = 0\) (orthogonal to the \(t\) column), exactly as \(X^\top(y - Xw) = 0\) demands.

Closest point. For any other point \(Xw'\) of the subspace, \(y - Xw' = (y - Xw) + X(w - w')\), the two pieces are orthogonal, so by Pythagoras \(\lVert y - Xw' \rVert^2 = \lVert y - Xw \rVert^2 + \lVert X(w - w') \rVert^2 \ge \lVert y - Xw \rVert^2\).

QR by Gram-Schmidt. \(X\) has columns \(a_1 = (1, 1, 1)\) and \(a_2 = (0, 1, 2)\). Step 1: \(q_1 = a_1 / \lVert a_1 \rVert = (1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3}\), and \(r_{11} = \sqrt{3} = 1.732\). Step 2: the coefficient of \(a_2\) along \(q_1\) is \(r_{12} = q_1 \cdot a_2 = 3/\sqrt{3} = 1.732\); subtract it: \(a_2 - r_{12} q_1 = (0, 1, 2) - (1, 1, 1) = (-1, 0, 1)\). Its length is \(r_{22} = \sqrt{2} = 1.414\), so \(q_2 = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt{2}\), and

\[ R = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{3} & \sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{check: } \sqrt{3}\, q_1 + \sqrt{2}\, q_2 = (1,1,1) + (-1,0,1) = (0,1,2) = a_2 \]

Solve. \(Q^\top y = (5/\sqrt{3}, 1/\sqrt{2}) = (2.887, 0.707)\). Back-substitute from the bottom row: \(\sqrt{2}\, w_1 = 1/\sqrt{2}\) gives \(w_1 = 0.5\); \(\sqrt{3}\,(w_0 + w_1) = 5/\sqrt{3}\) gives \(w_0 = 5/3 - 0.5 = 1.167\).

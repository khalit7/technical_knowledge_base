2. Matrices as maps: what a matrix does to the plane

The question. A matrix is a table of numbers. Why do people say it "rotates" or "stretches" things, and how can you see what a given table does without computing anything?

A matrix is a rectangular table of numbers; an \(m \times n\) matrix has \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns, and \(A_{ij}\) is the entry in row \(i\), column \(j\) (row first, always). Multiplying a matrix by a vector, \(Ax\), takes a vector with \(n\) entries and returns one with \(m\) entries. So a matrix is a map (a function) from \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to \(\mathbb{R}^m\), and a very special kind: it is linear, meaning

\[ A(x + y) = Ax + Ay, \qquad A(c\,x) = c\,(Ax) \]

In plain words: the map of a sum is the sum of the maps, and stretching the input stretches the output by the same factor. Straight lines stay straight, the origin stays put, and evenly spaced grid lines stay evenly spaced.

The columns tell you everything

Write \(x = (x_1, x_2) = x_1 e_1 + x_2 e_2\), where \(e_1 = (1, 0)\) and \(e_2 = (0, 1)\) are the unit arrows along the axes (the standard basis). Linearity gives \(Ax = x_1 (Ae_1) + x_2 (Ae_2)\), and \(Ae_1\) is just the first column of \(A\), \(Ae_2\) the second. So:

The one picture to keep
The columns of a matrix are where the axis arrows land. Know where \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) go, and you know where every point goes: the whole grid follows them.

The running 2x2 matrix on this page (it comes from the old page, every number checked) is

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \]

Its first column \((3, 4)\) is where \(e_1\) lands and its second column \((0, 5)\) is where \(e_2\) lands. The unit square becomes the parallelogram with sides \((3, 4)\) and \((0, 5)\), and the unit circle becomes an ellipse.

The determinant: how much area scales

The determinant of a 2x2 matrix is

\[ \det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc \]

and it is the signed factor by which the map scales area: the unit square (area 1) becomes a parallelogram of area \(|\det A|\). For \(A\): \(3 \cdot 5 - 0 \cdot 4 = 15\). A negative determinant means the map also flips orientation (a mirror image, like the reflection preset). A determinant of 0 means the plane is squashed onto a line or a point (the projection preset): area 0, and the map cannot be undone, because many inputs land on the same output. In \(n\) dimensions the determinant scales volume the same way.

Non-square matrices change the dimension. The tiny model's \(W\) is \(3 \times 2\): it takes the 2-number input to 3 scores, so it maps the plane into a flat plane sitting inside 3-D space. Its columns \((1, 0, 1)\) and \((0, 1, -2)\) are where \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) land. A \(2 \times 3\) matrix goes the other way and must squash a whole direction of 3-D space to zero (section 6 says exactly which one).

What it is used for in ML
Every dense layer, every attention projection and every embedding lookup is a linear map; a network is linear maps alternating with simple non-linear functions (activations), because a stack of purely linear maps would collapse into one linear map (section 3). Thinking of a weight matrix as "what it does to directions" is what makes rank, conditioning and low-rank methods make sense.

14. Low-rank structure in ML: what is really low rank, and what is not

The question. LoRA, MLA, GaLore and many compression papers all say "low rank". Low rank of what, exactly? Pretrained weights, the updates fine-tuning makes, the gradients, or the cached keys and values? The answers differ, and real numbers settle them.

Pretrained weights: concentrated, but not low rank

The old page said trained weight matrices "are often effectively low rank". Measured on GPT-2 small (the released weights, 2,048 tokens of WikiText-2 test text, script in this page's source folder), that does not hold in the sense that matters for compression:

k90: singular directions needed to keep 90% of the squared Frobenius norm, out of 768; "random" is a Gaussian matrix of the same shape and norm. Output error: relative error of the layer's output on the real inputs that reach it, \(\lVert X(W - W_k) \rVert_F / \lVert XW \rVert_F\). Loss: GPT-2's next-token loss on the same text with only that one matrix replaced by its rank-64 truncation (unchanged model: nats).

Three readings. First, every real spectrum is more concentrated than random (k90 near 300 against 391 for the attention matrices), so there is structure. Second, it is nowhere near rank 64: keeping 64 of 768 directions leaves a relative output error of 42% for the MLP input matrix and 70% to 86% for the other five. Third, how much a matrix matters is not how compressible it is: cutting the MLP input matrix to rank 64 raises the loss by 0.72 nats, cutting the key matrix by 0.20. Truncating every attention and MLP matrix in all 12 layers to 90% of its rank, which saves no parameters at all, already raises the loss from 3.17 to 3.91; at 50% of the rank it is 8.50, close to the 10.8 of a uniform guess over 50,257 tokens (). Real weights carry information in their small singular values too. The {{Low-rank lab|#t-lowrank}} tab lets you pick the matrix and \(k\).

Fine-tuning updates: genuinely low rank

LoRA (Hu et al. 2021) does not compress the weights; it constrains the change. The layer computes \(W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A x\) with the pretrained \(W_0\) frozen, \(B\) of shape \(d \times r\), \(A\) of shape \(r \times k\) and \(r \ll \min(d, k)\). \(BA\) is a sum of \(r\) outer products, so its rank is at most \(r\). \(B\) starts at zero, so training starts exactly at the pretrained model, and after training \(BA\) can be merged into \(W_0\) for free inference. For \(d = k = 4096\), \(r = 8\): \(2 \times 4096 \times 8 = 65{,}536\) trainable numbers instead of \(16{,}777{,}216\), 0.39%.

Correction carried from the root
The old page said "ranks as low as 1 to 8 were competitive on GPT-3". The paper's words: "a rank as small as one suffices for adapting both \(W_q\) and \(W_v\)" on GPT-3, with Table 6 testing \(r = 1, 2, 4, 8, 64\); and the low "intrinsic rank" of the update is the authors' hypothesis, which they then test ({{LoRA, arXiv 2106.09685, sections 4 and 7|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685}}). The idea goes back to Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer and Gupta: "by optimizing only 200 trainable parameters randomly projected back into the full space, we can tune a RoBERTa model to achieve 90% of the full parameter performance levels on MRPC" ({{Intrinsic dimensionality, arXiv 2012.13255|https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13255}}).

Two real adapters, measured. Two public GPT-2 LoRA adapters on Hugging Face target the attention input matrix c_attn (\(768 \to 2304\)) in all 12 layers: monsterapi/gpt2_alpaca-lora (\(r = 8\), \(\alpha = 16\), trained on Alpaca) and harigovind511/GPT2-Guanaco-LoRA (\(r = 32\), \(\alpha = 8\), a QLoRA run on a 4-bit base). Rebuilding each \(\Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r} BA\) and taking its SVD:

  • Exactly \(r\) singular values are nonzero (the next is about \(10^{-14}\), rounding), as the algebra says.
  • Inside its budget the update is far lower rank still: the largest singular value alone carries a median of \(\lVert \Delta W \rVert_F^2\) for the \(r = 8\) adapter and for the \(r = 32\) one. Most of the rank budget is unused.
  • The update is small: \(\lVert \Delta W \rVert_F / \lVert W_0 \rVert_F\) is 2% to 8% (Alpaca) and 1.5% to 4% (Guanaco), growing with depth.
  • The paper's "amplification factor" (section 7.3 and appendix H.4: \(\lVert \Delta W_q \rVert_F / \lVert U^\top W_q V^\top \rVert_F\), with \(U, V\) the top-\(r\) singular directions of \(\Delta W_q\)) was "\(21.5 \approx 6.91/0.32\) for \(r = 4\)" on GPT-3's layer 48, and "only around 2" at \(r = 64\). On these adapters it is 0.6 to 3.4 (Alpaca) and 0.2 to 0.6 (Guanaco). It does not reproduce at this scale; GPT-2 small, different tasks, ranks and scales (\(\alpha/r\) = 2 and 0.25) are all different from the paper's setting, so this is a measurement, not a refutation. The paper's other finding does hold here: \(W_q\) projected on the update's directions is larger than on random directions (median ratio 1.6 and 1.3), so the update is correlated with the pretrained weight.

The {{LoRA paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81018f15edd19fa5c28a}} trains LoRA live on a toy where the true update is known, and {{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba08eef56b3e3b4eb}} covers the variants (DoRA, rsLoRA, PiSSA) and current practice.

Cached keys and values: MLA

Multi-head latent attention (DeepSeek-V2 and V3) caches, per token and layer, one compressed vector \(c = W^{DKV} h\) of \(d_c = 512\) numbers (plus a 64-number key for the rotary position part), and rebuilds every head's keys and values from it with up-projections \(k = W^{UK} c\), \(v = W^{UV} c\). The map from hidden state to keys is the product \(W^{UK} W^{DKV}\), a factorisation through a 512-wide bottleneck: exactly the \(BA\) shape. Because \(q^\top k = (W^Q h)^\top W^{UK} c = h^\top \big((W^Q)^\top W^{UK}\big) c\), the up-projection can be absorbed into the query side and the full keys never built. With \(n_h = 128\) heads of \(d_h = 128\), the cache is \(512 + 64 = 576\) numbers per token per layer against \(2 \times 128 \times 128 = 32{,}768\) for standard multi-head attention. The animated version is on {{DeepSeek|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}.

Gradients: GaLore

GaLore keeps full-rank weights but notices that the gradient of a weight matrix is approximately low rank (the root shows one example's gradient is exactly rank 1; a batch sums a few such outer products). Every \(T\) steps it takes the SVD of the current gradient \(G\) (\(m \times n\), \(m \le n\)), keeps the top \(r\) left singular vectors as a projector \(P\) (\(m \times r\)), runs Adam on the small matrix \(P^\top G\) (\(r \times n\)), and maps the update back with \(P\). Adam's two moment matrices shrink from \(2mn\) numbers to \(2nr\), plus \(mr\) for \(P\). The paper uses \(T = 200\) and scale \(\alpha = 0.25\) for its LLaMA pretraining runs and reports that it "reduces memory usage by up to 65.5% in optimizer states" ({{GaLore, Zhao et al. 2024, arXiv 2403.03507|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03507}}). For one \(4096 \times 4096\) matrix at \(r = 128\): 33,554,432 moment numbers become \(4096 \times 128 + 2 \times 4096 \times 128 = 1{,}572{,}864\).

The pattern
Low rank is a fact about changes and products (fine-tuning updates, gradients, the query-key product of one head, the KV bottleneck a model is trained with), much more than about pretrained weight matrices themselves. Methods that impose low rank during training (LoRA, MLA, GaLore) work; compressing a finished model by truncated SVD costs accuracy fast.

10. The SVD: every matrix is rotate, stretch, rotate

Eigenvectors need a square matrix and may not exist. The singular value decomposition describes what any matrix does, including a \(768 \times 3072\) weight, and always exists: a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and another rotation. Predict first: does applying \(A\)'s three SVD factors one at a time end on the same ellipse as applying \(A\) in one step? Then step through it, and compare with the eigendecomposition on the same circle.

The same circle under A in one step, under its SVD, and under its eigendecomposition

Drawn to scale: the circle has radius 1, the ellipse's long axis is 6.708. Only the SVD keeps the two marked arrows at 90° through every step; the eigen route skews the grid on the way.

\[ A = U \Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^\top \]
\(V\)
an \(n \times n\) orthogonal matrix; its columns \(v_i\) (the right singular vectors) are the input directions. \(V^\top\) rotates \(v_i\) onto the \(i\)-th axis.
\(\Sigma\)
an \(m \times n\) matrix, zero except for the singular values \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\) on its diagonal: the stretch along each axis.
\(U\)
an \(m \times m\) orthogonal matrix; its columns \(u_i\) (the left singular vectors) are the output directions.
\(r\)
the rank: the number of nonzero singular values.

There are orthonormal input directions \(v_i\) that the matrix sends to orthogonal output directions, \(A v_i = \sigma_i u_i\). The sum form reads the matrix as \(r\) rank-1 layers, largest first. Every matrix, of any shape, has an SVD.

How to get it. \(A^\top A = V \Sigma^\top \Sigma V^\top\), so the right singular vectors are the eigenvectors of the symmetric PSD matrix \(A^\top A\), the singular values are the square roots of its eigenvalues, and \(u_i = A v_i / \sigma_i\). For \(A\), \(A^\top A\) has eigenvalues 45 and 5, so \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} = 6.708\) and \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt{5} = 2.236\) (worked in the closed block below).

Eigenvalues are not singular values
\(A\)'s eigenvalues are 3 and 5, its singular values 6.708 and 2.236: a non-symmetric matrix can stretch some direction (by 6.708) more than it scales any eigenvector (at most 5), and its eigenvectors need not be orthogonal. For the symmetric PSD \(S = W^\top W\) the two coincide: eigenvalues and singular values are both 6 and 1, and \(U = V\), so the second rotation exactly undoes the first (switch to S above). Stretch, norms and conditioning are singular-value questions.

The SVD answers the earlier sections at once. Rank = number of nonzero \(\sigma_i\); effective rank = number of \(\sigma_i\) above a tolerance. Four subspaces: \(u_1..u_r\) span the column space, \(u_{r+1}..u_m\) the left null space, \(v_1..v_r\) the row space, \(v_{r+1}..v_n\) the null space. Norms: spectral \(\sigma_1\), Frobenius \(\sqrt{\sum \sigma_i^2}\), nuclear \(\sum \sigma_i\). Pseudo-inverse: \(A^{+} = V \Sigma^{+} U^\top\), with \(\Sigma^{+}\) inverting the nonzero \(\sigma_i\) and leaving zeros; \(A^{+} y\) is the least-squares solution of shortest length. Condition number: \(\kappa = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}\) (section 11).

Eckart-Young: truncation is arithmetic

Keep only the \(k\) largest layers, \(A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top\). The Eckart-Young(-Mirsky) theorem says no rank-\(k\) matrix is closer to \(A\), in either the spectral or the Frobenius norm, and the errors are exactly

\[ \lVert A - A_k \rVert_2 = \sigma_{k+1}, \qquad \lVert A - A_k \rVert_F = \sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2 + \sigma_{k+2}^2 + \dots + \sigma_r^2} \]

The singular values tell you, before you compress anything, exactly what you will lose: a spectrum that falls fast compresses well; a flat one does not. Storing \(A_k\) for an \(m \times n\) matrix costs \(k(m + n)\) numbers (fold each \(\sigma_i\) into \(u_i\)) instead of \(mn\): for \(768 \times 768\) at \(k = 64\), 98,304 instead of 589,824.

For \(A\), the best rank-1 approximation misses by exactly \(\sigma_2 = 2.236\) in both norms. On a real matrix, the query matrix of GPT-2 small's layer 5 (768 by 768) needs of its 768 singular directions to keep 90% of its squared Frobenius norm; a random matrix of the same shape needs . Real weights are more concentrated than noise, but far from low rank; section 12 shows what truncation does to the model's loss.

PCA is the SVD of centred data

Stack \(n\) examples as the rows of \(X\), subtract each column's mean, and the covariance is \(C = \frac{1}{n} X^\top X\). Its eigenvectors (equivalently the right singular vectors of \(X\)) are the principal directions, and the variance along the \(i\)-th is \(\sigma_i^2/n\). For the centred points \((2, 1), (-2, -1), (1, 1), (-1, -1)\): \(C = \begin{pmatrix} 2.5 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues 3.427 and 0.073, and the first principal direction \((0.851, 0.526)\) carries \(3.427/3.5 = 97.9\%\) of the variance: these points are almost a line. Centre first: without it the first "principal component" mostly points at the mean.

What it is used for in ML
Low-rank compression and LoRA initialisations ({{PiSSA|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02948}} starts LoRA from the top singular directions); PCA and whitening; the pseudo-inverse; spectral norms and condition numbers; Muon's orthogonalised update, which replaces \(U\Sigma V^\top\) by \(UV^\top\) (section 11); and analysing what a trained weight or update does (section 12).

Signs chosen so both factors are rotations (old page, checked). \(A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25 \end{pmatrix}\). Eigenvalues: \((25 - \lambda)^2 = 400\), so \(\lambda = 45\) or 5. Eigenvectors: \(v_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt{2}\), \(v_2 = (-1, 1)/\sqrt{2}\). Then \(u_1 = A v_1 / \sigma_1 = (3, 9)/(\sqrt{2}\sqrt{45}) = (1, 3)/\sqrt{10}\) and \(u_2 = A v_2/\sigma_2 = (-3, 1)/(\sqrt{2}\sqrt{5}) = (-3, 1)/\sqrt{10}\); orthogonal, \(-3 + 3 = 0\). So \(V^\top\) is a rotation by \(-45°\) (it takes \(v_1\) to the horizontal axis), \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(6.708, 2.236)\) stretches, and \(U\) is a rotation by \(71.57°\) (the angle of \((1, 3)\)). Both have determinant \(+1\). Checks: \(|\det A| = 15 = 6.708 \times 2.236\); \(\lVert A \rVert_F^2 = 50 = 45 + 5\).

\[A_1 = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top = \sqrt{45} \cdot \frac{(1, 3)}{\sqrt{10}} \cdot \frac{(1, 1)}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{3}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \]

The error \(A - A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\) has spectral and Frobenius norm \(\sqrt{2.25 \cdot 2 + 0.25 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{5} = 2.236 = \sigma_2\), as the theorem says (the two norms agree because the error has rank 1); \(A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & 1.5 \\ 4.5 & 4.5 \end{pmatrix}\).

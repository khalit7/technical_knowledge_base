7. Norms: how big is a vector, a matrix, an update

The question. Weight decay, gradient clipping, spectral normalisation and "the update was too large" all measure size. Size in what sense? There are several, and the choice decides what a regulariser pushes towards.

Vector norms

A norm is a length: it is zero only for the zero vector, scales with the vector (\(\lVert c x \rVert = |c| \lVert x \rVert\)) and obeys the triangle inequality (\(\lVert x + y \rVert \le \lVert x \rVert + \lVert y \rVert\)). Three are common:

\[ \lVert x \rVert_1 = \sum_i |x_i|, \qquad \lVert x \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i x_i^2}, \qquad \lVert x \rVert_\infty = \max_i |x_i| \]
The numbers
Tiny model input \(x = (2, 1)\): \(\lVert x \rVert_1 = 3\), \(\lVert x \rVert_2 = 2.236\), \(\lVert x \rVert_\infty = 2\). Old page, checked: \((3, -4)\) gives 7, 5 and 4. Always \(\lVert x \rVert_\infty \le \lVert x \rVert_2 \le \lVert x \rVert_1\).

The unit "circle" of each norm: every point at distance 1 from the origin. L1 is a diamond with corners on the axes, L2 the round circle, L-infinity a square. The dot is where a growing ball first touches a straight line of equal loss (the dashed line): for L1 at a corner, where one coordinate is exactly 0.

Why L1 gives sparsity. Minimising a loss subject to \(\lVert w \rVert_1 \le c\) means growing the diamond until it first touches a contour of the loss. A diamond is pointed at the axes, so the first contact is usually at a corner, where some coordinates are exactly zero. The round L2 ball has no corners: it shrinks every coordinate a little and zeroes none. That is lasso against ridge regression (L1 and L2 penalties on a linear model; {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}} owns the full story).

Matrix norms

  • Frobenius: treat the matrix as one long vector, \(\lVert A \rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{ij} A_{ij}^2}\). Weight decay penalises \(\lVert W \rVert_F^2\).
  • Spectral (operator 2-norm): the largest factor by which the map can stretch any vector, \(\lVert A \rVert_2 = \max_{x \ne 0} \lVert Ax \rVert_2 / \lVert x \rVert_2 = \sigma_{\max}(A)\), the largest singular value (section 12).
  • Nuclear: the sum of the singular values, \(\lVert A \rVert_* = \sum_i \sigma_i\); the closest convex stand-in for rank, used when a method wants low rank but needs a smooth objective.

All three are functions of the singular values: Frobenius is \(\sqrt{\sum \sigma_i^2}\), spectral is \(\sigma_1\), nuclear is \(\sum \sigma_i\).

The numbers

For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) (singular values \(\sqrt{45} = 6.708\) and \(\sqrt{5} = 2.236\), derived in section 12): Frobenius \(\sqrt{9 + 0 + 16 + 25} = \sqrt{50} = 7.071 = \sqrt{45 + 5}\); spectral 6.708; nuclear \(6.708 + 2.236 = 8.944\).

For the tiny model's \(W\) (singular values \(\sqrt{6} = 2.449\) and 1): Frobenius \(\sqrt{7} = 2.646\), spectral 2.449, nuclear 3.449. For its rank-1 gradient \(g x^\top\) all three coincide: \(\lVert g \rVert \lVert x \rVert = 1.153 \times 2.236 = 2.579\), because a rank-1 matrix has a single nonzero singular value.

Why the spectral norm is a Lipschitz constant. \(\lVert Ax - Ay \rVert = \lVert A(x - y) \rVert \le \sigma_{\max} \lVert x - y \rVert\): no two inputs can end up further apart than \(\sigma_{\max}\) times their distance. Spectral normalisation divides a layer's weights by \(\sigma_{\max}\) to make that factor 1 ({{Miyato et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.05957}}), and it is why the size of a layer's largest singular value controls how much it can amplify a perturbation.

What it is used for in ML
Gradient clipping rescales the whole gradient when its L2 norm (over all parameters together) exceeds a threshold; weight decay shrinks the Frobenius norm; adversarial robustness budgets are usually L-infinity balls; spectral norms bound how much a layer amplifies; optimisers such as Muon choose the update with the best decrease per unit of spectral norm (section 13).

8. Orthogonality, projections and QR

The question. Why are rotations "nice" for numerics, and how do you find the point of a subspace closest to a vector, which is what every least-squares fit and every PCA does?

Orthonormal vectors and orthogonal matrices

Vectors are orthonormal when each has length 1 and every pair is orthogonal. A square matrix \(Q\) whose columns are orthonormal is an orthogonal matrix, and that fact fits in one equation, \(Q^\top Q = I\) (entry \((i, j)\) of \(Q^\top Q\) is column \(i\) dotted with column \(j\): 1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere). So its inverse is its transpose, free to compute. Orthogonal matrices are rotations and reflections: they keep every length and angle, \(\lVert Qx \rVert^2 = x^\top Q^\top Q x = x^\top x\). Multiplying by them never amplifies rounding errors, which is why stable algorithms are built from them. The rotation by angle \(\theta\) is \(\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}\); RoPE, the position encoding of most current LLMs, rotates pairs of query and key coordinates by such matrices.

Projection onto a subspace

Section 1 projected onto one direction. To project \(y\) onto the column space of a matrix \(X\) (all combinations \(Xw\)), look for the \(w\) whose leftover \(y - Xw\) is orthogonal to every column of \(X\):

\[ X^\top (y - Xw) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; X^\top X\, w = X^\top y \;\Rightarrow\; \hat y = Xw = \underbrace{X (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top}_{P}\, y \]

\(P\) is the projection matrix. Projecting twice changes nothing (\(P^2 = P\)) and \(P\) is symmetric. Why is the orthogonal leftover the closest point? For any other point \(Xw'\) of the subspace, \(y - Xw' = (y - Xw) + X(w - w')\), the two pieces are orthogonal, so by Pythagoras

\[\lVert y - Xw' \rVert^2 = \lVert y - Xw \rVert^2 + \lVert X(w - w') \rVert^2 \ge \lVert y - Xw \rVert^2\]

.

Gram-Schmidt and the QR decomposition

Given independent columns, Gram-Schmidt makes them orthonormal one at a time: subtract from each column its projections onto the ones already done, then scale to length 1. Recording the coefficients gives \(X = QR\): \(Q\) has orthonormal columns and \(R\) is upper triangular (zeros below the diagonal).

Worked: QR of the least-squares matrix in section 9

\(X\) has columns \(a_1 = (1, 1, 1)\) and \(a_2 = (0, 1, 2)\). Step 1: \(q_1 = a_1 / \lVert a_1 \rVert = (1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3}\), and \(r_{11} = \sqrt{3} = 1.732\). Step 2: the coefficient of \(a_2\) along \(q_1\) is \(r_{12} = q_1 \cdot a_2 = 3/\sqrt{3} = 1.732\); subtract it: \(a_2 - r_{12} q_1 = (0, 1, 2) - (1, 1, 1) = (-1, 0, 1)\). Its length is \(r_{22} = \sqrt{2} = 1.414\), so \(q_2 = (-1, 0, 1)/\sqrt{2}\). So

\[ R = \begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{3} & \sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{check: } \sqrt{3}\, q_1 + \sqrt{2}\, q_2 = (1,1,1) + (-1,0,1) = (0,1,2) = a_2 \]

Library routines (LAPACK, behind NumPy and PyTorch) compute QR with Householder reflections rather than textbook Gram-Schmidt, because classical Gram-Schmidt loses orthogonality in floating point when columns are nearly parallel (Trefethen and Bau, lectures 8 to 10).

What it is used for in ML
Orthogonal initialisation keeps signal norms constant through deep linear stacks; RoPE is a rotation; least squares, linear probes and PCA are projections; QR is the stable way to solve least squares and to orthonormalise a basis (randomized SVD routines, such as PyTorch's torch.svd_lowrank, orthonormalise with QR at every pass).

9. Least squares: the best fit is a projection

The question. Three points, a straight line that cannot pass through all of them. Which line is best, and why does the answer come out of a matrix equation?

Fit \(\hat y = w_0 + w_1 t\) to the points \((t, y) = (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2)\) (the old page's example, checked). Each point is one equation, three equations for two unknowns, so there is no exact solution. Stack them: a column of ones for the intercept and a column of \(t\) values,

\[ X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad y = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \text{minimise } \lVert y - Xw \rVert_2^2 \]

The predictions \(Xw\) range over the column space of \(X\), a plane in \(\mathbb{R}^3\); \(y\) is not on it. The closest point is the projection of section 8, so the best \(w\) solves the normal equations \(X^\top X w = X^\top y\). No calculus is needed: the derivation is the orthogonality argument above. (The same equations come from setting the gradient \(2X^\top(Xw - y)\) to zero; deriving such gradients is on {{Matrix calculus and backprop|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}}.)

The numbers

\(X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) (3 points, sum of \(t\) is 3, sum of \(t^2\) is 5) and \(X^\top y = (5, 6)\). The 2x2 inverse is \(\frac{1}{\det}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}\) with \(\det = 15 - 9 = 6\), so \(w = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & -3 \\ -3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = (1.167, 0.5)\).

Predictions \(1.167, 1.667, 2.167\); residuals \(y - \hat y = (-0.167, 0.333, -0.167)\). They sum to 0 (orthogonal to the column of ones) and \(0 \cdot (-0.167) + 1 \cdot 0.333 + 2 \cdot (-0.167) = 0\) (orthogonal to the \(t\) column), exactly as \(X^\top(y - Xw) = 0\) demands.

The same answer by QR, without forming \(X^\top X\): with \(X = QR\) the normal equations become \(R w = Q^\top y\). \(Q^\top y = (5/\sqrt{3}, 1/\sqrt{2}) = (2.887, 0.707)\). Back-substitute from the bottom row: \(\sqrt{2}\, w_1 = 1/\sqrt{2}\) gives \(w_1 = 0.5\); \(\sqrt{3}\,(w_0 + w_1) = 5/\sqrt{3}\) gives \(w_0 = 5/3 - 0.5 = 1.167\).

Why bother with QR? Forming \(X^\top X\) squares the condition number (section 13): here \(\kappa(X) = 2.92\) but \(\kappa(X^\top X) = 8.55\), the square of 2.924. Harmless at this size, ruinous for an ill-conditioned problem in float32, as section 13 shows on a real run.

Ridge regression adds \(\lambda \lVert w \rVert^2\) to the objective, which changes the equations to \((X^\top X + \lambda I) w = X^\top y\). Adding \(\lambda I\) adds \(\lambda\) to every eigenvalue of \(X^\top X\) (7.162 and 0.838 here), so the condition number drops from \(7.162 / 0.838 = 8.55\) to \(8.162 / 1.838 = 4.44\) at \(\lambda = 1\): regularisation is also a numerical fix. The pseudo-inverse \(X^{+}\) (section 12) gives the least-squares solution even when \(X\) is rank-deficient, choosing the shortest \(w\) among the many that fit equally well.

What it is used for in ML
Linear regression and linear probes; the last layer of a network with squared loss; the closed-form ridge solution behind kernel methods; and the mental model for every projection: training a linear readout is projecting targets onto the span of the features.

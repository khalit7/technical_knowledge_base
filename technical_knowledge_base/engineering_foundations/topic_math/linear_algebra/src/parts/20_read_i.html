12. The SVD: every matrix is rotate, stretch, rotate

The question. Eigenvectors need a square matrix and may not exist. Is there a description of what any matrix does, including a \(768 \times 3072\) weight, that always works?

The picture: watch it happen

Take the unit circle and the grid of the plane, apply \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\), and the circle becomes an ellipse. The singular value decomposition says this always happens in three simple moves: a rotation, a stretch along the axes, and another rotation. Step through the same circle under \(A\) in one go, then under its three SVD factors. Drawn to scale: the circle has radius 1, the ellipse's long axis is 6.708.

The words, then the formula

\[ A = U \Sigma V^\top = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\, u_i v_i^\top \]
\(V\)
an \(n \times n\) orthogonal matrix; its columns \(v_i\) (the right singular vectors) are the input directions. \(V^\top\) rotates \(v_i\) onto the \(i\)-th axis.
\(\Sigma\)
an \(m \times n\) matrix, zero except for the singular values \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\) on its diagonal: the stretch along each axis.
\(U\)
an \(m \times m\) orthogonal matrix; its columns \(u_i\) (the left singular vectors) are the output directions.
\(r\)
the rank: the number of nonzero singular values.

In plain words: there are orthonormal input directions \(v_i\) that the matrix sends to orthogonal output directions, \(A v_i = \sigma_i u_i\). The sum form reads the matrix as \(r\) rank-1 layers, largest first. Every matrix, of any shape, has an SVD.

Computing it by hand

Since \(A^\top A = V \Sigma^\top U^\top U \Sigma V^\top = V \Sigma^\top \Sigma V^\top\), the right singular vectors are the eigenvectors of the symmetric PSD matrix \(A^\top A\), and the singular values are the square roots of its eigenvalues. Then \(u_i = A v_i / \sigma_i\).

Worked on \(A\) (old page, checked; signs chosen so both factors are rotations)

\(A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25 \end{pmatrix}\). Eigenvalues: \((25 - \lambda)^2 = 400\), so \(\lambda = 45\) or 5, and \(\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} = 6.708\), \(\sigma_2 = \sqrt{5} = 2.236\). Eigenvectors: \(v_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt{2}\), \(v_2 = (-1, 1)/\sqrt{2}\).

\(u_1 = A v_1 / \sigma_1 = (3, 9)/(\sqrt{2}\sqrt{45}) = (1, 3)/\sqrt{10}\) and

\[u_2 = A v_2/\sigma_2 = (-3, 1)/(\sqrt{2}\sqrt{5}) = (-3, 1)/\sqrt{10}\]

; orthogonal, \(-3 + 3 = 0\).

So \(V^\top\) is a rotation by \(-45°\) (it takes \(v_1\) to the horizontal axis), \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(6.708, 2.236)\) stretches, and \(U\) is a rotation by \(71.57°\) (the angle of \((1, 3)\)). Both have determinant \(+1\). Checks: \(|\det A| = 15 = 6.708 \times 2.236\); \(\lVert A \rVert_F^2 = 50 = 45 + 5\).

Eigenvalues are not singular values. \(A\)'s eigenvalues are 3 and 5, its singular values 6.708 and 2.236: a non-symmetric matrix can stretch some direction (by 6.708) more than it scales any eigenvector (at most 5). For the symmetric PSD \(S = W^\top W\) the two coincide: eigenvalues and singular values are both 6 and 1, and \(U = V\), so the second rotation exactly undoes the first (switch to S in the animation).

The SVD answers the earlier sections at once

  • Rank = number of nonzero \(\sigma_i\); effective rank = number of \(\sigma_i\) above a tolerance.
  • Four subspaces: \(u_1..u_r\) span the column space, \(u_{r+1}..u_m\) the left null space, \(v_1..v_r\) the row space, \(v_{r+1}..v_n\) the null space.
  • Norms: spectral \(\sigma_1\), Frobenius \(\sqrt{\sum \sigma_i^2}\), nuclear \(\sum \sigma_i\).
  • Pseudo-inverse: \(A^{+} = V \Sigma^{+} U^\top\), with \(\Sigma^{+}\) inverting the nonzero \(\sigma_i\) and leaving zeros; \(A^{+} y\) is the least-squares solution of shortest length.
  • Condition number: \(\kappa = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}\) (section 13).

Eckart-Young: truncation is arithmetic

Keep only the \(k\) largest layers, \(A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sigma_i u_i v_i^\top\). The Eckart-Young(-Mirsky) theorem says no rank-\(k\) matrix is closer to \(A\), in either the spectral or the Frobenius norm, and the errors are exactly

\[ \lVert A - A_k \rVert_2 = \sigma_{k+1}, \qquad \lVert A - A_k \rVert_F = \sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2 + \sigma_{k+2}^2 + \dots + \sigma_r^2} \]

In plain words: the singular values tell you, before you compress anything, exactly what you will lose. A spectrum that falls fast compresses well; a flat one does not. Storing \(A_k\) for an \(m \times n\) matrix costs \(k(m + n)\) numbers (fold each \(\sigma_i\) into \(u_i\)) instead of \(mn\): for \(768 \times 768\) at \(k = 64\), 98,304 instead of 589,824.

Worked (old page, checked)
\[A_1 = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^\top = \sqrt{45} \cdot \frac{(1, 3)}{\sqrt{10}} \cdot \frac{(1, 1)}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{3}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 3 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & 1.5 \\ 4.5 & 4.5 \end{pmatrix}\]. The error \(A - A_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & -1.5 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\) has spectral and Frobenius norm \(\sqrt{2.25 \cdot 2 + 0.25 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{5} = 2.236 = \sigma_2\), as the theorem says (the two norms agree because the error has rank 1).

On a real matrix. The query matrix of GPT-2 small's layer 5 (768 by 768) needs of its 768 singular directions to keep 90% of its squared Frobenius norm; a random matrix of the same shape needs . Real weights are more concentrated than noise, but far from low rank. The {{Low-rank lab|#t-lowrank}} tab shows every spectrum and what truncation does to the model's loss.

PCA is the SVD of centred data

Stack \(n\) examples as the rows of \(X\), subtract each column's mean, and the covariance is \(C = \frac{1}{n} X^\top X\). Its eigenvectors (equivalently the right singular vectors of \(X\)) are the principal directions, and the variance along the \(i\)-th is \(\sigma_i^2/n\).

Worked: four centred points
Points \((2, 1), (-2, -1), (1, 1), (-1, -1)\) (each column already sums to 0). \(C = \frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix} 10 & 6 \\ 6 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2.5 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Trace 3.5, determinant \(2.5 - 2.25 = 0.25\), so \(\lambda = (3.5 \pm \sqrt{3.5^2 - 1})/2 = 3.427\) and 0.073. The first principal direction is \((0.851, 0.526)\) and carries \(3.427/3.5 = 97.9\%\) of the variance: these points are almost a line.
What it is used for in ML
Low-rank compression and LoRA initialisations ({{PiSSA|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02948}} starts LoRA from the top singular directions); PCA and whitening; the pseudo-inverse; spectral norms and condition numbers; Muon's orthogonalised update, which replaces \(U\Sigma V^\top\) by \(UV^\top\) (section 13); and analysing what a trained weight or update does (section 14).

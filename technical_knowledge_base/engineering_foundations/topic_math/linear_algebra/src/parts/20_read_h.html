10. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors: the directions a map does not turn

The question. Apply the same matrix over and over (a recurrent network, a power iteration, a long product of layers). What happens to a vector, and why does the answer depend on one or two special numbers?

The picture first

Most arrows change direction when a matrix acts on them. A few special ones only get longer or shorter (or flip). These are the eigenvectors, and the factor is the eigenvalue:

\[ A v = \lambda v, \qquad v \ne 0 \]

In plain words: along an eigenvector, the matrix is just multiplication by the number \(\lambda\). ("Eigen" is German for "own": the matrix's own directions.)

How to find them

\(Av = \lambda v\) means \((A - \lambda I) v = 0\) for a nonzero \(v\), so \(A - \lambda I\) has a nonzero null space, so it squashes area to zero, so its determinant is zero. That gives the characteristic equation:

\[ \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 \]
Worked: \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) (old page, checked)

\(\det \begin{pmatrix} 3 - \lambda & 0 \\ 4 & 5 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = (3 - \lambda)(5 - \lambda) - 0 \cdot 4 = 0\), so \(\lambda = 3\) or \(\lambda = 5\). (A triangular matrix always has its diagonal entries as eigenvalues.)

For \(\lambda = 3\): \((A - 3I)v = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix} v = 0\) needs \(4v_1 + 2v_2 = 0\), so \(v = (1, -2)\). Check: \(A(1, -2) = (3, 4 - 10) = (3, -6) = 3\,(1, -2)\). For \(\lambda = 5\): \(v = (0, 1)\); check \(A(0, 1) = (0, 5)\).

Two checks that always hold: the eigenvalues add up to the trace (sum of the diagonal), \(3 + 5 = 8 = 3 + 5\); they multiply to the determinant, \(3 \times 5 = 15\).

The two eigenvectors \((1, -2)\) and \((0, 1)\) are not orthogonal: their dot product is \(-2\). For a non-symmetric matrix that is normal; section 11 shows symmetric matrices are different.

Why eigenvalues rule repeated application

If \(v_1, v_2\) are eigenvectors that form a basis, write any input as \(x = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2\). Then \(A^k x = c_1 \lambda_1^k v_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^k v_2\): each component is multiplied by its own eigenvalue \(k\) times. The largest \(|\lambda|\) wins, and the size of the result grows like \(|\lambda_{\max}|^k\) when it is above 1 and shrinks to zero when every \(|\lambda| \lt 1\). Written for the whole matrix: \(A = P \Lambda P^{-1}\), with the eigenvectors as the columns of \(P\) and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of \(\Lambda\), and then \(A^k = P \Lambda^k P^{-1}\): powers of a matrix are powers of numbers in the eigenvector basis.

Power iteration, on \(A\)
Start from \((1, 1)\), multiply by \(A\) and rescale to length 1, repeatedly. The angle to the dominant eigenvector \((0, 1)\) (eigenvalue 5) goes 18.4, 9.0, 4.8, 2.7, 1.6, 0.9, 0.5, 0.3 degrees: it shrinks by about the ratio \(3/5 = 0.6\) each step, because the other component is multiplied by 3 while the dominant one is multiplied by 5. This is how the largest singular value of a layer is estimated cheaply in spectral normalisation (one step per training step).

Exploding and vanishing gradients. A plain recurrent network applies the same matrix at every time step, and the gradient flowing back through \(T\) steps is multiplied by (the transpose of) that matrix \(T\) times. If its largest eigenvalue magnitude is above 1 the gradient can explode; if all are below 1 it vanishes. Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio state it as: "it is sufficient for \(\lambda_1 \lt 1/\gamma\), where \(\lambda_1\) is the absolute value of the largest eigenvalue of the recurrent weight matrix", for the vanishing gradient problem to occur, with \(\gamma\) a bound on the activation's derivative; for linear units (\(\gamma = 1\)) that is the eigenvalue-below-1 picture above ({{Pascanu et al. 2013, section 2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.5063}}).

No real eigenvectors. A rotation by 30 degrees turns every arrow, so it has no real eigenvector: its characteristic equation \(\lambda^2 - 2\cos(30°)\lambda + 1 = 0\) has complex roots \(\cos 30° \pm i \sin 30°\). Some matrices have too few eigenvectors to form a basis (the shear \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) has only the direction \((1, 0)\)). Both are reasons the SVD, which always exists, is usually the better tool for "what does this matrix do".

What it is used for in ML
The Hessian's eigenvalues are the curvatures of the loss along its eigenvectors, and the largest one sets the largest stable learning rate (\(2/\lambda_{\max}\), on the root's Reading tab); PCA is the eigendecomposition of a covariance; PageRank and power iteration find a dominant eigenvector; stability of recurrent and state-space models is an eigenvalue condition.

11. Symmetric matrices, the spectral theorem, positive definiteness

The question. Covariance matrices and Hessians are symmetric. What does symmetry buy, and what does it mean for a matrix to be "positive"?

A matrix is symmetric when \(S = S^\top\) (mirror image across the diagonal). The tiny model gives one for free: \(S = W^\top W\), which is always symmetric because \((W^\top W)^\top = W^\top W\).

\[ S = W^\top W = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ -2 & 5 \end{pmatrix} \]

The spectral theorem

Spectral theorem
A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and a full set of orthonormal eigenvectors, so \(S = Q \Lambda Q^\top\) with \(Q\) orthogonal: rotate into the eigenvector axes, stretch along each axis, rotate back.
Why eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal (two lines)

Take \(S v_1 = \lambda_1 v_1\) and \(S v_2 = \lambda_2 v_2\) with \(\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2\). Compute \(v_1^\top S v_2\) two ways: as \(v_1^\top (\lambda_2 v_2) = \lambda_2\, v_1 \cdot v_2\), and, because \(S = S^\top\), as \((S v_1)^\top v_2 = \lambda_1\, v_1 \cdot v_2\). Subtract: \((\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\, v_1 \cdot v_2 = 0\), and since the eigenvalues differ, \(v_1 \cdot v_2 = 0\). (That the eigenvalues are real follows from the same trick with complex conjugates; the full theorem with repeated eigenvalues is in Strang's 18.06 lecture 25.)

Worked: \(S = W^\top W\)

\[\det(S - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)(5 - \lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 6)(\lambda - 1)\]

, so the eigenvalues are 6 and 1. For 6: \((S - 6I)v = \begin{pmatrix} -4 & -2 \\ -2 & -1 \end{pmatrix} v = 0\) gives \(v = (1, -2)/\sqrt{5}\). For 1: \(v = (2, 1)/\sqrt{5}\). Their dot product is \(2 - 2 = 0\): orthogonal, as promised.

A surprise: \((2, 1)\) is the tiny model's input \(x\). Check \(Sx = (4 - 2, -4 + 5) = (2, 1) = 1 \cdot x\). So \(\lVert Wx \rVert^2 = x^\top W^\top W x = x^\top S x = 1 \cdot \lVert x \rVert^2 = 5\), and indeed the scores \(z = (2, 1, 0)\) have \(\lVert z \rVert^2 = 4 + 1 + 0 = 5\). This input happens to sit on the direction \(W\) stretches least.

Quadratic forms and positive definiteness

A symmetric matrix defines a quadratic form, the number \(x^\top S x\). For \(S\) above, \(x^\top S x = 2a^2 - 4ab + 5b^2\) with \(x = (a, b)\). The matrix is positive definite (PD) when \(x^\top S x \gt 0\) for every nonzero \(x\), and positive semi-definite (PSD) when \(x^\top S x \ge 0\). Geometrically, the surface \(f(x) = x^\top S x\) is a bowl, and its level sets \(x^\top S x = 1\) are ellipses with semi-axes \(1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) along the eigenvectors (\(1/\sqrt{6} = 0.408\) and \(1\) here).

Four equivalent tests for a symmetric matrix being PD:

  1. every eigenvalue is positive (6 and 1: yes);
  2. every leading upper-left determinant is positive (\(2 \gt 0\), \(\det S = 10 - 4 = 6 \gt 0\): yes);
  3. it can be written \(B^\top B\) with \(B\) of full column rank (\(S = W^\top W\), and \(W\) has rank 2: yes), because \(x^\top B^\top B x = \lVert Bx \rVert^2\);
  4. the Cholesky factorisation succeeds (next).

\(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is PSD but not PD: eigenvalues 2 and 0, and \(x = (1, -1)\) gives \(x^\top S x = 0\).

Cholesky: the square root of a PD matrix

Every PD matrix factors as \(S = L L^\top\) with \(L\) lower triangular and positive diagonal. Solve for the entries one at a time from the top-left.

Worked (old page, checked)
\(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\): \(l_{11} = \sqrt{4} = 2\); \(l_{21} = 2 / l_{11} = 1\); \(l_{22} = \sqrt{3 - l_{21}^2} = \sqrt{2} = 1.414\). So \(L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & \sqrt{2} \end{pmatrix}\) and \(LL^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 + 2 \end{pmatrix}\). For \(S = W^\top W\): \(l_{11} = \sqrt{2}\), \(l_{21} = -2/\sqrt{2} = -\sqrt{2}\), \(l_{22} = \sqrt{5 - 2} = \sqrt{3}\). If the matrix is not PD, a step asks for the square root of a negative number and the factorisation fails, which is the cheapest PD test there is.

Sampling a correlated Gaussian. To draw from a Gaussian with mean \(\mu\) and covariance \(\Sigma\), draw independent standard normals \(z\) and output \(\mu + Lz\): its covariance is \(L\,I\,L^\top = \Sigma\). With \(z = (1, -1)\) and the \(L\) above, \(Lz = (2, 1 - 1.414) = (2, -0.414)\). It is the full-covariance form of the reparameterisation trick used to train VAEs ({{Variational inference and generative-model maths|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}).

What it is used for in ML
Covariances and Gram matrices (\(X^\top X\)) are PSD; the Hessian at a strict local minimum is PD; Gaussian processes and Kalman filters solve with Cholesky; second-order and natural-gradient methods need PD curvature matrices and add a multiple of \(I\) ("damping") to make them so.

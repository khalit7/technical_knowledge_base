3. Matrix multiplication, four ways, and what it costs

An \(m \times n\) matrix \(P\) times an \(n \times p\) matrix \(Q\) is the \(m \times p\) matrix \(PQ\): the inner sizes must match and vanish, the outer sizes survive. There are four ways to read the same product, and each is the natural reading in some part of ML. Take

\[ P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad Q = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}, \quad PQ = \begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix} \]
  1. Row times column (dot products). \((PQ)_{ij} = \sum_k P_{ik} Q_{kj}\), so \((PQ)_{11} = 1 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 7 = 19\). Attention scores read this way: every query (a row) against every key (a column).
  2. Column picture. Column \(j\) of \(PQ\) is a combination of the columns of \(P\) with weights from column \(j\) of \(Q\): \(5\,(1, 3) + 7\,(2, 4) = (19, 43)\). So every output of a layer lies in the span of the weight matrix's columns (section 5).
  3. Sum of outer products. An outer product \(u v^\top\) is a whole matrix with entries \(u_i v_j\), of rank 1 (every row is a multiple of \(v\)). \(PQ\) is the sum over \(k\) of (column \(k\) of \(P\)) times (row \(k\) of \(Q\)): \((1, 3)(5, 6) + (2, 4)(7, 8)\), that is \(\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 15 & 18 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 14 & 16 \\ 28 & 32 \end{pmatrix}\). This is the SVD's reading (section 10), LoRA's \(BA\) (a sum of \(r\) rank-1 layers) and the gradient of a layer (\(\nabla_W L = g\,x^\top\), the root's rank-1 gradient).
  4. Composition. \((PQ)x = P(Qx)\): apply \(Q\) first, then \(P\); with \(x = e_1\), \(Qx = (5, 7)\), then \(P(5, 7) = (19, 43)\). Read right to left. That is why order matters: \(QP = \begin{pmatrix} 23 & 34 \\ 31 & 46 \end{pmatrix} \ne PQ\), because "rotate then stretch" is not "stretch then rotate".

Four orders, the same 8 multiplications: the readings differ in grouping, not in work

Predict before you step: which reading builds the answer one whole matrix at a time? The same product in every mode, drawn to the same layout; it waits on step 1 until you press play or step.

Transpose. \(P^\top\) swaps rows and columns, \((P^\top)_{ij} = P_{ji}\). So \(a^\top b\) (a row times a column) is a dot product and \(a b^\top\) (a column times a row) an outer product. Transposing a product reverses the order, \((PQ)^\top = Q^\top P^\top\) (entry \((i, j)\) of \((PQ)^\top\) is row \(i\) of \(Q^\top\) dotted with column \(j\) of \(P^\top\)). That reversal is what makes backpropagation multiply by \(W^\top\) on the way back.

Why stacked linear layers collapse. Two linear layers in a row, \(W_2 (W_1 x)\), are one linear layer \((W_2 W_1) x\): however many you stack, without a non-linear function between them the network can only ever compute one matrix. The activation function (ReLU, GELU) between layers is what stops the collapse.

What a product costs. An \(m \times n\) times \(n \times p\) product does \(m \cdot n \cdot p\) multiplications and about as many additions, usually counted as \(2mnp\) floating-point operations (FLOPs). For one GPT-2 small layer of 768 by 768 applied to 1,024 tokens that is \(2 \times 1024 \times 768 \times 768 \approx 1.2\) billion FLOPs. Counting FLOPs for whole networks and for the backward pass belongs to {{Matrix calculus and backprop|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}}.

4. Shapes, layouts, broadcasting and einsum

Write the shape beside every symbol: \(W\) is \(3 \times 2\), \(x\) has 2 entries, so \(Wx\) has 3. A real model holds activations as a tensor of shape \((B, T, d)\): \(B\) sequences in the batch, \(T\) tokens each, \(d\) numbers per token; a layer acts on the last axis only, with the same matrix for every token.

Three layouts for the same layer. Papers treat one input as a column and write \(y = Wx + b\) with \(W\) of shape (outputs by inputs). Code stacks inputs as rows and computes \(Y = XW^\top + b\): PyTorch's nn.Linear stores weight with shape (out_features, in_features) and computes \(y = xA^\top + b\) ({{PyTorch nn.Linear docs|https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Linear.html}}). GPT-2's original code uses a third: its Conv1D stores the weight as (in, out) and computes \(xW + b\); Hugging Face's own docstring says it "works like a linear layer but the weights are transposed" ({{transformers pytorch_utils.py|https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/pytorch_utils.py}}). Section 12's GPT-2 measurements use this layout. The transpose between conventions is the same map; mixing them inside one derivation is the bug. Reading a model's code against its paper is mostly translating shapes and layouts: which axis is the batch, which is heads, whether the weight is stored (out, in) or (in, out).

Broadcasting. Adding a bias of shape \((d,)\) to activations of shape \((B, T, d)\) works because libraries broadcast: they line up shapes from the right, and an axis of size 1 (or a missing axis) is stretched to match. NumPy's rule, word for word: "two dimensions are compatible when they are equal, or one of them is 1" ({{NumPy broadcasting|https://numpy.org/doc/stable/user/basics.broadcasting.html}}). So \((3, 1) + (1, 2)\) gives \((3, 2)\), and \((B, T, d) + (d,)\) gives \((B, T, d)\). The same rule produces the commonest silent bug:

A (3, 1) column minus a (3,) row is a 3 x 3 table, not three differences

Predictions of shape (3, 1), \((1, 2, 3)\) as a column, minus targets of shape (3,), also \((1, 2, 3)\). Instead of three differences of 0 you get every prediction minus every target, \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\); its mean square is \(12/9 = 1.333\), though the predictions are perfect and the true mean squared error is 0. No error is raised. Fix: make the shapes equal (pred.squeeze(-1)) and assert shapes before every loss.

Einsum: every axis named

Einstein summation labels every axis with a letter. The rule: a letter that appears in the inputs but not in the output is summed over; every other letter is kept, in the order the output names them. torch.einsum, numpy.einsum and jax.numpy.einsum all read the same strings, and writing one is the cure when a shape bug survives ten minutes of staring.

Reading the shape off the string. In bhqd,bhkd->bhqk with \(b = 2\), \(h = 8\), \(q = k = 128\), \(d = 64\) (the old page's example), \(d\) is summed away and the output is \((2, 8, 128, 128)\). Counting the multiply-adds from the same string (one per combination of all distinct letters, here \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 128 \cdot 64\)) and seeing why attention is quadratic in length is done on {{Matrix calculus and backprop|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}}. "Splitting heads" in code is a reshape from \((B, T, 768)\) to \((B, T, 12, 64)\) and a transpose of two axes, no arithmetic; the einops library writes it as a named reshape that documents intent, rearrange(x, 'b t (h d) -> b h t d', h=12).

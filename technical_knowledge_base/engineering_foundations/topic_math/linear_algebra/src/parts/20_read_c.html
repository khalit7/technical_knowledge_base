1. Vectors: arrows and lists, length, dot product, cosine, projection

The question. A model holds a word, an image patch or a whole sentence as a list of numbers. What can you do with such a list, and why does multiplying two of them pair by pair and adding tell you how similar they are?

Two pictures of the same thing

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. The running example from {{Topic: math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} is the input of a tiny three-word language model, \(x = (2, 1)\). You can read it two ways, and you need both:

  • As a list: two numbers in a fixed order, \(x_1 = 2\) and \(x_2 = 1\). This is how code stores it (a tensor of shape (2,)). How many numbers it holds is its dimension, written \(x \in \mathbb{R}^2\) ("x is a member of the set of all lists of 2 real numbers").
  • As an arrow from the origin: two steps right, one step up. This is how you reason about it. Adding vectors is placing arrows head to tail, \((2,1) + (1,-2) = (3,-1)\); multiplying by a number (a scalar) stretches the arrow, \(3 \cdot (2,1) = (6,3)\).

A GPT-2 small token is a list of 768 numbers. Nobody can draw 768 axes, but every rule on this page works the same in 768 dimensions as in 2, which is why the 2-D pictures are worth learning.

The tiny model's input \(x = (2, 1)\) and its three word rows: cat \((1, 0)\), dog \((0, 1)\), sat \((1, -2)\). The dashed line is the projection of \(x\) onto the cat arrow. Every number comes from the root's model.

Length: the norm

The length of an arrow follows from Pythagoras' theorem. For \(x = (2, 1)\) the arrow is the long side of a right triangle with sides 2 and 1:

\[ \lVert x \rVert_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \dots + x_n^2} = \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236 \]
\(\lVert x \rVert_2\)
the length, or L2 norm, of \(x\). The subscript 2 says "squares, then square root"; other norms are in section 7.
\(x_i\)
entry \(i\) of \(x\).

In plain words: square every entry, add, take the square root. A unit vector has length 1; dividing any nonzero vector by its length gives the unit vector pointing the same way, \(x / \lVert x \rVert = (0.894, 0.447)\).

The dot product: two definitions that agree

The dot product (or inner product) of two vectors of the same dimension multiplies them entry by entry and adds:

\[ a \cdot b = a^\top b = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i b_i \]

The tiny model scores each word this way: \(x \cdot w_{\text{cat}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 0 = 2\), \(x \cdot w_{\text{dog}} = 1\), \(x \cdot w_{\text{sat}} = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-2) = 0\). The \(a^\top b\) notation reads the first vector as a row and the second as a column; it is the same number (the transpose \(\top\) is explained in section 3).

The second definition is geometric. If \(\theta\) is the angle between the arrows,

\[ a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta \]
Why the two definitions agree (the derivation, every step)

Draw \(a\), \(b\) and the third side of their triangle, \(a - b\). The law of cosines (Pythagoras for a triangle without a right angle) says \(\lVert a - b \rVert^2 = \lVert a \rVert^2 + \lVert b \rVert^2 - 2 \lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta\).

Now expand the left side with entries:

\[\lVert a - b \rVert^2 = \sum_i (a_i - b_i)^2 = \sum_i a_i^2 - 2\sum_i a_i b_i + \sum_i b_i^2 = \lVert a \rVert^2 - 2\, a \cdot b + \lVert b \rVert^2\]

.

The two expressions are equal; cancel \(\lVert a \rVert^2 + \lVert b \rVert^2\) from both sides and divide by \(-2\): \(a \cdot b = \lVert a \rVert \lVert b \rVert \cos\theta\). Check on \(x\) and \(w_{\text{cat}}\): \(2 = 2.236 \times 1 \times \cos\theta\), so \(\cos\theta = 0.894\), an angle of 26.6 degrees, which is the angle of the arrow \((2, 1)\) above the horizontal.

Cosine similarity

Divide out the lengths and only the direction is left:

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{a \cdot b}{\lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert} \]

In plain words: 1 means the arrows point the same way, 0 means at right angles (orthogonal), \(-1\) means opposite. Length does not matter.

The numbers
Tiny model: \(\cos(x, w_{\text{cat}}) = 2/\sqrt{5} = 0.894\), \(\cos(x, w_{\text{dog}}) = 1/\sqrt{5} = 0.447\), \(\cos(x, w_{\text{sat}}) = 0\): the right answer, sat, is exactly orthogonal to the input, which is why its score is 0 however long the arrows are. From the old page, checked: \(a = (1, 2, 2)\), \(b = (2, 1, 2)\) give \(a \cdot b = 8\), \(\lVert a \rVert = \lVert b \rVert = 3\), \(\cos\theta = 8/9 = 0.889\); and \((3, -4) \cdot (4, 3) = 12 - 12 = 0\), orthogonal.

Projection: how much of one vector lies along another

The projection of \(a\) onto the direction of \(b\) is the shadow \(a\) casts on the line through \(b\):

\[ \operatorname{proj}_b(a) = \frac{a \cdot b}{b \cdot b}\, b \]

The fraction is a number (how many copies of \(b\)); multiplying by \(b\) turns it back into an arrow. What is left over, \(a - \operatorname{proj}_b(a)\), is orthogonal to \(b\). Example: projecting \(x = (2, 1)\) onto \(b = (1, 1)\): \(x \cdot b = 3\), \(b \cdot b = 2\), so the projection is \(1.5\,(1, 1) = (1.5, 1.5)\) and the leftover is \((0.5, -0.5)\); check \((0.5, -0.5) \cdot (1, 1) = 0\). Projection is the idea behind least squares (section 9), PCA (section 12) and attention reading values (section 15).

What it is used for in ML
Embeddings are vectors and similar meanings point in similar directions; retrieval ranks documents by cosine similarity (usually after scaling every embedding to length 1, so the dot product is the cosine); attention scores are dot products of queries and keys; a logit is a dot product of the final hidden state with a row of the output matrix.

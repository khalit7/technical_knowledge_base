A computer stores a real number as a sign, a power of two and a fixed number of binary digits. So it can only hold numbers from a finite grid: the grid is dense near zero and sparse far away, every result is snapped to the nearest grid point, and the grid ends (past it lies infinity). Everything on this page follows from that picture: why \(0.1 + 0.2 \ne 0.3\), why softmax needs the max subtracted, why fp16 training needs loss scaling and bf16 does not, why adding a million numbers in 16 bits returns garbage, and why the same model gives different answers on different runs.

The one idea to keep
Every floating-point result is the true result times \((1 + \delta)\) with \(|\delta|\) at most half the format's epsilon, as long as nothing overflows or underflows. Trouble comes from three places only: leaving the range (overflow to infinity, underflow to zero), subtracting nearly equal numbers (cancellation), and piling up many small errors (long sums). Stable algorithms are rearrangements that avoid all three.

1. How a computer stores a number

The question. A memory cell holds only 0s and 1s. How do you get from a row of bits to \(-13\), to \(0.1\), to \(3 \times 10^{38}\)?

Binary: place values are powers of two

In decimal, \(413\) means \(4 \times 100 + 1 \times 10 + 3 \times 1\): each place is worth ten times the place to its right. In binary each place is worth twice the place to its right, and each digit (a bit) is 0 or 1. So

\[ 1101_2 = 1 \times 8 + 1 \times 4 + 0 \times 2 + 1 \times 1 = 13. \]

The subscript 2 says "read in base 2". Places to the right of the binary point are worth \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \dots\), so \(0.101_2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac18 = 0.625\). A fraction has a finite binary expansion only if its denominator is a power of two. \(\tfrac{1}{10}\) is not, so in binary it repeats forever, \(0.1 = 0.0\overline{0011}_2\), exactly as \(\tfrac13 = 0.333\ldots\) repeats in decimal. Any computer that stores a fixed number of bits must cut \(0.1\) off somewhere; that cut is the first rounding error you meet, and it is why \(0.1 + 0.2\) prints as 0.30000000000000004 in Python (measured).

Integers and two's complement

An unsigned 8-bit integer holds \(0\) to \(2^8 - 1 = 255\). For negative numbers, every modern machine uses two's complement: the top bit is worth \(-2^{7} = -128\) instead of \(+128\). So an 8-bit signed integer (int8) holds \(-128\) to \(127\), and

\[ -13 = 11110011_2 = -128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 2 + 1. \]

The trick that makes it popular: adding works with the same circuit as unsigned addition, and negating is "flip every bit, add 1" (\(13 = 00001101_2\), flipped \(11110010_2\), plus one \(11110011_2\)). The price is that integers wrap around: in int8, \(127 + 1 = -128\) (derived; NumPy does the same and warns). Integers are exact inside their range and evenly spaced, which is why quantised weights are stored as int8 or int4 codes with a separate scale (the {{Quantization and precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}} page).

Why integers are not enough: the floating point idea

ML needs both \(3 \times 10^{-8}\) (a typical small gradient) and \(5 \times 10^{4}\) (a large activation) in the same tensor. An evenly spaced grid fine enough for the first needs about \(10^{12}\) steps to reach the second, about 41 bits, and it would waste nearly all of them on precision nobody needs at large values. The fix is scientific notation in base 2: store a number as

\[ x = (-1)^{s} \times m \times 2^{E}, \qquad 1 \le m \lt 2 . \]

Read it as: a sign \(s\) (0 for plus, 1 for minus), a significand \(m\) (also called the mantissa: the digits, always between 1 and 2), and an exponent \(E\) (an integer: which power of two). Example: \(13 = 1.625 \times 2^{3}\) because \(1.625 \times 8 = 13\), and in binary \(1101_2 = 1.101_2 \times 2^{3}\): moving the binary point three places is multiplying by \(2^3\). The point "floats" to just after the first 1, hence floating point. With a fixed number of bits for \(m\), every number carries the same number of significant binary digits whatever its size: the gaps between neighbours grow in proportion to the number. That proportional grid is the whole design.

What it is used for in ML
Every weight, activation and gradient is a floating-point number; token ids, counts and quantised codes are integers. Integer wraparound appears in practice when an int32 index overflows past \(2^{31} - 1 \approx 2.1 \times 10^{9}\) elements, the size of a single large embedding or attention tensor.

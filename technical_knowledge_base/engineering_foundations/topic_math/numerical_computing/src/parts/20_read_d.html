3. Gaps between floats, machine epsilon and rounding

The question. How close together are neighbouring floats, how wrong can one rounding make a number, and which way does a tie go?

The grid doubles its spacing every power of two

Between two consecutive powers of two, \(2^E\) and \(2^{E+1}\) (one binade), a format with \(p\) significant bits has exactly \(2^{p-1}\) evenly spaced values. The spacing there is one ulp ("unit in the last place"):

\[ \operatorname{ulp}(x) = 2^{\,E - (p - 1)} \quad \text{for } 2^E \le |x| \lt 2^{E+1} . \]

In words: the gap is the value of the last stored bit. For float32 (\(p = 24\)) the gap is \(2^{-23}\) between 1 and 2, \(2^{-22}\) between 2 and 4, and \(2^{-13} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-4}\) between 1024 and 2048. So float32 can tell 1000.0 from 1000.0001 but not from 1000.00001. Below the smallest normal number the spacing stops shrinking: subnormals are evenly spaced. Machine epsilon and unit roundoff

Machine epsilon \(\varepsilon\) is the gap between 1 and the next float: \(\varepsilon = 2^{-(p-1)}\). The unit roundoff \(u = \varepsilon / 2 = 2^{-p}\) is the largest relative error of one rounding. Libraries report \(\varepsilon\) (torch.finfo(...).eps, numpy.finfo(...).eps, C's FLT_EPSILON); numerical analysis texts usually write \(u\) ({{Higham 2002|https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9780898718027}}, chapter 2). Mixing them up is a factor of two.

Why one rounding costs at most \(u\) relative error. Take \(x\) with \(2^E \le |x| \lt 2^{E+1}\). Neighbouring floats there are \(2^{E-p+1}\) apart, so the nearest one is at most half that away: \(|\operatorname{fl}(x) - x| \le 2^{E-p}\). Divide by \(|x| \ge 2^E\):
\[ \frac{|\operatorname{fl}(x) - x|}{|x|} \le \frac{2^{E-p}}{2^{E}} = 2^{-p} = u . \]
Here \(\operatorname{fl}(x)\) means "\(x\) rounded to the format". This holds for every \(x\) in the normal range. Check on the loss: float32 \(3.2 \times 10^{-8} \le u = 5.96 \times 10^{-8}\); float16 \(2.408203125\), error \(2.5 \times 10^{-4} \le 2^{-11} = 4.9 \times 10^{-4}\); bfloat16 \(2.40625\), error \(5.6 \times 10^{-4} \le 2^{-8} = 3.9 \times 10^{-3}\) (all derived from the bits above). In the subnormal range the bound fails: there the absolute error is at most half of the smallest subnormal, and the relative error can reach 100%.

A handy reading: \(p\) bits are about \(p \log_{10} 2 \approx 0.301\,p\) decimal digits. float64 carries about 16 digits, float32 about 7.2, float16 about 3.3 and bfloat16 about 2.4 (derived). bfloat16 cannot tell 256 from 257: both round to 256.

Round to nearest, ties to even

IEEE 754 requires every basic operation (\(+, -, \times, \div, \sqrt{\ }\), and the fused multiply-add) to return the exactly rounded result: compute as if with infinite precision, then round once. The default rounding mode is round to nearest, ties to even: pick the nearest float, and if the exact result sits exactly halfway between two, pick the one whose last bit is 0. Ties to even, rather than ties away from zero, avoids a systematic upward drift when many ties occur. Three measured examples in float16 and bfloat16:

SumNeighboursResultWhy
float16 \(1 + 2^{-11}\)\(1\) and \(1 + 2^{-10}\)\(1\)exactly halfway; 1 has last bit 0 (even)
float16 \(1 + 3 \cdot 2^{-11}\)\(1 + 2^{-10}\) and \(1 + 2^{-9}\)\(1.001953125\)halfway; \(1 + 2^{-9}\) is the even one, so the tie goes up
bfloat16 \(1 + 2^{-8}\)\(1\) and \(1 + 2^{-7}\)\(1\)halfway, ties to even: the addition is lost entirely

The last row is the whole story of why bf16 weights need help: any update smaller than half an ulp of the weight disappears, every time. Section 11 measures how often that happens on a real model.

Stochastic rounding

Nearest rounding is deterministic and, for a tiny repeated update, always wrong in the same direction. Stochastic rounding rounds up with probability equal to how far the exact value lies toward the upper neighbour: if \(x\) sits a fraction \(t\) of the way from \(a\) to the next float \(b\), it returns \(b\) with probability \(t\) and \(a\) otherwise, so on average it returns \(x\) exactly (\(\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{SR}(x)] = (1 - t)a + tb = x\)). Example (derived): a bfloat16 weight \(0.107\) sits where the gap is \(2^{-11} \approx 0.000488\); an update of \(10^{-4}\) is \(0.205\) of a gap, so nearest rounding never moves the weight, while stochastic rounding moves it one gap up about one step in five and after 1,000 such updates the weight has moved about \(0.1\) in expectation, as it should. Gupta et al. trained networks with 16-bit fixed point "when using stochastic rounding" ({{Gupta et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02551}}); Zamirai et al. used it, and Kahan summation (section 7), to train in bf16 alone ({{Zamirai et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06192}}); NVIDIA's 4-bit pretraining recipe uses it on gradients "for unbiased gradient estimation" ({{NVIDIA 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25149}}).

What it is used for in ML
Choosing a dtype is choosing \(\varepsilon\): an update, a residual or a loss difference smaller than \(\varepsilon\) times the value it is added to is invisible. Tolerances in tests (torch.testing.assert_close) are set from \(\varepsilon\) of the dtype for the same reason.

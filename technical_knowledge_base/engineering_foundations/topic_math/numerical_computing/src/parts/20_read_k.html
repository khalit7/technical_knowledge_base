10. NaN hunting: where non-finite numbers come from and how to find the first one

Step 41,200 logs loss: nan and every weight is now NaN. NaN never appears from nowhere: some operation received an input outside its domain or produced a result outside the format's range, and from there it spreads through every operation that touches it:

One NaN in, a whole row out, then one row of weights, then everything

A real hunt, on the running example (PyTorch 2.14.1, measured, src/inputs/nan_demo.py): the naive softmax cross-entropy on \((1000, 999, 0)\), target sat, returns loss = inf, not NaN (\(p_{\text{sat}} = 1/\infty = 0\), \(-\ln 0 = \infty\)), and the backward pass returns gradients [nan, nan, nan]. Wrapped in torch.autograd.detect_anomaly(), it stops at the culprit: Function 'DivBackward0' returned nan values in its 0th output, the division by the overflowed sum. The fused F.cross_entropy on the same logits returns 1000.3132 with gradient [0.7311, 0.2689, -1.0].

  1. Check where it first appears. Log the loss and the global gradient norm every step; a norm that jumps by orders of magnitude a few steps before the NaN points to an instability (learning rate, a layer's logits growing), not a single bad operation. With GradScaler, a few skipped steps are normal; skipped steps every iteration are not.
  2. Find the first non-finite activation with a forward hook on every module that checks torch.isfinite(output).all() and records the module name; the first to fail is the culprit, everything after it collateral.
  3. Find the first non-finite gradient. torch.autograd.detect_anomaly() (or set_detect_anomaly(True)) records forward tracebacks so the backward error can name "the forward operation that created the failing backward function", and with check_nan=True "any backward computation that generates 'nan' value will raise an error"; "This mode should be enabled only for debugging" ({{anomaly_mode.py, v2.14.1|https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/blob/v2.14.1/torch/autograd/anomaly_mode.py}}).
  4. Replay the batch with the saved weights in float32, then float64. If float64 is finite, it is a precision or range problem (section 3); if not, it is the data or the maths (a log of 0, a division by a zero norm).
  5. Fix the cause, not the symptom: the stable form (sections 7 and 8), the risky operation in float32, a lower learning rate or clipping for an instability. torch.nan_to_num on a loss hides the evidence; skipping a step on a non-finite loss is acceptable only as a guard while you look.
Two published instabilities with this shape

Two published instabilities had this shape, and the fixes are architectural (owned by {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}}): PaLM's auxiliary z-loss, \(10^{-4} \cdot \log^2 Z\), keeps the softmax normaliser near 1; ViT-22B saw attention logits "grow to over 50000 in magnitude" without normalisation, near float16's 65,504, and fixed it by applying LayerNorm to queries and keys ({{Dehghani et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05442}}, appendix B).

Depth: research directions and a mistakes checklist

Where research is now (2026)
  • Fewer bits for training. FP8 training at 671B parameters with fine-grained scaling (DeepSeek-V3, December 2024; the {{DeepSeek-V3 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81#s-fp8}} animates it in its FP8 training section) and NVFP4 pretraining of a 12B model on 10 trillion tokens, "the longest publicly documented training run in 4-bit precision to date", using random Hadamard transforms, two-dimensional block scaling, stochastic rounding of gradients and a few layers kept in higher precision ({{NVIDIA, Sep 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.25149}}). Every ingredient is this page's: range (scales), precision (rounding), and accumulation. Depth: {{Quantization and precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}.
  • 16-bit-only training. Can the float32 master copy go? Zamirai et al. show nearest rounding "cancels small updates" in bf16 and that stochastic rounding and Kahan summation on the weight update recover "0.1% lower to 0.2% higher validation accuracy compared to 32-bit training" across seven applications ({{2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06192}}).
  • Determinism as a feature. Batch-invariant kernels make inference reproducible at a speed cost ({{Thinking Machines 2025|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/}}); on-policy reinforcement learning needs the sampler's and the trainer's log-probabilities to match bit for bit.
  • Accurate kernels, one pass. Online softmax ({{Milakov and Gimelshein 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.02867}}) inside FlashAttention, with error analysis of shifted log-sum-exp and softmax ({{Blanchard, Higham and Higham 2021|https://doi.org/10.1093/imanum/draa038}}).
  • Stability by architecture. QK normalisation, z-loss and softmax capping keep logits in range so that low precision survives (section 10).
Mistakes checklist (each with the section that explains it)
  • Comparing floats with ==: \(0.1 + 0.2 \ne 0.3\) in every binary format; compare with torch.testing.assert_close or math.isclose(a, b, rel_tol=...), a few times the dtype's \(\varepsilon\) (section 2).
  • torch.log(torch.softmax(z)) or Sigmoid then BCELoss: use log_softmax, cross_entropy on logits, binary_cross_entropy_with_logits (sections 7 and 8).
  • A running sum, metric or optimiser state in bf16: a bf16 running sum of ones stops at 256; a bf16 sum of a thousand numbers in \([0, 1)\) is 50% low (section 5). Keep master weights and Adam's moments in float32.
  • Believing machine epsilon is the smallest positive number: \(\varepsilon\) is the gap above 1; the smallest positive float32 is \(1.4 \times 10^{-45}\) and the smallest normal \(1.18 \times 10^{-38}\); libraries' eps is \(2u\).
  • fp16 without a loss scaler, or bf16 with one "to be safe": fp16 without scaling silently zeroes gradients (8.2% on SmolLM2); bf16 does not need one, and a misconfigured scaler only adds skipped steps (section 9).
  • Clipping gradients before unscaling them: with GradScaler they are \(S\) times too large until scaler.unscale_(optimizer).
  • Expecting bit-identical results across GPUs, batch sizes or thread counts: compare with tolerances; for exact reproducibility fix hardware, versions, batch shapes and use deterministic algorithms (section 6).
  • A variance as \(\overline{x^2} - \bar x^2\): \(-8\) on three float32 numbers near 10,000; use two passes or Welford (section 4).
  • Logits or targets in bf16: 999 and 1000 are the same bfloat16 number, so are 256 and 257; keep logits, losses and anything compared by small differences in float32.
  • Trusting an FP8 cast's overflow behaviour: PyTorch saturates \(1000 \to 448\) in E4M3, ml_dtypes returns NaN (section 3).
  • Letting the compiler reassociate: fast-math flags can delete Kahan's correction term as algebraically zero (section 5).

9. Mixed-precision training: master weights, loss scaling, and why bf16 skips it

The recipe that made 16-bit training work is {{Micikevicius et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.03740}}: "maintaining a master copy of weights in FP32, loss-scaling that minimizes gradient values becoming zeros, and FP16 arithmetic with accumulation in FP32". Each of the three fixes one failure of model.half(). To see the failures on a real model, this page ran one training step of SmolLM2-135M (134,515,008 parameters, 30 layers) on 4 sequences of 128 tokens of Tiny Shakespeare: in float32 on the CPU as the reference (loss 3.5607), then in pure float16 and pure bfloat16 on the laptop's GPU (MPS), keeping every one of the 134.5 million gradients (measured; src/inputs/measure_b.py).

Failure 1: small updates vanish (fix: float32 master weights)

A weight update is the learning rate times something of order one (Adam's normalised step). Added to a 16-bit weight, it disappears whenever it is below half the weight's gap (section 2). On SmolLM2's real weights (median \(|w| = 0.107\)), the fraction an update of size \(\eta\) would not change at all:

Measured: (w.to(dtype) - eta * sign(g).to(dtype)) == w.to(dtype) over all 134.5M weights. The two 83.8% cells are the same weights: both conditions reduce to \(|w| \ge 2^{-5}\). Typical LLM learning rates are \(10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-3}\), decayed lower at the end of training.

At a median weight of 0.107, bfloat16's gap is \(2^{-11} = 0.00049\), so any step below \(0.00024\) is lost: most of a run at learning rate \(10^{-4}\) would not train. The fix is a float32 master copy: the optimiser adds the update in float32 and a 16-bit copy is made for the next forward pass. The bytes this costs (2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16 per parameter with Adam) are counted on {{Quantization and precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}.

Failure 2: small gradients underflow (fix: loss scaling, float16 only)

In the float32 reference step the median \(|g|\) is \(3.2 \times 10^{-5}\), below float16's smallest normal number (\(2^{-14} = 6.1 \times 10^{-5}\)): 68% of SmolLM2's gradients live in float16's subnormal range, 7.2% are below its smallest subnormal \(2^{-24}\), and 5.0% are below \(2^{-25}\) and round to exactly 0 when cast (measured). The 2017 paper found the same order on a speech model: "approximately 5% of weight gradient values have exponents smaller than \(-24\)". Loss scaling multiplies the loss by \(S\) before the backward pass; by the chain rule every gradient is multiplied by \(S\), shifting the histogram right by \(\log_2 S\) binades; the gradients are divided by \(S\) in float32 before the update, so the step is unchanged. Too small an \(S\) leaves gradients in the underflow zone; too large pushes the largest past 65,504 into infinity. Predict the largest safe \(k\), then drag:

So on this model a static scale between \(2^{8}\) and \(2^{16}\) works and \(2^{20}\) is fatal: one overflow in the backward pass becomes infinity, then NaN, and spreads to 96.5 million gradients. Because the right scale changes during training, frameworks adjust it. PyTorch's GradScaler starts at init_scale=2.0**16; when a step produces inf or NaN gradients it skips the step and multiplies the scale by backoff_factor=0.5, and after growth_interval=2000 clean steps it multiplies by growth_factor=2.0 ({{grad_scaler.py, lines 123 to 131, v2.14.1|https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/blob/v2.14.1/torch/amp/grad_scaler.py#L123-L131}}). The loop: scaler.scale(loss).backward(), scaler.step(opt), scaler.update(), and scaler.unscale_(opt) before gradient clipping, so the threshold applies to the true gradients.

What the 2017 paper itself reported

An update becomes zero "when the magnitude of a normalized weight value is at least 2048 times larger that of the weight update" (float16 has 10 mantissa bits, \(2^{11} = 2048\)); on their Mandarin speech model, dropping the float32 master copy cost "80% relative accuracy loss" (§3.1). An SSD detector "diverges when gradients are not scaled, but scaling them by a factor of 8 (increasing the exponents by 3) is sufficient", and the factors used across its models ranged "from 8 to 32K"; it proposed, without running it, a dynamic scale that is lowered and the step skipped on overflow.

Why bfloat16 mostly does not need loss scaling

bfloat16 has float32's 8 exponent bits, so its smallest normal number is \(1.2 \times 10^{-38}\); SmolLM2's smallest gradient is \(2^{-51} \approx 4 \times 10^{-16}\), 22 orders of magnitude inside the range. In the pure bfloat16 run only 0.00005% of gradients became 0, with no scaling. bfloat16's problem is the other axis: with 8 significant bits its gradients had a median relative error of 4.8%, against 0.55% for float16 at a good scale (measured). Range is fixed by the format; precision by float32 master weights and float32 accumulation. Google: "Unlike FP16, which typically requires special handling via techniques such as loss scaling, BF16 comes close to being a drop-in replacement for FP32" ({{Wang and Kanwar 2019|https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/bfloat16-the-secret-to-high-performance-on-cloud-tpus}}). "Mostly": hardware that flushes bfloat16 subnormals (section 1) has a smaller range at the bottom, and FP8 and 4-bit training are back to needing scales, per tensor or per block. The standard loop is with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16) around the forward pass, with float32 parameters and optimiser.

Failure 3: large activations overflow (fix: bf16, or keep the risky ops in float32)

float16's 65,504 is reachable inside a network: attention scores before the softmax, the sum of squares inside a norm, a residual stream with outlier features, the logits of a large vocabulary. One overflow gives \(\infty\), the next softmax gives NaN. That is why autocast keeps exactly those operations in float32 (softmax, layer_norm, norm, sum, exp, the losses; section 7) and runs matrix multiplies in 16 bits with float32 accumulation.

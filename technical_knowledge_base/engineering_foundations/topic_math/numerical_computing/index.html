The idea in one screen

A computer stores a real number as a sign, a power of two and a fixed number of binary digits. So it can only hold numbers from a finite grid: the grid is dense near zero and sparse far away, every result is snapped to the nearest grid point, and the grid ends (past it lies infinity). Exponent bits buy range; mantissa bits buy precision. Everything on this page follows from that picture: why 0.1+0.2≠0.30.1 + 0.2 \ne 0.3, why softmax needs the max subtracted, why fp16 training needs loss scaling and bf16 does not, why adding a million numbers in 16 bits returns garbage, and why the same model gives different answers on different runs.

The one idea to keep
Every floating-point result is the true result times (1+δ)(1 + \delta) with |δ||\delta| at most half the format's epsilon, as long as nothing overflows or underflows. Trouble comes from three places only: leaving the range (overflow to infinity, underflow to zero), subtracting nearly equal numbers (cancellation), and piling up many small errors (long sums). Stable algorithms are rearrangements that avoid all three.

You need: nothing beyond calculus. 1. A float, bit by bit

In binary each place is worth twice the place to its right, and places after the point are worth 12,14,18,…\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \dots. A fraction has a finite binary expansion only if its denominator is a power of two, so 0.1=0.0001120.1 = 0.0\overline{0011}_2 repeats forever and any fixed number of bits must cut it off: that is why 0.1+0.20.1 + 0.2 prints as 0.30000000000000004 in Python (measured). ML needs 3×10−83 \times 10^{-8} (a small gradient) and 5×1045 \times 10^{4} (a large activation) in the same tensor; an evenly spaced grid fine enough for the first needs about 101210^{12} steps, about 41 bits, to reach the second. So numbers are stored in base-2 scientific notation, x=(−1)s×m×2Ex = (-1)^{s} \times m \times 2^{E} with 1≤m<21 \le m \lt 2: 13=11012=1.1012×23=1.625×813 = 1101_2 = 1.101_2 \times 2^{3} = 1.625 \times 8. The point "floats" to just after the first 1, and every number carries the same number of significant bits whatever its size: the gaps between neighbours grow in proportion to the number.

Integers and two's complement (for quantised codes and index overflow)

An unsigned 8-bit integer holds 00 to 28−1=2552^8 - 1 = 255. For negative numbers every modern machine uses two's complement: the top bit is worth −27=−128-2^{7} = -128 instead of +128+128, so int8 holds −128-128 to 127127 and −13=111100112=−128+64+32+16+2+1-13 = 11110011_2 = -128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 2 + 1. Adding uses the same circuit as unsigned addition, and negating is "flip every bit, add 1" (13=00001101213 = 00001101_2, flipped 11110010211110010_2, plus one 11110011211110011_2). The price is that integers wrap around: in int8, 127+1=−128127 + 1 = -128 (derived; NumPy does the same and warns). Integers are exact inside their range and evenly spaced, which is why quantised weights are stored as int8 or int4 codes with a separate scale (Quantization and precision). Token ids, counts and quantised codes are integers; wraparound appears in practice when an int32 index overflows past 231−1≈2.1×1092^{31} - 1 \approx 2.1 \times 10^{9} elements, the size of a single large embedding or attention tensor.

Hardware that flushes subnormals to zero

Some hardware drops subnormals for speed (flush to zero): Google's TPU blog says "bfloat16 handles denormals differently from FP32: it flushes them to zero" (Wang and Kanwar 2019), and PyTorch's notes say AMD MI200's FP16 and BF16 matrix instructions "flush input and output denormal values to zero" (numerical accuracy notes, v2.14.1). On such hardware the bottom of the range is 2−1262^{-126}, not 2−1332^{-133} (bfloat16's smallest subnormal).

2. Gaps between floats, machine epsilon and rounding

Between two consecutive powers of two, 2E2^E and 2E+12^{E+1} (one binade), a format with pp significant bits has exactly 2p−12^{p-1} evenly spaced values, so the gap there, one ulp ("unit in the last place"), is the value of the last stored bit. For float32 it is 2−232^{-23} between 1 and 2 and 2−13≈1.2×10−42^{-13} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-4} between 1024 and 2048: float32 can tell 1000.0 from 1000.0001 but not from 1000.00001. Below the smallest normal number the spacing stops shrinking (subnormals are evenly spaced). FP8 E4M3 (p=4p = 4: 8 values per binade) is small enough to draw every value:

ulp⁡(x)=2E−(p−1)for 2E≤|x|<2E+1.\operatorname{ulp}(x) = 2^{\,E - (p - 1)} \quad \text{for } 2^E \le |x| \lt 2^{E+1} .

Machine epsilon ε=2−(p−1)\varepsilon = 2^{-(p-1)} is the gap between 1 and the next float. The unit roundoff u=ε/2=2−pu = \varepsilon / 2 = 2^{-p} is the largest relative error of one rounding: libraries report ε\varepsilon (torch.finfo(...).eps, C's FLT_EPSILON), numerical analysis texts write uu (Higham 2002, chapter 2), and mixing them up is a factor of two. pp bits are about 0.301p0.301\,p decimal digits: float64 about 16, float32 about 7.2, float16 about 3.3 and bfloat16 about 2.4 (derived). bfloat16 cannot tell 256 from 257: both round to 256. An update, a residual or a loss difference smaller than ε\varepsilon times the value it is added to is invisible, which is also why test tolerances (torch.testing.assert_close) are set from the dtype's ε\varepsilon.

Why one rounding costs at most uu relative error, checked on the loss

Take xx with 2E≤|x|<2E+12^E \le |x| \lt 2^{E+1}. Neighbouring floats there are 2E−p+12^{E-p+1} apart, so the nearest one is at most half that away: |fl⁡(x)−x|≤2E−p|\operatorname{fl}(x) - x| \le 2^{E-p}. Divide by |x|≥2E|x| \ge 2^E:

|fl⁡(x)−x||x|≤2E−p2E=2−p=u.\frac{|\operatorname{fl}(x) - x|}{|x|} \le \frac{2^{E-p}}{2^{E}} = 2^{-p} = u .

Here fl⁡(x)\operatorname{fl}(x) means "xx rounded to the format". Check on the loss: float32 3.2×10−8≤u=5.96×10−83.2 \times 10^{-8} \le u = 5.96 \times 10^{-8}; float16 2.4082031252.408203125, error 2.5×10−4≤2−11=4.9×10−42.5 \times 10^{-4} \le 2^{-11} = 4.9 \times 10^{-4}; bfloat16 2.406252.40625, error 5.6×10−4≤2−8=3.9×10−35.6 \times 10^{-4} \le 2^{-8} = 3.9 \times 10^{-3} (all derived from the bits). In the subnormal range the bound fails: the absolute error is at most half the smallest subnormal, and the relative error can reach 100%.

Round to nearest, ties to even

IEEE 754 requires every basic operation (+,−,×,÷, +, -, \times, \div, \sqrt{\ } and the fused multiply-add) to be exactly rounded: computed as if with infinite precision, then rounded once. The default mode is round to nearest, ties to even: on an exact tie, pick the neighbour whose last bit is 0, which avoids the upward drift of ties away from zero. Measured:

Where stochastic rounding is used

Gupta et al. trained networks with 16-bit fixed point "when using stochastic rounding" (Gupta et al. 2015); Zamirai et al. used it, and Kahan summation (section 5), to train in bf16 alone (Zamirai et al. 2020); NVIDIA's 4-bit pretraining recipe uses it on gradients "for unbiased gradient estimation" (NVIDIA 2025).

4. The error of one operation, conditioning and cancellation

Because IEEE 754 rounds each basic operation once, correctly, every operation obeys the standard model (Higham 2002, chapter 2): the computed result is the exact result of the exact inputs, nudged by a relative error of at most uu (5.96×10−85.96 \times 10^{-8} in float32), as long as it neither overflows nor underflows.

fl⁡(x∘y)=(x∘y)(1+δ),|δ|≤u,∘∈{+,−,×,÷}\operatorname{fl}(x \circ y) = (x \circ y)(1 + \delta), \qquad |\delta| \le u, \qquad \circ \in \{+, -, \times, \div\}

One operation is almost perfect; problems come from errors already in the inputs and from many operations in a row. The fused multiply-add (FMA, required since IEEE 754-2008) computes a×b+ca \times b + c with one rounding instead of two; matrix-multiply units are built from it. Two ways to measure a wrong answer: the forward error is the distance from the true answer; the backward error asks how much the input would have to change for the computed answer to be exact. An algorithm whose answers are exact for inputs perturbed by a few uu is backward stable, and

forward error≲κ×backward error.\text{forward error} \lesssim \kappa \times \text{backward error} .

So there are two separate ways to get a bad answer: a badly conditioned problem (large κ\kappa: any algorithm loses digits) or an unstable algorithm (large backward error on a well-conditioned problem: a better algorithm fixes it). The naive softmax of section 7 is the second kind.

5. Summation: order matters, and long sums drift

A loss is a mean over a batch, a matrix multiply is millions of sums, a gradient is a sum over tokens. Each ++ rounds, and the rounding depends on the operands' sizes, so floating-point addition is not associative. Measured in float64: (0.1+0.2)+0.3=0.6000000000000001(0.1 + 0.2) + 0.3 = 0.6000000000000001 but 0.1+(0.2+0.3)=0.60.1 + (0.2 + 0.3) = 0.6; (1016+1)−1016=0(10^{16} + 1) - 10^{16} = 0 but (1016−1016)+1=1(10^{16} - 10^{16}) + 1 = 1, because near 101610^{16} the gap is 2, so 1016+110^{16} + 1 is a tie that rounds back and the 1 is absorbed. Addition is still commutative (a+b=b+aa + b = b + a exactly); it is chains of additions in different orders that differ.

Adding x1,…,xnx_1, \dots, x_n left to right (recursive summation), x1x_1 and x2x_2 pass through n−1n - 1 roundings, x3x_3 through n−2n - 2, and so on, so to first order in uu

|sˆn−sn|≤(n−1)u∑i=1n|xi|.|\hat s_n - s_n| \le (n - 1)\, u \sum_{i=1}^{n} |x_i| .
Where that bound comes from, in three lines

sˆ2=(x1+x2)(1+δ2)\hat s_2 = (x_1 + x_2)(1 + \delta_2), sˆ3=((x1+x2)(1+δ2)+x3)(1+δ3)\hat s_3 = \bigl((x_1 + x_2)(1 + \delta_2) + x_3\bigr)(1 + \delta_3), and so on. Multiplying out, sˆn=∑ixi∏k=max⁡(i,2)n(1+δk)\hat s_n = \sum_i x_i \prod_{k = \max(i, 2)}^{n} (1 + \delta_k). Each product of at most n−1n - 1 factors (1+δk)(1 + \delta_k) lies within (1±u)n−1=1±(n−1)u+O(u2)(1 \pm u)^{n-1} = 1 \pm (n-1)u + O(u^2). Subtract the exact sum and bound each term by |xi|(n−1)u|x_i|\,(n-1)u. The full statement with the O(u2)O(u^2) term is in Higham 2002, chapter 4.

The bound grows with nn and is relative to ∑|xi|\sum|x_i|, not to the answer: with mixed signs the answer can be tiny and the relative error huge. Worse is stagnation: once the running sum is large, each small term is below half a gap and lost entirely. Adding 1 over and over (measured), float16 stops at 2,048, bfloat16 at 256, float32 at 16,777,216: these are 2p2^{p}, where the gap becomes 2 and adding 1 is a tie that rounds back (to even). Three fixes:

  • Pairwise summation: add neighbours in pairs, then pairs of pairs. Each term passes through ⌈log2⁡n⌉\lceil \log_2 n \rceil roundings, so the bound becomes ⌈log2⁡n⌉u∑|xi|\lceil \log_2 n \rceil\, u \sum|x_i|: 20 instead of a million for n=106n = 10^6. GPUs do this naturally (a tree reduction); NumPy uses "partial pairwise summation" (its sum docstring).
  • Kahan (compensated) summation (Kahan 1965) carries a second number cc holding what the last addition lost and feeds it into the next one. Its bound is (2u+O(nu2))∑|xi|\bigl(2u + O(n u^2)\bigr)\sum|x_i|: independent of nn while nu≪1n u \ll 1.
  • Accumulate in higher precision: 16-bit inputs added in a float32 accumulator. Tensor cores do this for fp16 and bf16 matrix multiplies (PyTorch notes: "intermediate accumulations (reduction) in single-precision"); FP8 recipes promote partial sums periodically: DeepSeek-V3 every 128 elements (Quantization and precision).

6. Determinism: why the same code gives different numbers

Fix every seed, run the same step twice, and the losses can still differ in the sixth digit. The cause is the order of additions. A parallel reduction splits a sum into chunks, adds each chunk, then adds the chunk totals; the chunking depends on the thread count, the block sizes a kernel picks for this shape, and whether a matrix multiply splits its inner dimension (split-K). A kernel that lets many threads add into one location uses atomic adds, and then the order is whichever thread finishes first.

So "nondeterminism" has two meanings. Run-to-run: the same call on the same input differs between runs (atomics, the MPS index_add_ row). Thinking Machines showed that LLM inference is mostly the second kind: the forward pass "involves no operations that require atomic adds" and is run-to-run deterministic, but kernels are not batch invariant, so a request's numbers depend on how many other requests share its batch. In training, the backward pass is where atomics live: gradients of embeddings, gathers and scatters add many contributions into the same row. Bit-identical reruns matter for bisecting a divergence, for on-policy RL (sampler and trainer must compute the same log-probabilities, Thinking Machines' motivation) and for regression tests, which should compare with a tolerance, not ==; expect small differences between runs and providers at temperature 0.

Why not Sigmoid then BCELoss?
Because the probability has already rounded to 0 or 1 before the loss sees it. PyTorch patches the symptom: "BCELoss clamps its log function outputs to be greater than or equal to -100", so the x=30x = 30 example returns 100 instead of 30, a finite but wrong loss with a wrong gradient. Under autocast it refuses outright: "binary_cross_entropy and BCELoss raise an error in autocast-enabled regions", and the docs say to use binary_cross_entropy_with_logits (loss.py, amp docs, v2.14.1). Reward models trained with −ln⁡σ(rw−rl)-\ln\sigma(r_w - r_l) and DPO's loss are exactly a logsigmoid.
The derivations, and the kernels PyTorch runs

logsigmoid. For x≥0x \ge 0, ln⁡σ(x)=−ln⁡(1+e−x)\ln\sigma(x) = -\ln(1 + e^{-x}). For x<0x \lt 0, multiply top and bottom by exe^{x}: σ(x)=ex/(1+ex)\sigma(x) = e^{x}/(1 + e^{x}), so ln⁡σ(x)=x−ln⁡(1+ex)\ln\sigma(x) = x - \ln(1 + e^{x}). Both are the one formula above. PyTorch's CPU kernel is that line, computed in float32 even for 16-bit inputs: min_vec - buffer_vec0.log1p() with buffer_vec0 = data_vec0.abs().neg().exp() (Activation.cpp, lines 33 to 48).

BCE. Two facts: 1−σ(x)=σ(−x)1 - \sigma(x) = \sigma(-x), and ln⁡σ(−x)=ln⁡σ(x)−x\ln\sigma(-x) = \ln\sigma(x) - x (from σ(−x)=e−xσ(x)\sigma(-x) = e^{-x}\sigma(x)). Substitute: ℓ=−yln⁡σ(x)−(1−y)(ln⁡σ(x)−x)\ell = -y \ln\sigma(x) - (1 - y)\bigl(\ln\sigma(x) - x\bigr), which collects to (1−y)x−ln⁡σ(x)(1 - y)\,x - \ln\sigma(x), and the logsigmoid line gives the last form.

softplus is a smooth ReLU and the negative of ln⁡σ(−x)\ln\sigma(-x). PyTorch's nn.Softplus(beta=1.0, threshold=20.0) takes a shortcut: when βx>20\beta x \gt 20 it returns xx unchanged. What it drops, log1p⁡(e−20)=2.1×10−9\operatorname{log1p}(e^{-20}) = 2.1 \times 10^{-9}, is a thousand times smaller than float32's gap at 20 (1.9×10−61.9 \times 10^{-6}), so the shortcut is exact in float32 (derived).

Epsilons. Formulas that divide by something that can be 0 (a standard deviation, a norm, the root of a second moment) add a small constant ϵ\epsilon, not machine epsilon: LayerNorm's default 10−510^{-5} turns a constant row's 0/00/0 into 0; Adam's 10−810^{-8} is itself 0 in float16, one reason optimiser state stays in float32.

9. Mixed-precision training: master weights, loss scaling, and why bf16 skips it

The recipe that made 16-bit training work is Micikevicius et al. 2017: "maintaining a master copy of weights in FP32, loss-scaling that minimizes gradient values becoming zeros, and FP16 arithmetic with accumulation in FP32". Each of the three fixes one failure of model.half(). To see the failures on a real model, this page ran one training step of SmolLM2-135M (134,515,008 parameters, 30 layers) on 4 sequences of 128 tokens of Tiny Shakespeare: in float32 on the CPU as the reference (loss 3.5607), then in pure float16 and pure bfloat16 on the laptop's GPU (MPS), keeping every one of the 134.5 million gradients (measured; src/inputs/measure_b.py).

Failure 1: small updates vanish (fix: float32 master weights)

A weight update is the learning rate times something of order one (Adam's normalised step). Added to a 16-bit weight, it disappears whenever it is below half the weight's gap (section 2). On SmolLM2's real weights (median |w|=0.107|w| = 0.107), the fraction an update of size η\eta would not change at all:

At a median weight of 0.107, bfloat16's gap is 2−11=0.000492^{-11} = 0.00049, so any step below 0.000240.00024 is lost: most of a run at learning rate 10−410^{-4} would not train. The fix is a float32 master copy: the optimiser adds the update in float32 and a 16-bit copy is made for the next forward pass. The bytes this costs (2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 16 per parameter with Adam) are counted on Quantization and precision.

Failure 2: small gradients underflow (fix: loss scaling, float16 only)

In the float32 reference step the median |g||g| is 3.2×10−53.2 \times 10^{-5}, below float16's smallest normal number (2−14=6.1×10−52^{-14} = 6.1 \times 10^{-5}): 68% of SmolLM2's gradients live in float16's subnormal range, 7.2% are below its smallest subnormal 2−242^{-24}, and 5.0% are below 2−252^{-25} and round to exactly 0 when cast (measured). The 2017 paper found the same order on a speech model: "approximately 5% of weight gradient values have exponents smaller than −24-24". Loss scaling multiplies the loss by SS before the backward pass; by the chain rule every gradient is multiplied by SS, shifting the histogram right by log2⁡S\log_2 S binades; the gradients are divided by SS in float32 before the update, so the step is unchanged. Because the right scale changes during training, frameworks adjust it. What the 2017 paper itself reported

An update becomes zero "when the magnitude of a normalized weight value is at least 2048 times larger that of the weight update" (float16 has 10 mantissa bits, 211=20482^{11} = 2048); on their Mandarin speech model, dropping the float32 master copy cost "80% relative accuracy loss" (§3.1). An SSD detector "diverges when gradients are not scaled, but scaling them by a factor of 8 (increasing the exponents by 3) is sufficient", and the factors used across its models ranged "from 8 to 32K"; it proposed, without running it, a dynamic scale that is lowered and the step skipped on overflow.

Why bfloat16 mostly does not need loss scaling

bfloat16 has float32's 8 exponent bits, so its smallest normal number is 1.2×10−381.2 \times 10^{-38}; SmolLM2's smallest gradient is 2−51≈4×10−162^{-51} \approx 4 \times 10^{-16}, 22 orders of magnitude inside the range. In the pure bfloat16 run only 0.00005% of gradients became 0, with no scaling. bfloat16's problem is the other axis: with 8 significant bits its gradients had a median relative error of 4.8%, against 0.55% for float16 at a good scale (measured). Range is fixed by the format; precision by float32 master weights and float32 accumulation. Google: "Unlike FP16, which typically requires special handling via techniques such as loss scaling, BF16 comes close to being a drop-in replacement for FP32" (Wang and Kanwar 2019). "Mostly": hardware that flushes bfloat16 subnormals (section 1) has a smaller range at the bottom, and FP8 and 4-bit training are back to needing scales, per tensor or per block. The standard loop is with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16) around the forward pass, with float32 parameters and optimiser.

Failure 3: large activations overflow (fix: bf16, or keep the risky ops in float32)

float16's 65,504 is reachable inside a network: attention scores before the softmax, the sum of squares inside a norm, a residual stream with outlier features, the logits of a large vocabulary. One overflow gives ∞\infty, the next softmax gives NaN. That is why autocast keeps exactly those operations in float32 (softmax, layer_norm, norm, sum, exp, the losses; section 7) and runs matrix multiplies in 16 bits with float32 accumulation.

  1. Check where it first appears. Log the loss and the global gradient norm every step; a norm that jumps by orders of magnitude a few steps before the NaN points to an instability (learning rate, a layer's logits growing), not a single bad operation. With GradScaler, a few skipped steps are normal; skipped steps every iteration are not.
  2. Find the first non-finite activation with a forward hook on every module that checks torch.isfinite(output).all() and records the module name; the first to fail is the culprit, everything after it collateral.
  3. Find the first non-finite gradient. torch.autograd.detect_anomaly() (or set_detect_anomaly(True)) records forward tracebacks so the backward error can name "the forward operation that created the failing backward function", and with check_nan=True "any backward computation that generates 'nan' value will raise an error"; "This mode should be enabled only for debugging" (anomaly_mode.py, v2.14.1).
  4. Replay the batch with the saved weights in float32, then float64. If float64 is finite, it is a precision or range problem (section 3); if not, it is the data or the maths (a log of 0, a division by a zero norm).
  5. Fix the cause, not the symptom: the stable form (sections 7 and 8), the risky operation in float32, a lower learning rate or clipping for an instability. torch.nan_to_num on a loss hides the evidence; skipping a step on a non-finite loss is acceptable only as a guard while you look.
Two published instabilities with this shape

Two published instabilities had this shape, and the fixes are architectural (owned by Normalisation, residuals and stability): PaLM's auxiliary z-loss, 10−4⋅log2⁡Z10^{-4} \cdot \log^2 Z, keeps the softmax normaliser near 1; ViT-22B saw attention logits "grow to over 50000 in magnitude" without normalisation, near float16's 65,504, and fixed it by applying LayerNorm to queries and keys (Dehghani et al. 2023, appendix B).

Depth: research directions and a mistakes checklist

Where research is now (2026)
  • Fewer bits for training. FP8 training at 671B parameters with fine-grained scaling (DeepSeek-V3, December 2024; the DeepSeek-V3 paper page animates it in its FP8 training section) and NVFP4 pretraining of a 12B model on 10 trillion tokens, "the longest publicly documented training run in 4-bit precision to date", using random Hadamard transforms, two-dimensional block scaling, stochastic rounding of gradients and a few layers kept in higher precision (NVIDIA, Sep 2025). Every ingredient is this page's: range (scales), precision (rounding), and accumulation. Depth: Quantization and precision.
  • 16-bit-only training. Can the float32 master copy go? Zamirai et al. show nearest rounding "cancels small updates" in bf16 and that stochastic rounding and Kahan summation on the weight update recover "0.1% lower to 0.2% higher validation accuracy compared to 32-bit training" across seven applications (2020).
  • Determinism as a feature. Batch-invariant kernels make inference reproducible at a speed cost (Thinking Machines 2025); on-policy reinforcement learning needs the sampler's and the trainer's log-probabilities to match bit for bit.
  • Accurate kernels, one pass. Online softmax (Milakov and Gimelshein 2018) inside FlashAttention, with error analysis of shifted log-sum-exp and softmax (Blanchard, Higham and Higham 2021).
  • Stability by architecture. QK normalisation, z-loss and softmax capping keep logits in range so that low precision survives (section 10).
Mistakes checklist (each with the section that explains it)
  • Comparing floats with ==: 0.1+0.2≠0.30.1 + 0.2 \ne 0.3 in every binary format; compare with torch.testing.assert_close or math.isclose(a, b, rel_tol=...), a few times the dtype's ε\varepsilon (section 2).
  • torch.log(torch.softmax(z)) or Sigmoid then BCELoss: use log_softmax, cross_entropy on logits, binary_cross_entropy_with_logits (sections 7 and 8).
  • A running sum, metric or optimiser state in bf16: a bf16 running sum of ones stops at 256; a bf16 sum of a thousand numbers in [0,1)[0, 1) is 50% low (section 5). Keep master weights and Adam's moments in float32.
  • Believing machine epsilon is the smallest positive number: ε\varepsilon is the gap above 1; the smallest positive float32 is 1.4×10−451.4 \times 10^{-45} and the smallest normal 1.18×10−381.18 \times 10^{-38}; libraries' eps is 2u2u.
  • fp16 without a loss scaler, or bf16 with one "to be safe": fp16 without scaling silently zeroes gradients (8.2% on SmolLM2); bf16 does not need one, and a misconfigured scaler only adds skipped steps (section 9).
  • Clipping gradients before unscaling them: with GradScaler they are SS times too large until scaler.unscale_(optimizer).
  • Expecting bit-identical results across GPUs, batch sizes or thread counts: compare with tolerances; for exact reproducibility fix hardware, versions, batch shapes and use deterministic algorithms (section 6).
  • A variance as x2−x‾2\overline{x^2} - \bar x^2: −8-8 on three float32 numbers near 10,000; use two passes or Welford (section 4).
  • Logits or targets in bf16: 999 and 1000 are the same bfloat16 number, so are 256 and 257; keep logits, losses and anything compared by small differences in float32.
  • Trusting an FP8 cast's overflow behaviour: PyTorch saturates 1000→4481000 \to 448 in E4M3, ml_dtypes returns NaN (section 3).
  • Letting the compiler reassociate: fast-math flags can delete Kahan's correction term as algebraically zero (section 5).

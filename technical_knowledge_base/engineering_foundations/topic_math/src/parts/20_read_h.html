The same object in different hats

Two objects turn up in several of the sections above under different names. Seeing that they are one thing each is the payoff of learning the areas together.

Cross-entropy wears four hats

The old version of this page used a set of scores read four ways; here it is again, checked. Take the same scores \(z = (2, 1, 0)\) and probabilities \(p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\), but suppose the right word had been cat (the model's favourite) rather than sat.

Probability: a likelihood

Model the next word as a categorical draw. The negative log-likelihood of cat is \(-\ln 0.665 = 0.408\) nats; minimising its average over a dataset is maximum-likelihood estimation (section 2).

Information: a code length

Divided by \(\ln 2\), the same number is 0.588 bits: the length of cat's codeword in a code built from the model's bet. Averaged over real data it is the cross-entropy, the data's entropy plus the KL divergence from the data to the model (section 3).

Calculus: prediction minus target

Its gradient with respect to the scores is \(p - y = (0.665 - 1, 0.245, 0.090)\) \(= (-0.335, 0.245, 0.090)\), adding up to 0 (section 4).

Contrastive learning: InfoNCE

Read cat as an anchor's matching partner (the positive) and dog and sat as two non-matching candidates, with similarity scores 2, 1 and 0. Then 0.408 is the InfoNCE loss with \(N = 3\) candidates, and it certifies that the two views share at least \(\ln 3 - 0.408 = 0.691\) nats of mutual information (how much knowing one tells you about the other), by the bound \(I \ge \ln N - L\) ({{van den Oord et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748}}).

With the real answer, sat, the same reading gives a loss of 2.408, and the bound \(\ln 3 - 2.408\) is negative: it certifies nothing. The bound can never exceed \(\ln N\), which is why contrastive models such as CLIP train with huge batches (32,768 pairs, so \(N - 1\) negatives for each positive; {{Radford et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020}}).

Curvature is read three ways

For a yes-or-no prediction with score \(z\) and probability \(p = \sigma(z)\), three quantities from three different areas turn out to be the same number, \(p(1 - p)\):

Optimisation: the Hessian

The second derivative of the binary cross-entropy loss with respect to \(z\) is \(p(1 - p)\), whatever the label \(y\).

Statistics: a variance

A Bernoulli label that is 1 with probability \(p\) has variance \(p(1 - p)\).

Probability: the Fisher information

The score (the slope of the log-likelihood with respect to \(z\)) is \(y - p\); its variance, the Fisher information, is \(p(1 - p)\).
The numbers (checked)
At \(z = 0\) (\(p = 0.5\)) all three are 0.25, their largest value. At \(z = \ln 9 = 2.197\) (\(p = 0.9\)) all three are 0.09. A confident prediction sits in a flat part of the loss. The tiny model shows the many-word version: the Hessian of its loss with respect to the scores, \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top\), is exactly the covariance matrix of the one-hot draw, whose diagonal holds the per-word variances \(p_i(1 - p_i) = (0.223, 0.185, 0.082)\) (a simulation of 200,000 draws gives 0.222, 0.185, 0.081), and it is the Fisher information of the categorical distribution in its scores. The ratio of its largest to smallest nonzero eigenvalue is the condition number of section 5.
Optional: why this matters for natural gradients, and a correction to the old page

Because, for these matched pairs (sigmoid with BCE, softmax with cross-entropy, identity with squared error), the curvature with respect to the scores does not depend on the label, it equals its own average over labels, the Fisher. That is why natural-gradient methods and K-FAC precondition with the Fisher, and why the curvature of TRPO's KL constraint is the Fisher matrix. A correction to the old page: this equality is exact with respect to the scores only. With respect to the weights of a deep network the Hessian gains an extra term (the residual \(p - y\) times the curvature of the network itself), so there the Fisher equals the Hessian only approximately; it is exactly the "generalised Gauss-Newton" part of it ({{Martens 2020, New insights and perspectives on the natural gradient method|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.1193}}).

Which area to reach for is a diagnosis
Cannot see why a loss is that loss and not another: probability (choose the distribution and the loss is decided). A run has run out of memory and you cannot see where it went: calculus, and nearly always the backward pass's stored activations. A representation has collapsed, or a fine-tune has barely moved anything: linear algebra, and the word is rank. An evaluation number will not say what it means: information theory. A claimed gain might be noise: statistics. The loss oscillates and explodes after a learning-rate change: optimisation, and the word is curvature.

Fourteen equations that carry modern ML, quoted exactly as their papers print them, decoded symbol by symbol. The aim is to read the next paper's maths without getting stuck: every symbol named, every shape given with real sizes, every equation run once on tiny numbers.

How to read any equation: the five steps from the {{Reading tab ("How to read an equation in a paper")|#t-read}}, used on every card
  1. Name every symbol. Find the line where the paper defines it; the same letter means different things in different papers (\(\beta\) is a decay rate in Adam, a KL weight in DPO, a noise level in DDPM). Here: tap a symbol.
  2. Write the shape beside every symbol. Check that every product's inner sizes match; most misreadings fail this test.
  3. Read the operations from the inside out, in words. Here: the "Read it aloud" sentence.
  4. Plug in tiny numbers. Two or three tokens, a 3 by 3 matrix: compute it once by hand. Here: "With tiny numbers".
  5. Ask why each piece is there. Why the square root, why the hat, why this subscript under the E. Here: "Traps".

Each card links the paper (arXiv id, version, equation number and PDF page) and the knowledge base pages that teach the mechanism. Every worked number is recomputed by src/notation/recompute.py; numbers marked illustrative are made-up inputs run through the real formula.

Scaled dot-product attention

Vaswani et al. 2017, Attention Is All You Need · arXiv 1706.03762v7 · Eq. (1), PDF page 4 · §3.2.1
\[\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V\]
As printed, with the paper's capital T for the transpose. The paper prints the softmax argument in plain round brackets.

Tap a symbol

Tap any symbol in the equation (the shaded parts; a wide equation scrolls sideways) or a chip above: its name, meaning, shape with concrete sizes, and where the paper defines it.
\(Q\): queries
Meaning
One row per position that is asking a question: what am I looking for?
Shape
\(n \times d_k\): here \(3 \times 4\) (3 tokens, \(d_k = 4\)); in the paper's base model \(d_k = 64\).
Defined
§3.2.1, p. 4: queries "packed together into a matrix Q".
\(K\): keys
Meaning
One row per position that can be looked at: what do I contain, as a label to match against?
Shape
\(m \times d_k\): here \(3 \times 4\). Self-attention has \(m = n\); cross-attention can differ.
Defined
§3.2.1, p. 4.
\({}^{T}\): transpose
Meaning
Swap rows and columns, so \(QK^T\) is every query dotted with every key. Other papers write \(K^\top\) or \(K'\): same thing.
Shape
\(K^T\) is \(d_k \times m\): here \(4 \times 3\).
Defined
Not defined in the paper: standard notation.
\(V\): values
Meaning
One row per position: what that position hands over if you attend to it.
Shape
\(m \times d_v\): here \(3 \times 2\); paper base \(d_v = 64\).
Defined
§3.2.1, p. 4.
\(d_k\): key dimension
Meaning
Length of each query and key vector. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) keeps the dot products from growing with \(d_k\) (footnote 4: if the components are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, \(q \cdot k\) has variance \(d_k\)).
Shape
A number: here 4, so \(\sqrt{d_k} = 2\); paper base 64, so 8.
Defined
§3.2.1 and footnote 4, p. 4; values in §3.2.2, p. 5.
\(\mathrm{softmax}\): softmax, row by row
Meaning
Turns each row of scores into weights that are positive and sum to 1: \(\mathrm{softmax}(z)_j = e^{z_j} / \sum_l e^{z_l}\). Applied to each row separately (one query at a time); the paper says so in words ("apply a softmax function to obtain the weights on the values").
Shape
\(n \times m\) in, \(n \times m\) out: here \(3 \times 3\).
Defined
§3.2.1, p. 4 (in words; the formula itself is not printed).
\(\mathrm{Attention}\): the output
Meaning
For each query, a weighted average of the value rows.
Shape
\(n \times d_v\): here \(3 \times 2\).
Defined
Eq. (1), p. 4.

Read it aloud

Attention of Q, K, V equals: take every query dotted with every key, divide by the square root of the key length, turn each row into weights with a softmax, and use those weights to average the rows of V.

With tiny numbers

Real tiny matrices: 3 tokens, dₖ = 4, dᵥ = 2. Step through them in the animation below; the numbers are recomputed in recompute.py.

A smaller case is worked in the {{Reading tab, section "How to read an equation in a paper"|#t-read}}: one query against the tiny model's three keys, dk = 2, scores (2, 1, 0), weights (0.576, 0.284, 0.140). This one adds a second and third query so the whole matrix form, and every shape, shows.

All steps at once (static)
  1. \[\begin{gathered}Q=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\\ K=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\\ V=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

    The inputs: three queries, three keys, three values.

  2. \[\begin{gathered}QK^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\\ =\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 2\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

    Each cell is one query dotted with one key: row 1, column 1 is 1·1 + 0·0 + 1·1 + 0·0 = 2.

  3. \[\frac{QK^{T}}{\sqrt{4}}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 0.500 \\ 0 & 1.500 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\]

    Divide every score by √d_k = 2.

  4. \[\mathrm{softmax}(\cdot)=\begin{bmatrix}0.506 & 0.186 & 0.307 \\ 0.122 & 0.547 & 0.331 \\ 0.333 & 0.333 & 0.333\end{bmatrix}\]

    Softmax each row: every row now sums to 1. Row 3 had equal scores, so its weights are equal.

  5. \[\begin{gathered}\begin{bmatrix}0.506 & 0.186 & 0.307 \\ 0.122 & 0.547 & 0.331 \\ 0.333 & 0.333 & 0.333\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{bmatrix}\\ =\begin{bmatrix}0.814 & 0.494 \\ 0.453 & 0.878 \\ 0.667 & 0.667\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

    Each output row is a weighted average of V's rows; shapes (3×3)(3×2) give 3×2.

Traps

  • The softmax runs along each row (over keys), never over the whole matrix and never down a column. In code: softmax(scores, dim=-1).
  • Rows are tokens. ML papers usually stack examples or positions as rows (\(n \times d\)), so a layer is \(XW\), not \(Wx\). Check which way round a paper writes it before multiplying.
  • \(\sqrt{d_k}\) is the square root of the key length, not of the sequence length. Without it, scores grow with \(d_k\) and the softmax saturates (Vaswani et al. footnote 4).
  • The decoder's mask is not in Eq. (1): §3.2.3 says it is applied by setting illegal positions to \(-\infty\) before the softmax.

Multi-head attention

Read it aloud

Multi-head attention runs h small attentions in parallel, each on its own learned projections of Q, K and V, glues their outputs side by side and mixes them with one more matrix W O.

With tiny numbers

Two tokens, d_model = 4, h = 2 heads, d_k = d_v = 2. The projections are chosen so head 1 reads dimensions 1 and 2 and head 2 reads dimensions 3 and 4 (illustrative weights, real arithmetic).

  1. \[\begin{gathered}X=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\\ W_1^{Q}=W_1^{K}=W_1^{V}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

    Self-attention: Q = K = V = X. Head 1's projections keep the first two coordinates.

  2. \[\begin{gathered}XW_1=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\\ XW_2=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

    Each head sees a 2 × 2 slice: shape (2×4)(4×2) = 2×2.

  3. \[\begin{gathered}\mathrm{head}_1=\begin{bmatrix}0.670 & 0.330 \\ 0.330 & 0.670\end{bmatrix}\\ \mathrm{head}_2=\begin{bmatrix}1.888 & 0.112 \\ 0.112 & 1.888\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

    Eq. (1) inside each head, with √d_k = √2.

  4. \[\mathrm{Concat}=\begin{bmatrix}0.670 & 0.330 & 1.888 & 0.112 \\ 0.330 & 0.670 & 0.112 & 1.888\end{bmatrix}\]

    Side by side: 2 × (2+2) = 2 × 4.

  5. \[\begin{gathered}W^{O}=\begin{bmatrix}0.500 & 0 & 0.500 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.500 & 0 & 0.500 \\ 0.500 & 0 & 0.500 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.500 & 0 & 0.500\end{bmatrix}\\ \mathrm{Concat}\,W^{O}=\begin{bmatrix}1.279 & 0.221 & 1.279 & 0.221 \\ 0.221 & 1.279 & 0.221 & 1.279\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

    W O mixes heads: each output column takes half of one head's column and half of the other's.

Traps

  • Superscripts \(Q, K, V, O\) on \(W\) are names, not powers; the subscript \(i\) is the head index. \(W_i^Q\) reads "W, head i, for queries".
  • \(h\) here is the number of heads; in the LayerNorm and many RNN papers \(h\) is a hidden state. Same letter, different object: always check the paper's own definition.
  • Splitting into heads costs no extra compute: with \(d_k = d_{\text{model}}/h\) the paper notes the total cost is similar to one full-width head (§3.2.2).

Read it aloud

h at time t equals f of: g over sigma, times element by element the vector a minus its mean, plus b; where mu is the average of a's H entries and sigma the square root of their average squared deviation.

With tiny numbers

One example, H = 4, f = identity (as in a Transformer). Gain and bias are illustrative.

  1. \[\begin{gathered}\mathbf{a}=\begin{bmatrix}2 & 4 & 6 & 8\end{bmatrix}\\ \mu=\tfrac14(2+4+6+8)=5\end{gathered}\]

    The mean of this one example's four entries.

  2. \[\begin{gathered}\sigma=\sqrt{\tfrac14\left((-3)^2+(-1)^2+1^2+3^2\right)}\\ =\sqrt{5}=2.236\end{gathered}\]

    Divide by H = 4, not 3.

  3. \[\frac{\mathbf{a}-\mu}{\sigma}=\begin{bmatrix}-1.34 & -0.45 & 0.45 & 1.34\end{bmatrix}\]

    Now mean 0 and (population) standard deviation 1.

  4. \[\begin{gathered}\mathbf{g}=\begin{bmatrix}1 & 0.500 & 1 & 2\end{bmatrix}\\ \mathbf{b}=\begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}\\ \mathbf{g}\odot\frac{\mathbf{a}-\mu}{\sigma}+\mathbf{b}\\ =\begin{bmatrix}-1.342 & -0.224 & 1.447 & 2.683\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

    Scale entry by entry, then shift.

  5. \[\begin{gathered}\text{with } H-1:\ \sigma=\sqrt{20/3}=2.582\\ \text{first entry } -1.162 \text{ instead of } -1.342\end{gathered}\]

    The trap: dividing by H − 1 gives different outputs. PyTorch's LayerNorm uses the paper's 1/H (biased) version.

Traps

  • Bold \(\mathbf{a}\) is the vector; plain \(a_i\) is one entry; the superscript \(t\) is a time step, not a power. \(\sigma^t\) is "sigma at time t", not sigma to the t.
  • The paper's \(\sigma\) has no \(\epsilon\). Code adds one inside the square root: PyTorch's LayerNorm computes \((x - \mathrm{E}[x])/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[x] + \epsilon}\) with \(\epsilon = 10^{-5}\) by default and the biased variance (docs).
  • The averages run over the features of one example (the layer), not over the batch. Eq. (2), batch normalisation, averages over examples with \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P(\mathbf{x})}\).

The language-modelling objective (next-token log-likelihood)

Radford et al. 2018, Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training (GPT-1) · Eq. (1), PDF page 3 · §3.1 · Not on arXiv; OpenAI's PDF.
\[L_{1}(\mathcal{U})=\sum_{i}\log P(u_{i}\mid u_{i-k},\ldots,u_{i-1};\Theta)\]

Read it aloud

L one of U equals the sum, over every position i, of the log of the probability the model with parameters Theta gives to token u i, given the k tokens before it.

With tiny numbers

Vocabulary (the, cat, sat); corpus "the cat sat"; the model's logits at each position are illustrative, the arithmetic is real. Same logits z = (2, 1, 0) as the Reading tab's running example.

  1. \[\begin{gathered}P(\cdot\mid\text{the})=\mathrm{softmax}(2,1,0)\\ =\begin{bmatrix}0.665 & 0.245 & 0.090\end{bmatrix}\\ \Rightarrow P(\text{cat}\mid\text{the})=0.245\end{gathered}\]

    Position 2: the right answer, cat, gets 0.245.

  2. \[\begin{gathered}P(\cdot\mid\text{the cat})=\mathrm{softmax}(0,1,3)\\ =\begin{bmatrix}0.042 & 0.114 & 0.844\end{bmatrix}\\ \Rightarrow P(\text{sat}\mid\ldots)=0.844\end{gathered}\]

    Position 3: sat gets 0.844.

  3. \[\begin{gathered}L_1=\log 0.245+\log 0.844\\ =-1.408+(-0.170)=-1.577\end{gathered}\]

    Sum the logs (natural log). Position 1 has no context, so it contributes nothing here.

  4. \[\begin{gathered}\text{loss}=-\frac{L_1}{2}=0.789\ \text{nats/token}\\ =1.138\ \text{bits/token}\\ \text{perplexity}=e^{0.789}=2.20\end{gathered}\]

    What a training log prints: the negative, averaged per token.

Traps

  • \(L_1\) is maximised; a "loss" is minimised. Papers switch between the two: check the sign and whether the text says maximise or minimise.
  • The sum is over tokens, so \(L_1\) grows with corpus length; training logs report the per-token mean. Comparing a summed and a mean number is a factor of the token count.
  • \(\log\) is natural (nats). Bits per token divide by \(\ln 2\); bits per byte also divide by bytes per token, and only that is comparable across tokenizers.
  • \(;\Theta\) marks parameters, \(\mid\) marks conditioning. Some papers write \(P_\Theta(u_i \mid \ldots)\) or \(p_\theta\): the same thing.

With tiny numbers

Conventions that trip people up

Habits that papers rarely state. Each comes with a tiny example; "Seen in" opens the equation above where it appears. When a paper defines its notation (often in a short section or a footnote), its definition wins over any of these.

Vectors are columns

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix}\\ \mathbf{x}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}1&2&3\end{bmatrix}\\ \mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=\textstyle\sum_i w_i x_i\end{gathered}\]

A bold lower-case letter is a column vector unless the paper says otherwise, so \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}\) is a single number (a dot product) and \(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{w}^\top\) is a matrix (an outer product).

Seen in:

Data matrices: one row per example

\[\begin{gathered}X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\\ n\ \text{rows (examples or tokens)}\\ d\ \text{columns (features)}\\ XW\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d'}\end{gathered}\]

Stacked data puts examples in rows, so a layer is applied as \(XW\) with \(W\) of shape \(d\times d'\). The same layer on one column vector is \(W^\top\mathbf{x}\) or, with \(W\) stored the other way, \(W\mathbf{x}\). Check which a paper uses before multiplying.

Seen in:

Shapes multiply by the inner sizes

\[\begin{gathered}(a\times b)(b\times c)=(a\times c)\\ A\in\mathbb{R}^{a\times b}\end{gathered}\]

\(\mathbb{R}^{a\times b}\) means \(a\) rows and \(b\) columns of real numbers. A product needs the inner sizes to match and keeps the outer ones. Writing the shape under every symbol catches most misreadings.

Seen in:

Transpose and Hermitian marks

\[\begin{gathered}A^{T}=A^{\top}=A'\\ A^{H}=A^{*}=A^{\dagger}=\overline{A}^{\top}\end{gathered}\]

\(T\), \(\top\) and a prime all mean transpose. \(H\), \(*\) and \(\dagger\) mean conjugate transpose, which matters only for complex numbers (Fourier features, the complex form of RoPE); for real matrices it equals the transpose. Careful: \(*\) can also mean \"optimal\" (\(\theta^*\)) or convolution.

Seen in:

Numerator versus denominator layout

\[\begin{gathered}L=\mathbf{u}^{\top}W\mathbf{x},\ \ \mathbf{u}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix},\ \mathbf{x}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\\5\end{bmatrix},\ W\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times3}:\\ \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}=\mathbf{u}\mathbf{x}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}3&4&5\\6&8&10\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{denominator layout: }2\times3,\ \text{what autograd returns}}\\ \underbrace{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{u}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}3&6\\4&8\\5&10\end{bmatrix}}_{\text{numerator layout: }3\times2}\end{gathered}\]

Each entry is the same partial derivative, \(\partial L/\partial W_{ij}=u_i x_j\) (nudge \(W_{12}\) by 0.01 and \(L\) moves by \(1\cdot4\cdot0.01\)); the layouts only disagree on how to arrange them. Denominator layout gives the derivative the shape of the thing you differentiate by (\(2\times3\), like \(W\)); numerator layout gives the transposed, Jacobian-style shape (\(3\times2\); for a vector \(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n\), a \(1\times n\) row). Autograd always returns a gradient with the parameter's own shape, so a paper written in numerator layout hands you the transpose of W.grad. Authors mix the two (Wikipedia, Matrix calculus). Rule: if a derived gradient's shape does not match the parameter, transpose and check one entry.

Seen in:

\(\nabla\) and \(\partial\)

\[\begin{gathered}\nabla_{\theta}L=\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_1},\ldots,\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_n}\right)\\ \nabla_\theta (\theta_1^2+3\theta_2)=(2\theta_1,\ 3)\end{gathered}\]

\(\partial L/\partial\theta_i\) is one partial derivative; \(\nabla_\theta L\) collects all of them, one per parameter. The subscript says which variables move; everything else is held fixed.

Seen in:

\(\arg\max\) versus \(\max\)

\[\begin{gathered}z=(2,1,0):\\ \max_{j} z_j=2\\ \arg\max_{j} z_j=1\ \ (\text{the first entry})\end{gathered}\]

\(\max\) returns the best value; \(\arg\max\) returns where it is (which token, which parameters). Greedy decoding is an \(\arg\max\); \(\max_{\pi}\) in an RL objective means \"choose the policy that makes this largest\".

Seen in:

\(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\): the subscript says what is random

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)]=\sum_x p(x)f(x)\\ \text{fair die: }\mathbb{E}[x]=\tfrac{1+2+\cdots+6}{6}=3.5\end{gathered}\]

Read \(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[\cdot]\) as \"the average of the bracket when x is drawn from p\". Several variables under the E (\(\mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_0,\boldsymbol\epsilon}\)) are all random. A hat, \(\hat{\mathbb{E}}\), or \(\frac1N\sum\), means a sample average you actually compute. When the distribution depends on the parameters (RLHF), you cannot just differentiate inside.

Seen in:

\(\sim\) means "drawn from"

\[\begin{gathered}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\\ (x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}\end{gathered}\]

Between two things, a tilde means \"is distributed as\" or \"is sampled from\". On top of a letter (\(\widetilde{r}\)) it means a modified version. In \(a \sim b\) between numbers, some authors mean \"roughly\".

Seen in:

\(\log\) is the natural log

\[\begin{gathered}\log x=\ln x\ \ (\text{nats})\\ \text{bits}=\frac{\text{nats}}{\ln 2}\\ 1\ \text{nat}=1.443\ \text{bits}\end{gathered}\]

ML papers mean base \(e\) unless they say bits or write \(\log_2\). Information theory books often default to base 2. A loss of 0.788 nats per token is 1.137 bits per token.

Seen in:

The indicator \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\)

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbb{1}[y=k]=\begin{cases}1&\text{if } y=k\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\\ -\sum_k\mathbb{1}[y=k]\log p_k=-\log p_y\end{gathered}\]

Also written \(\mathbf{1}\{\cdot\}\), \(I(\cdot)\) or \([\cdot]\) (the Iverson bracket). It turns a condition into 0 or 1, which is how one-hot labels pick out a single term of a sum.

Seen in:

Norms \(\|\cdot\|\)

\[\begin{gathered}\mathbf{x}=(3,4):\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_2=5\\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_1=7\\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty=4\\ \|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2=25\end{gathered}\]

No subscript usually means the Euclidean (L2) norm; \(\|A\|_F\) is the Frobenius norm, the L2 norm of all a matrix's entries. A squared norm is a sum of squares, so \(\|\boldsymbol\epsilon-\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta\|^2\) is a squared error. Single bars \(|o|\) mean absolute value, or the size of a set or sequence.

Seen in:

\(:=\), \(\triangleq\) versus \(=\)

\[\begin{gathered}L_{\text{simple}}:=\ldots\\ \hat{L}\triangleq\ldots\\ a\equiv b\\ a\approx b\end{gathered}\]

\(:=\), \(\triangleq\) and often \(\equiv\) introduce a definition: nothing to check, just a new name. \(=\) claims two things are equal (worth checking). \(\approx\) is an approximation; \(\equiv\) can also mean \"identically equal\" or congruence.

Seen in:

\(\propto\) (proportional to)

\[\begin{gathered}p(\theta\mid D)\propto p(D\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\\ \mathrm{softmax}(z)_j\propto e^{z_j}\end{gathered}\]

Equal up to a factor that does not depend on the variable of interest. The missing factor is usually a normaliser you can compute later (dividing by the sum over j).

Seen in:

Hats, bars, tildes, stars

\[\begin{gathered}\hat{A}_t,\ \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t,\ \hat{L}\\ \bar{x},\ \bar{\alpha}_t\\ \widetilde{r}_i\\ \theta^{*},\ \pi^{*}\end{gathered}\]

Hat: estimated or predicted (and in Adam, bias-corrected). Bar: a mean, but in DDPM a cumulative product, and in complex maths a conjugate. Tilde: a transformed or approximate version. Star: optimal. Each paper defines its own; these are only the usual readings.

Seen in:

\(\mathrm{sg}[\cdot]\): stop-gradient

\[L=\log p(x\mid z_q(x))+\|\mathrm{sg}[z_e(x)]-e\|_2^2+\beta\|z_e(x)-\mathrm{sg}[e]\|_2^2\]

\(p(y\mid x;\theta)\): bar versus semicolon

\[p(y\mid x;\theta)=p_\theta(y\mid x)\]

The bar conditions on data (\"given x\"); the semicolon, or a subscript, names the parameters that define the distribution. Parameters are settings, not random events, in this notation.

\(\odot\), \(\otimes\), \(\circ\)

\[\begin{gathered}(1,2)\odot(3,4)=(3,8)\\ (1,2)^\top\otimes(3,4)=\begin{bmatrix}3&4\\6&8\end{bmatrix}\end{gathered}\]

\(\odot\) multiplies entry by entry (the Hadamard product; some write \(\circ\)). \(\otimes\) is the Kronecker or outer product. \(\circ\) between functions is composition, \((f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))\).

3. The same object in different hats

Two objects turn up on several child pages under different names. Seeing that each is one thing is the payoff of learning the areas together, and no single child can show it.

Cross-entropy wears four hats

Same scores \(z = (2, 1, 0)\) and bet \(p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\), but suppose the right word had been cat, the model's favourite. One number, 0.408 nats, read four ways:

Probability: a likelihood

\(-\ln 0.665 = 0.408\)
The negative log-likelihood of cat under a categorical model; minimising its average is maximum likelihood. {{Probability, section 7|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c#rd-s7}}

Information: a code length

0.588 bits
The same number divided by \(\ln 2\): cat's codeword length in a code built from the bet. Averaged over real data it is entropy plus KL. {{Information theory, section 3|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5#it-s3}}

Calculus: prediction minus target

\((-0.335, 0.245, 0.090)\)
Its gradient on the scores, \(p - y\), adding up to 0. {{Calculus and optimisation, section 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eb8b49ed6a0f897f3b#rd-loss}}

Contrastive learning: InfoNCE

\(\ln 3 - 0.408 = 0.691\)
Read cat as the positive and dog and sat as two negatives with similarities 2, 1 and 0: 0.408 is the InfoNCE loss with \(N = 3\), and it certifies at least 0.691 nats of mutual information by \(I \ge \ln N - L\) ({{van den Oord et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748}}). {{Information theory, section 9|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5#it-s9}}

With the real answer, sat, the loss is 2.408 and the bound \(\ln 3 - 2.408\) is negative: it certifies nothing. The bound can never exceed \(\ln N\), which is why contrastive models such as CLIP train with huge batches (32,768 pairs; {{Radford et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020}}).

Curvature is read three ways

For a yes-or-no prediction with score \(z\) and probability \(p = \sigma(z)\), three quantities from three areas are the same number, \(p(1 - p)\): 0.25 at \(z = 0\) (its largest value) and 0.09 at \(z = \ln 9 = 2.197\) (\(p = 0.9\)). Drag \(z\) in the {{Gradient lab|#t-grad}}, panel 4, which computes each one by its own route.

Optimisation: the Hessian

The second derivative of binary cross-entropy in \(z\) is \(p(1 - p)\), whatever the label \(y\). {{Calculus and optimisation, section 3|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eb8b49ed6a0f897f3b#rd-hess}}

Statistics: a variance

A Bernoulli label that is 1 with probability \(p\) has variance \(p(1 - p)\). {{Probability, section 3|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c#rd-s3}}

Probability: the Fisher information

The score (the slope of the log-likelihood in \(z\)) is \(y - p\); its variance, the Fisher information, is \(p(1 - p)\). {{Statistics, section 2|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b#rd-s2}}

A confident prediction therefore sits in a flat part of the loss. On the tiny model the many-word version holds: the Hessian of the loss in the scores, \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top\), is exactly the covariance matrix of the one-hot draw, with per-word variances \(p_i(1 - p_i) = (0.223, 0.185, 0.082)\) (a simulation of 200,000 draws gives 0.222, 0.185, 0.081), and its largest eigenvalue, times \(\lVert x \rVert^2 = 5\), is section 2's \(\lambda_{\max} = 1.855\).

Why this matters for natural gradients, and a correction to the old page

For the matched pairs (sigmoid with binary cross-entropy, softmax with cross-entropy, identity with squared error) the curvature in the scores does not depend on the label, so it equals its own average over labels, the Fisher. That is why natural-gradient methods and K-FAC precondition with the Fisher, and why the curvature of TRPO's KL constraint is the Fisher matrix. A correction to the old page: the equality is exact with respect to the scores only. With respect to the weights of a deep network the Hessian gains an extra term (the residual \(p - y\) times the curvature of the network itself), so there the Fisher equals the Hessian only approximately; it is exactly the "generalised Gauss-Newton" part of it ({{Martens 2020, New insights and perspectives on the natural gradient method|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.1193}}).

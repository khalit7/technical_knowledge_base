6. Statistics: was the improvement real?

The question this step raises. On "the cat" the loss fell from 2.408 to 1.773. That was guaranteed: the step was computed from this very sentence. The real question is whether the model got better at text it has not seen, and one sentence cannot tell you.

The picture: one noisy measurement against the average of many

Score the model before and after the step on held-out sentences (text kept aside, never trained on), and record, for each sentence, how much its loss changed. Some sentences improve, some get worse; the changes scatter. Below, each dot is one sentence's change, the thick line is the running average and the band is its 95% interval. With one sentence the band is so wide it says nothing; as sentences accumulate it narrows. illustrative The changes are simulated: drawn at random around a true average of \(-0.1\) nats with a spread of 0.5.

The words, then the formula

The mean of \(n\) measurements is their average. Their standard deviation \(s\) is their typical distance from the mean (the square root of the average squared distance, the variance). The mean is itself uncertain, and its uncertainty, the standard error, shrinks with the square root of \(n\):

\[ \bar{d} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i, \qquad \mathrm{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad \text{95\% interval: } \bar{d} \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE} \]
\(d_i\)
the change in loss on sentence \(i\) (after minus before; negative means better).
\(\bar{d}\)
the mean change (the bar means "average").
\(s\)
the standard deviation of the individual changes.
1.96
for a bell-shaped (normal) spread, 95% of the probability lies within 1.96 standard deviations of the centre.

In plain words: averaging \(n\) noisy numbers divides the noise by \(\sqrt{n}\). Four times as much data halves the error bar; a hundred times as much divides it by ten.

The numbers
With a spread of \(s = 0.5\) nats per sentence, the standard error is 0.5 for one sentence, 0.25 for 4, 0.125 for 16, 0.05 for 100 and 0.025 for 400. A true improvement of 0.1 nats is about as big as the 95% half-width at 100 sentences (\(1.96 \times 0.05 = 0.098\)), so around 100 is where it starts to show; at 400 it is clear. For an accuracy near 50% on 500 questions, each answer is a 0-or-1 with spread \(\sqrt{0.5 \times 0.5} = 0.5\), so the standard error is \(\sqrt{0.25/500} = 0.022\) and the 95% interval is about \(\pm 4.4\) points: a 1-point gain on a 500-question benchmark is invisible.

Why 1.96 works even though one sentence's change is not bell-shaped: the central limit theorem says the average of many independent measurements is approximately normal whatever the shape of each one, with standard deviation \(s/\sqrt{n}\).

Compare on the same sentences. Sentences differ wildly in difficulty: a model's loss might range over several nats from one sentence to the next, while the before-and-after change on each sentence varies far less illustrative. Subtracting per sentence (a paired comparison) cancels the shared difficulty, which is why the dots above are per-sentence changes rather than two separate averages. Comparing two unpaired averages would carry all the difficulty noise twice and need far more sentences for the same certainty.

Optional: the same square root inside training (minibatch noise)

The same \(\sqrt{n}\) runs through training itself: a minibatch gradient is the average of the gradients of \(B\) examples, a noisy estimate of the full-data gradient whose noise shrinks like \(1/\sqrt{B}\). That noise is why stochastic gradient descent wobbles, and part of why it escapes saddles.

What it is used for in ML
Error bars on benchmark scores and on the difference between two checkpoints; deciding how many evaluation examples you need; A/B tests; variance across random seeds; batch size and gradient noise; refusing to believe a one-point gain on a small evaluation set.

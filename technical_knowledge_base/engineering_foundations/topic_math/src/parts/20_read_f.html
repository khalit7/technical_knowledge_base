5. Optimisation: how far to step

The question this step raises. The gradient says which way is downhill. How far should the model move? A tiny step wastes time; a huge one might jump over the valley and land higher than it started.

The picture: one step, before and after

The model moves every weight a little against its gradient. Press play to watch one step of the tiny model: the gradient, the new weights, the new scores and the new bet, with the loss falling. Then choose a bigger step size and run it again.

The words, then the formula

Gradient descent replaces every weight by itself minus a small multiple of its slope:

\[ W \leftarrow W - \eta\, \nabla_W L \]
\(\leftarrow\)
"replace with": the left side takes the value computed on the right.
\(\eta\)
the learning rate (Greek "eta"), the step size, a positive number you choose.
\(\nabla_W L\)
the gradient from section 4, the same shape as \(W\).

In plain words: move each number a little in the direction that lowers the loss, in proportion to how much it matters.

The numbers
With \(\eta = 0.1\) the rows become cat \((0.867, -0.067)\), dog \((-0.049, 0.976)\), sat \((1.182, -1.909)\). The new scores are \((1.667, 0.878, 0.455)\), the bet \((0.571, 0.259, 0.170)\) and the loss 1.773, down from 2.408. A shortcut shows why: the new scores are \(W'x = Wx - \eta\, g\, x^\top x\) \(= z - \eta \lVert x \rVert^2 g\), and \(\lVert x \rVert^2 = 5\), so each score moves by \(-0.5\) times its gradient. With \(\eta = 0.3\) one step already flips the bet to sat (0.459, loss 0.778); with \(\eta = 1\) the loss drops to 0.011. Repeating \(\eta = 0.1\) for 20 steps brings it to 0.083.

Curvature: why too big a step blows up

The slope changes as you move; how fast it changes is the curvature, the second derivative. Take the simplest bowl, \(L(\theta) = \frac12 \lambda \theta^2\), whose slope is \(\lambda\theta\) and whose curvature is the constant \(\lambda\) (Greek "lambda"). One gradient step gives

\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \lambda \theta = (1 - \eta\lambda)\,\theta \]

so each step multiplies the distance from the bottom by \(1 - \eta\lambda\). That shrinks the distance only if \(|1 - \eta\lambda| < 1\), that is if \(0 < \eta < 2/\lambda\). Between \(1/\lambda\) and \(2/\lambda\) the steps overshoot to the other side but still shrink; above \(2/\lambda\) every step lands further away than the last. Watch both sides of the line:

With many weights, the curvature is a matrix, the Hessian \(H\), holding the second derivative for every pair of weights. Its eigenvalues are the curvatures along its principal directions, the directions in which the bowl is a simple parabola. Gradient descent stays stable only if \(\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}\), with \(\lambda_{\max}\) the largest eigenvalue: the steepest direction sets the limit for all of them. The condition number \(\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) says how lopsided the bowl is. Because the step must be small enough for the steepest direction, the flattest one creeps: for curvatures 1 and 10 (\(\kappa = 10\)) the limit is \(\eta = 0.2\), the best fixed step \(2/11 = 0.182\) shrinks the error only to \(9/11 = 0.818\) of itself per step, and cutting it a thousandfold takes 35 steps. That long narrow valley is the picture behind most slow training.

The numbers for the tiny model
For softmax cross-entropy the Hessian with respect to the scores is \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top\) (a matrix with \(p_i(1 - p_i)\) on the diagonal and \(-p_i p_j\) elsewhere). At \(p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\) its eigenvalues are 0, 0.119 and 0.371. With respect to \(W\) each is multiplied by \(\lVert x \rVert^2 = 5\), so \(\lambda_{\max} = 1.855\) and the local limit is \(\eta \approx 2/1.855 = 1.08\). The step with \(\eta = 1\) was just inside it. The limit moves as training goes: as the bet grows confident, \(p(1 - p)\) shrinks and the bowl flattens.
Optional: what practical optimisers (Newton, Adam, normalisation) do about curvature, and convexity

What practical optimisers do about it (intuition only). Newton's method divides the gradient by the curvature, \(-H^{-1} g\), so every direction gets the right step at once; for a billion weights \(H\) has \(10^{18}\) entries, so it is out of reach, but boosted trees use it: each XGBoost leaf takes the Newton step \(-G/(H + \lambda)\) from its summed gradients and curvatures (this \(\lambda\) is a regularisation strength, not an eigenvalue). Adam gives each weight its own step size, dividing by the square root of an exponential moving average of that weight's squared gradients; its authors relate that average to the diagonal of the Fisher information, a curvature-like quantity met again below, but it is not a Newton step ({{Kingma and Ba 2014|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980}}). Normalisation layers and careful initialisation make the bowl rounder (a smaller \(\kappa\)). In full-batch training of real networks, the top curvature tends to rise until it sits just above \(2/\eta\), and training still progresses at that "edge of stability" ({{Cohen et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00065}}).

The softmax cross-entropy loss is convex in the scores (its Hessian has no negative eigenvalues: every chord lies above the curve, so any minimum is the global one). A deep network's loss in its weights is not, and in high dimensions most points where the gradient is zero are saddles rather than minima ({{Dauphin et al. 2014|https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2572}}).

What it is used for in ML
Choosing and scheduling learning rates (warmup exists partly because curvature is high early); diagnosing a loss that oscillates and then explodes right after the learning rate was raised; why Adam, momentum, normalisation and good initialisation help; second-order methods (Newton, L-BFGS, K-FAC, XGBoost's leaves).

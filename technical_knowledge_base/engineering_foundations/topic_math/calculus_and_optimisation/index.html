A gradient is a local straight-line model of the loss; a Hessian is a local bowl-shaped model; every optimiser is a rule for how far to trust that model. Gradient descent trusts the straight line for a small step. Newton's method jumps to the bottom of the bowl. Momentum, Adam, L-BFGS and trust regions are compromises between the two, and convexity and constraints say when the answer they find is the right one.

1. The derivative: a slope, and the best straight-line fit

The question. The loss depends on a weight. If I nudge the weight a little, how much does the loss move, and in which direction?

The picture

Draw the curve f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2 and pick the point x=3x = 3, where f=9f = 9. Draw a straight line through that point and a second point a distance hh to the right: a secant. Its slope is rise over run. Now slide the second point towards the first. The secant swings round and settles on one line that just grazes the curve: the tangent. The slope of the tangent is the derivative.

The secant through (3,9)(3, 9) and (3+h,(3+h)2)(3+h, (3+h)^2) (orange) turns into the tangent (blue) as hh shrinks. Its slope is 6+h6 + h, heading for 6; the gap between the tangent line and the curve at 3+h3 + h is exactly h2h^2.

The words, then the formula

The derivative is the limit of the secant slope as the gap shrinks to zero:

f′(x)=limh→0⁡f(x+h)−f(x)hf'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}
f′(x)f'(x)
the derivative of ff at xx, also written dfdx\frac{df}{dx} ("d f by d x"). It is a number for each xx, so it is itself a function.
hh
a small change in the input.
limh→0⁡\lim_{h \to 0}
"the limit as hh goes to 0": the value the expression settles on as hh gets as close to zero as you like, without being zero (at zero the fraction would be 0/0).

In plain words: the derivative is how many units the output moves per unit the input moves, measured over a gap so small that the curve looks straight.

The numbers
For f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2 at 3: (3+h)2−9h=6h+h2h=6+h\frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = 6 + h. With h=1,0.1,0.01,0.001h = 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 the secant slopes are 7, 6.1, 6.01, 6.001, and the limit is f′(3)=6f'(3) = 6. In general (x+h)2−x2h=2x+h→2x\frac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} = 2x + h \to 2x, so f′(x)=2xf'(x) = 2x.

The second reading: the best linear approximation

Rearranging the definition gives the reading that matters most for optimisation. Near xx, the function is a straight line plus an error that shrinks faster than the step:

f(x+h)=f(x)+f′(x)h+r(h),r(h)h→0 as h→0f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)\,h + r(h), \qquad \frac{r(h)}{h} \to 0 \text{ as } h \to 0

r(h)r(h) is the remainder, the part the line misses. For x2x^2 at 3 the line is 9+6h9 + 6h and the remainder is exactly h2h^2: 1, 0.01, 0.0001, 0.000001 for the four gaps above. Halve the step and the error quarters. No other straight line has that property, which is why the tangent is called the best linear approximation: any other slope mm leaves an error of about (6−m)h(6 - m)h, which shrinks only as fast as hh itself.

What it is used for in ML
Every gradient-based optimiser rests on this second reading: for a small enough step the loss changes by (slope times step). Gradient checking (compare the analytic slope with (f(x+h)−f(x−h))/2h(f(x+h) - f(x-h))/2h) rests on the first. The root's Gradient lab (tab Gradient lab, panel 1) runs that check on five losses.

The tiny model. Hold everything fixed except the score of sat, s=zsats = z_{\text{sat}}. The loss is L(s)=−s+ln⁡(e2+e1+es)L(s) = -s + \ln(e^2 + e^1 + e^s), and at s=0s = 0 its slope is −0.910-0.910 (section 5 derives it). 3. Many inputs: partial derivatives, the gradient, and steepest ascent

The question. A model has millions of weights, not one. Which way should all of them move together so the loss falls fastest?

The picture

A function of two inputs is a landscape: the inputs are east and north, the output is height. A contour joins points of equal height, like the lines on a hiking map. Stand at a point and turn on the spot: in some directions the ground rises, in others it falls, and along the contour it is level. The gradient is the arrow pointing straight up the steepest slope, crossing the contours at right angles.

The bowl f(x,y)=x2+3y2f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2 at the point (1,1)(1, 1). Grey: contours. Blue: the gradient (2,6)(2, 6). Orange: the unit direction uu you face. The slope you feel, Duf=∇f⋅uD_u f = \nabla f \cdot u, is largest (6.325) when uu points along the gradient, zero along the contour, and most negative pointing the opposite way.

The words, then the formulas

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative taken along one input while the others are held fixed. For f(x,y)=x2+3y2f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2, holding yy fixed gives ∂f/∂x=2x\partial f/\partial x = 2x (the 3y23y^2 is a constant, slope 0), and holding xx fixed gives ∂f/∂y=6y\partial f/\partial y = 6y. The curly ∂\partial ("partial") signals that other inputs exist and are frozen.

The gradient stacks all the partial derivatives into one vector with the same shape as the input:

∇f(θ)=(∂f∂θ1,∂f∂θ2,…,∂f∂θn)\nabla f(\theta) = \Big( \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_n} \Big)
θ\theta
the vector of all inputs (in ML, all the weights), θ=(θ1,…,θn)\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n). Greek "theta".
∇f\nabla f
"nabla f" or "grad f": the gradient, a vector of nn slopes. Often written gg.

The directional derivative is the slope along any unit direction uu (a vector of length 1): Duf=limt→0⁡f(θ+tu)−f(θ)tD_u f = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(\theta + t u) - f(\theta)}{t}. Using the linear approximation in every coordinate at once, f(θ+tu)≈f(θ)+t∑i∂f∂θiuif(\theta + t u) \approx f(\theta) + t \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_i} u_i, so

Duf=∇f⋅u=‖∇f‖‖u‖cos⁡ϕ=‖∇f‖cos⁡ϕD_u f = \nabla f \cdot u = \lVert \nabla f \rVert \, \lVert u \rVert \cos \phi = \lVert \nabla f \rVert \cos \phi

where ϕ\phi is the angle between uu and the gradient and ‖⋅‖\lVert \cdot \rVert is length. The cosine is largest (1) at ϕ=0\phi = 0: the gradient is the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is that steepest slope. At ϕ=180°\phi = 180° the slope is −‖∇f‖-\lVert \nabla f \rVert: −∇f-\nabla f is steepest descent. At ϕ=90°\phi = 90° the slope is 0, so the gradient is perpendicular to the contour.

The numbers
At (1,1)(1, 1): ∇f=(2,6)\nabla f = (2, 6), length 40=6.325\sqrt{40} = 6.325. Facing east, u=(1,0)u = (1, 0): slope 2. Facing north-east, u=(1,1)/2u = (1, 1)/\sqrt2: slope (2+6)/2=5.657(2 + 6)/\sqrt2 = 5.657. Facing along the gradient: 6.325, and no direction does better. The tiny model: the gradient of the loss with respect to the three scores is g=(0.665,0.245,−0.910)g = (0.665, 0.245, -0.910), length 1.153. Moving the cat score alone has slope 0.665; the sat score alone, −0.910-0.910; the best unit move, −g/‖g‖=(−0.577,−0.212,0.789)-g/\lVert g \rVert = (-0.577, -0.212, 0.789), lowers the loss at rate 1.153, faster than any single score could.

A vector function, one with several outputs, has one gradient per output; stacked as rows they form the Jacobian matrix. It belongs to the sibling page (Matrix calculus and backprop, section 2); this page needs only gradients of the one scalar that matters, the loss.

What it is used for in ML
Gradient descent moves every weight along −∇L-\nabla L, the steepest local descent. "Steepest" depends on how you measure length: with a different norm (a per-coordinate scale, as in Adam; a curvature-weighted one, as in Newton and natural gradient) the steepest direction changes, which is a clean way to see every optimiser (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.4).

4. Taylor expansion: the model every optimiser uses

The question. The straight-line approximation ignores how the slope itself changes. How much better is a curved approximation, and how many terms do we need?

The picture

Near a point, approximate the curve first by its tangent line (order 1), then by the parabola that matches its value, slope and bend (order 2). The parabola hugs the curve over a much wider range.

f(x)=exf(x) = e^x (grey) with its first-order (orange) and second-order (blue) Taylor models at aa; the readouts compare them with ff at a+0.5a + 0.5.

The words, then the formulas

In one variable, with the second derivative f′′f'' (the slope of the slope):

f(a+h)≈f(a)+f′(a)h+12f′′(a)h2f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h + \tfrac12 f''(a)\,h^2

The 12\tfrac12 makes the parabola's second derivative equal f′′(a)f''(a): differentiating 12f′′(a)h2\tfrac12 f''(a) h^2 twice gives f′′(a)f''(a). Taylor's theorem says the error of the order-kk model shrinks like hk+1h^{k+1} when ff is smooth enough (Nocedal and Wright, Theorem 2.1, states the multivariate version optimisers use). For exe^x at a=0a = 0 and h=0.5h = 0.5: order 1 gives 1.5, order 2 gives 1.625, the truth is 1.6487.

With many inputs the slope becomes the gradient gg and the bend becomes a matrix, the Hessian HH, whose entry Hij=∂2L/∂θi∂θjH_{ij} = \partial^2 L / \partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j says how the slope in direction ii changes as you move in direction jj:

L(θ+δ)≈L(θ)+g⊤δ+12δ⊤HδL(\theta + \delta) \approx L(\theta) + g^\top \delta + \tfrac12\, \delta^\top H \delta
δ\delta
the step, a vector the size of θ\theta (Greek "delta").
g⊤δg^\top \delta
the gradient dotted with the step, ∑igiδi\sum_i g_i \delta_i: the linear (slope) term.
δ⊤Hδ\delta^\top H \delta
∑i,jδiHijδj\sum_{i,j} \delta_i H_{ij} \delta_j: the curvature felt along the step, one number.

5. The gradient of every standard loss: prediction minus target

The question. Backprop starts from the gradient of the loss with respect to the model's raw output. What is it for each loss, and why do they all look the same?

Setup and words. The model's raw output before any squashing is the logit zz (a score, any real number). A final activation turns it into a prediction: nothing (identity) for regression, the sigmoid σ\sigma for yes/no, the softmax for one of KK classes. The loss compares the prediction with the target yy. The gradient ∂L/∂z\partial L / \partial z is what backprop pushes into the network, so it is the one derivative worth knowing by heart. The root's Gradient lab (panel 1, Loss explorer) checks each one numerically with sliders; here is each derivation in full.

Mean squared error, and the factor of a half

L=12(yˆ−y)2, yˆ=z⇒∂L∂z=(yˆ−y)⋅1=yˆ−yL = \tfrac12 (\hat y - y)^2,\ \hat y = z \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = (\hat y - y) \cdot 1 = \hat y - y

The chain rule: the slope of 12u2\tfrac12 u^2 is uu, and yˆ=z\hat y = z has slope 1. The half is a convention. Without it, L=(yˆ−y)2L = (\hat y - y)^2 has gradient 2(yˆ−y)2(\hat y - y); averaged over a batch of nn, L=1n∑i(yˆi−yi)2L = \frac1n \sum_i (\hat y_i - y_i)^2 gives each example the gradient 2n(yˆi−yi)\frac{2}{n}(\hat y_i - y_i) (the old page's "2/n2/n", which the root's Gradient lab corrected by stating both conventions). PyTorch's MSELoss has no half. The constant only rescales the learning rate. Example: z=1.5z = 1.5, y=1y = 1: with the half, L=0.125L = 0.125 and gradient 0.5; without it, L=0.25L = 0.25 and gradient 1.0.

For linear regression, yˆ=Xw\hat y = Xw, with the batch-mean loss without the half: ∇wL=2nX⊤(Xw−y)\nabla_w L = \frac{2}{n} X^\top (Xw - y), and setting it to zero gives the normal equations X⊤Xw=X⊤yX^\top X w = X^\top y (XX is the n×dn \times d table of inputs, one row per example). At the optimum the residual Xw−yXw - y is perpendicular to every column of XX. Binary cross-entropy with a sigmoid

p=σ(z),L=−[yln⁡p+(1−y)ln⁡(1−p)],y∈{0,1}p = \sigma(z), \qquad L = -\big[ y \ln p + (1 - y) \ln (1 - p) \big], \qquad y \in \{0, 1\}

Two slopes, then the chain rule. The slope of the loss in pp is −yp+1−y1−p=p−yp(1−p)-\frac{y}{p} + \frac{1 - y}{1 - p} = \frac{p - y}{p(1 - p)} (put both over p(1−p)p(1-p)); it blows up as p→0p \to 0 or 1. The slope of pp in zz is p(1−p)p(1 - p) (section 2). Multiply:

∂L∂z=p−yp(1−p)⋅p(1−p)=p−y\frac{\partial L}{\partial z} = \frac{p - y}{p(1 - p)} \cdot p(1 - p) = p - y

The p(1−p)p(1-p) cancels exactly, so nothing divides by a saturated probability. The loss itself is computed stably on the logit as max⁡(z,0)−yz+ln⁡(1+e−|z|)\max(z, 0) - yz + \ln(1 + e^{-|z|}), which never exponentiates a large positive number (PyTorch's BCEWithLogitsLoss). 6. Critical points and the Hessian: minimum, maximum or saddle

The question. The gradient is zero. Have we found the bottom, or are we balanced on a ridge?

The picture

Where the gradient is zero the ground is flat in every direction: a critical (or stationary) point. Four shapes share that flatness: a bowl (minimum), an upside-down bowl (maximum), a horse saddle (up in one direction, down in another), and a trough flat along its floor. Only the second derivatives tell them apart.

Contours of four functions around the origin, each with its Hessian's eigenvectors drawn as arrows (blue: positive eigenvalue, the surface curves up; orange: negative, it curves down; grey: zero, drawn short). The other arrows are proportional to the size of the eigenvalue.

The words, then the formulas

The Hessian is the n×nn \times n table of second derivatives, Hij=∂2L∂θi∂θjH_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \theta_i\, \partial \theta_j}. When those second derivatives are continuous, the order does not matter (Schwarz's theorem: ∂i∂jL=∂j∂iL\partial_i \partial_j L = \partial_j \partial_i L), so HH is symmetric. Example: f=x2yf = x^2 y has fx=2xyf_x = 2xy, fy=x2f_y = x^2, and both mixed derivatives equal 2x2x.

A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues λi\lambda_i and perpendicular eigenvectors uiu_i (the spectral theorem; Linear algebra for ML, sections 10 and 11). Along a unit eigenvector the Taylor model at a critical point (g=0g = 0) is a plain parabola:

L(θ∗+tui)≈L(θ∗)+12λit2L(\theta^* + t\,u_i) \approx L(\theta^*) + \tfrac12 \lambda_i\, t^2

So each eigenvalue is the curvature along its direction, and the second-derivative test follows:

7. Gradient descent, derived from the first-order model

The question. "Step against the gradient" sounds obvious. What problem does it actually solve, and when is the loss guaranteed to fall?

The first-order model L(θ)+g⊤δL(\theta) + g^\top \delta is a tilted plane with no bottom: minimising it alone says "step infinitely far downhill". So add a penalty for stepping far from where the model was measured, with a strength 1/η1/\eta:

δ∗=arg⁡minδ⁡[L(θ)+g⊤δ+12η‖δ‖2]\delta^* = \arg\min_\delta \Big[ L(\theta) + g^\top \delta + \frac{1}{2\eta} \lVert \delta \rVert^2 \Big]

(arg⁡minδ⁡\arg\min_\delta means "the δ\delta that makes this smallest".) The bracket is a bowl in δ\delta; its gradient is g+δ/ηg + \delta/\eta, which is zero at δ∗=−ηg\delta^* = -\eta\, g. That is gradient descent:

θk+1=θk−η∇L(θk)\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \eta\, \nabla L(\theta_k)

In plain words: the learning rate η\eta is how far you trust a straight-line model. Replace ‖δ‖2\lVert \delta \rVert^2 by another distance and you get another optimiser: a per-coordinate scaling gives Adagrad and Adam's shape, δ⊤Hδ\delta^\top H \delta gives Newton, a KL divergence gives natural gradient.

When is a decrease guaranteed? The descent lemma

Suppose the curvature never exceeds β\beta in any direction, H⪯βIH \preceq \beta I (written: βI−H\beta I - H has no negative eigenvalue; such an LL is called β\beta-smooth). Then the second-order term is at most β2‖δ‖2\frac{\beta}{2}\lVert\delta\rVert^2, so for the step δ=−ηg\delta = -\eta g:

L(θ−ηg)≤L(θ)−η‖g‖2+βη22‖g‖2=L(θ)−η(1−βη2)‖g‖2L(\theta - \eta g) \le L(\theta) - \eta \lVert g \rVert^2 + \frac{\beta \eta^2}{2} \lVert g \rVert^2 = L(\theta) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{\beta\eta}{2}\Big)\lVert g \rVert^2

The bracket is positive whenever η<2/β\eta \lt 2/\beta: the loss must fall. The guaranteed fall is largest at η=1/β\eta = 1/\beta, where it is ‖g‖2/(2β)\lVert g \rVert^2 / (2\beta) (Boyd and Vandenberghe, equation 9.17). Example: f=x2+3y2f = x^2 + 3y^2 has β=6\beta = 6; from (1,1)(1, 1), where ‖g‖2=40\lVert g \rVert^2 = 40, the step η=1/6\eta = 1/6 must lower ff by at least 40/12=3.3340/12 = 3.33 (it lowers it from 4 to 0.444, a fall of 3.56).

What it is used for in ML
The 2/β2/\beta bound is the stability limit of section 8; "lower the learning rate" is the first fix for a loss that rises. 8. The learning-rate limit 2/λmax⁡2/\lambda_{\max} and the rate (κ−1)/(κ+1)(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1), derived

The question. Why does training blow up above some learning rate, and why is it slow in a long narrow valley even at the best one?

Near a minimum every smooth loss looks like its second-order Taylor model, so study a quadratic bowl L(θ)=12θ⊤HθL(\theta) = \tfrac12 \theta^\top H \theta with minimum at 0. Its gradient is HθH\theta, so one step is

θk+1=θk−ηHθk=(I−ηH)θk\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \eta H \theta_k = (I - \eta H)\,\theta_k

Step 1: split into eigen-directions. Write θ=∑iciui\theta = \sum_i c_i u_i along the Hessian's eigenvectors. Since Hui=λiuiH u_i = \lambda_i u_i, the matrix I−ηHI - \eta H multiplies the coordinate cic_i by the single number 1−ηλi1 - \eta\lambda_i. The directions do not interact: after kk steps, ci(k)=(1−ηλi)kci(0)c_i^{(k)} = (1 - \eta\lambda_i)^k\, c_i^{(0)}.

Step 2: the stability limit. cic_i shrinks only if |1−ηλi|<1|1 - \eta\lambda_i| \lt 1, that is 0<ηλi<20 \lt \eta\lambda_i \lt 2. With ηλi\eta\lambda_i between 1 and 2 the coordinate flips sign each step but shrinks (oscillates inward); above 2 it flips and grows. Every direction must be stable, and the steepest is the hardest, so

η<2λmax⁡\eta \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}

Step 3: the rate. The error shrinks per step by the worst factor, r(η)=maxi⁡|1−ηλi|r(\eta) = \max_i |1 - \eta\lambda_i|. Because |1−ηλ||1 - \eta\lambda| is V-shaped in λ\lambda, the worst case is always at an end: r(η)=max⁡(|1−ηλmin⁡|,|1−ηλmax⁡|)r(\eta) = \max(|1 - \eta\lambda_{\min}|, |1 - \eta\lambda_{\max}|). 9. Momentum and Nesterov: the square root of the condition number

The question. Gradient descent zig-zags across a narrow valley and creeps along it. Can remembering the previous step fix both?

Heavy-ball momentum (Polyak 1964) keeps a running velocity. In the form used by Goh 2017, "Why Momentum Really Works":

zk+1=βzk+∇L(θk),θk+1=θk−αzk+1z_{k+1} = \beta z_k + \nabla L(\theta_k), \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha\, z_{k+1}

α\alpha is the step size, β∈[0,1)\beta \in [0, 1) the momentum (how much of the old velocity survives). Steps that agree from one iteration to the next (along the valley floor) add up, to about 1/(1−β)1/(1 - \beta) times a single step; steps that flip sign (across the walls) cancel.

On a quadratic, split into eigen-directions as before. Each coordinate now carries two numbers (position and velocity), updated by a fixed 2x2 matrix whose eigenvalues set the rate. Goh's analysis (section "The Dynamics of Momentum") gives three results:

  • Stability: 0<αλi<2+2β0 \lt \alpha\lambda_i \lt 2 + 2\beta. With β=0.9\beta = 0.9 the step can be almost twice as large as plain gradient descent's limit: on diag(1, 10), up to 0.38 rather than 0.2.
  • Best settings: α=(2λmin⁡+λmax⁡)2\alpha = \Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}}}\Big)^2, β=(λmax⁡−λmin⁡λmax⁡+λmin⁡)2\beta = \Big(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}} - \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}}\Big)^2.
  • Best rate: κ−1κ+1\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}: the condition number enters through its square root.
The numbers

Nesterov's accelerated gradient (Nesterov 1983, in the form of Sutskever et al. 2013) evaluates the gradient at the point momentum is about to carry you to, rather than where you stand:

vk+1=μvk−η∇L(θk+μvk),θk+1=θk+vk+1v_{k+1} = \mu v_k - \eta \nabla L(\theta_k + \mu v_k), \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + v_{k+1}

The look-ahead corrects an overshoot one step earlier. On smooth convex functions (not just quadratics) it provably reaches a loss gap of order 1/k21/k^2 after kk steps, against 1/k1/k for gradient descent, and no method using only gradients can do better in the worst case (Nesterov, Introductory Lectures on Convex Optimization, 2004, sections 2.1 and 2.2). Heavy ball has no such guarantee beyond quadratics.

10. Newton's method, derived from the second-order model

The question. If the Taylor model includes the curvature, why not jump straight to its minimum?

Minimise the second-order model m(δ)=L+g⊤δ+12δ⊤Hδm(\delta) = L + g^\top \delta + \tfrac12 \delta^\top H \delta. Its gradient in δ\delta is g+Hδg + H\delta (the derivative of 12δ⊤Hδ\tfrac12\delta^\top H\delta is HδH\delta because HH is symmetric). Setting it to zero:

Hδ=−g⇒δNewton=−H−1gH \delta = -g \quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta_{\text{Newton}} = -H^{-1} g

This is a minimum of the model only if H≻0H \succ 0 (positive definite). In one dimension it is δ=−L′/L′′\delta = -L'/L'': the slope divided by the curvature. In eigen-coordinates it divides each direction's gradient by that direction's curvature, so flat directions get big steps and steep ones small steps, all at once: the condition number no longer matters.

Why Newton is fast near the answer, and dangerous far from it

  • Quadratic convergence. Near a minimum with H≻0H \succ 0 and a smooth Hessian, the error is roughly squared each step: the number of correct digits doubles (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.5.3). On the 31-parameter real logistic regression the loss gap after Newton steps 5 to 8 is 1.6×10−31.6 \times 10^{-3}, 6.1×10−56.1 \times 10^{-5}, 1.3×10−71.3 \times 10^{-7}, 6.4×10−136.4 \times 10^{-13}.
  • Affine invariance. Rescaling or rotating the parameters (θ=Aϕ\theta = A\phi) changes Newton's steps in exactly the matching way, so units do not matter; gradient descent's steps change shape (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.5.1).
  • Far from the minimum the model lies. The parabola is only good locally; with small curvature it sends you far (the w=8w = 8 start). The fix is damping: a backtracking line search on the Newton direction, a trust region (minimise the model only within a radius), or Levenberg-Marquardt (H+λI)−1g(H + \lambda I)^{-1} g, which slides between Newton (λ=0\lambda = 0) and a short gradient step (large λ\lambda).
  • Saddles attract it. With a negative eigenvalue, −H−1g-H^{-1}g moves uphill along that direction, towards the saddle. Dauphin et al. (2014, section 3): "the saddle point θ∗\theta^* becomes an attractor for the Newton method". Their saddle-free Newton divides by |λi||\lambda_i| instead (the Optimisation lab has it).

Why it is not used to train large networks

For nn parameters, HH has n2n^2 entries and solving Hδ=−gH\delta = -g costs about n3n^3 operations. At n=109n = 10^9: 101810^{18} entries, 2 exabytes in 16-bit numbers, before any solve. Minibatch noise makes the curvature estimate itself noisy, and the many saddles make an undamped step unsafe. So practice uses approximations that need only gradients or Hessian-vector products:

  • Gauss-Newton and the Fisher matrix: positive semidefinite stand-ins for HH (for the canonical losses of section 5, the Fisher equals the Gauss-Newton part of the Hessian; the root's Gradient lab, panel 4, and Martens 2020, section 9). Natural gradient preconditions by the inverse Fisher.
  • Hessian-free (Martens 2010): solve Hδ=−gH\delta = -g approximately by conjugate gradient, using only Hessian-vector products.
  • K-FAC (Martens and Grosse 2015): approximate each layer's block of the Fisher by a Kronecker product of two small matrices.
  • Shampoo, SOAP, Muon: per-layer matrix preconditioners in practice; owned by Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers (section 4).
  • Diagonal scalings: Adam divides by the square root of an exponential moving average of squared gradients; its authors relate that average to the diagonal of the Fisher (Kingma and Ba 2014, section 5). It is a crude per-coordinate scale, not a Newton step (no inverse curvature, and a square root).
  • Quasi-Newton: next section.

11. Quasi-Newton: BFGS and L-BFGS, and a race on real data

The question. Can we get Newton's speed without ever computing a Hessian?

Yes, by learning the curvature from how the gradient changes. After a step sk=θk+1−θks_k = \theta_{k+1} - \theta_k the gradient changes by yk=gk+1−gky_k = g_{k+1} - g_k. For a quadratic yk=Hsky_k = H s_k exactly, so ask the curvature estimate Bk+1B_{k+1} to satisfy the secant equation Bk+1sk=ykB_{k+1} s_k = y_k. It has many solutions; BFGS (Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb, Shanno, 1970) picks the one closest to the previous estimate in a weighted norm, and keeps the inverse Mk=Bk−1M_k = B_k^{-1} directly:

Mk+1=(I−ρkskyk⊤)Mk(I−ρkyksk⊤)+ρksksk⊤,ρk=1yk⊤skM_{k+1} = (I - \rho_k s_k y_k^\top)\, M_k\, (I - \rho_k y_k s_k^\top) + \rho_k s_k s_k^\top, \qquad \rho_k = \frac{1}{y_k^\top s_k}

The step is −Mkgk-M_k g_k followed by a line search. The update needs yk⊤sk>0y_k^\top s_k \gt 0 (the curvature condition; the Wolfe line-search conditions guarantee it), which keeps MM positive definite (Nocedal and Wright, Numerical Optimization, 2nd edition, chapter 6). 13. Convexity: when every minimum is the minimum

The question. Gradient descent finds a point where the gradient is zero. When can we be sure there is no better point somewhere else?

The picture

A function is convex if the straight line (chord) between any two points on its graph never dips below the graph: a bowl, not a wave. Then the bottom of the bowl is unique in value, and any flat point is it.

Between a=−1a = -1 and b=3b = 3; move the weight tt. Green dot: the function at the mixed point f(ta+(1−t)b)f(ta + (1-t)b); orange dot: the chord at the same place, tf(a)+(1−t)f(b)t f(a) + (1-t) f(b). For a convex function the green dot is never above the orange one. The last function is not convex: for some tt the chord passes below the graph.

Three equivalent tests

  1. Chord (the definition): f(tx+(1−t)y)≤tf(x)+(1−t)f(y)f(t x + (1-t) y) \le t f(x) + (1-t) f(y) for all x,yx, y and t∈[0,1]t \in [0, 1]. Example: f=x2f = x^2, x=−1x = -1, y=3y = 3, t=0.5t = 0.5: f(1)=1≤12(1+9)=5f(1) = 1 \le \tfrac12(1 + 9) = 5.
  2. Tangent (if differentiable): f(y)≥f(x)+∇f(x)⊤(y−x)f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x): the function lies above every tangent plane. So if ∇f(x)=0\nabla f(x) = 0, then f(y)≥f(x)f(y) \ge f(x) for every yy: a flat point is a global minimum (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 3.1.3).
  3. Curvature (if twice differentiable): H(x)⪰0H(x) \succeq 0 everywhere, no negative curvature in any direction (section 3.1.4). This is the practical test.

Building convex functions (section 3.2): non-negative weighted sums of convex functions are convex; a convex function of an affine map Ax+bAx + b is convex; the pointwise maximum of convex functions is convex; ln⁡∑jezj\ln \sum_j e^{z_j} (log-sum-exp) is convex. Strict convexity (H≻0H \succ 0) makes the minimiser unique.

Why deep networks are not convex. Swap two hidden units of a layer (their incoming and outgoing weights together): the network computes exactly the same function, so the loss is the same at two different points θA\theta_A and θB\theta_B. If the loss were convex, the midpoint would be at least as good; but the midpoint averages the two units into identical copies, usually a worse network. So there are many separate minima of equal value. Convexity in the logits says nothing about convexity in earlier layers' weights.

Why convex problems are easy

No bad local minima, a certificate of optimality (∇f=0\nabla f = 0, or the KKT conditions with constraints), and rates that depend only on the condition number: with μI⪯H⪯βI\mu I \preceq H \preceq \beta I (strong convexity μ>0\mu \gt 0, smoothness β\beta), gradient descent converges linearly at 1−μ/β1 - \mu/\beta per step (section 8), Nesterov at about 1−μ/β1 - \sqrt{\mu/\beta}, and Newton quadratically near the end.

Jensen's inequality

The chord definition with many points, or a whole distribution, is Jensen's inequality (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 3.1.8): for convex ff and a random variable XX,

f(𝔼[X])≤𝔼[f(X)]f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[f(X)]

(𝔼\mathbb{E} is the average, or expected value.) For a concave ff (one whose negative is convex, such as ln⁡\ln) it reverses: ln⁡𝔼[X]≥𝔼[ln⁡X]\ln \mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\ln X]. Example: XX is 1 or 4 with equal chance. ln⁡𝔼[X]=ln⁡2.5=0.916\ln \mathbb{E}[X] = \ln 2.5 = 0.916; 𝔼[ln⁡X]=12(0+1.386)=0.693\mathbb{E}[\ln X] = \tfrac12(0 + 1.386) = 0.693.

14. Constrained optimisation: Lagrange multipliers, KKT, duality

The question. Sometimes the parameters must satisfy a condition: probabilities sum to 1, a weight vector stays inside a ball, a policy stays close to the old one. How do you optimise on a constraint?

The picture

Walk along the constraint curve g(x)=0g(x) = 0 (gg here is a constraint function, not a gradient) and watch ff. Where ff stops changing, every direction you may move (tangent to the curve) is perpendicular to ∇f\nabla f. The curve's own normal is ∇g\nabla g. So at the optimum the two gradients are parallel: the contour of ff just touches the constraint.

Maximise f=x+yf = x + y on the unit circle x2+y2=1x^2 + y^2 = 1. Grey lines: contours of ff. Blue: ∇f=(1,1)\nabla f = (1, 1), the same everywhere. Orange: ∇g=(2x,2y)\nabla g = (2x, 2y), pointing out of the circle. At 45° they are parallel and ff is largest, 2\sqrt2.

The words, then the formulas

∇f(x∗)=λ∇g(x∗),ℒ(x,λ)=f(x)−λg(x)\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda\, \nabla g(x^*), \qquad \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda\, g(x)
λ\lambda
the Lagrange multiplier, the factor between the two gradients (a different λ\lambda from an eigenvalue or a penalty strength).
ℒ\mathcal L
the Lagrangian. Setting its gradient in xx to zero gives the parallel condition; setting its derivative in λ\lambda to zero gives back g(x)=0g(x) = 0. One unconstrained system replaces the constrained problem.

The multiplier is a price. If the constraint is g(x)=cg(x) = c, then λ=df∗/dc\lambda = df^*/dc: how much the best value improves per unit the constraint is loosened (the "shadow price").

Inequality constraints: the KKT conditions

To minimise f(x)f(x) subject to hi(x)≤0h_i(x) \le 0, the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions at a solution x∗x^* with multipliers μi\mu_i are (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 5.5.3):

  1. Stationarity: ∇f(x∗)+∑iμi∇hi(x∗)=0\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_i \mu_i \nabla h_i(x^*) = 0.
  2. Primal feasibility: hi(x∗)≤0h_i(x^*) \le 0.
  3. Dual feasibility: μi≥0\mu_i \ge 0 (a constraint can only push, not pull).
  4. Complementary slackness: μihi(x∗)=0\mu_i h_i(x^*) = 0: either the constraint is tight (hi=0h_i = 0) or its multiplier is zero.

For a convex problem with a strictly feasible point (Slater's condition) they are necessary and sufficient. Example: minimise (x−2)2(x - 2)^2 subject to x≤1x \le 1, so h=x−1h = x - 1. Unconstrained the answer would be 2, outside; so the constraint binds, x∗=1x^* = 1, and stationarity 2(x∗−2)+μ=02(x^* - 2) + \mu = 0 gives μ=2≥0\mu = 2 \ge 0. With x≤3x \le 3 instead, the unconstrained 2 is feasible, the constraint is slack and μ=0\mu = 0.

Duality, in brief

Minimise the Lagrangian over xx for fixed multipliers: d(μ)=minx⁡[f(x)+∑iμihi(x)]d(\mu) = \min_x \big[f(x) + \sum_i \mu_i h_i(x)\big], the dual function. For any μ≥0\mu \ge 0 it is a lower bound on the best feasible value (weak duality), because on feasible points the added terms are ≤0\le 0. The dual problem maximises that bound; for convex problems under Slater's condition the best bound equals the answer (strong duality; Boyd and Vandenberghe, sections 5.2.2 and 5.2.3). The dual is sometimes easier: the SVM's dual has one multiplier per training example and only inner products xi⊤xjx_i^\top x_j, which is what lets kernels replace them.

15. Constraints in ML: weight decay, SVMs, softmax, and the KL-constrained step

The question. Where do these multipliers show up in models you train?

Regularisation is a constraint in disguise

Minimising the loss subject to ‖w‖2≤t\lVert w \rVert^2 \le t has, by the KKT conditions, the same solution as minimising the loss plus μ‖w‖2\mu \lVert w \rVert^2 for a matching μ≥0\mu \ge 0: the weight-decay strength is the multiplier of a norm-ball constraint (a smaller ball, a larger multiplier). Regularisation covers its practice.

Support vectors are complementary slackness

The SVM minimises 12‖w‖2\tfrac12\lVert w \rVert^2 subject to yi(w⊤xi+b)≥1y_i(w^\top x_i + b) \ge 1 for every example. Complementary slackness says each example's multiplier αi\alpha_i is zero unless its constraint is tight, that is unless it sits exactly on the margin (or violates it, in the soft-margin version). The solution w=∑iαiyixiw = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i depends only on those examples: the support vectors. See Classical ML, section 5.

16. Stochastic gradients: noise, batch size, and why SGD still converges

The question. Nobody computes the gradient over the whole dataset; we use a minibatch. Why does a noisy gradient work at all, and what does the noise cost?

The full loss is an average over nn examples, L(θ)=1n∑iℓi(θ)L(\theta) = \frac1n \sum_i \ell_i(\theta), so its gradient is the average of per-example gradients. A minibatch of BB examples drawn at random gives gˆ=1B∑i∈batch∇ℓi\hat g = \frac1B \sum_{i \in \text{batch}} \nabla \ell_i. Two facts follow from basic probability:

  • Unbiased: 𝔼[gˆ]=∇L\mathbb{E}[\hat g] = \nabla L. On average the minibatch points the right way.
  • Variance falls as 1/B1/B: 𝔼‖gˆ−∇L‖2=σ2B⋅n−Bn−1\mathbb{E}\lVert \hat g - \nabla L \rVert^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{B} \cdot \frac{n - B}{n - 1}, where σ2\sigma^2 is the average squared distance of one example's gradient from the mean, and the last factor corrects for sampling without replacement (it is 0 at B=nB = n).

What the noise does to convergence

Repeat the descent lemma of section 7 with gˆ\hat g and take expectations: the noise adds a term βη22⋅\frac{\beta\eta^2}{2}\cdot (variance) that does not shrink as the gradient does. So with a constant step SGD does not converge to the minimum; it converges to a region around it whose size is proportional to η\eta. For a strongly convex, smooth loss, Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal 2018 (Theorem 4.6) bound the expected gap by a term that decays geometrically plus a floor proportional to the step. With a decreasing step satisfying the Robbins-Monro conditions

∑kηk=∞,∑kηk2<∞\sum_k \eta_k = \infty, \qquad \sum_k \eta_k^2 \lt \infty

(steps large enough in total to get anywhere, small enough that the noise they add stays finite), the gap goes to zero; with ηk=β/(γ+k)\eta_k = \beta/(\gamma + k) it falls like 1/k1/k (Theorem 4.7, after Robbins and Monro 1951).

Why schedules decay the learning rate at the end of training; why a larger batch tolerates a larger step (less noise per step: the linear-scaling heuristic of Goyal et al. 2017) until the curvature limit of section 8 takes over; why L-BFGS (section 11) does not carry over to minibatches. The noise also helps: it pushes the iterate off saddles and plateaus. In practice: Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers (sections 1 and 7).

17. The landscape of a deep network: saddles, sharpness and the edge of stability

The question. A deep network's loss is not convex. Should we worry about bad local minima, and does the step-size theory of section 8 still apply?

Saddles, not local minima

Dauphin et al. 2014 gather arguments from statistical physics and random-matrix theory that for typical high-dimensional error functions "the ratio of the number of saddle points to local minima increases exponentially with the dimensionality". The intuition: at a critical point each of the nn Hessian eigenvalues must be positive for a minimum, and if signs were anything like independent, all-positive would be exponentially rare. They also report that critical points with high error tend to have a large index (many negative eigenvalues), so the minima that exist tend to have low error, and they check it on small networks trained on down-sampled MNIST and CIFAR-10. Saddles are slow because they are surrounded by plateaus; gradient descent escapes along negative curvature, slowly; Newton is attracted to them (section 10). The Optimisation lab's saddle function shows all three.

The edge of stability

Section 8 says gradient descent with step η\eta is unstable if the top Hessian eigenvalue, the sharpness, exceeds 2/η2/\eta. Cohen et al. 2021 measured the sharpness during full-batch training of real networks and found two things. Progressive sharpening: while the sharpness is below 2/η2/\eta it "tends to continually rise". The edge of stability: once it reaches 2/η2/\eta it stops there: "the maximum eigenvalue of the training loss Hessian hovers just above the value 2/(step size), and the training loss behaves non-monotonically over short timescales, yet consistently decreases over long timescales". The quadratic theory would predict divergence; the network instead adjusts its own curvature.

What it means. The step-size limit is real, but in a network it is not a fixed property of the problem: training drives the curvature to whatever the learning rate allows. A higher learning rate selects flatter regions. This is one reason the learning rate acts as a regulariser, and why sharpness-aware minimisation (SAM; Foret et al. 2021), which minimises the worst loss in a small ball around the weights, looks for flat minima on purpose. Whether flatness causes better generalisation is still debated (sharpness changes under reparameterisation: Dinh et al. 2017).

Other second-order quantities you will meet in papers

  • Influence functions estimate how the model would change without one training example, −H−1∇ℓi-H^{-1}\nabla \ell_i (Koh and Liang 2017).
  • Optimal Brain Damage prunes the weights with the smallest 12Hiiwi2\tfrac12 H_{ii} w_i^2, the second-order estimate of the loss increase when wiw_i is set to zero (LeCun et al. 1989).
  • Hessian spectra of trained networks: a bulk near zero plus a few large outliers, roughly one per class (Sagun et al. 2017, Ghorbani et al. 2019).

19. Common mistakes and misconceptions

Treating g=0g = 0 as a minimum
In high dimensions a stationary point is far more likely to be a saddle (section 17). Check the curvature: the smallest Hessian eigenvalue, estimated by power iteration on H−cIH - cI or by Lanczos.
"Blame the data" for a learning-rate blow-up
A loss that oscillates and then grows right after the learning rate was raised is the 2/λmax⁡2/\lambda_{\max} limit (section 8). In full-batch training the network may instead sit at the edge of stability, with a loss that wobbles but falls (section 17).
Quoting (κ−1)/(κ+1)(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1) as gradient descent's rate at any step
It holds only at the best fixed step 2/(λmax⁡+λmin⁡)2/(\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min}); at the safe step 1/λmax⁡1/\lambda_{\max} the rate is 1−1/κ1 - 1/\kappa, and anything above 2/λmax⁡2/\lambda_{\max} diverges on a quadratic.
Running Newton with an indefinite Hessian or from far away
Undamped, it steps towards maxima and saddles, and from a flat region it jumps far (the w=8w = 8 start in section 10 diverges). Use a line search, a trust region, H+λIH + \lambda I, or |H||H|.
"Adam is a second-order method"
It divides by the square root of a moving average of squared gradients, a diagonal quantity related to the Fisher, not the Hessian, and not inverted (section 10).
MSE for classification, or squashing twice
On a sigmoid output MSE's gradient vanishes exactly when the model is confidently wrong (203 times smaller than cross-entropy's at z=−6z = -6). Passing probabilities to BCEWithLogitsLoss or CrossEntropyLoss, which expect logits, squashes twice and shrinks gradients. Computing log(softmax(z)) in two steps underflows; use the fused log-softmax.
Forgetting which MSE convention is in use
12(yˆ−y)2\tfrac12(\hat y - y)^2 has gradient yˆ−y\hat y - y; (yˆ−y)2(\hat y - y)^2 has 2(yˆ−y)2(\hat y - y); the batch mean divides by nn. Each changes the effective learning rate by a constant (section 5).
Assuming the whole network is convex because cross-entropy is
Cross-entropy is convex in the logits; the loss in earlier layers' weights is not (the permutation argument, section 13).
Using L-BFGS with minibatches
Gradient differences then measure sampling noise as much as curvature, and the estimate degrades; L-BFGS is for full-batch, deterministic objectives (section 11).
Expecting a constant SGD step to converge
It stalls at a noise floor proportional to the step (section 16); decay the step, or grow the batch.
Mixing up the lambdas
On this page λ\lambda is an eigenvalue (sections 6 to 9), a Lagrange multiplier (section 14) and an L2 penalty (sections 11 and 12). The KKT view links the last two: a penalty strength is a multiplier.
Reading sharpness as a fixed property of the problem
In a network the curvature moves with training and is driven to 2/η2/\eta by the learning rate itself (section 17).

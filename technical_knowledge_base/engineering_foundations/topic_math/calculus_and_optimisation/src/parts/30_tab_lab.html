Optimisation lab: eight methods, four landscapes, and the local Taylor model at every step

Pick a function and a method, then step through the run. At every iterate the inspector shows the gradient, the Hessian and its eigenvalues, what kind of point the curvature says this is, and the step Newton would take from here; on the plot the dashed curves are the contours of the second-order Taylor model at that point and the arrows are the Hessian's eigenvectors (blue: curves up, orange: curves down). Click anywhere on the plot to start from there. This lab is about the second-order view; for thirteen practical optimisers raced against each other see the {{Optimiser race|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}} tab of Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers.

What to try
  • Quadratic, Newton: one step to the minimum from anywhere, whatever \(\kappa\) and rotation (section 10).
  • Quadratic, gradient descent, \(\kappa = 10\): the recommended step \(2/(\kappa + 1)\) gives the rate 0.818; raise \(\eta\) past \(2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.2\) and it diverges (section 8).
  • Quadratic, Adam, rotation 0° then 45°: per-coordinate scaling helps when the valley lines up with the axes and much less when it is diagonal.
  • Rosenbrock, gradient descent then BFGS: thousands of tiny steps along the curved valley against a few dozen; the inspector shows the Hessian is indefinite near the start (a negative eigenvalue) though the function has one minimum.
  • Saddle, start near the ridge (click just off \(y = 0\)): gradient descent lingers on the plateau and then escapes; Newton converges to the saddle at the origin (Dauphin et al.'s "attractor"); saddle-free Newton escapes to a minimum.
  • Logistic loss (real data): convex everywhere (both eigenvalues positive at every step); Newton needs about five steps; gradient descent's speed is set by the condition number at the optimum.

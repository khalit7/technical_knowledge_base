8. Convexity: when every minimum is the minimum

A function is convex if the chord between any two points on its graph never dips below the graph: a bowl, not a wave.

Between \(a = -1\) and \(b = 3\): green dot, the function at the mixed point \(f(ta + (1-t)b)\); orange dot, the chord at the same place, \(t f(a) + (1-t) f(b)\). For a convex function green is never above orange. Pick the last function and find a \(t\) where the chord passes below the graph.
  1. Chord (the definition): \(f(t x + (1-t) y) \le t f(x) + (1-t) f(y)\) for all \(x, y\) and \(t \in [0, 1]\). For \(x^2\), \(x = -1\), \(y = 3\), \(t = 0.5\): \(f(1) = 1 \le \tfrac12(1 + 9) = 5\).
  2. Tangent (if differentiable): \(f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x)\): the function lies above every tangent plane. So if \(\nabla f(x) = 0\), then \(f(y) \ge f(x)\) for every \(y\): a flat point is a global minimum (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 3.1.3).
  3. Curvature (if twice differentiable): \(H(x) \succeq 0\) everywhere (section 3.1.4). This is the practical test; strict convexity (\(H \succ 0\)) makes the minimiser unique.

Why deep networks are not convex. Swap two hidden units of a layer (their incoming and outgoing weights together): the network computes exactly the same function, so the loss is the same at two different points. If the loss were convex the midpoint would be at least as good, but the midpoint averages the two units into identical copies, usually a worse network. So there are many separate minima of equal value, and convexity in the logits says nothing about earlier layers' weights. What convexity buys: no bad local minima, a certificate of optimality (\(\nabla f = 0\), or the KKT conditions with constraints), and rates that depend only on the condition number: with \(\mu I \preceq H \preceq \beta I\), gradient descent converges linearly at \(1 - \mu/\beta\) per step (section 4), Nesterov at about \(1 - \sqrt{\mu/\beta}\), and Newton quadratically near the end.

Jensen's inequality

The chord definition over a whole distribution is Jensen's inequality (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 3.1.8): for convex \(f\), \(f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[f(X)]\), and for a concave \(f\) such as \(\ln\) it reverses. Example: \(X\) is 1 or 4 with equal chance: \(\ln \mathbb{E}[X] = \ln 2.5 = 0.916\) against \(\mathbb{E}[\ln X] = \tfrac12(0 + 1.386) = 0.693\). It is why KL divergence is never negative ({{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}):

\[ -\mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\ln(q/p)] \le \ln \mathbb{E}_p[q/p] = \ln \sum_x q(x) = 0 \]

and it gives the evidence lower bound (ELBO) of VAEs and diffusion models from \(\ln \mathbb{E}_q[p(x, z)/q(z)]\) ({{Variational inference and generative-model maths|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}); EM is coordinate ascent on that bound.

Building convex functions

Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 3.2: non-negative weighted sums of convex functions are convex; a convex function of an affine map \(Ax + b\) is convex; the pointwise maximum of convex functions is convex; \(\ln \sum_j e^{z_j}\) (log-sum-exp) is convex. These rules give every "yes" row of the table.

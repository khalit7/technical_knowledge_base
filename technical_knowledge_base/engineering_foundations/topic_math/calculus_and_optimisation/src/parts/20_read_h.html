13. Convexity: when every minimum is the minimum

The question. Gradient descent finds a point where the gradient is zero. When can we be sure there is no better point somewhere else?

The picture

A function is convex if the straight line (chord) between any two points on its graph never dips below the graph: a bowl, not a wave. Then the bottom of the bowl is unique in value, and any flat point is it.

Between \(a = -1\) and \(b = 3\); move the weight \(t\). Green dot: the function at the mixed point \(f(ta + (1-t)b)\); orange dot: the chord at the same place, \(t f(a) + (1-t) f(b)\). For a convex function the green dot is never above the orange one. The last function is not convex: for some \(t\) the chord passes below the graph.

Three equivalent tests

  1. Chord (the definition): \(f(t x + (1-t) y) \le t f(x) + (1-t) f(y)\) for all \(x, y\) and \(t \in [0, 1]\). Example: \(f = x^2\), \(x = -1\), \(y = 3\), \(t = 0.5\): \(f(1) = 1 \le \tfrac12(1 + 9) = 5\).
  2. Tangent (if differentiable): \(f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x)\): the function lies above every tangent plane. So if \(\nabla f(x) = 0\), then \(f(y) \ge f(x)\) for every \(y\): a flat point is a global minimum (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 3.1.3).
  3. Curvature (if twice differentiable): \(H(x) \succeq 0\) everywhere, no negative curvature in any direction (section 3.1.4). This is the practical test.

Building convex functions (section 3.2): non-negative weighted sums of convex functions are convex; a convex function of an affine map \(Ax + b\) is convex; the pointwise maximum of convex functions is convex; \(\ln \sum_j e^{z_j}\) (log-sum-exp) is convex. Strict convexity (\(H \succ 0\)) makes the minimiser unique.

Why deep networks are not convex. Swap two hidden units of a layer (their incoming and outgoing weights together): the network computes exactly the same function, so the loss is the same at two different points \(\theta_A\) and \(\theta_B\). If the loss were convex, the midpoint would be at least as good; but the midpoint averages the two units into identical copies, usually a worse network. So there are many separate minima of equal value. Convexity in the logits says nothing about convexity in earlier layers' weights.

Why convex problems are easy

No bad local minima, a certificate of optimality (\(\nabla f = 0\), or the KKT conditions with constraints), and rates that depend only on the condition number: with \(\mu I \preceq H \preceq \beta I\) (strong convexity \(\mu \gt 0\), smoothness \(\beta\)), gradient descent converges linearly at \(1 - \mu/\beta\) per step (section 8), Nesterov at about \(1 - \sqrt{\mu/\beta}\), and Newton quadratically near the end.

Jensen's inequality

The chord definition with many points, or a whole distribution, is Jensen's inequality (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 3.1.8): for convex \(f\) and a random variable \(X\),

\[ f(\mathbb{E}[X]) \le \mathbb{E}[f(X)] \]

(\(\mathbb{E}\) is the average, or expected value.) For a concave \(f\) (one whose negative is convex, such as \(\ln\)) it reverses: \(\ln \mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[\ln X]\). Example: \(X\) is 1 or 4 with equal chance. \(\ln \mathbb{E}[X] = \ln 2.5 = 0.916\); \(\mathbb{E}[\ln X] = \tfrac12(0 + 1.386) = 0.693\).

What it is used for in ML
KL divergence is never negative: \(-\mathrm{KL}(p \Vert q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\ln(q/p)] \le \ln \mathbb{E}_p[q/p] = \ln \sum_x q(x) = 0\) ({{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}). The evidence lower bound (ELBO) of variational autoencoders and diffusion models is Jensen applied to \(\ln \mathbb{E}_q[p(x, z)/q(z)]\) ({{Variational inference and generative-model maths|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}). EM is coordinate ascent on that bound.

14. Constrained optimisation: Lagrange multipliers, KKT, duality

The question. Sometimes the parameters must satisfy a condition: probabilities sum to 1, a weight vector stays inside a ball, a policy stays close to the old one. How do you optimise on a constraint?

The picture

Walk along the constraint curve \(g(x) = 0\) (\(g\) here is a constraint function, not a gradient) and watch \(f\). Where \(f\) stops changing, every direction you may move (tangent to the curve) is perpendicular to \(\nabla f\). The curve's own normal is \(\nabla g\). So at the optimum the two gradients are parallel: the contour of \(f\) just touches the constraint.

Maximise \(f = x + y\) on the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\). Grey lines: contours of \(f\). Blue: \(\nabla f = (1, 1)\), the same everywhere. Orange: \(\nabla g = (2x, 2y)\), pointing out of the circle. At 45° they are parallel and \(f\) is largest, \(\sqrt2\).

The words, then the formulas

\[ \nabla f(x^*) = \lambda\, \nabla g(x^*), \qquad \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda\, g(x) \]
\(\lambda\)
the Lagrange multiplier, the factor between the two gradients (a different \(\lambda\) from an eigenvalue or a penalty strength).
\(\mathcal L\)
the Lagrangian. Setting its gradient in \(x\) to zero gives the parallel condition; setting its derivative in \(\lambda\) to zero gives back \(g(x) = 0\). One unconstrained system replaces the constrained problem.

The multiplier is a price. If the constraint is \(g(x) = c\), then \(\lambda = df^*/dc\): how much the best value improves per unit the constraint is loosened (the "shadow price").

The numbers (old page, recomputed)
\((1, 1) = \lambda(2x, 2y)\) gives \(x = y = 1/(2\lambda)\); the constraint gives \(x = y = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.707\), \(\lambda = 0.707\), \(f^* = \sqrt2 = 1.414\). Check the price: on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = c\) the optimum is \(f^* = \sqrt{2c}\), whose derivative \(1/\sqrt{2c}\) is 0.707 at \(c = 1\), equal to \(\lambda\).

Inequality constraints: the KKT conditions

To minimise \(f(x)\) subject to \(h_i(x) \le 0\), the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions at a solution \(x^*\) with multipliers \(\mu_i\) are (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 5.5.3):

  1. Stationarity: \(\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_i \mu_i \nabla h_i(x^*) = 0\).
  2. Primal feasibility: \(h_i(x^*) \le 0\).
  3. Dual feasibility: \(\mu_i \ge 0\) (a constraint can only push, not pull).
  4. Complementary slackness: \(\mu_i h_i(x^*) = 0\): either the constraint is tight (\(h_i = 0\)) or its multiplier is zero.

For a convex problem with a strictly feasible point (Slater's condition) they are necessary and sufficient. Example: minimise \((x - 2)^2\) subject to \(x \le 1\), so \(h = x - 1\). Unconstrained the answer would be 2, outside; so the constraint binds, \(x^* = 1\), and stationarity \(2(x^* - 2) + \mu = 0\) gives \(\mu = 2 \ge 0\). With \(x \le 3\) instead, the unconstrained 2 is feasible, the constraint is slack and \(\mu = 0\).

Duality, in brief

Minimise the Lagrangian over \(x\) for fixed multipliers: \(d(\mu) = \min_x \big[f(x) + \sum_i \mu_i h_i(x)\big]\), the dual function. For any \(\mu \ge 0\) it is a lower bound on the best feasible value (weak duality), because on feasible points the added terms are \(\le 0\). The dual problem maximises that bound; for convex problems under Slater's condition the best bound equals the answer (strong duality; Boyd and Vandenberghe, sections 5.2.2 and 5.2.3). The dual is sometimes easier: the SVM's dual has one multiplier per training example and only inner products \(x_i^\top x_j\), which is what lets kernels replace them.

15. Constraints in ML: weight decay, SVMs, softmax, and the KL-constrained step

The question. Where do these multipliers show up in models you train?

Regularisation is a constraint in disguise

Minimising the loss subject to \(\lVert w \rVert^2 \le t\) has, by the KKT conditions, the same solution as minimising the loss plus \(\mu \lVert w \rVert^2\) for a matching \(\mu \ge 0\): the weight-decay strength is the multiplier of a norm-ball constraint (a smaller ball, a larger multiplier). {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}} covers its practice.

Support vectors are complementary slackness

The SVM minimises \(\tfrac12\lVert w \rVert^2\) subject to \(y_i(w^\top x_i + b) \ge 1\) for every example. Complementary slackness says each example's multiplier \(\alpha_i\) is zero unless its constraint is tight, that is unless it sits exactly on the margin (or violates it, in the soft-margin version). The solution \(w = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i\) depends only on those examples: the support vectors. See {{Classical ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ffb886ff30176bc6ce}}, section 5.

Softmax is the maximum-entropy distribution

Among all distributions \(p\) over \(K\) outcomes with scores \(s_k\), find the one with the largest entropy \(-\sum_k p_k \ln p_k\) (the least committed one) whose average score is a fixed value \(\bar s\). Lagrangian, with one multiplier per constraint:

\[ \mathcal L = -\sum_k p_k \ln p_k + \beta\Big(\sum_k p_k s_k - \bar s\Big) + \nu\Big(\sum_k p_k - 1\Big) \]

Differentiate in \(p_k\): \(-\ln p_k - 1 + \beta s_k + \nu = 0\), so \(p_k = e^{\nu - 1} e^{\beta s_k}\). The sum-to-one constraint fixes \(e^{\nu - 1} = 1/\sum_j e^{\beta s_j}\):

\[ p_k = \frac{e^{\beta s_k}}{\sum_j e^{\beta s_j}} \]

the softmax, with inverse temperature \(\beta\) equal to the multiplier of the average-score constraint ({{Jaynes 1957|https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.106.620}}; Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 7.2, solve the same problem as a convex program). With the tiny model's scores \((2, 1, 0)\) and \(\beta = 1\) it is the root's \((0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\).

The KL-constrained update: TRPO and natural gradient

TRPO ({{Schulman et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477}}, equation 55 in appendix C) improves a policy by maximising a surrogate objective \(L(\theta)\) "subject to \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta_{\text{old}}, \theta) \le \delta\)": the new policy may not move more than \(\delta\) in KL divergence. Approximate both sides by Taylor: the objective to first order, \(g^\top \Delta\), and the KL to second order, \(\tfrac12 \Delta^\top F \Delta\), where \(F\) (the Hessian of the KL at \(\Delta = 0\)) is the Fisher information matrix. The Lagrangian \(g^\top \Delta - \tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\Delta^\top F \Delta - 2\delta)\) is stationary at \(\Delta \propto F^{-1} g\), the natural gradient direction, and the constraint fixes the length:

\[ \Delta = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{g^\top F^{-1} g}}\; F^{-1} g \]

which is the paper's "\(\beta = \sqrt{2\delta / s^\top A s}\)" with \(s = F^{-1}g\) (they write \(A\) for the Fisher), solved by conjugate gradient with Fisher-vector products and followed by a line search.

The numbers: one Bernoulli policy
A policy that takes an action with probability \(p = \sigma(\theta)\) has Fisher information \(F = p(1 - p)\) in the logit \(\theta\) (the root's Bernoulli curvature). With budget \(\delta = 0.01\) the allowed logit step is \(\sqrt{2\delta/F}\): at \(p = 0.5\), \(F = 0.25\), step 0.283 (to \(p = 0.570\), true KL 0.0100); at \(p = 0.9\), \(F = 0.09\), step 0.471 (to \(p = 0.935\), true KL 0.0088). The same budget allows a bigger parameter step where the distribution is less sensitive: the whole point of measuring steps in KL rather than in parameters.

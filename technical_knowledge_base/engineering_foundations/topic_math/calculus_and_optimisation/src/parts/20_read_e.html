6. Critical points and the Hessian: minimum, maximum or saddle

The question. The gradient is zero. Have we found the bottom, or are we balanced on a ridge?

The picture

Where the gradient is zero the ground is flat in every direction: a critical (or stationary) point. Four shapes share that flatness: a bowl (minimum), an upside-down bowl (maximum), a horse saddle (up in one direction, down in another), and a trough flat along its floor. Only the second derivatives tell them apart.

Contours of four functions around the origin, each with its Hessian's eigenvectors drawn as arrows (blue: positive eigenvalue, the surface curves up; orange: negative, it curves down; grey: zero, drawn short). The other arrows are proportional to the size of the eigenvalue.

The words, then the formulas

The Hessian is the \(n \times n\) table of second derivatives, \(H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \theta_i\, \partial \theta_j}\). When those second derivatives are continuous, the order does not matter (Schwarz's theorem: \(\partial_i \partial_j L = \partial_j \partial_i L\)), so \(H\) is symmetric. Example: \(f = x^2 y\) has \(f_x = 2xy\), \(f_y = x^2\), and both mixed derivatives equal \(2x\).

A symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) and perpendicular eigenvectors \(u_i\) (the spectral theorem; {{Linear algebra for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d89c26e1dab2fcb701}}, sections 10 and 11). Along a unit eigenvector the Taylor model at a critical point (\(g = 0\)) is a plain parabola:

\[ L(\theta^* + t\,u_i) \approx L(\theta^*) + \tfrac12 \lambda_i\, t^2 \]

So each eigenvalue is the curvature along its direction, and the second-derivative test follows:

The number of negative eigenvalues, as a fraction of \(n\), is the critical point's index; section 17 uses it.

Hessians of the losses in section 5

Differentiate the gradients once more. For softmax cross-entropy, \(\nabla_z L = p - e_t\), and the derivative of \(p\) is the softmax Jacobian, so

\[ \nabla_z^2 L = \operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top \]

The label \(t\) has disappeared: the curvature does not depend on the target (the root's {{Gradient lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}}, panel 4, shows the same for the Bernoulli case, \(p(1-p)\), and why it equals the Fisher information). For one sigmoid output the Hessian is the single number \(p(1 - p)\). For logistic regression with inputs \(x_i\), the chain rule through \(z_i = w^\top x_i\) gives \(\nabla_w^2 L = \frac1n \sum_i p_i(1 - p_i)\, x_i x_i^\top = \frac1n X^\top S X\) with \(S = \operatorname{diag}(p_i(1-p_i))\).

The numbers
Tiny model, in the scores: at \(p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\) the eigenvalues of \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\) are 0, 0.119 and 0.371 (the zero is the direction \((1, 1, 1)\): shifting every score equally changes nothing). In the weights each is multiplied by \(\lVert x \rVert^2 = 5\), so \(\lambda_{\max} = 1.855\). In the input \(x\) (from the sibling page): \(W^\top (\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top) W = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1848 & -0.2289 \\ -0.2289 & 0.6007 \end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues 0.084 and 0.702. Real logistic regression (569 breast-cancer patients, 30 features plus an intercept, section 11): at the start the Hessian's eigenvalues run from 0.0018 to 3.32, a condition number of 1,855; at the optimum from 0.0018 to 0.150, a condition number of 85.9.
What it is used for in ML
Sharpness (the top eigenvalue) and the learning-rate limit (section 8); classifying where training stalls (section 17); Newton and its approximations (sections 10 and 11); Laplace approximations, influence functions \(H^{-1}\nabla L\), Optimal Brain Damage pruning (score \(\tfrac12 H_{ii} w_i^2\)). Computing \(Hv\) without building \(H\) (two backward passes) is on {{Matrix calculus and backprop|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}}, section 12.

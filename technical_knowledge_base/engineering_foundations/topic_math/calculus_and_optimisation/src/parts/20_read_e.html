4. Gradient descent, its step limit \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) and its rate \((\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)\)

Gradient descent is the first-order model plus a step penalty

The plane \(L(\theta) + g^\top \delta\) has no bottom, so add a penalty for stepping far from where it was measured:

\[ \delta^* = \arg\min_\delta \Big[ L(\theta) + g^\top \delta + \frac{1}{2\eta} \lVert \delta \rVert^2 \Big] = -\eta\, g \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \eta\, \nabla L(\theta_k) \]

The learning rate \(\eta\) is how far you trust a straight-line model. Replace \(\lVert \delta \rVert^2\) by another distance and you get another optimiser: a per-coordinate scaling gives Adagrad and Adam's shape, \(\delta^\top H \delta\) gives Newton, a KL divergence gives natural gradient.

If the curvature never exceeds \(\beta\) in any direction (\(H \preceq \beta I\): the loss is \(\beta\)-smooth), the loss is guaranteed to fall whenever \(\eta \lt 2/\beta\), and the guaranteed fall is largest at \(\eta = 1/\beta\) (the descent lemma). Real networks are not globally smooth in a useful way, so practice measures the local curvature instead (section 11); in the {{Optimisation lab|#t-lab}} you can watch the guarantee fail on Rosenbrock's valley when \(\eta\) is too large.

Derivation: the descent lemma

With \(H \preceq \beta I\) (\(\beta I - H\) has no negative eigenvalue) the second-order term is at most \(\frac{\beta}{2}\lVert\delta\rVert^2\), so for the step \(\delta = -\eta g\):

\[ L(\theta - \eta g) \le L(\theta) - \eta \lVert g \rVert^2 + \frac{\beta \eta^2}{2} \lVert g \rVert^2 = L(\theta) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{\beta\eta}{2}\Big)\lVert g \rVert^2 \]

The bracket is positive whenever \(\eta \lt 2/\beta\). At \(\eta = 1/\beta\) the guaranteed fall is \(\lVert g \rVert^2 / (2\beta)\) (Boyd and Vandenberghe, equation 9.17). Example: \(f = x^2 + 3y^2\) has \(\beta = 6\); from \((1, 1)\), where \(\lVert g \rVert^2 = 40\), the step \(\eta = 1/6\) must lower \(f\) by at least \(40/12 = 3.33\) (it lowers it from 4 to 0.444, a fall of 3.56).

On a quadratic every eigen-direction is its own one-dimensional problem

Near a minimum every smooth loss looks like \(L(\theta) = \tfrac12 \theta^\top H \theta\), whose gradient is \(H\theta\), so one step is \(\theta_{k+1} = (I - \eta H)\,\theta_k\). Along each eigenvector of \(H\) this multiplies the coordinate by the single number \(1 - \eta\lambda_i\); the directions do not interact. A coordinate shrinks only if \(|1 - \eta\lambda_i| \lt 1\) (with \(\eta\lambda_i\) between 1 and 2 it flips sign each step but shrinks; above 2 it flips and grows), and the steepest direction is the hardest, so

\[ \eta \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}, \qquad \eta^* = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min}}, \qquad r(\eta^*) = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1}, \quad \kappa = \frac{\lambda_{\max}}{\lambda_{\min}} \]
The flat direction wants a big step, the steep one a small step; the best step balances them
Predict first: at \(\kappa = 100\), how much slower is the best rate than at \(\kappa = 10\)? Then drag. Orange: the flat direction's factor \(|1 - \eta\lambda_{\min}|\); blue: the steep one's \(|1 - \eta\lambda_{\max}|\); the rate is the higher of the two (thick), lowest where they cross (green, \(\eta^*\)). The step axis is a fraction of the limit \(2/\lambda_{\max}\); moving \(\kappa\) resets the step to \(\eta^*\).

The rate is the worse of the two ends because \(|1 - \eta\lambda|\) is V-shaped in \(\lambda\); setting \(1 - \eta\lambda_{\min} = \eta\lambda_{\max} - 1\) gives \(\eta^*\). This is the rate {{Linear algebra for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d89c26e1dab2fcb701}} (section 11) quotes, and its correction stands: the rate holds only at this best fixed step; at any other step it is slower.

The numbers (old page and root, recomputed)
\(H = \operatorname{diag}(1, 10)\), \(\kappa = 10\): divergence above \(\eta = 0.2\); best step \(2/11 = 0.182\), contraction \(9/11 = 0.818\) per step, so a thousandfold cut takes \(\ln 1000 / \ln(11/9) = 34.4\), that is 35 steps (the script runs them: from \((1, 1)\) the largest coordinate after 35 steps is 0.00089). With the safe textbook step \(\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max} = 0.1\) the factor is \(1 - 1/\kappa = 0.9\) and it takes 66 steps. The tiny model at its start has \(\lambda_{\max} = 1.855\) in the weights, so its local limit is \(\eta \approx 1.08\) (the root's step with \(\eta = 1\) was just inside it).
Checked on real data
Logistic regression on the breast-cancer data (section 6) has, at its optimum, \(\lambda_{\max} = 0.150\), \(\lambda_{\min} = 0.00175\), \(\kappa = 85.9\). The theory predicts that gradient descent with \(\eta^* = 13.1\) contracts the error by \((\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1) = 0.977\) per step near the optimum. Measured over the iterations where the loss gap falls from \(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{-12}\): 0.974 per step, within the drift of the Hessian along the path. The same step is far above the limit at the start, where \(\lambda_{\max} = 3.32\) gives \(2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.60\); it survives only because the logistic loss's gradient is bounded, which is exactly why practice uses warmup or a line search.

What it means in training: the largest usable learning rate is set by the sharpest direction and the speed by the flattest. That is why normalisation layers and good initialisation, which make the bowl rounder, speed training ({{Normalisation and initialisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81369e1cc38ed4e11d48}}), and why learning-rate warmup exists (curvature is often high early). The root's {{Gradient lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} (panel 3) animates the bowl; for a strongly convex, smooth function (\(\mu I \preceq H \preceq \beta I\)) the same reasoning bounds the loss gap: at \(\eta = 1/\beta\) it shrinks by at least \(1 - \mu/\beta = 1 - 1/\kappa\) per step (Boyd and Vandenberghe, equation 9.18).

Momentum: the condition number enters through its square root

Heavy-ball momentum (Polyak 1964) keeps a running velocity: steps that agree from one iteration to the next (along the valley floor) add up, to about \(1/(1 - \beta)\) times a single step, and steps that flip sign (across the walls) cancel. On a quadratic with the best settings the rate becomes \(\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}\) ({{Goh 2017, "Why Momentum Really Works"|https://distill.pub/2017/momentum/}}). On diag(1, 10) that is 0.519 per step, about 11 steps for a thousandfold cut against plain gradient descent's 35; for \(\kappa = 100\), 0.818 against 0.980, about 34 steps against 345. Nesterov's accelerated gradient evaluates the gradient where momentum is about to carry you, and on smooth convex functions (not just quadratics) provably reaches a loss gap of order \(1/k^2\) after \(k\) steps, against \(1/k\) for gradient descent; no method using only gradients can do better in the worst case. How momentum, Nesterov and Adam behave on real networks: {{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}} (sections 2 to 6, and its Optimiser race tab with thirteen update rules).

Momentum and Nesterov: the update rules, the best settings and a real-data check

In Goh's form: \(z_{k+1} = \beta z_k + \nabla L(\theta_k)\), \(\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha\, z_{k+1}\), with step \(\alpha\) and momentum \(\beta \in [0, 1)\). On a quadratic each eigen-coordinate carries position and velocity, updated by a fixed 2x2 matrix whose eigenvalues set the rate. Goh's analysis (section "The Dynamics of Momentum") gives three results:

  • Stability: \(0 \lt \alpha\lambda_i \lt 2 + 2\beta\). With \(\beta = 0.9\) the step can be almost twice as large as plain gradient descent's limit: on diag(1, 10), up to 0.38 rather than 0.2.
  • Best settings: \(\alpha = \Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}}}\Big)^2\), \(\beta = \Big(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}} - \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}}\Big)^2\).
  • Best rate: \(\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}\).

diag(1, 10): \(\alpha = (2/(1 + 3.162))^2 = 0.231\), \(\beta = (2.162/4.162)^2 = 0.270\), rate \((3.162 - 1)/(3.162 + 1) = 0.519\), so a thousandfold reduction takes \(\ln 1000/\ln(1/0.519) = 10.5\) steps. Running those settings in the page's script measures 0.524 per step (the rate is approached, with a polynomial factor, because the 2x2 matrices at the optimum have repeated eigenvalues).

Nesterov (Nesterov 1983, in the form of {{Sutskever et al. 2013|http://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/sutskever13.pdf}}): \(v_{k+1} = \mu v_k - \eta \nabla L(\theta_k + \mu v_k)\), \(\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + v_{k+1}\). The look-ahead corrects an overshoot one step earlier. The \(1/k^2\) bound and its optimality are in Nesterov, Introductory Lectures on Convex Optimization, 2004, sections 2.1 and 2.2; heavy ball has no such guarantee beyond quadratics.

On real data: on the breast-cancer logistic regression, Nesterov with the textbook constants (\(\eta = 1/\beta\) with the global smoothness bound \(\beta = 3.32\), momentum \((\sqrt{\kappa} - 1)/(\sqrt{\kappa} + 1) = 0.955\)) reaches a loss gap of \(10^{-6}\) in 274 iterations, where plain gradient descent at the same step does not get there in 500 (section 6 has the full race).

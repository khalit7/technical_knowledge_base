5. Newton's method: jump to the bottom of the local bowl

Minimise the second-order model \(m(\delta) = L + g^\top \delta + \tfrac12 \delta^\top H \delta\): its gradient \(g + H\delta\) is zero at

\[ H \delta = -g \quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta_{\text{Newton}} = -H^{-1} g \]

This is a minimum of the model only if \(H \succ 0\). In one dimension it is \(-L'/L''\), slope over curvature. In eigen-coordinates it divides each direction's gradient by that direction's curvature, so flat directions get big steps and steep ones small steps at once: the condition number no longer matters.

The before and after: one gradient step against one Newton step

Both methods on the same real function: the logistic-regression loss of one weight \(w\) on one breast-cancer feature (the "worst concave points" measurement, standardised; label 1 is malignant), with the small L2 penalty of section 6. Each step first draws the model the method trusts at the current point (gradient descent: the tangent line, with \(\eta = 1/\beta = 3.97\) from the largest curvature this function can have, \(\beta = 0.252\); Newton: the touching parabola), then moves to the point that model chooses. Predict before pressing play: from \(w = 8\), where the loss is almost flat, where does Newton go?

The numbers
From \(w = 0\): loss 0.693, slope \(-0.384\), curvature 0.252. The first steps of both methods coincide (1.524), because the curvature here equals the bound \(\beta\). Then the curvature falls (to 0.0228 at the optimum \(w^* = 3.551\)): Newton lands at 2.539, 3.262, 3.526, 3.551; gradient descent at 1.934, 2.204, 2.404, ..., still at 3.483 after 30 steps, contracting by \(1 - 0.0228/0.252 = 0.91\) per step. From \(w = 8\) the curvature is only 0.0039, so the parabola is nearly flat and its bottom is far away: Newton jumps to \(-2.58\), then 14.2, \(-13.3\), 366, and diverges, while gradient descent creeps safely downhill (7.84, 7.68, ...).

Fast near the answer, dangerous far from it

  • Quadratic convergence. Near a minimum with \(H \succ 0\) and a smooth Hessian the error is roughly squared each step: the correct digits double (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.5.3). On the 31-parameter real logistic regression the loss gap after Newton steps 5 to 8 is \(1.6 \times 10^{-3}\), \(6.1 \times 10^{-5}\), \(1.3 \times 10^{-7}\), \(6.4 \times 10^{-13}\).
  • Affine invariance. Rescaling or rotating the parameters (\(\theta = A\phi\)) changes Newton's steps in exactly the matching way, so units do not matter; gradient descent's steps change shape (section 9.5.1).
  • Far from the minimum the model lies (the \(w = 8\) start). The fix is damping: a backtracking line search on the Newton direction, a trust region (minimise the model only within a radius), or Levenberg-Marquardt \((H + \lambda I)^{-1} g\), which slides between Newton (\(\lambda = 0\)) and a short gradient step (large \(\lambda\)).
  • Saddles attract it. With a negative eigenvalue, \(-H^{-1}g\) moves uphill along that direction, towards the saddle: "the saddle point \(\theta^*\) becomes an attractor for the Newton method" (Dauphin et al., {{2014, section 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2572}}). Their saddle-free Newton divides by \(|\lambda_i|\) instead (the {{Optimisation lab|#t-lab}} has it).

Why it is too expensive at scale

For \(n\) parameters, \(H\) has \(n^2\) entries and solving \(H\delta = -g\) costs about \(n^3\) operations. At \(n = 10^9\): \(10^{18}\) entries, 2 exabytes in 16-bit numbers, before any solve. Minibatch noise makes the curvature estimate itself noisy, and the many saddles make an undamped step unsafe. So practice uses stand-ins that need only gradients or Hessian-vector products: Gauss-Newton and Fisher matrices, Hessian-free conjugate gradient, K-FAC, Shampoo, SOAP and Muon, diagonal scalings such as Adam, and quasi-Newton (next section).

The approximations to Newton, one line each
  • Gauss-Newton and the Fisher matrix: positive semidefinite stand-ins for \(H\) (for the canonical losses of section 2, the Fisher equals the Gauss-Newton part of the Hessian; the root's Gradient lab, panel 4, and {{Martens 2020, section 9|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.1193}}). Natural gradient preconditions by the inverse Fisher.
  • Hessian-free ({{Martens 2010|https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~jmartens/docs/Deep_HessianFree.pdf}}): solve \(H\delta = -g\) approximately by conjugate gradient, using only Hessian-vector products.
  • K-FAC ({{Martens and Grosse 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.05671}}): approximate each layer's block of the Fisher by a Kronecker product of two small matrices.
  • Shampoo, SOAP, Muon: per-layer matrix preconditioners in practice; owned by {{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}} (section 4).
  • Diagonal scalings: Adam divides by the square root of an exponential moving average of squared gradients; its authors relate that average to the diagonal of the Fisher ({{Kingma and Ba 2014, section 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980}}). It is a crude per-coordinate scale, not a Newton step (no inverse curvature, and a square root).

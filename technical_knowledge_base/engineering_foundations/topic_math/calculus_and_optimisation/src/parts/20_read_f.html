7. Gradient descent, derived from the first-order model

The question. "Step against the gradient" sounds obvious. What problem does it actually solve, and when is the loss guaranteed to fall?

The first-order model \(L(\theta) + g^\top \delta\) is a tilted plane with no bottom: minimising it alone says "step infinitely far downhill". So add a penalty for stepping far from where the model was measured, with a strength \(1/\eta\):

\[ \delta^* = \arg\min_\delta \Big[ L(\theta) + g^\top \delta + \frac{1}{2\eta} \lVert \delta \rVert^2 \Big] \]

(\(\arg\min_\delta\) means "the \(\delta\) that makes this smallest".) The bracket is a bowl in \(\delta\); its gradient is \(g + \delta/\eta\), which is zero at \(\delta^* = -\eta\, g\). That is gradient descent:

\[ \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \eta\, \nabla L(\theta_k) \]

In plain words: the learning rate \(\eta\) is how far you trust a straight-line model. Replace \(\lVert \delta \rVert^2\) by another distance and you get another optimiser: a per-coordinate scaling gives Adagrad and Adam's shape, \(\delta^\top H \delta\) gives Newton, a KL divergence gives natural gradient.

When is a decrease guaranteed? The descent lemma

Suppose the curvature never exceeds \(\beta\) in any direction, \(H \preceq \beta I\) (written: \(\beta I - H\) has no negative eigenvalue; such an \(L\) is called \(\beta\)-smooth). Then the second-order term is at most \(\frac{\beta}{2}\lVert\delta\rVert^2\), so for the step \(\delta = -\eta g\):

\[ L(\theta - \eta g) \le L(\theta) - \eta \lVert g \rVert^2 + \frac{\beta \eta^2}{2} \lVert g \rVert^2 = L(\theta) - \eta\Big(1 - \frac{\beta\eta}{2}\Big)\lVert g \rVert^2 \]

The bracket is positive whenever \(\eta \lt 2/\beta\): the loss must fall. The guaranteed fall is largest at \(\eta = 1/\beta\), where it is \(\lVert g \rVert^2 / (2\beta)\) (Boyd and Vandenberghe, equation 9.17). Example: \(f = x^2 + 3y^2\) has \(\beta = 6\); from \((1, 1)\), where \(\lVert g \rVert^2 = 40\), the step \(\eta = 1/6\) must lower \(f\) by at least \(40/12 = 3.33\) (it lowers it from 4 to 0.444, a fall of 3.56).

What it is used for in ML
The \(2/\beta\) bound is the stability limit of section 8; "lower the learning rate" is the first fix for a loss that rises. Real networks are not globally smooth in a useful way, so practice measures the local curvature instead (section 17), and in the {{Optimisation lab|#t-lab}} you can watch the descent lemma fail on Rosenbrock's valley when \(\eta\) is too large.

8. The learning-rate limit \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) and the rate \((\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)\), derived

The question. Why does training blow up above some learning rate, and why is it slow in a long narrow valley even at the best one?

Near a minimum every smooth loss looks like its second-order Taylor model, so study a quadratic bowl \(L(\theta) = \tfrac12 \theta^\top H \theta\) with minimum at 0. Its gradient is \(H\theta\), so one step is

\[ \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \eta H \theta_k = (I - \eta H)\,\theta_k \]

Step 1: split into eigen-directions. Write \(\theta = \sum_i c_i u_i\) along the Hessian's eigenvectors. Since \(H u_i = \lambda_i u_i\), the matrix \(I - \eta H\) multiplies the coordinate \(c_i\) by the single number \(1 - \eta\lambda_i\). The directions do not interact: after \(k\) steps, \(c_i^{(k)} = (1 - \eta\lambda_i)^k\, c_i^{(0)}\).

Step 2: the stability limit. \(c_i\) shrinks only if \(|1 - \eta\lambda_i| \lt 1\), that is \(0 \lt \eta\lambda_i \lt 2\). With \(\eta\lambda_i\) between 1 and 2 the coordinate flips sign each step but shrinks (oscillates inward); above 2 it flips and grows. Every direction must be stable, and the steepest is the hardest, so

\[ \eta \lt \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}} \]

Step 3: the rate. The error shrinks per step by the worst factor, \(r(\eta) = \max_i |1 - \eta\lambda_i|\). Because \(|1 - \eta\lambda|\) is V-shaped in \(\lambda\), the worst case is always at an end: \(r(\eta) = \max(|1 - \eta\lambda_{\min}|, |1 - \eta\lambda_{\max}|)\). Increasing \(\eta\) helps the flat direction (the first term falls) and hurts the steep one (the second rises once \(\eta \gt 1/\lambda_{\max}\)). The best \(\eta\) balances them: \(1 - \eta\lambda_{\min} = \eta\lambda_{\max} - 1\), so

\[ \eta^* = \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min}}, \qquad r(\eta^*) = 1 - \frac{2\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min}} = \frac{\lambda_{\max} - \lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min}} = \frac{\kappa - 1}{\kappa + 1} \]

where \(\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) is the condition number (divide top and bottom by \(\lambda_{\min}\)). This is the rate {{Linear algebra for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d89c26e1dab2fcb701}} (section 13) quotes, and its correction stands: the rate holds only at this best fixed step; at any other step it is slower.

The contraction factor of the flat direction \(|1 - \eta\lambda_{\min}|\) (orange) and of the steep one \(|1 - \eta\lambda_{\max}|\) (blue) against the step, as a fraction of the stability limit \(2/\lambda_{\max}\). The rate is the higher of the two (thick line); its lowest point (green) is where they cross, at \(\eta^*\). Moving \(\kappa\) resets the step to \(\eta^*\).
The numbers (old page and root, recomputed)
\(H = \operatorname{diag}(1, 10)\), \(\kappa = 10\). Divergence above \(\eta = 2/10 = 0.2\). Best step \(2/11 = 0.182\), contraction \(9/11 = 0.818\) per step, so cutting the error a thousandfold takes \(\ln 1000 / \ln(11/9) = 34.4\), that is 35 steps (the script runs them: from \((1, 1)\) the largest coordinate after 35 steps is 0.00089). With the safe textbook step \(\eta = 1/\lambda_{\max} = 0.1\) the factor is \(1 - 1/\kappa = 0.9\) and it takes 66 steps. For a strongly convex, smooth function with \(\mu I \preceq H \preceq \beta I\) the same reasoning bounds the loss gap: at \(\eta = 1/\beta\) it shrinks by at least \(1 - \mu/\beta = 1 - 1/\kappa\) per step (Boyd and Vandenberghe, equation 9.18). The tiny model at its start has \(\lambda_{\max} = 1.855\) in the weights, so its local limit is \(\eta \approx 1.08\) (the root's step with \(\eta = 1\) was just inside it).
Checked on real data
Logistic regression on the breast-cancer data (section 11) has, at its optimum, \(\lambda_{\max} = 0.150\), \(\lambda_{\min} = 0.00175\), \(\kappa = 85.9\). The theory predicts that gradient descent with \(\eta^* = 2/(\lambda_{\max} + \lambda_{\min}) = 13.1\) contracts the error by \((\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1) = 0.977\) per step once it is near the optimum. Measured over the iterations where the loss gap falls from \(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{-12}\): 0.974 per step. The prediction holds to within the drift of the Hessian along the path. The same step is far above the limit at the start, where \(\lambda_{\max} = 3.32\) gives \(2/\lambda_{\max} = 0.60\); it survives only because the logistic loss's gradient is bounded, which is exactly why practice uses warmup or a line search.
What it is used for in ML
The largest usable learning rate is set by the sharpest direction and the speed by the flattest: that is why normalisation layers and good initialisation, which make the bowl rounder, speed training ({{Normalisation and initialisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81369e1cc38ed4e11d48}}), and why learning-rate warmup exists (curvature is often high early). 9. Momentum and Nesterov: the square root of the condition number

The question. Gradient descent zig-zags across a narrow valley and creeps along it. Can remembering the previous step fix both?

Heavy-ball momentum (Polyak 1964) keeps a running velocity. In the form used by {{Goh 2017, "Why Momentum Really Works"|https://distill.pub/2017/momentum/}}:

\[ z_{k+1} = \beta z_k + \nabla L(\theta_k), \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \alpha\, z_{k+1} \]

\(\alpha\) is the step size, \(\beta \in [0, 1)\) the momentum (how much of the old velocity survives). Steps that agree from one iteration to the next (along the valley floor) add up, to about \(1/(1 - \beta)\) times a single step; steps that flip sign (across the walls) cancel.

On a quadratic, split into eigen-directions as before. Each coordinate now carries two numbers (position and velocity), updated by a fixed 2x2 matrix whose eigenvalues set the rate. Goh's analysis (section "The Dynamics of Momentum") gives three results:

  • Stability: \(0 \lt \alpha\lambda_i \lt 2 + 2\beta\). With \(\beta = 0.9\) the step can be almost twice as large as plain gradient descent's limit: on diag(1, 10), up to 0.38 rather than 0.2.
  • Best settings: \(\alpha = \Big(\frac{2}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\min}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}}}\Big)^2\), \(\beta = \Big(\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}} - \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}} + \sqrt{\lambda_{\min}}}\Big)^2\).
  • Best rate: \(\frac{\sqrt\kappa - 1}{\sqrt\kappa + 1}\): the condition number enters through its square root.
The numbers
diag(1, 10): \(\alpha = (2/(1 + 3.162))^2 = 0.231\), \(\beta = (2.162/4.162)^2 = 0.270\), rate \((3.162 - 1)/(3.162 + 1) = 0.519\) per step, so a thousandfold reduction takes \(\ln 1000/\ln(1/0.519) = 10.5\), about 11 steps against plain gradient descent's 35. Running those settings in the page's script measures 0.524 per step (the rate is approached, with a polynomial factor, because the 2x2 matrices at the optimum have repeated eigenvalues). For \(\kappa = 100\) the difference is 0.980 against 0.818: about 345 steps against 34.

Nesterov's accelerated gradient (Nesterov 1983, in the form of {{Sutskever et al. 2013|http://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/sutskever13.pdf}}) evaluates the gradient at the point momentum is about to carry you to, rather than where you stand:

\[ v_{k+1} = \mu v_k - \eta \nabla L(\theta_k + \mu v_k), \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + v_{k+1} \]

The look-ahead corrects an overshoot one step earlier. On smooth convex functions (not just quadratics) it provably reaches a loss gap of order \(1/k^2\) after \(k\) steps, against \(1/k\) for gradient descent, and no method using only gradients can do better in the worst case (Nesterov, Introductory Lectures on Convex Optimization, 2004, sections 2.1 and 2.2). Heavy ball has no such guarantee beyond quadratics.

On real data
On the breast-cancer logistic regression, Nesterov with the textbook constants (\(\eta = 1/\beta\) with the global smoothness bound \(\beta = 3.32\), momentum \((\sqrt{\kappa} - 1)/(\sqrt{\kappa} + 1) = 0.955\)) reaches a loss gap of \(10^{-6}\) in 274 iterations, where plain gradient descent at the same step does not get there in 500.

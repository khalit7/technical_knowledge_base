10. Newton's method, derived from the second-order model

The question. If the Taylor model includes the curvature, why not jump straight to its minimum?

Minimise the second-order model \(m(\delta) = L + g^\top \delta + \tfrac12 \delta^\top H \delta\). Its gradient in \(\delta\) is \(g + H\delta\) (the derivative of \(\tfrac12\delta^\top H\delta\) is \(H\delta\) because \(H\) is symmetric). Setting it to zero:

\[ H \delta = -g \quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta_{\text{Newton}} = -H^{-1} g \]

This is a minimum of the model only if \(H \succ 0\) (positive definite). In one dimension it is \(\delta = -L'/L''\): the slope divided by the curvature. In eigen-coordinates it divides each direction's gradient by that direction's curvature, so flat directions get big steps and steep ones small steps, all at once: the condition number no longer matters.

The before and after: one gradient step against one Newton step

The animation runs both methods on the same real function: the logistic-regression loss with one weight \(w\) on one feature of the breast-cancer data (the "worst concave points" measurement, standardised; label 1 is malignant), plus the small L2 penalty of section 11. At each step it draws the model the method trusts at the current point (gradient descent: the tangent line, with the step \(\eta = 1/\beta = 3.97\) set by the largest curvature this function can have, \(\beta = 0.252\); Newton: the touching parabola) and moves to the point the model chooses.

The numbers
From \(w = 0\): loss 0.693, slope \(-0.384\), curvature 0.252. Gradient descent's first step and Newton's first step coincide (1.524), because the curvature here equals the bound \(\beta\). After that the curvature falls (to 0.0228 at the optimum \(w^* = 3.551\)) and the two part ways: Newton lands at 2.539, 3.262, 3.526, 3.551; gradient descent at 1.934, 2.204, 2.404, ..., still at 3.483 after 30 steps, contracting by \(1 - 0.0228/0.252 = 0.91\) per step. From \(w = 8\) the curvature is only 0.0039, so the parabola is nearly flat and its bottom is far away: Newton jumps to \(-2.58\), then 14.2, \(-13.3\), 366, and diverges. Gradient descent creeps safely downhill (7.84, 7.68, ...).

Why Newton is fast near the answer, and dangerous far from it

  • Quadratic convergence. Near a minimum with \(H \succ 0\) and a smooth Hessian, the error is roughly squared each step: the number of correct digits doubles (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.5.3). On the 31-parameter real logistic regression the loss gap after Newton steps 5 to 8 is \(1.6 \times 10^{-3}\), \(6.1 \times 10^{-5}\), \(1.3 \times 10^{-7}\), \(6.4 \times 10^{-13}\).
  • Affine invariance. Rescaling or rotating the parameters (\(\theta = A\phi\)) changes Newton's steps in exactly the matching way, so units do not matter; gradient descent's steps change shape (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.5.1).
  • Far from the minimum the model lies. The parabola is only good locally; with small curvature it sends you far (the \(w = 8\) start). The fix is damping: a backtracking line search on the Newton direction, a trust region (minimise the model only within a radius), or Levenberg-Marquardt \((H + \lambda I)^{-1} g\), which slides between Newton (\(\lambda = 0\)) and a short gradient step (large \(\lambda\)).
  • Saddles attract it. With a negative eigenvalue, \(-H^{-1}g\) moves uphill along that direction, towards the saddle. Dauphin et al. ({{2014, section 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2572}}): "the saddle point \(\theta^*\) becomes an attractor for the Newton method". Their saddle-free Newton divides by \(|\lambda_i|\) instead (the {{Optimisation lab|#t-lab}} has it).

Why it is not used to train large networks

For \(n\) parameters, \(H\) has \(n^2\) entries and solving \(H\delta = -g\) costs about \(n^3\) operations. At \(n = 10^9\): \(10^{18}\) entries, 2 exabytes in 16-bit numbers, before any solve. Minibatch noise makes the curvature estimate itself noisy, and the many saddles make an undamped step unsafe. So practice uses approximations that need only gradients or Hessian-vector products:

  • Gauss-Newton and the Fisher matrix: positive semidefinite stand-ins for \(H\) (for the canonical losses of section 5, the Fisher equals the Gauss-Newton part of the Hessian; the root's Gradient lab, panel 4, and {{Martens 2020, section 9|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.1193}}). Natural gradient preconditions by the inverse Fisher.
  • Hessian-free ({{Martens 2010|https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~jmartens/docs/Deep_HessianFree.pdf}}): solve \(H\delta = -g\) approximately by conjugate gradient, using only Hessian-vector products.
  • K-FAC ({{Martens and Grosse 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.05671}}): approximate each layer's block of the Fisher by a Kronecker product of two small matrices.
  • Shampoo, SOAP, Muon: per-layer matrix preconditioners in practice; owned by {{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}} (section 4).
  • Diagonal scalings: Adam divides by the square root of an exponential moving average of squared gradients; its authors relate that average to the diagonal of the Fisher ({{Kingma and Ba 2014, section 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980}}). It is a crude per-coordinate scale, not a Newton step (no inverse curvature, and a square root).
  • Quasi-Newton: next section.

11. Quasi-Newton: BFGS and L-BFGS, and a race on real data

The question. Can we get Newton's speed without ever computing a Hessian?

Yes, by learning the curvature from how the gradient changes. After a step \(s_k = \theta_{k+1} - \theta_k\) the gradient changes by \(y_k = g_{k+1} - g_k\). For a quadratic \(y_k = H s_k\) exactly, so ask the curvature estimate \(B_{k+1}\) to satisfy the secant equation \(B_{k+1} s_k = y_k\). It has many solutions; BFGS (Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb, Shanno, 1970) picks the one closest to the previous estimate in a weighted norm, and keeps the inverse \(M_k = B_k^{-1}\) directly:

\[ M_{k+1} = (I - \rho_k s_k y_k^\top)\, M_k\, (I - \rho_k y_k s_k^\top) + \rho_k s_k s_k^\top, \qquad \rho_k = \frac{1}{y_k^\top s_k} \]

The step is \(-M_k g_k\) followed by a line search. The update needs \(y_k^\top s_k \gt 0\) (the curvature condition; the Wolfe line-search conditions guarantee it), which keeps \(M\) positive definite (Nocedal and Wright, Numerical Optimization, 2nd edition, chapter 6). Each update is a rank-two correction costing \(n^2\), and \(M\) still has \(n^2\) entries.

L-BFGS ({{Liu and Nocedal 1989|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01589116}}) keeps only the last \(m\) pairs \((s_k, y_k)\), typically 5 to 20, and applies \(M_k\) to the gradient with a "two-loop recursion" of \(4mn\) multiplications, never storing a matrix (Nocedal and Wright, chapter 7). Memory \(2mn\) numbers instead of \(n^2\). It is scikit-learn's default solver for logistic regression. It works on full-batch, deterministic problems; with minibatch noise, \(y_k\) is dominated by sampling noise rather than curvature and the estimate degrades.

12. XGBoost's leaf weights are Newton steps

The question. Boosted trees are not trained by gradient descent at all. Where does calculus come in?

XGBoost adds one regression tree \(f_t\) per round to the current prediction \(\hat y_i^{(t-1)}\). {{Chen and Guestrin 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.02754}} write the objective at round \(t\) as the loss plus a penalty on the tree, \(\Omega(f) = \gamma T + \tfrac12 \lambda \lVert w \rVert^2\) (\(T\) leaves with values \(w_j\); their equation 2), and take a second-order Taylor expansion of each example's loss around the current prediction (their equation 3):

\[ \tilde{\mathcal L}^{(t)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ g_i f_t(x_i) + \tfrac12 h_i f_t(x_i)^2 \Big] + \Omega(f_t), \qquad g_i = \frac{\partial l}{\partial \hat y}\Big|_{\hat y_i^{(t-1)}},\ h_i = \frac{\partial^2 l}{\partial \hat y^2}\Big|_{\hat y_i^{(t-1)}} \]

Derivation of the leaf weight. A tree gives every example in leaf \(j\) (the set \(I_j\)) the same value \(w_j\). Group the sum by leaves, with \(G_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} g_i\) and \(H_j = \sum_{i \in I_j} h_i\):

\[ \tilde{\mathcal L} = \sum_{j=1}^{T} \Big[ G_j w_j + \tfrac12 (H_j + \lambda) w_j^2 \Big] + \gamma T \]

Each bracket is a parabola in its own \(w_j\). Its slope \(G_j + (H_j + \lambda) w_j\) is zero at

\[ w_j^* = -\frac{G_j}{H_j + \lambda}, \qquad \tilde{\mathcal L}^* = -\frac12 \sum_{j=1}^T \frac{G_j^2}{H_j + \lambda} + \gamma T \]

(their equations 5 and 6; substitute \(w_j^*\) back: \(G_j w_j^* + \tfrac12 (H_j + \lambda) w_j^{*2} = -\tfrac12 G_j^2/(H_j + \lambda)\)). That is a Newton step \(-G/H\) for the leaf, damped by \(\lambda\). A split of a leaf into left and right children is worth the drop in that objective (their equation 7):

\[ \text{Gain} = \frac12 \Big[ \frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda} \Big] - \gamma \]
Logistic loss, current prediction 0 (so \(p = 0.5\), \(g = p - y\), \(h = p(1-p) = 0.25\) for everyone), \(\lambda = 1\). Click a label to flip it; click "split here" positions to move the split.

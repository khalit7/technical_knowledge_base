1. The derivative: a slope, and the best straight-line fit

The question. The loss depends on a weight. If I nudge the weight a little, how much does the loss move, and in which direction?

The picture

Draw the curve \(f(x) = x^2\) and pick the point \(x = 3\), where \(f = 9\). Draw a straight line through that point and a second point a distance \(h\) to the right: a secant. Its slope is rise over run. Now slide the second point towards the first. The secant swings round and settles on one line that just grazes the curve: the tangent. The slope of the tangent is the derivative.

The secant through \((3, 9)\) and \((3+h, (3+h)^2)\) (orange) turns into the tangent (blue) as \(h\) shrinks. Its slope is \(6 + h\), heading for 6; the gap between the tangent line and the curve at \(3 + h\) is exactly \(h^2\).

The words, then the formula

The derivative is the limit of the secant slope as the gap shrinks to zero:

\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \]
\(f'(x)\)
the derivative of \(f\) at \(x\), also written \(\frac{df}{dx}\) ("d f by d x"). It is a number for each \(x\), so it is itself a function.
\(h\)
a small change in the input.
\(\lim_{h \to 0}\)
"the limit as \(h\) goes to 0": the value the expression settles on as \(h\) gets as close to zero as you like, without being zero (at zero the fraction would be 0/0).

In plain words: the derivative is how many units the output moves per unit the input moves, measured over a gap so small that the curve looks straight.

The numbers
For \(f(x) = x^2\) at 3: \(\frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = 6 + h\). With \(h = 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001\) the secant slopes are 7, 6.1, 6.01, 6.001, and the limit is \(f'(3) = 6\). In general \(\frac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} = 2x + h \to 2x\), so \(f'(x) = 2x\).

The second reading: the best linear approximation

Rearranging the definition gives the reading that matters most for optimisation. Near \(x\), the function is a straight line plus an error that shrinks faster than the step:

\[ f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)\,h + r(h), \qquad \frac{r(h)}{h} \to 0 \text{ as } h \to 0 \]

\(r(h)\) is the remainder, the part the line misses. For \(x^2\) at 3 the line is \(9 + 6h\) and the remainder is exactly \(h^2\): 1, 0.01, 0.0001, 0.000001 for the four gaps above. Halve the step and the error quarters. No other straight line has that property, which is why the tangent is called the best linear approximation: any other slope \(m\) leaves an error of about \((6 - m)h\), which shrinks only as fast as \(h\) itself.

What it is used for in ML
Every gradient-based optimiser rests on this second reading: for a small enough step the loss changes by (slope times step). Gradient checking (compare the analytic slope with \((f(x+h) - f(x-h))/2h\)) rests on the first. The root's {{Gradient lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} (tab Gradient lab, panel 1) runs that check on five losses.

The tiny model. Hold everything fixed except the score of sat, \(s = z_{\text{sat}}\). The loss is \(L(s) = -s + \ln(e^2 + e^1 + e^s)\), and at \(s = 0\) its slope is \(-0.910\) (section 5 derives it). The linear approximation says raising \(s\) by 0.1 changes the loss by \(-0.091\), to 2.3166; the true value is 2.3170. The missing 0.0004 is the curvature, the subject of section 4.

2. Rules of differentiation, each with a worked case

The question. Taking limits by hand for every function would be slow. Which few rules let you differentiate anything built from simple pieces?

Six rules cover every loss and layer on this page. Each is the limit definition worked out once; the derivation of the chain rule, the one that matters most, is below.

RuleFormulaWorked case
Constant, power\((c)' = 0\), \((x^n)' = n x^{n-1}\)\((x^3)' = 3x^2\); at 2: 12
Sum and constant multiple\((a f + b g)' = a f' + b g'\)\((3x^2 + 5x)' = 6x + 5\)
Product\((fg)' = f'g + fg'\)\((x^2 e^x)' = (2x + x^2)e^x\); at 1: \(3e = 8.155\)
Quotient\((f/g)' = (f'g - fg')/g^2\)\(\big(\frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}\big)' = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2}\)
Exponential, log\((e^x)' = e^x\), \((\ln x)' = 1/x\)\((\ln(1 + x^2))' = \frac{2x}{1 + x^2}\)
Chain\((f(g(x)))' = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\)\((\ln(1 + e^x))' = \frac{e^x}{1 + e^x} = \sigma(x)\)

Here \(\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})\) is the sigmoid, which squashes any number into (0, 1), and \(\ln(1 + e^x)\) is the softplus: the table's last line says softplus's slope is the sigmoid. Every line of the table was checked symbolically (sympy) by the page's script.

Derivation: why the chain rule multiplies slopes

Let \(u = g(x)\). A step \(h\) in \(x\) moves \(u\) by \(\Delta u = g(x + h) - g(x) \approx g'(x)\,h\) (the linear approximation of \(g\)). That moves \(f\) by about \(f'(u)\,\Delta u\) (the linear approximation of \(f\) at \(u\)). Substituting: \(\Delta f \approx f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\,h\). Divide by \(h\) and let it shrink; both approximations become exact in the limit, so \((f \circ g)'(x) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)\). In words: if \(u\) moves 3 units per unit of \(x\), and \(f\) moves 2 units per unit of \(u\), then \(f\) moves 6 units per unit of \(x\).

The sigmoid's slope, a result you will use constantly

Apply the quotient (or chain) rule to \(\sigma(x) = (1 + e^{-x})^{-1}\):

\[ \sigma'(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}} \cdot \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = \sigma(x)\,\big(1 - \sigma(x)\big) \]

The second factor is \(1 - \sigma(x)\) because \(\frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x}}\). At \(x = 0\), \(\sigma = 0.5\) and the slope is \(0.25\), its largest value; far from zero it falls towards 0. That fall is why a sigmoid output trained with squared error stalls (section 5). The same steps give \(\tanh'(x) = 1 - \tanh^2(x)\).

1. The local model: derivative, gradient, Taylor expansion

The derivative is the best straight-line fit

Near \(x\), a smooth function is a straight line plus an error that shrinks faster than the step:

\[ f(x + h) = f(x) + f'(x)\,h + r(h), \qquad \frac{r(h)}{h} \to 0 \text{ as } h \to 0 \]

For \(x^2\) at 3 the line is \(9 + 6h\) and the remainder is exactly \(h^2\): 1, 0.01, 0.0001, 0.000001 for \(h = 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001\). Halve the step and the error quarters. No other straight line has that property, which is why the tangent is called the best linear approximation: any other slope \(m\) leaves an error of about \((6 - m)h\), which shrinks only as fast as \(h\) itself. Every gradient-based optimiser rests on this reading (for a small enough step the loss changes by slope times step); gradient checking, comparing the analytic slope with \((f(x+h) - f(x-h))/2h\), rests on the limit definition (the root's {{Gradient lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}}, panel 1, runs that check on five losses).

Refresher: the limit definition, the secant picture and the six rules
The secant through \((3, 9)\) and \((3+h, (3+h)^2)\) (orange) turns into the tangent (blue) as \(h\) shrinks. Its slope is \(6 + h\), heading for 6; the gap between the tangent line and the curve at \(3 + h\) is exactly \(h^2\).

The derivative is the limit of the secant slope as the gap shrinks to zero, \(f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}\) (also written \(\frac{df}{dx}\); at \(h = 0\) the fraction would be 0/0, so the limit is the value it settles on as \(h\) gets as close to zero as you like). For \(f(x) = x^2\) at 3: \(\frac{(3+h)^2 - 9}{h} = 6 + h\); with \(h = 1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001\) the secant slopes are 7, 6.1, 6.01, 6.001, and the limit is \(f'(3) = 6\). In general \(f'(x) = 2x\).

\(\sigma(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-x})\) is the sigmoid and \(\ln(1 + e^x)\) the softplus: the last line says softplus's slope is the sigmoid. Every line was checked symbolically (sympy) by the page's script. Why the chain rule multiplies slopes: a step \(h\) moves \(u = g(x)\) by about \(g'(x)\,h\), which moves \(f\) by about \(f'(u)\,g'(x)\,h\); both approximations become exact in the limit. If \(u\) moves 3 units per unit of \(x\) and \(f\) moves 2 per unit of \(u\), \(f\) moves 6 per unit of \(x\).

The sigmoid's slope: \(\sigma'(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1 + e^{-x})^2} = \sigma(x)\,\big(1 - \sigma(x)\big)\), because \(\frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = 1 - \sigma(x)\). At \(x = 0\) it is \(0.25\), its largest value; far from zero it falls towards 0, which is why a sigmoid output trained with squared error stalls (section 2). The same steps give \(\tanh'(x) = 1 - \tanh^2(x)\). Many inputs: the gradient points up the steepest slope

A partial derivative is the derivative along one input with the others frozen: for \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2\), \(\partial f/\partial x = 2x\) and \(\partial f/\partial y = 6y\). The gradient \(\nabla f\) (often \(g\)) stacks them into a vector the shape of the input. Applying the linear approximation in every coordinate at once, the slope along a unit direction \(u\) is

\[ D_u f = \nabla f \cdot u = \lVert \nabla f \rVert \cos \phi \]
Turn on the spot at \((1, 1)\) on \(f = x^2 + 3y^2\): the slope you feel is largest (6.325) facing along the gradient \((2, 6)\) (blue), zero along the contour, most negative facing away. Facing east it is 2; north-east, \((2 + 6)/\sqrt2 = 5.657\).

where \(\phi\) is the angle between \(u\) and the gradient. So the gradient is the direction of steepest ascent, its length is that steepest slope, \(-\nabla f\) is steepest descent, and the gradient is perpendicular to the contour. The tiny model: the gradient of its loss in the three scores is \(g = (0.665, 0.245, -0.910)\), length 1.153. Moving the sat score alone has slope \(-0.910\); the best unit move, \(-g/\lVert g \rVert = (-0.577, -0.212, 0.789)\), lowers the loss at rate 1.153, faster than any single score could. "Steepest" depends on how length is measured: with a per-coordinate scale (Adam) or a curvature-weighted norm (Newton, natural gradient) the steepest direction changes, a clean way to see every optimiser (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.4). A vector function has one gradient per output; stacked as rows they form the Jacobian ({{Matrix calculus and backprop, section 2|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}}).

Taylor expansion: add the curvature

The second-order model matches the value, the slope and the bend (the second derivative \(f''\), the slope of the slope):

\[ f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h + \tfrac12 f''(a)\,h^2 \]
The parabola (blue) hugs \(e^x\) (grey) over a much wider range than the tangent (orange). At \(a = 0\), \(h = 0.5\): order 1 gives 1.5, order 2 gives 1.625, the truth is 1.6487.

The \(\tfrac12\) makes the parabola's second derivative equal \(f''(a)\). The error of the order-\(k\) model shrinks like \(h^{k+1}\) when \(f\) is smooth enough (Taylor's theorem; Nocedal and Wright, Theorem 2.1, states the multivariate version optimisers use). With many inputs the bend becomes the Hessian, \(H_{ij} = \partial^2 L / \partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j\), how the slope in direction \(i\) changes as you move in direction \(j\):

\[ L(\theta + \delta) \approx L(\theta) + g^\top \delta + \tfrac12\, \delta^\top H \delta \]

\(g^\top \delta\) is the slope term; \(\delta^\top H \delta = \sum_{i,j} \delta_i H_{ij} \delta_j\) is the curvature felt along the step \(\delta\), one number.

The numbers: the tiny model's first step
The root's step with learning rate 0.1 moves the scores by \(\delta = -0.5\,g = (-0.333, -0.122, 0.455)\) and the true loss falls from 2.408 to 1.773. The first-order model predicts \(2.408 - 0.5 \times 1.330 = 1.742\): it overshoots the drop, because the slope flattens as you go. Adding the curvature term with \(H = \operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\) (section 3) predicts 1.768, within 0.005 of the truth. For a nudge of 0.1 to the sat score alone the three values are 2.3166 (order 1), 2.3170 (order 2) and 2.3170 (true): the missing 0.0004 is the curvature.

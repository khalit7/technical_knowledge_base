3. Many inputs: partial derivatives, the gradient, and steepest ascent

The question. A model has millions of weights, not one. Which way should all of them move together so the loss falls fastest?

The picture

A function of two inputs is a landscape: the inputs are east and north, the output is height. A contour joins points of equal height, like the lines on a hiking map. Stand at a point and turn on the spot: in some directions the ground rises, in others it falls, and along the contour it is level. The gradient is the arrow pointing straight up the steepest slope, crossing the contours at right angles.

The bowl \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2\) at the point \((1, 1)\). Grey: contours. Blue: the gradient \((2, 6)\). Orange: the unit direction \(u\) you face. The slope you feel, \(D_u f = \nabla f \cdot u\), is largest (6.325) when \(u\) points along the gradient, zero along the contour, and most negative pointing the opposite way.

The words, then the formulas

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative taken along one input while the others are held fixed. For \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 3y^2\), holding \(y\) fixed gives \(\partial f/\partial x = 2x\) (the \(3y^2\) is a constant, slope 0), and holding \(x\) fixed gives \(\partial f/\partial y = 6y\). The curly \(\partial\) ("partial") signals that other inputs exist and are frozen.

The gradient stacks all the partial derivatives into one vector with the same shape as the input:

\[ \nabla f(\theta) = \Big( \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_1}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_2}, \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_n} \Big) \]
\(\theta\)
the vector of all inputs (in ML, all the weights), \(\theta = (\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n)\). Greek "theta".
\(\nabla f\)
"nabla f" or "grad f": the gradient, a vector of \(n\) slopes. Often written \(g\).

The directional derivative is the slope along any unit direction \(u\) (a vector of length 1): \(D_u f = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(\theta + t u) - f(\theta)}{t}\). Using the linear approximation in every coordinate at once, \(f(\theta + t u) \approx f(\theta) + t \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_i} u_i\), so

\[ D_u f = \nabla f \cdot u = \lVert \nabla f \rVert \, \lVert u \rVert \cos \phi = \lVert \nabla f \rVert \cos \phi \]

where \(\phi\) is the angle between \(u\) and the gradient and \(\lVert \cdot \rVert\) is length. The cosine is largest (1) at \(\phi = 0\): the gradient is the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is that steepest slope. At \(\phi = 180°\) the slope is \(-\lVert \nabla f \rVert\): \(-\nabla f\) is steepest descent. At \(\phi = 90°\) the slope is 0, so the gradient is perpendicular to the contour.

The numbers
At \((1, 1)\): \(\nabla f = (2, 6)\), length \(\sqrt{40} = 6.325\). Facing east, \(u = (1, 0)\): slope 2. Facing north-east, \(u = (1, 1)/\sqrt2\): slope \((2 + 6)/\sqrt2 = 5.657\). Facing along the gradient: 6.325, and no direction does better. The tiny model: the gradient of the loss with respect to the three scores is \(g = (0.665, 0.245, -0.910)\), length 1.153. Moving the cat score alone has slope 0.665; the sat score alone, \(-0.910\); the best unit move, \(-g/\lVert g \rVert = (-0.577, -0.212, 0.789)\), lowers the loss at rate 1.153, faster than any single score could.

A vector function, one with several outputs, has one gradient per output; stacked as rows they form the Jacobian matrix. It belongs to the sibling page ({{Matrix calculus and backprop, section 2|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}}); this page needs only gradients of the one scalar that matters, the loss.

What it is used for in ML
Gradient descent moves every weight along \(-\nabla L\), the steepest local descent. "Steepest" depends on how you measure length: with a different norm (a per-coordinate scale, as in Adam; a curvature-weighted one, as in Newton and natural gradient) the steepest direction changes, which is a clean way to see every optimiser (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 9.4).

4. Taylor expansion: the model every optimiser uses

The question. The straight-line approximation ignores how the slope itself changes. How much better is a curved approximation, and how many terms do we need?

The picture

Near a point, approximate the curve first by its tangent line (order 1), then by the parabola that matches its value, slope and bend (order 2). The parabola hugs the curve over a much wider range.

\(f(x) = e^x\) (grey) with its first-order (orange) and second-order (blue) Taylor models at \(a\); the readouts compare them with \(f\) at \(a + 0.5\).

The words, then the formulas

In one variable, with the second derivative \(f''\) (the slope of the slope):

\[ f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h + \tfrac12 f''(a)\,h^2 \]

The \(\tfrac12\) makes the parabola's second derivative equal \(f''(a)\): differentiating \(\tfrac12 f''(a) h^2\) twice gives \(f''(a)\). Taylor's theorem says the error of the order-\(k\) model shrinks like \(h^{k+1}\) when \(f\) is smooth enough (Nocedal and Wright, Theorem 2.1, states the multivariate version optimisers use). For \(e^x\) at \(a = 0\) and \(h = 0.5\): order 1 gives 1.5, order 2 gives 1.625, the truth is 1.6487.

With many inputs the slope becomes the gradient \(g\) and the bend becomes a matrix, the Hessian \(H\), whose entry \(H_{ij} = \partial^2 L / \partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j\) says how the slope in direction \(i\) changes as you move in direction \(j\):

\[ L(\theta + \delta) \approx L(\theta) + g^\top \delta + \tfrac12\, \delta^\top H \delta \]
\(\delta\)
the step, a vector the size of \(\theta\) (Greek "delta").
\(g^\top \delta\)
the gradient dotted with the step, \(\sum_i g_i \delta_i\): the linear (slope) term.
\(\delta^\top H \delta\)
\(\sum_{i,j} \delta_i H_{ij} \delta_j\): the curvature felt along the step, one number.
The numbers: the tiny model's first step
The root's step with learning rate 0.1 moves the scores by \(\delta = -0.5\,g = (-0.333, -0.122, 0.455)\) and the true loss falls from 2.408 to 1.773. The first-order model predicts \(2.408 + g^\top \delta = 2.408 - 0.5 \times 1.330 = 1.742\): it overshoots the drop, because the slope flattens as you go. Adding the curvature term with \(H = \operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\) (derived in section 6) predicts 1.768, within 0.005 of the truth. For the 0.1 nudge of section 1 the three values are 2.3166 (order 1), 2.3170 (order 2) and 2.3170 (true).

9. Constraints: Lagrange multipliers, KKT, and where ML uses them

Sometimes the parameters must satisfy a condition: probabilities sum to 1, a weight vector stays inside a ball, a policy stays close to the old one. Walk along the constraint curve \(g(x) = 0\) (here \(g\) is a constraint function, not a gradient): where \(f\) stops changing, every direction you may move is perpendicular to \(\nabla f\), and the curve's own normal is \(\nabla g\), so at the optimum the two gradients are parallel.

Maximise \(f = x + y\) on the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\). Grey: contours of \(f\); blue: \(\nabla f = (1, 1)\), the same everywhere; orange: \(\nabla g = (2x, 2y)\). Move round the circle: at 45° the two arrows are parallel and \(f\) is largest, \(\sqrt2\).
\[ \nabla f(x^*) = \lambda\, \nabla g(x^*), \qquad \mathcal{L}(x, \lambda) = f(x) - \lambda\, g(x) \]

\(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier and \(\mathcal L\) the Lagrangian: its gradient in \(x\) gives the parallel condition and its derivative in \(\lambda\) gives back the constraint, so one unconstrained system replaces the constrained problem. The multiplier is a price: with the constraint \(g(x) = c\), \(\lambda = df^*/dc\), how much the best value improves per unit the constraint is loosened. In the figure, \((1, 1) = \lambda(2x, 2y)\) and the constraint give \(x = y = 0.707\), \(\lambda = 0.707\), \(f^* = 1.414\); on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = c\) the optimum is \(f^* = \sqrt{2c}\), whose derivative \(1/\sqrt{2c}\) is 0.707 at \(c = 1\), equal to \(\lambda\).

Inequality constraints: the KKT conditions

To minimise \(f(x)\) subject to \(h_i(x) \le 0\), the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions at a solution with multipliers \(\mu_i\) are (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 5.5.3): stationarity \(\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_i \mu_i \nabla h_i(x^*) = 0\); primal feasibility \(h_i(x^*) \le 0\); dual feasibility \(\mu_i \ge 0\) (a constraint can only push, not pull); complementary slackness \(\mu_i h_i(x^*) = 0\): either the constraint is tight or its multiplier is zero. For a convex problem with a strictly feasible point (Slater's condition) they are necessary and sufficient. Example: minimise \((x - 2)^2\) subject to \(x \le 1\): the unconstrained answer 2 is outside, so the constraint binds, \(x^* = 1\), and \(2(x^* - 2) + \mu = 0\) gives \(\mu = 2 \ge 0\). With \(x \le 3\) instead, the constraint is slack and \(\mu = 0\).

Where the multipliers show up in models you train

  • Weight decay is a constraint in disguise. Minimising the loss subject to \(\lVert w \rVert^2 \le t\) has, by KKT, the same solution as minimising the loss plus \(\mu \lVert w \rVert^2\) for a matching \(\mu \ge 0\): the decay strength is the multiplier of a norm ball (a smaller ball, a larger multiplier). Practice: {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}.
  • Support vectors are complementary slackness. The SVM minimises \(\tfrac12\lVert w \rVert^2\) subject to \(y_i(w^\top x_i + b) \ge 1\); each example's multiplier \(\alpha_i\) is zero unless it sits on the margin (or violates it, soft margin), so \(w = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i x_i\) depends only on those examples ({{Classical ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ffb886ff30176bc6ce}}, section 5).
  • Softmax is the maximum-entropy distribution with a fixed average score: \(p_k = e^{\beta s_k}/\sum_j e^{\beta s_j}\), the inverse temperature \(\beta\) being the multiplier of the average-score constraint ({{Jaynes 1957|https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.106.620}}). With the tiny model's scores \((2, 1, 0)\) and \(\beta = 1\) it is the root's \((0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\).
  • TRPO's KL-constrained step is the natural gradient. Maximise the surrogate's first-order model \(g^\top \Delta\) subject to the second-order KL \(\tfrac12 \Delta^\top F \Delta \le \delta\) (\(F\), the Hessian of the KL, is the Fisher information): the Lagrangian is stationary at \(\Delta \propto F^{-1} g\), the natural gradient direction, with the length fixed by the budget ({{Schulman et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477}}, appendix C).
\[ \Delta = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{g^\top F^{-1} g}}\; F^{-1} g \]
The numbers: one Bernoulli policy
A policy that takes an action with probability \(p = \sigma(\theta)\) has Fisher information \(F = p(1 - p)\) in the logit (the root's Bernoulli curvature). With budget \(\delta = 0.01\) the allowed logit step is \(\sqrt{2\delta/F}\): at \(p = 0.5\), \(F = 0.25\), step 0.283 (to \(p = 0.570\), true KL 0.0100); at \(p = 0.9\), \(F = 0.09\), step 0.471 (to \(p = 0.935\), true KL 0.0088). The same budget allows a bigger parameter step where the distribution is less sensitive: the whole point of measuring steps in KL rather than in parameters.
Derivations: softmax as maximum entropy, TRPO's step, and duality

Softmax. Among distributions \(p\) over \(K\) outcomes with scores \(s_k\), maximise the entropy \(-\sum_k p_k \ln p_k\) with average score fixed at \(\bar s\):

\[ \mathcal L = -\sum_k p_k \ln p_k + \beta\Big(\sum_k p_k s_k - \bar s\Big) + \nu\Big(\sum_k p_k - 1\Big) \]

Differentiating in \(p_k\): \(-\ln p_k - 1 + \beta s_k + \nu = 0\), so \(p_k = e^{\nu - 1} e^{\beta s_k}\), and the sum-to-one constraint fixes \(e^{\nu - 1} = 1/\sum_j e^{\beta s_j}\) (Boyd and Vandenberghe, section 7.2, solve the same problem as a convex program).

TRPO. The paper (equation 55 in appendix C) maximises \(L(\theta)\) "subject to \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\theta_{\text{old}}, \theta) \le \delta\)". The Lagrangian \(g^\top \Delta - \tfrac{\lambda}{2}(\Delta^\top F \Delta - 2\delta)\) gives \(\Delta \propto F^{-1}g\); the paper's "\(\beta = \sqrt{2\delta / s^\top A s}\)" is the length above with \(s = F^{-1}g\) (they write \(A\) for the Fisher), solved by conjugate gradient with Fisher-vector products and followed by a line search. TRPO and the clipped objective of PPO that replaced it: {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}; the KL penalty in RLHF and GRPO: {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}.

Duality, in brief. Minimise the Lagrangian over \(x\) for fixed multipliers: \(d(\mu) = \min_x [f(x) + \sum_i \mu_i h_i(x)]\), the dual function. For any \(\mu \ge 0\) it is a lower bound on the best feasible value (weak duality), because on feasible points the added terms are \(\le 0\). The dual problem maximises that bound; for convex problems under Slater's condition the best bound equals the answer (strong duality; Boyd and Vandenberghe, sections 5.2.2 and 5.2.3). The SVM's dual has one multiplier per training example and only inner products \(x_i^\top x_j\), which is what lets kernels replace them. Convex optimisation in full: Boyd and Vandenberghe, chapters 2 to 5.

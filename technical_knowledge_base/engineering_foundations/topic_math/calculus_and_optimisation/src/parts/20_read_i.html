16. Stochastic gradients: noise, batch size, and why SGD still converges

The question. Nobody computes the gradient over the whole dataset; we use a minibatch. Why does a noisy gradient work at all, and what does the noise cost?

The full loss is an average over \(n\) examples, \(L(\theta) = \frac1n \sum_i \ell_i(\theta)\), so its gradient is the average of per-example gradients. A minibatch of \(B\) examples drawn at random gives \(\hat g = \frac1B \sum_{i \in \text{batch}} \nabla \ell_i\). Two facts follow from basic probability:

  • Unbiased: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat g] = \nabla L\). On average the minibatch points the right way.
  • Variance falls as \(1/B\): \(\mathbb{E}\lVert \hat g - \nabla L \rVert^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{B} \cdot \frac{n - B}{n - 1}\), where \(\sigma^2\) is the average squared distance of one example's gradient from the mean, and the last factor corrects for sampling without replacement (it is 0 at \(B = n\)).
Measured: the squared error of the minibatch gradient of the breast-cancer logistic loss at \(w = 0\), averaged over 4,000 random batches per size (dots), against the formula (line). Log-log axes; a slope of \(-1\) is the \(1/B\) law. Here \(\sigma^2 = 5.74\) against a squared gradient length of 2.01: one example's gradient is mostly noise.

What the noise does to convergence

Repeat the descent lemma of section 7 with \(\hat g\) and take expectations: the noise adds a term \(\frac{\beta\eta^2}{2}\cdot\) (variance) that does not shrink as the gradient does. So with a constant step SGD does not converge to the minimum; it converges to a region around it whose size is proportional to \(\eta\). For a strongly convex, smooth loss, {{Bottou, Curtis and Nocedal 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04838}} (Theorem 4.6) bound the expected gap by a term that decays geometrically plus a floor proportional to the step. With a decreasing step satisfying the Robbins-Monro conditions

\[ \sum_k \eta_k = \infty, \qquad \sum_k \eta_k^2 \lt \infty \]

(steps large enough in total to get anywhere, small enough that the noise they add stays finite), the gap goes to zero; with \(\eta_k = \beta/(\gamma + k)\) it falls like \(1/k\) (Theorem 4.7, after {{Robbins and Monro 1951|https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177729586}}).

SGD with batches of 8 on the same logistic regression (71 steps per epoch, geometric mean of 3 seeds), loss gap per epoch. Constant steps 2 and 0.5 stall at floors near \(10^{-2}\) and \(10^{-3}\), roughly proportional to the step; step 0.1 is still falling after 150 epochs; the decaying step \(2/(1 + k/500)\) starts fast and keeps falling.
What it is used for in ML
Why schedules decay the learning rate at the end of training; why a larger batch tolerates a larger step (less noise per step: the linear-scaling heuristic of Goyal et al. 2017) until the curvature limit of section 8 takes over; why L-BFGS (section 11) does not carry over to minibatches. The noise also helps: it pushes the iterate off saddles and plateaus. In practice: {{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}} (sections 1 and 7).

17. The landscape of a deep network: saddles, sharpness and the edge of stability

The question. A deep network's loss is not convex. Should we worry about bad local minima, and does the step-size theory of section 8 still apply?

Saddles, not local minima

{{Dauphin et al. 2014|https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2572}} gather arguments from statistical physics and random-matrix theory that for typical high-dimensional error functions "the ratio of the number of saddle points to local minima increases exponentially with the dimensionality". The intuition: at a critical point each of the \(n\) Hessian eigenvalues must be positive for a minimum, and if signs were anything like independent, all-positive would be exponentially rare. They also report that critical points with high error tend to have a large index (many negative eigenvalues), so the minima that exist tend to have low error, and they check it on small networks trained on down-sampled MNIST and CIFAR-10. Saddles are slow because they are surrounded by plateaus; gradient descent escapes along negative curvature, slowly; Newton is attracted to them (section 10). The {{Optimisation lab|#t-lab}}'s saddle function shows all three.

The edge of stability

Section 8 says gradient descent with step \(\eta\) is unstable if the top Hessian eigenvalue, the sharpness, exceeds \(2/\eta\). {{Cohen et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00065}} measured the sharpness during full-batch training of real networks and found two things. Progressive sharpening: while the sharpness is below \(2/\eta\) it "tends to continually rise". The edge of stability: once it reaches \(2/\eta\) it stops there: "the maximum eigenvalue of the training loss Hessian hovers just above the value 2/(step size), and the training loss behaves non-monotonically over short timescales, yet consistently decreases over long timescales". The quadratic theory would predict divergence; the network instead adjusts its own curvature.

Measured for this page
A small network (64 inputs, two hidden layers of 64 tanh units, 10 outputs; 8,970 parameters) trained by full-batch gradient descent on 1,000 of scikit-learn's bundled 8x8 handwritten digits, with squared error on one-hot targets (one of the two losses Cohen et al. study; they report both). Every 25 steps the sharpness is measured by power iteration with Hessian-vector products (the vector \(v\) is repeatedly replaced by \(Hv/\lVert Hv \rVert\); the Rayleigh quotient \(v^\top H v\) converges to the top eigenvalue; checked once against SciPy's Lanczos solver: 10.106 against 10.107).
Top: sharpness (top Hessian eigenvalue) against step, with the line \(2/\eta\) for each learning rate. Bottom: the training loss (log scale); the strip under it shows every step of the \(\eta = 0.2\) run over the 100-step stretch with the most up-and-down changes (steps 3,260 to 3,360), where the short-timescale oscillation is visible. All runs start from the same initialisation (sharpness 8.6).
What it shows
At \(\eta = 0.02, 0.05, 0.1\) (limits 100, 40, 20) the sharpness falls briefly and then rises steadily (progressive sharpening, to 9.5, 10.7 and 11.7 by step 6,000) and the loss falls at every single step. At \(\eta = 0.2\) (limit 10) the sharpness reaches 10 at step 825 and then hovers: its mean over the second half of training is 10.02, and the loss rises on 1,790 of 6,000 steps while still falling overall, to 0.0069. At \(\eta = 0.3\) (limit 6.67) the start is already too sharp; the sharpness drops to the limit within about 125 steps and stays there (mean 6.70; the loss rises on 2,411 steps). Two more seeds give second-half means of 10.01 and 10.02 (\(\eta = 0.2\)) and 6.71 and 6.71 (\(\eta = 0.3\)). The phenomenon reproduces on this small network.

What it means. The step-size limit is real, but in a network it is not a fixed property of the problem: training drives the curvature to whatever the learning rate allows. A higher learning rate selects flatter regions. This is one reason the learning rate acts as a regulariser, and why sharpness-aware minimisation (SAM; {{Foret et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.01412}}), which minimises the worst loss in a small ball around the weights, looks for flat minima on purpose. Whether flatness causes better generalisation is still debated (sharpness changes under reparameterisation: {{Dinh et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.04933}}).

Other second-order quantities you will meet in papers

  • Influence functions estimate how the model would change without one training example, \(-H^{-1}\nabla \ell_i\) ({{Koh and Liang 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.04730}}).
  • Optimal Brain Damage prunes the weights with the smallest \(\tfrac12 H_{ii} w_i^2\), the second-order estimate of the loss increase when \(w_i\) is set to zero ({{LeCun et al. 1989|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1989/hash/6c9882bbac1c7093bd25041881277658-Abstract.html}}).
  • Hessian spectra of trained networks: a bulk near zero plus a few large outliers, roughly one per class ({{Sagun et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04454}}, {{Ghorbani et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10159}}).

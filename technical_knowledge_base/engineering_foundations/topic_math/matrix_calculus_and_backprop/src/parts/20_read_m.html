12. Second derivatives: Hessian-vector products without the Hessian

The question. Curvature (how the slope itself changes) sets learning-rate limits and powers Newton-type methods. The Hessian of a model with \(n\) parameters has \(n^2\) entries. How can you use it at all?

The Hessian \(H\) of a scalar \(L\) is the Jacobian of its gradient: \(H_{ij} = \partial^2 L / \partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j\), an \(n \times n\) symmetric matrix (the order of differentiation does not matter for smooth functions). Its meaning, its eigenvalues and Newton's method are the calculus sibling's subject ({{Calculus and optimisation for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eb8b49ed6a0f897f3b}}). This page's concern is how to compute with it. Almost every use needs only products \(Hv\) with chosen vectors, and those can be had without ever forming \(H\):

\[ Hv = \nabla_\theta\big(\nabla_\theta L \cdot v\big) = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\, \nabla_\theta L(\theta + \varepsilon v)\Big|_{\varepsilon = 0} \]

Left: take the gradient, dot it with the fixed vector \(v\) (a scalar), and take the gradient of that: two reverse passes, "double backward". Right: the directional derivative of the gradient along \(v\): one forward-mode pass through the gradient computation, "forward-over-reverse". Pearlmutter's 1994 paper gives "an exact and numerically stable procedure for computing [\(Hv\)], which takes about as much computation, and is about as local, as a gradient evaluation", in "O(n) time and space" against the \(O(n^2)\) of forming \(H\) ({{Pearlmutter 1994, abstract and section 1|https://www.bcl.hamilton.ie/~barak/papers/nc-hessian.pdf}}). JAX's cookbook recommends the second nesting: "forward-over-reverse is typically the most efficient", because forward mode suits the outer, square Jacobian of \(\nabla L: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n\) ({{The Autodiff Cookbook|https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/notebooks/autodiff_cookbook.html}}). Baydin et al. note that the nestings perform "the same arithmetic operation sequence" and differ in overheads (footnote 16).

# PyTorch, double backward
g, = torch.autograd.grad(loss_fn(theta), theta, create_graph=True)   # keep the graph of the gradient
Hv, = torch.autograd.grad(g @ v, theta)
# PyTorch or JAX, forward-over-reverse
Hv = torch.func.jvp(torch.func.grad(loss_fn), (theta,), (v,))[1]
Hv = jax.jvp(jax.grad(loss_fn), (theta,), (v,))[1]
The tiny model
As a function of the input \(x\), the loss has Hessian \(W^\top(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top)W\): the softmax Jacobian of section 2, sandwiched by the linear layer. Its entries are \(H_{11} = 0.1848\), \(H_{12} = H_{21} = -0.2289\), \(H_{22} = 0.6007\). The HVP along \(v = (1, 0)\) is its first column, \((0.1848, -0.2289)\); PyTorch's double backward, jax.jvp(jax.grad(...)) and jax.hessian all give these numbers.

Two things to read off the measurement. The explicit Hessian grows like \(n\) gradients in time and \(n^2\) in memory, so beyond a few thousand parameters only HVPs are practical. And the advice on nesting is implementation-specific: in these PyTorch runs the double backward was as fast as forward-over-reverse or faster at every size (16.4 against 19.9 ms at \(n = 102{,}720\)), the opposite of JAX's recommendation for JAX, which is the "implementation-dependent" overhead Baydin et al.'s footnote warns about.

What HVPs are used for: Newton-type steps without the matrix (solve \(H\delta = -g\) by conjugate gradients, which needs only \(Hv\): "Hessian-free" optimisation); the sharpness \(\lambda_{\max}(H)\) by power iteration (\(v \leftarrow Hv/\lVert Hv \rVert\)), the quantity behind the learning-rate ceiling \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) and the edge-of-stability observations; the trace of \(H\) by Hutchinson's estimator, the average of \(v^\top H v\) over random sign vectors \(v\); and influence functions, which need \(H^{-1}\) times a gradient. Many second-order optimisers (K-FAC, Shampoo) instead approximate the curvature with structured matrices built from gradients and activations; that is the optimisers page's subject ({{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}}).

13. Einsum: one notation for every contraction, and its FLOP count

The question. Attention, batched matrix products, outer products and traces all look different on paper. Is there one way to write, cost and differentiate them all?

Einsum (Einstein summation) labels every axis with a letter. Two rules: a letter that appears in the inputs but not in the output is summed over; the output's letters, in the order written, give its shape (so a transpose is just a relabelling). Carried from the old Linear algebra page (checked):

operationeinsum
matrix productij,jk->ik
batched matrix productbij,bjk->bik
attention scores, per batch and headbhqd,bhkd->bhqk
attention outputbhqk,bhkd->bhqd
outer product; tracei,j->ij; ii->

The cost falls out of the letters. A contraction does one multiply-add for every combination of all its distinct letters, so it costs the product of all the letter sizes in multiply-adds, twice that in FLOPs. For attention scores with batch \(b = 2\), heads \(h = 8\), \(q = k = 128\) positions and head width \(d = 64\): \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 128 \cdot 64 = 16{,}777{,}216\) multiply-adds (33.6 million FLOPs, which is NumPy's einsum_path count, 3.355e+07). Doubling the sequence length doubles both \(q\) and \(k\), so the cost quadruples: attention's quadratic cost, read off the string.

Order matters for three or more operands. \(ABv\) with \(1000 \times 1000\) matrices can be computed as \((AB)v\) or \(A(Bv)\). NumPy's np.einsum_path('ij,jk,k->i', A, B, v, optimize='optimal') reports (real output, NumPy 2.5.3): "Naive FLOP count: 3.000e+09", "Optimized FLOP count: 4.000e+06", "Theoretical speedup: 750.000", by contracting k,jk->j first. (NumPy's count is the multiply-adds times the number of operands, 3 here, so its naive count is 3 times \(10^9\).) Its "optimal" strategy "combinatorially explores all possible ways of contracting the listed tensors"; the default "greedy" one is "Equivalent to the 'optimal' path for most contractions" ({{numpy.einsum_path|https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.einsum_path.html}}). This is the same lesson as section 4: multiply by the vector first.

The backward pass of an einsum is an einsum. For \(C\) = einsum('ij,jk->ik', A, B), the gradient for one operand is the einsum of the output's gradient with the other operands, written into that operand's letters: \(\bar A\) = einsum('ik,jk->ij', C̄, B) \(= \bar C B^\top\) and \(\bar B\) = einsum('ij,ik->jk', A, C̄) \(= A^\top \bar C\), the rules of 5a with no transposes to remember. Each backward einsum has the same set of letters as the forward one, so the same cost: a two-operand contraction costs twice as much backward, which is section 8 again.

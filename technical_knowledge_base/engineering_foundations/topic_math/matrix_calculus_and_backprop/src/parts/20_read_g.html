9. Memory: what the backward pass must keep, and checkpointing

Every rule in section 6 needs something from the forward pass: a linear layer's weight gradient needs its input \(x\); GELU needs its input; softmax and tanh need their outputs; layer norm needs its input and its statistics. These activations must be kept from the moment the forward pass computes them until the backward pass uses them, so at the turn from forward to backward almost all of them are alive at once. Inference keeps nothing: each value can be freed as soon as the next layer has read it.

Activation checkpointing (also gradient checkpointing or rematerialisation) keeps only some activations and recomputes the missing ones from the nearest checkpoint during the backward pass. Chen et al.: "we design an algorithm that costs \(O(\sqrt{n})\) memory to train a n layer network, with only the computational cost of an extra forward pass per mini-batch" ({{Chen et al. 2016, abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.06174}}). The argument (their section 4.3, Eq. 1): split \(n\) layers into \(k\) segments, keep the segment boundaries, and hold one segment's \(n/k\) activations at a time; memory is about \(n/k + k\), smallest at \(k = \sqrt n\), where it is \(2\sqrt n\) instead of \(n\), and each segment is recomputed once, so the extra work is at most one forward pass.

The counted recompute is 42.9 GFLOP, not the blocks' full forward 60.1, because non-reentrant checkpointing "stops recomputation as soon as all needed intermediate activations have been recomputed" ({{torch.utils.checkpoint docs|https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/checkpoint.html}}): each block's last matrix product is never redone, since its saved input is the GELU output, already rebuilt. In time, the whole step went from 0.31 to 0.42 s, about 35% more.

10. Vanishing and exploding gradients: a property of the product

By the chain rule, the gradient that reaches layer 0 of an \(n\)-layer chain is a product of \(n\) transposed Jacobians, \(\bar h_0 = J_1^\top J_2^\top \cdots J_n^\top\, \bar h_n\), so its size is bounded by the product of their largest stretches (spectral norms):

\[ \lVert \bar h_0 \rVert \le \lVert J_1 \rVert\, \lVert J_2 \rVert \cdots \lVert J_n \rVert\; \lVert \bar h_n \rVert \]

If each layer scales a typical vector by a factor \(g\), thirty layers scale it by about \(g^{30}\): \(0.9^{30} = 0.042\) and \(1.1^{30} = 17.4\). The gradient vanishes or explodes geometrically with depth, and no single layer is to blame: a 10% shortfall per layer is enough. Tanh's slope is at most 1, so at \(g = 1\) every factor shrinks a little and the gradient at layer 0 is 0.039 of what entered; raising \(g\) makes the weights larger but also pushes the units into saturation, where the slope is near 0. The cures all act on the factors: initialisation that sets \(g \approx 1\) for the activation used, and normalisation that keeps it there ({{Normalisation and initialisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81369e1cc38ed4e11d48}}); residual connections, whose factor \(I + J_F\) keeps an identity path through every layer (section 3), with the branches scaled down at depth ({{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}}); gradient clipping against the occasional explosion ({{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}}); and, in recurrent networks, where the same Jacobian is multiplied once per time step, gated cells ({{Sequence models: the pre-transformer lineage|n:3c65c17b0d0d818da4ded61ace6847ce}}, backpropagation through time).

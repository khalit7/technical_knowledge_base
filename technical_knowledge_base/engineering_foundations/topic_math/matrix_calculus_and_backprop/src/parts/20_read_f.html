7. Differentials and the trace trick: matrix gradients without indices

Index-by-index derivations get long for expressions like \(\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X B)\) or \(\log\det\Sigma\). Instead, take the differential \(dL\), the first-order change in \(L\) when the input changes by a small \(dX\), with the familiar rules applied to whole matrices (keeping the order of factors). Since \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top dX) = \sum_{ij} G_{ij}\,dX_{ij}\) is exactly the linear prediction with slopes \(G_{ij}\), the identification rule reads the gradient off:

\[ dL = \operatorname{tr}\!\big(G^\top dX\big) \text{ for every } dX \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_X L = G \]

The recipe: take the differential, use \(\operatorname{tr}(ABC) = \operatorname{tr}(CAB)\) to move \(dX\) to the far right inside a trace, and read off what stands in front of it, transposed. For a linear layer, \(dL = \bar y^\top (dW)\,x = \operatorname{tr}(x\,\bar y^\top\, dW)\), so \(\nabla_W L = \bar y\,x^\top\): section 6a in one line.

Three facts about the trace (the sum of the diagonal) do the rest: a scalar is its own trace; the trace is unchanged by cycling a product, \(\operatorname{tr}(ABC) = \operatorname{tr}(CAB) = \operatorname{tr}(BCA)\); and \(\operatorname{tr}(G^\top dX) = \sum_{ij} G_{ij}\,dX_{ij}\). Minka's classic note states the recipe as "1. compute the differential 2. massage the result into canonical form after which the derivative is immediately read off as the coefficient of dx, dx, or dX" ({{Minka 2000, Old and New Matrix Algebra Useful for Statistics, p. 2|https://tminka.github.io/papers/matrix/minka-matrix.pdf}}, based on Magnus and Neudecker). His canonical form is \(dy = \operatorname{tr}(A\,dX)\) with the derivative laid out by \(X^\top\), so his \(A\) is the transpose of the gradient \(G\) here: the same rule in the other layout.

A quadratic form \(f = x^\top A x\): \(df = dx^\top A x + x^\top A\,dx = x^\top A^\top dx + x^\top A\,dx\) (a scalar equals its transpose), so \(\nabla_x f = (A + A^\top)\,x\), which is \(2Ax\) when \(A\) is symmetric. With \(A\) rows \((2, 1)\), \((0, 3)\) and \(x = (1, 2)\): \(f = 2x_1^2 + x_1x_2 + 3x_2^2\), and both routes give \((6, 13)\).

A log-determinant (in every Gaussian log-likelihood and in normalising flows): \(d\ln\det X = \operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}dX)\), so \(\nabla_X \ln\det X = X^{-\top}\). With \(X\) rows \((2, 1)\), \((1, 3)\): \(\det X = 5\) and \(X^{-\top}\) has rows \((0.6, -0.2)\), \((-0.2, 0.4)\). Check one entry: raising \(X_{11}\) by \(h\) makes the determinant \(5 + 3h\), so \(\ln\det\) rises by about \(3h/5 = 0.6h\).

Least squares \(L = \lVert Xw - y \rVert^2\), with \(X\) the data matrix: \(dL = 2(Xw - y)^\top X\,dw\), so \(\nabla_w L = 2X^\top(Xw - y)\), and setting it to zero gives the normal equations \(X^\top X w = X^\top y\). Carried from the old Linear algebra page (checked): fit \(\hat y = w_0 + w_1 t\) to \((t, y) = (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 2)\); \(X^\top X\) has rows \((3, 3)\), \((3, 5)\) and \(X^\top y = (5, 6)\), so \(w = \tfrac{1}{6}(7, 3) = (1.167, 0.5)\). The residuals \((-0.167, 0.333, -0.167)\) add up to 0 and are orthogonal to \(t\), which is what \(\nabla_w L = 0\) says. Why solvers prefer QR to forming \(X^\top X\) (it squares the condition number, here 2.92 to 8.55) is on {{Linear algebra for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d89c26e1dab2fcb701}}.

8. Why the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass

Count one linear layer. Forward computes \(Y = XW^\top\): one matrix product, \(B \times n \times m\) multiply-adds. Backward (6a) computes two of the same size: \(\bar X = \bar Y W\) and \(\bar W = \bar Y^\top X\). Almost all of a transformer's arithmetic is matrix products, and a multiply-add is 2 FLOPs, so a model with \(N\) weights spends about \(2N\) FLOPs per token forward and \(4N\) backward. Kaplan et al.: "Accounting for the backwards pass (approximately twice the compute as the forwards pass), we then define the estimated non-embedding compute as \(C \approx 6N\) floating point operators per training token" ({{Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models, section 2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361}}; {{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b08a1debb0c15cd252}}). Their forward count is \(2N + 2\,n_{\text{layer}}\,n_{\text{ctx}}\,d_{\text{model}}\) (Eq. 2.2); the second term, attention over the context, is left out of the 6N.

Backward is exactly twice forward in FLOPs, less than twice in time

Measured on a small GPT-style stack: eight pre-norm blocks (width 512, 8 heads, MLP width 2048, vocabulary 8,192, tied embeddings, 29.4 million parameters, 25.2 million outside the embedding), a batch of 2 sequences of 512 tokens, float32, PyTorch 2.14.1; FLOPs from torch.utils.flop_counter.FlopCounterMode (matrix products only), times medians of 15 rounds on 2 CPU threads (src/inputs/measure/). FLOPs: exactly 2.00, forward 68.7 GFLOP and backward 137.4 GFLOP. Time: 1.44, 0.182 s backward against 0.126 s forward: the matrix products alone take 2.1 times as long backward (0.122 s against 0.057 s), but the forward pass also spends 0.026 s on softmax exponentials whose backward costs 0.006 s, and elementwise and memory-bound operations do not double.

So: "about twice" is a FLOP statement
It is exact for the matrix products, which is why FLOP budgets use 6N (and add the attention term at long context). Wall-clock time also depends on memory traffic and on the non-matmul operations, so measure it rather than assume it.
Where 6N and the measured 206.2 GFLOP differ; the runs under load (1 min)

By kind of product: mm 60.1 against 120.3 GFLOP, the attention bmm 8.6 against 17.2. Kaplan's 6N with \(N\) = 25.2 million gives 154.9 GFLOP for the step; the counter's total is 206.2. The gap is the two things 6N leaves out on purpose: the tied output layer (25.8 GFLOP, because it reuses the embedding) and attention over the context (another 25.8 at 512 tokens); 0.2 GFLOP goes the other way, because 6N counts the biases and norm weights as if they were matrix weights. Two earlier timing runs on the same machine under load gave backward-to-forward ratios of 1.95 and 1.57: wall-clock ratios depend on the hardware and on how much of the work is matrix products.

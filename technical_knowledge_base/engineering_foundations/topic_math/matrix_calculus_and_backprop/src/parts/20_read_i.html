Depth: second order, einsum, mistakes and research

Hessian-vector products: curvature without the Hessian, measured (4 min)

The Hessian \(H\) of a scalar \(L\) is the Jacobian of its gradient, \(H_{ij} = \partial^2 L / \partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j\), an \(n \times n\) symmetric matrix; its meaning, its eigenvalues and Newton's method are on {{Calculus and optimisation for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eb8b49ed6a0f897f3b}}. Almost every use needs only products \(Hv\), which never need \(H\):

\[ Hv = \nabla_\theta\big(\nabla_\theta L \cdot v\big) = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\, \nabla_\theta L(\theta + \varepsilon v)\Big|_{\varepsilon = 0} \]

Left: take the gradient, dot it with the fixed vector \(v\), and take the gradient of that: two reverse passes, "double backward". Right: the directional derivative of the gradient along \(v\): one forward-mode pass through the gradient computation, "forward-over-reverse". Pearlmutter's 1994 paper gives "an exact and numerically stable procedure for computing [\(Hv\)], which takes about as much computation, and is about as local, as a gradient evaluation", in "O(n) time and space" against the \(O(n^2)\) of forming \(H\) ({{Pearlmutter 1994, abstract and section 1|https://www.bcl.hamilton.ie/~barak/papers/nc-hessian.pdf}}). JAX's cookbook recommends the second nesting: "forward-over-reverse is typically the most efficient" ({{The Autodiff Cookbook|https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/notebooks/autodiff_cookbook.html}}); Baydin et al. note that the nestings perform "the same arithmetic operation sequence" and differ in overheads (footnote 16).

# PyTorch, double backward
g, = torch.autograd.grad(loss_fn(theta), theta, create_graph=True)   # keep the graph of the gradient
Hv, = torch.autograd.grad(g @ v, theta)
# PyTorch or JAX, forward-over-reverse
Hv = torch.func.jvp(torch.func.grad(loss_fn), (theta,), (v,))[1]
Hv = jax.jvp(jax.grad(loss_fn), (theta,), (v,))[1]

The tiny model. As a function of the input \(x\), the loss has Hessian \(W^\top(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top)W\): the softmax Jacobian sandwiched by the linear layer, with entries \(H_{11} = 0.1848\), \(H_{12} = H_{21} = -0.2289\), \(H_{22} = 0.6007\). The HVP along \(v = (1, 0)\) is its first column, \((0.1848, -0.2289)\); PyTorch's double backward, jax.jvp(jax.grad(...)) and jax.hessian all give these numbers.

The explicit Hessian grows like \(n\) gradients in time and \(n^2\) in memory, so beyond a few thousand parameters only HVPs are practical. The advice on nesting is implementation-specific: in these PyTorch runs double backward was as fast as forward-over-reverse or faster at every size (16.4 against 19.9 ms at \(n = 102{,}720\)), the opposite of JAX's recommendation for JAX, which is the "implementation-dependent" overhead Baydin et al.'s footnote warns about.

What HVPs are used for: Newton-type steps without the matrix (solve \(H\delta = -g\) by conjugate gradients: "Hessian-free" optimisation); the sharpness \(\lambda_{\max}(H)\) by power iteration (\(v \leftarrow Hv/\lVert Hv \rVert\)), behind the learning-rate ceiling \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) and the edge-of-stability observations; the trace of \(H\) by Hutchinson's estimator, the average of \(v^\top H v\) over random sign vectors; and influence functions, which need \(H^{-1}\) times a gradient. Second-order optimisers such as K-FAC and Shampoo instead approximate curvature with structured matrices built from gradients and activations ({{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}}).

Einsum: one notation for every contraction, its FLOP count and its backward pass (3 min)

Einsum labels every axis with a letter: a letter that appears in the inputs but not in the output is summed over, and the output's letters, in order, give its shape (so a transpose is a relabelling). Carried from the old Linear algebra page (checked):

operationeinsum
matrix productij,jk->ik
batched matrix productbij,bjk->bik
attention scores, per batch and headbhqd,bhkd->bhqk
attention outputbhqk,bhkd->bhqd
outer product; tracei,j->ij; ii->

The cost falls out of the letters. A contraction does one multiply-add per combination of all its distinct letters. For attention scores with batch \(b = 2\), heads \(h = 8\), \(q = k = 128\) and head width \(d = 64\): \(2 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 128 \cdot 64 = 16{,}777{,}216\) multiply-adds (33.6 million FLOPs, NumPy's einsum_path count, 3.355e+07). Doubling the sequence length doubles both \(q\) and \(k\), so the cost quadruples: attention's quadratic cost, read off the string.

Order matters for three or more operands. \(ABv\) with \(1000 \times 1000\) matrices: NumPy's np.einsum_path('ij,jk,k->i', A, B, v, optimize='optimal') reports (NumPy 2.5.3) "Naive FLOP count: 3.000e+09", "Optimized FLOP count: 4.000e+06", "Theoretical speedup: 750.000", by contracting k,jk->j first. (NumPy's count is the multiply-adds times the number of operands, 3 here.) Its "optimal" strategy "combinatorially explores all possible ways of contracting the listed tensors"; the default "greedy" one is "Equivalent to the 'optimal' path for most contractions" ({{numpy.einsum_path|https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.einsum_path.html}}). Section 3's lesson again: multiply by the vector first.

The backward pass of an einsum is an einsum. For \(C\) = einsum('ij,jk->ik', A, B), \(\bar A\) = einsum('ik,jk->ij', C̄, B) \(= \bar C B^\top\) and \(\bar B\) = einsum('ij,ik->jk', A, C̄) \(= A^\top \bar C\), rule 6a with no transposes to remember. Each backward einsum has the same letters as the forward one, so the same cost: a two-operand contraction costs twice as much backward, section 8 again.

Edit the sizes as letter=number pairs separated by spaces. The backward contractions are generated by the rule above (shown for two-operand contractions without a repeated letter inside one operand).

Mistakes the sections above do not already show (1 min)
  • "Checkpointing costs one whole extra forward pass." At most one. Non-reentrant checkpointing stops recomputing early (20.8% extra FLOPs measured, against 33% for a full forward of the step), and selective recomputation of only the attention core costs 2 to 3% ({{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}}).
  • Modifying a saved tensor in place raises an error only when the backward pass reaches that node, far from the line that caused it; torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True) reports the forward operation responsible.
  • Gradient accumulation over micro-batches is the same mechanism as the bug of forgetting zero_grad(), used on purpose: divide the loss by the number of micro-batches.
  • Breaking the graph without noticing. .detach(), .item(), .numpy(), in-place writes to .data, and integer operations such as argmax and rounding all stop gradients: the parameters before them silently get zero or no gradient. A straight-through estimator (a custom backward that passes \(\bar y\) through unchanged) is the deliberate version.
  • Mean against sum in the loss. With reduction="mean" each example's gradient carries a factor \(1/B\) (6d). Switching to a sum multiplies every gradient by the batch size, which acts like multiplying the learning rate by it.
Where research is going: attacking the memory and the ordering of reverse mode (2 min)
  • Not storing activations at all. Reversible networks reconstruct each layer's input from its output during the backward pass: RevNet is "a variant of ResNets where each layer's activations can be reconstructed exactly from the next layer's", so "the activation storage requirements are independent of depth" ({{Gomez et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.04585}}).
  • Differentiating a solution instead of the steps that found it. Implicit differentiation applies the implicit function theorem at a fixed point or an optimum. Deep equilibrium models "analytically backpropagate through the equilibrium point using implicit differentiation", so training needs "only constant memory, regardless of the effective "depth" of the network" ({{Bai, Kolter and Koltun 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01377}}); JAXopt made the technique modular ({{Blondel et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.15183}}).
  • Gradients from forward mode alone. The "forward gradient" multiplies a random direction \(v\) by the JVP along it: "an unbiased estimate of the gradient that can be evaluated in a single forward run of the function, entirely eliminating the need for backpropagation" ({{Baydin et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08587}}). Its variance grows with the number of parameters, which is why it has not replaced backpropagation for large models.
  • Splitting the backward pass. Rule 6a's two products, \(\bar X\) (needed at once by the previous layer) and \(\bar W\) (needed only by the optimiser), can be scheduled separately. Zero Bubble pipeline parallelism does exactly this, "to split the backward computation into two parts, one that computes gradient for the input and another that computes for the parameters", to fill pipeline bubbles ({{Qi et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10241}}; see {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}}).
  • Letting the compiler choose what to save. torch.compile traces forward and backward together (AOTAutograd) and decides which activations to save and which to recompute by a min-cut over the joint graph: "a min-cut based recomputation pass with AOTAutograd + NVFuser that consistently improves both memory and runtime" ({{He 2022, PyTorch developer forum|https://dev-discuss.pytorch.org/t/min-cut-optimal-recomputation-i-e-activation-checkpointing-with-aotautograd/467}}). Cheap elementwise operations are recomputed; matrix products and random operations are saved.

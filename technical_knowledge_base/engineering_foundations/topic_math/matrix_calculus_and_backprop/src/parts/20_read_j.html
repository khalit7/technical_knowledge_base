8. Why the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass

The question. "Training costs about three forward passes" and "6N FLOPs per token" are quoted everywhere. Where do they come from, and how exact are they?

Count one linear layer. The forward pass computes \(Y = XW^\top\): one matrix product, \(B \times n \times m\) multiply-adds. The backward pass (5a) computes two: \(\bar X = \bar Y W\) and \(\bar W = \bar Y^\top X\), each the same size. So every matrix product costs twice as much backward as forward, and almost all of a transformer's arithmetic is matrix products. A multiply-add is 2 floating-point operations (FLOPs), so a model with \(N\) weights spends about \(2N\) FLOPs per token forward and \(4N\) backward:

Kaplan et al.: "Accounting for the backwards pass (approximately twice the compute as the forwards pass), we then define the estimated non-embedding compute as \(C \approx 6N\) floating point operators per training token" ({{Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models, section 2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361}}; {{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b08a1debb0c15cd252}}). Their forward count is \(2N + 2\,n_{\text{layer}}\,n_{\text{ctx}}\,d_{\text{model}}\) (Eq. 2.2); the second term, attention over the context, is left out of the 6N.

Measured: a small GPT-style stack

Eight pre-norm blocks (width 512, 8 heads, MLP width 2048, vocabulary 8,192, tied embeddings, 29.4 million parameters, 25.2 million outside the embedding), a batch of 2 sequences of 512 tokens, float32, PyTorch 2.14.1. FLOPs are counted by torch.utils.flop_counter.FlopCounterMode, which counts matrix products only; times are medians of 15 rounds on 2 CPU threads (scripts in src/inputs/measure/).

  • FLOPs: exactly 2.00. Forward 68.7 GFLOP, backward 137.4 GFLOP, for both kinds of product (mm 60.1 against 120.3, the attention bmm 8.6 against 17.2). Kaplan's 6N with \(N\) = 25.2 million gives 154.9 GFLOP for the step; the counter's total is 206.2. The gap is the two things 6N leaves out on purpose: the tied output layer (25.8 GFLOP, because it reuses the embedding) and attention over the context (another 25.8 at 512 tokens); 0.2 GFLOP goes the other way, because 6N counts the biases and norm weights as if they were matrix weights.
  • Time: less than 2. On a quiet machine the backward pass took 0.182 s against 0.126 s forward, a ratio of 1.44. The profiler shows why: the matrix products alone take 2.1 times as long backward (0.122 s against 0.057 s), but the forward pass also spends 0.026 s on softmax exponentials whose backward costs 0.006 s, and the elementwise and memory-bound operations do not double. Two earlier runs on the same machine under load gave ratios of 1.95 and 1.57: wall-clock ratios depend on the hardware and on how much of the work is matrix products.
So: "about twice" is a FLOP statement
It is exact for the matrix products, which is why FLOP budgets use 6N (and add the attention term at long context). Wall-clock time also depends on memory traffic and on the non-matmul operations, so measure it rather than assume it.

9. Memory: what the backward pass must keep, and activation checkpointing

The question. Inference on a model fits in memory; training the same model on the same batch does not. What is all that extra memory?

Every rule in section 5 needs something from the forward pass: a linear layer's weight gradient needs its input \(x\); GELU needs its input; softmax and tanh need their outputs; layer norm needs its input and its statistics. These activations must be kept from the moment the forward pass computes them until the backward pass uses them, so at the turn from forward to backward almost all of them are alive at once. Inference keeps nothing: each value can be freed as soon as the next layer has read it.

Activation checkpointing (also called gradient checkpointing or rematerialisation) trades compute for memory: keep only some activations, the checkpoints, and during the backward pass recompute the missing ones from the nearest checkpoint. Chen et al. showed the trade can be made very cheap: "we design an algorithm that costs \(O(\sqrt{n})\) memory to train a n layer network, with only the computational cost of an extra forward pass per mini-batch" ({{Chen et al. 2016, abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.06174}}). The argument (their section 4.3, Eq. 1): split the \(n\) layers into \(k\) segments; keep the \(k\) segment boundaries, and during the backward pass hold one segment's \(n/k\) activations at a time. Memory is about \(n/k + k\), smallest at \(k = \sqrt n\), where it is \(2\sqrt n\) instead of \(n\); each segment is recomputed once, so the extra work is at most one forward pass.

One chain, four ways to run it

A chain of \(n\) layers \(h_k = \tanh(w_k\,h_{k-1})\) with a squared-error loss illustrative weights. The backward step through layer \(k\) needs \(h_{k-1}\) (for the weight's gradient) and \(h_k\) (tanh's derivative is \(1 - h_k^2\)): exactly what PyTorch saves for mul and tanh. The counted recompute is 42.9 GFLOP, not the blocks' full forward 60.1, because non-reentrant checkpointing "stops recomputation as soon as all needed intermediate activations have been recomputed" ({{torch.utils.checkpoint docs|https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/checkpoint.html}}): each block's last matrix product is never redone, since its saved input is the GELU output, already rebuilt. In time, the backward pass went from 0.182 to 0.288 s and the whole step from 0.31 to 0.42 s, about 35% more.

1. The shape of every derivative: gradients and Jacobians

For a scalar \(L\) of a vector or a matrix, the gradient holds one partial derivative per entry, laid out like the variable. Rule: the gradient of a scalar has the shape of the thing you differentiate with respect to. It is the most useful sanity check in this whole subject, and it is exactly what PyTorch stores in W.grad: for the tiny model's \(3 \times 2\) weight, \(\nabla_W L\) is \(3 \times 2\). With respect to the scores, the root page derives \(\nabla_z L = p - y = (0.665, 0.245, -0.910)\): raising the cat score by a small \(\varepsilon\) raises the loss by about \(0.665\varepsilon\); raising the sat score lowers it by about \(0.910\varepsilon\).

A function \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\) has one slope per (output, input) pair. Arranged with one row per output, they form the Jacobian:

\[ J = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \qquad J_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}, \quad J \text{ is } m \times n, \qquad f(x + \delta) \approx f(x) + J\delta \]

Row \(i\) is the gradient of output \(i\), laid flat; column \(j\) says how every output responds when only input \(j\) moves. Three Jacobians cover most of a network:

  • A linear map \(z = Wx\): \(\partial z_i/\partial x_j = W_{ij}\), so \(J = W\), here \(3 \times 2\).
  • An elementwise function \(h = \phi(a)\) (ReLU, tanh): output \(i\) depends only on input \(i\), so \(J = \operatorname{diag}(\phi'(a))\).
  • Softmax \(p_i = e^{z_i}/\sum_k e^{z_k}\): \(\partial p_i/\partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)\), so \(J = \operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top\), with the Kronecker delta \(\delta_{ij}\) (1 if \(i = j\), else 0) and the outer product \(p\,p^\top\).

Softmax's Jacobian, derived without skipping a step (2 min)

Write \(p_i = e^{z_i}/S\) with \(S = \sum_k e^{z_k}\). For the same index, the quotient rule (top' times bottom minus top times bottom', over bottom squared), with \(\partial e^{z_i}/\partial z_i = e^{z_i}\) and \(\partial S/\partial z_i = e^{z_i}\):

\[ \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_i} = \frac{e^{z_i} S - e^{z_i} e^{z_i}}{S^2} = p_i - p_i^2 = p_i(1 - p_i) \]

For a different index \(j \ne i\), the top does not move, the bottom does:

\[ \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} = \frac{0 \cdot S - e^{z_i} e^{z_j}}{S^2} = -p_i p_j \]

Both cases at once: \(\partial p_i/\partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j)\), that is \(J_{\text{softmax}} = \operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top\), where the outer product is a column times a row, a square table whose entry \((i, j)\) is \(p_i p_j\).

The shape of every derivative. The derivative of something with \(a\) entries with respect to something with \(b\) entries holds \(a \times b\) numbers (\(W\) here is \(p \times q\)):

derivative of ... with respect to ...scalar \(t\)vector \(x\) (\(n\))matrix \(W\) (\(p \times q\))
scalar \(L\)scalarvector, \(n\) (the gradient)matrix, \(p \times q\)
vector \(z\) (\(m\))vector, \(m\)matrix, \(m \times n\) (the Jacobian)3-index array, \(m \times p \times q\)
matrix \(Y\) (\(r \times s\))matrix, \(r \times s\)3-index array4-index array, \(r \times s \times p \times q\)

The bottom-right corner is where matrix calculus gets its reputation, and ML never needs it: the loss is a scalar, so the only derivatives that must be stored are in the top row, each shaped like its parameter. The big arrays appear only in the middle of the chain rule, and section 3 shows they can always be skipped.

The Jacobian with respect to a weight is mostly zeros

The derivative of \(z = Wx\) (3 numbers) with respect to \(W\) (6 numbers) is a \(3 \times 3 \times 2\) array of 18 numbers, \(\partial z_i / \partial W_{kl} = \delta_{ik}\, x_l\): score \(i\) depends only on row \(i\) of \(W\), so twelve of the 18 are zero. For one real layer with a \(4096 \times 4096\) weight the same array would have \(4096^3 \approx 6.9 \times 10^{10}\) entries, almost all zero.

Refresher: partial derivatives and the gradient on a function of two numbers (2 min)

Think of \(f(x_1, x_2)\) as the height of a landscape. The slope walking due east (only \(x_1\) changes) and due north (only \(x_2\)) are the partial derivatives; every other direction's slope is a mix of them. Stacking them gives \(\nabla_x f\), and the best linear prediction of a small step \(\delta\) is \(f(x + \delta) \approx f(x) + \nabla_x f \cdot \delta\). The slope along a unit direction \(u\), the directional derivative, is \(\nabla_x f \cdot u\); it is largest along the gradient, which is why training steps the other way.

Worked example: \(f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 3x_1x_2\) at \(x = (2, 1)\), where \(f = 4 + 6 = 10\). \(\partial f/\partial x_1 = 2x_1 + 3x_2 = 7\) and \(\partial f/\partial x_2 = 3x_1 = 6\), so \(\nabla f = (7, 6)\). Prediction for \(\delta = (0.01, 0)\): \(10 + 7 \times 0.01 = 10.07\); the exact value is \(2.01^2 + 3 \times 2.01 = 10.0701\). Along \(u = (0.6, 0.8)\) the slope is \(7 \times 0.6 + 6 \times 0.8 = 9.0\), a little under the steepest possible slope \(\lVert \nabla f \rVert = \sqrt{85} = 9.22\).

What gradients are used for in ML: every optimiser step (\(W \leftarrow W - \eta \nabla_W L\), learning rate \(\eta\)); gradient norms for clipping and for spotting exploding or vanishing training; saliency maps (the gradient of a class score with respect to the input pixels or tokens).

2. Numerator and denominator layout: why papers disagree

A paper writes \(\partial L/\partial W\) and its shape is the transpose of W.grad. Neither is wrong: the slopes are the same numbers, written the other way round.

Numerator layout (the Jacobian convention)

Rows follow the thing on top: \(\partial y/\partial x\) is \(m \times n\), the Jacobian of section 1. A scalar's derivative with respect to a vector is a row (\(1 \times n\)). The chain rule reads left to right in the order of the composition, with no transposes.

Denominator layout (the gradient convention)

Rows follow the thing underneath: \(\partial y/\partial x\) is \(n \times m\), the transpose. A scalar's derivative with respect to a vector is a column shaped like \(x\), which is what an update \(x \leftarrow x - \eta \nabla_x L\) needs.

The same six slopes, written both ways

Entry by entry they agree: \(\partial L/\partial W_{ij} = (p_i - y_i)\,x_j\), for example \(\partial L/\partial W_{\text{sat},1} = -0.910 \times 2 = -1.820\) (highlighted). In symbols: \(\partial z/\partial x\) for \(z = Wx\) is \(W\) (\(3 \times 2\)) in numerator layout and \(W^\top\) (\(2 \times 3\)) in denominator layout; \(\partial L/\partial z\) is the row \((0.665, 0.245, -0.910)\) or the same numbers as a column; \(\partial L/\partial W\) is \(x\,(p - y)^\top\), \(2 \times 3\), or \((p - y)\,x^\top\), \(3 \times 2\), what W.grad holds.

What frameworks do. A parameter's gradient (W.grad, jax.grad's output) always has the parameter's own shape, which for a scalar loss is the denominator layout. A full Jacobian comes back outputs first: torch.func.jacrev(f)(x) and jax.jacrev return an array of shape (output shape, then input shape), the numerator layout. This page makes the same choice: gradients in the parameter's shape, Jacobians as outputs by inputs.

The working rule
Derive in whichever layout you like, but never mix them in one derivation. Then check the shape: a scalar's gradient has the shape of the parameter. If yours is the transpose, transpose it and test one entry with a finite difference (section 11). The shapes alone nearly force many answers: for the squared error \(L = \lVert Wx - y \rVert^2\) with \(W\) of shape \(3 \times 2\), the only way to make \(3 \times 2\) from a 3-vector and a 2-vector is the outer product, \(\nabla_W L = 2(Wx - y)\,x^\top\).
Correction to the old Linear algebra page
The old page said numerator layout is "used by MML book, most papers". The MML book half is right (quoted below); "most papers" is not supported by any source found: the conventions are mixed within fields and often within one paper.
Who uses which, in their own words; the squared error checked (2 min)

There is no field-wide standard. Wikipedia's survey: "even within a given field different authors can be found using competing conventions. Authors of both groups often write as though their specific conventions were standard", and "some choose denominator layout for gradients (laying them out as column vectors), but numerator layout for the vector-by-vector derivative" ({{Wikipedia, Matrix calculus, Layout conventions|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_calculus#Layout_conventions}}). Two of the best-known ML texts use numerator layout and say so: Deisenroth, Faisal and Ong, "In this book, we use the numerator layout of the derivative" ({{Mathematics for Machine Learning, section 5.3, p. 150|https://mml-book.github.io/book/mml-book.pdf}}; they also write the gradient as a row vector, Eq. 5.40), and Parr and Howard, "We are using the so-called numerator layout but many papers and software will use the denominator layout" ({{The Matrix Calculus You Need For Deep Learning, section 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01528}}). Their reason is the chain rule: in numerator layout it reads left to right with no transposes.

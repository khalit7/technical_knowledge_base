The idea in one screen

Training needs the slope of one scalar loss with respect to every parameter. Backpropagation is the chain rule multiplied from the loss end: each layer turns the gradient at its output into the gradient at its input with one vector-Jacobian product (VJP), never building the Jacobian, so every slope comes out of one backward pass that costs about twice the forward pass in FLOPs and is paid for by keeping the forward pass's activations.

1. The shape of every derivative: gradients and Jacobians

For a scalar LL of a vector or a matrix, the gradient holds one partial derivative per entry, laid out like the variable. Rule: the gradient of a scalar has the shape of the thing you differentiate with respect to. It is the most useful sanity check in this whole subject, and it is exactly what PyTorch stores in W.grad: for the tiny model's 3×23 \times 2 weight, ∇WL\nabla_W L is 3×23 \times 2. With respect to the scores, the root page derives ∇zL=p−y=(0.665,0.245,−0.910)\nabla_z L = p - y = (0.665, 0.245, -0.910): raising the cat score by a small ε\varepsilon raises the loss by about 0.665ε0.665\varepsilon; raising the sat score lowers it by about 0.910ε0.910\varepsilon.

A function f:ℝn→ℝmf: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m has one slope per (output, input) pair. Arranged with one row per output, they form the Jacobian:

J=∂f∂x,Jij=∂fi∂xj,J is m×n,f(x+δ)≈f(x)+JδJ = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \qquad J_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}, \quad J \text{ is } m \times n, \qquad f(x + \delta) \approx f(x) + J\delta

Row ii is the gradient of output ii, laid flat; column jj says how every output responds when only input jj moves. Three Jacobians cover most of a network:

  • A linear map z=Wxz = Wx: ∂zi/∂xj=Wij\partial z_i/\partial x_j = W_{ij}, so J=WJ = W, here 3×23 \times 2.
  • An elementwise function h=ϕ(a)h = \phi(a) (ReLU, tanh): output ii depends only on input ii, so J=diag⁡(ϕ′(a))J = \operatorname{diag}(\phi'(a)).
  • Softmax pi=ezi/∑kezkp_i = e^{z_i}/\sum_k e^{z_k}: ∂pi/∂zj=pi(δij−pj)\partial p_i/\partial z_j = p_i(\delta_{ij} - p_j), so J=diag⁡(p)−pp⊤J = \operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top, with the Kronecker delta δij\delta_{ij} (1 if i=ji = j, else 0) and the outer product pp⊤p\,p^\top.

Refresher: partial derivatives and the gradient on a function of two numbers (2 min)

Think of f(x1,x2)f(x_1, x_2) as the height of a landscape. The slope walking due east (only x1x_1 changes) and due north (only x2x_2) are the partial derivatives; every other direction's slope is a mix of them. Stacking them gives ∇xf\nabla_x f, and the best linear prediction of a small step δ\delta is f(x+δ)≈f(x)+∇xf⋅δf(x + \delta) \approx f(x) + \nabla_x f \cdot \delta. The slope along a unit direction uu, the directional derivative, is ∇xf⋅u\nabla_x f \cdot u; it is largest along the gradient, which is why training steps the other way.

Worked example: f(x1,x2)=x12+3x1x2f(x_1, x_2) = x_1^2 + 3x_1x_2 at x=(2,1)x = (2, 1), where f=4+6=10f = 4 + 6 = 10. ∂f/∂x1=2x1+3x2=7\partial f/\partial x_1 = 2x_1 + 3x_2 = 7 and ∂f/∂x2=3x1=6\partial f/\partial x_2 = 3x_1 = 6, so ∇f=(7,6)\nabla f = (7, 6). Prediction for δ=(0.01,0)\delta = (0.01, 0): 10+7×0.01=10.0710 + 7 \times 0.01 = 10.07; the exact value is 2.012+3×2.01=10.07012.01^2 + 3 \times 2.01 = 10.0701. Along u=(0.6,0.8)u = (0.6, 0.8) the slope is 7×0.6+6×0.8=9.07 \times 0.6 + 6 \times 0.8 = 9.0, a little under the steepest possible slope ‖∇f‖=85=9.22\lVert \nabla f \rVert = \sqrt{85} = 9.22.

What gradients are used for in ML: every optimiser step (W←W−η∇WLW \leftarrow W - \eta \nabla_W L, learning rate η\eta); gradient norms for clipping and for spotting exploding or vanishing training; saliency maps (the gradient of a class score with respect to the input pixels or tokens).

2. Numerator and denominator layout: why papers disagree

A paper writes ∂L/∂W\partial L/\partial W and its shape is the transpose of W.grad. Neither is wrong: the slopes are the same numbers, written the other way round.

Numerator layout (the Jacobian convention)

Rows follow the thing on top: ∂y/∂x\partial y/\partial x is m×nm \times n, the Jacobian of section 1. A scalar's derivative with respect to a vector is a row (1×n1 \times n). The chain rule reads left to right in the order of the composition, with no transposes.

Denominator layout (the gradient convention)

Rows follow the thing underneath: ∂y/∂x\partial y/\partial x is n×mn \times m, the transpose. A scalar's derivative with respect to a vector is a column shaped like xx, which is what an update x←x−η∇xLx \leftarrow x - \eta \nabla_x L needs.

The same six slopes, written both ways

Entry by entry they agree: ∂L/∂Wij=(pi−yi)xj\partial L/\partial W_{ij} = (p_i - y_i)\,x_j, for example ∂L/∂Wsat,1=−0.910×2=−1.820\partial L/\partial W_{\text{sat},1} = -0.910 \times 2 = -1.820 (highlighted). In symbols: ∂z/∂x\partial z/\partial x for z=Wxz = Wx is WW (3×23 \times 2) in numerator layout and W⊤W^\top (2×32 \times 3) in denominator layout; ∂L/∂z\partial L/\partial z is the row (0.665,0.245,−0.910)(0.665, 0.245, -0.910) or the same numbers as a column; ∂L/∂W\partial L/\partial W is x(p−y)⊤x\,(p - y)^\top, 2×32 \times 3, or (p−y)x⊤(p - y)\,x^\top, 3×23 \times 2, what W.grad holds.

What frameworks do. A parameter's gradient (W.grad, jax.grad's output) always has the parameter's own shape, which for a scalar loss is the denominator layout. A full Jacobian comes back outputs first: torch.func.jacrev(f)(x) and jax.jacrev return an array of shape (output shape, then input shape), the numerator layout. This page makes the same choice: gradients in the parameter's shape, Jacobians as outputs by inputs.

The working rule
Derive in whichever layout you like, but never mix them in one derivation. Then check the shape: a scalar's gradient has the shape of the parameter. If yours is the transpose, transpose it and test one entry with a finite difference (section 11). The shapes alone nearly force many answers: for the squared error L=‖Wx−y‖2L = \lVert Wx - y \rVert^2 with WW of shape 3×23 \times 2, the only way to make 3×23 \times 2 from a 3-vector and a 2-vector is the outer product, ∇WL=2(Wx−y)x⊤\nabla_W L = 2(Wx - y)\,x^\top.

3. The chain rule as a product of Jacobians

Let y=g(x)y = g(x) and z=f(y)z = f(y). A small step δx\delta x moves yy by about JgδxJ_g\,\delta x, and that moves zz by about JfJ_f times it:

δz≈JfJgδx⟹Jf∘g(x)=Jf(g(x))Jg(x)\delta z \approx J_f\,J_g\,\delta x \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad J_{f \circ g}(x) = J_f\big(g(x)\big)\; J_g(x)

The Jacobian of a chain is the product of the Jacobians, in the order of the chain; entry by entry, ∂zi∂xk=∑j∂zi∂yj∂yj∂xk\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial x_k} = \sum_j \frac{\partial z_i}{\partial y_j}\frac{\partial y_j}{\partial x_k}, the effect of xkx_k on ziz_i through every intermediate yjy_j. The shapes line up by themselves, (m×p)(m \times p) times (p×n)(p \times n), and JfJ_f is evaluated at the value yy that gg actually produced, which is why the backward pass needs the forward pass's values.

For a scalar loss the last Jacobian is a single row, ∂L/∂y\partial L/\partial y. Written as gradients shaped like the variables, the chain rule becomes the vector-Jacobian product:

x‾=J⊤y‾(row form: ∂L∂x=∂L∂yJ),v‾:=∇vL, shaped like v\bar x = J^\top \bar y \qquad\text{(row form: } \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial y}\,J \text{)}, \qquad \bar v := \nabla_v L \text{, shaped like } v

Order matters: multiply from the loss end. For kk layers, ∂L∂x=∂L∂hkJkJk−1⋯J1\frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_k} J_k J_{k-1} \cdots J_1. Any bracketing gives the same answer, not the same cost. From the loss end every product is a row times a matrix: d2d^2 multiply-adds for width dd, kd2k d^2 in all. From the input end every product is a matrix times a matrix, d3d^3 each: at d=4096d = 4096 a factor of 4096 more. Section 4 turns the two orders into the two modes of automatic differentiation.

Several paths: gradients add. When one value feeds two places, the sum over intermediates becomes a sum over paths:

  • A residual connection y=x+F(x)y = x + F(x) has Jacobian I+JFI + J_F, so x‾=y‾+JF⊤y‾\bar x = \bar y + J_F^\top \bar y: the gradient arrives once through the identity path, untouched, and once through the block. That untouched copy is why very deep residual networks still train (section 10).
  • A weight used twice, such as an embedding tied to the output layer, gets the sum of the gradients from both uses. In PyTorch's graph of a transformer block (section 12), the embedding's accumulator is the very last node the engine runs, because it waits for both contributions.

4. Forward mode against reverse mode: why reverse wins for one loss

Automatic differentiation (AD) sees a program as a computational graph of primitive operations (a matrix product, an exponential, a sum), runs it, and applies the chain rule to each primitive operation as it runs, using that operation's known local derivative: exact up to rounding, works through loops and branches, and costs a small multiple of one evaluation. (Symbolic differentiation blows up in size and has no formula for code with branches; finite differences are approximate and need two evaluations per input, section 11.) The two modes are the two orders of section 3:

Forward mode: Jacobian-vector products

Pick one input direction uu and carry a tangent v˙\dot v (how fast each value changes along uu) alongside every value, in the order of the computation: z˙=J1u\dot z = J_1 u, p˙=J2z˙\dot p = J_2 \dot z, ... One pass gives JuJu: how every output changes along one input direction. A full gradient over nn inputs needs nn passes.

Reverse mode: vector-Jacobian products

Run forward once, keeping what each local derivative will need. Then carry an adjoint v‾\bar v backwards from one output: p‾\bar p, then z‾=J2⊤p‾\bar z = J_2^\top \bar p, then W‾\bar W and x‾\bar x. One backward pass gives the slope of one output with respect to every input. Backpropagation is reverse mode applied to a loss.

The rule. For f:ℝn→ℝmf: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m the whole Jacobian costs nn forward-mode passes or mm reverse-mode passes. The standard survey's bound: "ncops(f)n\,c\,\text{ops}(f)" in forward mode and "mcops(f)m\,c\,\text{ops}(f)" in reverse mode, "where c is a constant guaranteed to be c < 6 and typically c ∼ [2, 3]" (Baydin et al. 2018, section 3.2, p. 13, citing Griewank and Walther 2008). Training has m=1m = 1 and nn in the billions, so reverse mode, by a factor of nn. Forward mode wins when inputs are few and outputs many, such as the sensitivity of every output of a simulation to one hyperparameter. Measured with torch.func.jacfwd and jacrev on f(x)=Btanh⁡(Ax)f(x) = B\tanh(Ax) (hidden width 256, float64, two CPU threads):

The cheaper mode flips with the shape of the Jacobian

The price of reverse mode is memory. The backward sweep runs in the opposite order to the computation, so every value a local derivative needs must still exist when the sweep reaches it: stored during the forward pass, or recomputed. Forward mode carries its tangent along and can forget each value at once. Sections 8 and 9 measure both costs.

Dual numbers, JAX's cost notes, and a little history (2 min)

The cheap gradient principle. Griewank calls a gradient at a small constant multiple of the function's cost, whatever nn, the "Cheap Gradient Principle", and adds that "there is no corresponding Cheap Jacobian Principle" (Griewank 2012, p. 391).

Dual numbers. Forward mode has a neat algebraic form: numbers a+bεa + b\varepsilon with ε2=0\varepsilon^2 = 0. Then f(a+bε)=f(a)+f′(a)bεf(a + b\varepsilon) = f(a) + f'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon for any smooth ff (the Taylor series stops after the linear term), so evaluating with dual numbers carries the derivative along for free. PyTorch exposes it as torch.func.jvp and dual tensors in torch.autograd.forward_ad; JAX as jax.jvp.

JAX's documentation gives the same picture: for a VJP "the FLOP cost ... is only about three times the cost of evaluating f", and "though the FLOPs are friendly, memory scales with the depth of the computation"; for a JVP "the memory cost is independent of the depth of the computation"; and "jacfwd ... is more efficient for "tall" Jacobian matrices (more outputs than inputs), while jacrev ... is more efficient for "wide" Jacobian matrices" (JAX, The Autodiff Cookbook).

History. "Forward mode AD as a general method for evaluating partial derivatives was essentially discovered by Wengert (1964)"; the work of "Linnainmaa (1970, 1976) is often cited as the first published description of the reverse mode"; in machine learning it "has been reinvented several times ... until it was eventually brought to fame by Rumelhart et al. (1986)" (Baydin et al. 2018, section 3.3). Griewank's history adds that Ostrowski used the reverse idea some five years before Linnainmaa, in chemical engineering (Griewank 2012, p. 389). As Baydin et al. put it, "the backpropagation algorithm is only a special case of AD" (section 4.2).

6a. Linear layer: y=Wx+by = Wx + b

WW is m×nm \times n. One entry is yk=∑jWkjxj+bky_k = \sum_j W_{kj}\,x_j + b_k; WijW_{ij} appears only in output ii, times xjx_j; xjx_j appears in every output, times WkjW_{kj}; bkb_k once, with slope 1. So

A batch. PyTorch's nn.Linear stores WW as (out, in) and computes Y=XW⊤+bY = XW^\top + b for XX of shape B×nB \times n, one example per row:

W‾=Y‾⊤X(m×n),X‾=Y‾W(B×n),b‾=∑rowsY‾(m)\bar W = \bar Y^\top X \;(m \times n), \qquad \bar X = \bar Y\,W \;(B \times n), \qquad \bar b = \textstyle\sum_{\text{rows}} \bar Y \;(m)

A weight's gradient is (gradient coming in) times (input that went in), summed over the batch; an input's gradient is the gradient sent back through the transposed weights; a bias added to every row collects every row's gradient. 6c. Softmax: the VJP without the Jacobian

Multiply section 1's Jacobian (symmetric, so J⊤=JJ^\top = J) by an incoming p‾\bar p and simplify:

z‾=(diag⁡(p)−pp⊤)p‾=p⊙(p‾−(p⋅p‾)𝟏)\bar z = \big(\operatorname{diag}(p) - p\,p^\top\big)\,\bar p = p \odot \big(\bar p - (p \cdot \bar p)\,\mathbf{1}\big)

Subtract from each incoming gradient the probability-weighted average of all of them, then scale by the probability. Cost: one dot product and one pass, O(K)O(K), against O(K2)O(K^2) to build the Jacobian; for a 128,000-word vocabulary, a vector against a table of 16 billion numbers per token. PyTorch's softmax node saves only its output pp (_softmax_backward_data(grad, result, dim, ...), derivatives.yaml line 2237). 6d. Softmax cross-entropy: two VJPs that collapse

L=−ln⁡pyL = -\ln p_y gives p‾=−𝟏y/py\bar p = -\mathbf{1}_y / p_y (the one-hot vector of the right word, divided by its probability, which can be tiny), so p⋅p‾=−1p \cdot \bar p = -1, and softmax's VJP gives

The division by pyp_y cancels exactly. Computed separately, a probability that underflows to 0 would give p‾=−∞\bar p = -\infty and a NaN; fused, the gradient is just the probabilities minus the target. For a batch averaged over BB examples (reduction="mean") each row's gradient is (p−𝟏y)/B(p - \mathbf{1}_y)/B; with label smoothing or distillation targets qq, it is p−qp - q. 6e. Layer norm

Layer norm (Ba, Kiros and Hinton 2016) rescales each token's vector xx (NN entries) to mean 0 and variance 1, then applies a learned scale γ\gamma and shift β\beta: xˆ=(x−μ)r\hat x = (x - \mu)\,r with r=1/σ2+ϵr = 1/\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon} (PyTorch's rstd; ϵ\epsilon defaults to 10−510^{-5}), and y=γ⊙xˆ+βy = \gamma \odot \hat x + \beta. 7. Differentials and the trace trick: matrix gradients without indices

Index-by-index derivations get long for expressions like tr⁡(X⊤AXB)\operatorname{tr}(X^\top A X B) or log⁡det⁡Σ\log\det\Sigma. Instead, take the differential dLdL, the first-order change in LL when the input changes by a small dXdX, with the familiar rules applied to whole matrices (keeping the order of factors). Since tr⁡(G⊤dX)=∑ijGijdXij\operatorname{tr}(G^\top dX) = \sum_{ij} G_{ij}\,dX_{ij} is exactly the linear prediction with slopes GijG_{ij}, the identification rule reads the gradient off:

dL=tr⁡(G⊤dX) for every dX⟹∇XL=GdL = \operatorname{tr}\!\big(G^\top dX\big) \text{ for every } dX \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla_X L = G

The recipe: take the differential, use tr⁡(ABC)=tr⁡(CAB)\operatorname{tr}(ABC) = \operatorname{tr}(CAB) to move dXdX to the far right inside a trace, and read off what stands in front of it, transposed. For a linear layer, dL=y‾⊤(dW)x=tr⁡(xy‾⊤dW)dL = \bar y^\top (dW)\,x = \operatorname{tr}(x\,\bar y^\top\, dW), so ∇WL=y‾x⊤\nabla_W L = \bar y\,x^\top: section 6a in one line.

8. Why the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass

Count one linear layer. Forward computes Y=XW⊤Y = XW^\top: one matrix product, B×n×mB \times n \times m multiply-adds. Backward (6a) computes two of the same size: X‾=Y‾W\bar X = \bar Y W and W‾=Y‾⊤X\bar W = \bar Y^\top X. Almost all of a transformer's arithmetic is matrix products, and a multiply-add is 2 FLOPs, so a model with NN weights spends about 2N2N FLOPs per token forward and 4N4N backward. Kaplan et al.: "Accounting for the backwards pass (approximately twice the compute as the forwards pass), we then define the estimated non-embedding compute as C≈6NC \approx 6N floating point operators per training token" (Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models, section 2.1; paper page). Their forward count is 2N+2nlayernctxdmodel2N + 2\,n_{\text{layer}}\,n_{\text{ctx}}\,d_{\text{model}} (Eq. 2.2); the second term, attention over the context, is left out of the 6N.

Backward is exactly twice forward in FLOPs, less than twice in time

Measured on a small GPT-style stack: eight pre-norm blocks (width 512, 8 heads, MLP width 2048, vocabulary 8,192, tied embeddings, 29.4 million parameters, 25.2 million outside the embedding), a batch of 2 sequences of 512 tokens, float32, PyTorch 2.14.1; FLOPs from torch.utils.flop_counter.FlopCounterMode (matrix products only), times medians of 15 rounds on 2 CPU threads (src/inputs/measure/). 9. Memory: what the backward pass must keep, and checkpointing

Every rule in section 6 needs something from the forward pass: a linear layer's weight gradient needs its input xx; GELU needs its input; softmax and tanh need their outputs; layer norm needs its input and its statistics. These activations must be kept from the moment the forward pass computes them until the backward pass uses them, so at the turn from forward to backward almost all of them are alive at once. Inference keeps nothing: each value can be freed as soon as the next layer has read it.

Activation checkpointing (also gradient checkpointing or rematerialisation) keeps only some activations and recomputes the missing ones from the nearest checkpoint during the backward pass. Chen et al.: "we design an algorithm that costs O(n)O(\sqrt{n}) memory to train a n layer network, with only the computational cost of an extra forward pass per mini-batch" (Chen et al. 2016, abstract). The argument (their section 4.3, Eq. 1): split nn layers into kk segments, keep the segment boundaries, and hold one segment's n/kn/k activations at a time; memory is about n/k+kn/k + k, smallest at k=nk = \sqrt n, where it is 2n2\sqrt n instead of nn, and each segment is recomputed once, so the extra work is at most one forward pass.

10. Vanishing and exploding gradients: a property of the product

By the chain rule, the gradient that reaches layer 0 of an nn-layer chain is a product of nn transposed Jacobians, h‾0=J1⊤J2⊤⋯Jn⊤h‾n\bar h_0 = J_1^\top J_2^\top \cdots J_n^\top\, \bar h_n, so its size is bounded by the product of their largest stretches (spectral norms):

class SoftmaxCE(torch.autograd.Function):
    @staticmethod
    def forward(z, y):
        logp = z - torch.logsumexp(z, dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        return -logp.gather(-1, y[..., None]).squeeze(-1)

    @staticmethod
    def setup_context(ctx, inputs, output):
        z, y = inputs
        ctx.save_for_backward(torch.softmax(z, dim=-1), y)   # keep p, not the logits

    @staticmethod
    def backward(ctx, gL):
        p, y = ctx.saved_tensors
        gz = p.clone()
        gz.scatter_add_(-1, y[..., None], -torch.ones_like(y, dtype=p.dtype)[..., None])  # p - onehot
        return gL[..., None] * gz, None                      # times the incoming gradient; y gets none

    Hessian-vector products: curvature without the Hessian, measured (4 min)

    The Hessian HH of a scalar LL is the Jacobian of its gradient, Hij=∂2L/∂θi∂θjH_{ij} = \partial^2 L / \partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j, an n×nn \times n symmetric matrix; its meaning, its eigenvalues and Newton's method are on Calculus and optimisation for ML.     Hv=∇θ(∇θL⋅v)=ddε∇θL(θ+εv)|ε=0Hv = \nabla_\theta\big(\nabla_\theta L \cdot v\big) = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\, \nabla_\theta L(\theta + \varepsilon v)\Big|_{\varepsilon = 0}

    Left: take the gradient, dot it with the fixed vector vv, and take the gradient of that: two reverse passes, "double backward". Right: the directional derivative of the gradient along vv: one forward-mode pass through the gradient computation, "forward-over-reverse". Pearlmutter's 1994 paper gives "an exact and numerically stable procedure for computing [HvHv], which takes about as much computation, and is about as local, as a gradient evaluation", in "O(n) time and space" against the O(n2)O(n^2) of forming HH (Pearlmutter 1994, abstract and section 1). Doubling the sequence length doubles both qq and kk, so the cost quadruples: attention's quadratic cost, read off the string.

    Order matters for three or more operands. ABvABv with 1000×10001000 \times 1000 matrices: NumPy's np.einsum_path('ij,jk,k->i', A, B, v, optimize='optimal') reports (NumPy 2.5.3) "Naive FLOP count: 3.000e+09", "Optimized FLOP count: 4.000e+06", "Theoretical speedup: 750.000", by contracting k,jk->j first. (NumPy's count is the multiply-adds times the number of operands, 3 here.) Its "optimal" strategy "combinatorially explores all possible ways of contracting the listed tensors"; the default "greedy" one is "Equivalent to the 'optimal' path for most contractions" (numpy.einsum_path). Section 3's lesson again: multiply by the vector first.

    The backward pass of an einsum is an einsum. For CC = einsum('ij,jk->ik', A, B), A‾\bar A = einsum('ik,jk->ij', C̄, B) =C‾B⊤= \bar C B^\top and B‾\bar B = einsum('ij,ik->jk', A, C̄) =A⊤C‾= A^\top \bar C, rule 6a with no transposes to remember. Each backward einsum has the same letters as the forward one, so the same cost: a two-operand contraction costs twice as much backward, section 8 again.

    Mistakes the sections above do not already show (1 min)
    • "Checkpointing costs one whole extra forward pass." At most one. Non-reentrant checkpointing stops recomputing early (20.8% extra FLOPs measured, against 33% for a full forward of the step), and selective recomputation of only the attention core costs 2 to 3% (Distributed Training).
    • Modifying a saved tensor in place raises an error only when the backward pass reaches that node, far from the line that caused it; torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly(True) reports the forward operation responsible.
    • Gradient accumulation over micro-batches is the same mechanism as the bug of forgetting zero_grad(), used on purpose: divide the loss by the number of micro-batches.
    • Breaking the graph without noticing. .detach(), .item(), .numpy(), in-place writes to .data, and integer operations such as argmax and rounding all stop gradients: the parameters before them silently get zero or no gradient. A straight-through estimator (a custom backward that passes y‾\bar y through unchanged) is the deliberate version.
    • Mean against sum in the loss. With reduction="mean" each example's gradient carries a factor 1/B1/B (6d). Switching to a sum multiplies every gradient by the batch size, which acts like multiplying the learning rate by it.
    Where research is going: attacking the memory and the ordering of reverse mode (2 min)
    • Not storing activations at all. Reversible networks reconstruct each layer's input from its output during the backward pass: RevNet is "a variant of ResNets where each layer's activations can be reconstructed exactly from the next layer's", so "the activation storage requirements are independent of depth" (Gomez et al. 2017).
    • Differentiating a solution instead of the steps that found it. Implicit differentiation applies the implicit function theorem at a fixed point or an optimum. Deep equilibrium models "analytically backpropagate through the equilibrium point using implicit differentiation", so training needs "only constant memory, regardless of the effective "depth" of the network" (Bai, Kolter and Koltun 2019); JAXopt made the technique modular (Blondel et al. 2022).
    • Gradients from forward mode alone. The "forward gradient" multiplies a random direction vv by the JVP along it: "an unbiased estimate of the gradient that can be evaluated in a single forward run of the function, entirely eliminating the need for backpropagation" (Baydin et al. 2022). Its variance grows with the number of parameters, which is why it has not replaced backpropagation for large models.
    • Splitting the backward pass. Rule 6a's two products, X‾\bar X (needed at once by the previous layer) and W‾\bar W (needed only by the optimiser), can be scheduled separately. Zero Bubble pipeline parallelism does exactly this, "to split the backward computation into two parts, one that computes gradient for the input and another that computes for the parameters", to fill pipeline bubbles (Qi et al. 2023; see Distributed Training).
    • Letting the compiler choose what to save. torch.compile traces forward and backward together (AOTAutograd) and decides which activations to save and which to recompute by a min-cut over the joint graph: "a min-cut based recomputation pass with AOTAutograd + NVFuser that consistently improves both memory and runtime" (He 2022, PyTorch developer forum). Cheap elementwise operations are recomputed; matrix products and random operations are saved.

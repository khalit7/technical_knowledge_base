In one screen

A generative model explains each data point xx (an image, a sentence) by a hidden cause zz that we never observe. Training wants log⁡p(x)\log p(x), the log-probability the model gives the data, but that needs an average over every possible zz: an integral nobody can compute. Everything on this page is one trick for getting around that integral, and its consequences.

The trick: a lower bound

Pick a simple distribution q(z)q(z) and compute the ELBO (evidence lower bound) instead. Always log⁡p(x)=ELBO+DKL(q‖p(z|x))\log p(x) = \text{ELBO} + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \,\Vert\, p(z \mid x)), so the ELBO is below log⁡p(x)\log p(x) by exactly how far qq is from the true posterior. Raising it trains the model and fits qq at once (sections 1 to 4).

Gradients through samples

The ELBO is an average over samples of qq. Writing the sample as z=μ+σεz = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon (the reparameterisation trick) lets backprop pass through it, with far less noise than the score-function (REINFORCE) estimator: on this page's example, 6.2 against 71 at one dimension and 400,000 at a hundred (section 6).

The VAE

A network outputs q(z|x)q(z \mid x) (encoder), another outputs p(x|z)p(x \mid z) (decoder); the loss is reconstruction error plus DKL(q(z|x)‖𝒩(0,I))D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z\mid x) \,\Vert\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)), whose Gaussian closed form is derived in section 7. Trained here on MNIST in under two minutes (sections 7 to 10).

Diffusion is a VAE with a fixed encoder

The "encoder" adds Gaussian noise in 1,000 small steps; only the decoder (the denoiser) is learned. Its ELBO, worked out term by term, simplifies to "predict the noise that was added": the one-line loss of Ho et al. 2020 (sections 12 to 14).

Scores, speed, control

Predicting the noise is the same as estimating the score ∇xlog⁡p(x)\nabla_x \log p(x) (section 15). DDIM samples in 10 to 50 steps instead of 1,000 (16); classifier-free guidance is one line of arithmetic on two predictions (17).

Flow matching

Draw a straight line from a data point to a noise sample and train a network to predict its direction; integrate backwards to generate. The objective behind Stable Diffusion 3, FLUX.1 and Movie Gen (sections 18, 19).

1. Latent-variable models, and the integral you cannot do

The question. A model that generates images has to decide, somewhere, "this will be a 7, slanted, thick-stroked" before it decides any pixel. How do we write that down as probability, and why does it make training hard?

The story a latent-variable model tells. First draw a hidden value zz (the latent variable, Latin latere, to lie hidden) from a simple distribution, the prior p(z)p(z). Then draw the data xx from a distribution that depends on zz, the likelihood or decoder pθ(x|z)p_\theta(x \mid z), where θ\theta are the parameters we train (in a VAE, a neural network's weights). Together they define a joint distribution:

pθ(x,z)=p(z)pθ(x|z).p_\theta(x, z) = p(z)\, p_\theta(x \mid z).

We only ever see xx. The probability the model gives to an observed xx is therefore the marginal likelihood, also called the evidence: add up the joint over every value zz could have taken.

pθ(x)=∫p(z)pθ(x|z)dz.p_\theta(x) = \int p(z)\, p_\theta(x \mid z)\, dz .

In plain words: the chance of seeing this image is the average, over all possible hidden causes drawn from the prior, of how likely each cause makes the image.

Training by maximum likelihood (Probability for ML, section 10) means maximising ∑ilog⁡pθ(xi)\sum_i \log p_\theta(x_i) over the training set. So we need this integral, and its gradient with respect to θ\theta, for every example.

The running example: a model you can do by hand

Model A (used in sections 1 to 6 and in the ELBO lab)

Prior z∼𝒩(0,1)z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). Decoder x|z∼𝒩(z,s2)x \mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(z, s^2) with s=0.5s = 0.5: the data is the hidden value plus a little noise. We observe x=1.5x = 1.5.

Here the integral has a closed form. xx is a sum of two independent Gaussians, zz (variance 1) and the noise (variance s2=0.25s^2 = 0.25), so x∼𝒩(0,1.25)x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1.25) and

log⁡p(x=1.5)=−12log⁡(2π⋅1.25)−1.522⋅1.25=−1.0305−0.9000=−1.9305,\log p(x{=}1.5) = -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi \cdot 1.25) - \frac{1.5^2}{2 \cdot 1.25} = -1.0305 - 0.9000 = -1.9305,

that is p(x)=0.1451p(x) = 0.1451. Every number in sections 2 to 6 is measured against this −1.9305-1.9305 (src/recompute.py recomputes each one).

Why the integral is usually impossible. Model A is solvable only because a Gaussian pushed through a straight line stays Gaussian. Replace the mean zz by a neural network's output, x|z∼Bernoulli(decoderθ(z))x \mid z \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\text{decoder}_\theta(z)), and nothing cancels. Kingma and Welling name exactly this case: the integral is intractable "in cases of moderately complicated likelihood functions pθ(x|z)p_\theta(x \mid z), e.g. a neural network with a nonlinear hidden layer" (Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes, section 2.1). You could approximate it numerically, but the cost grows exponentially with the dimension of zz: a grid of 100 points per axis is 10410^4 decoder calls for a 2-D latent and 103210^{32} for a 16-D one. Naive Monte Carlo (draw zz from the prior, average p(x|z)p(x \mid z)) is unbiased but almost every prior sample explains a given image terribly, so the average is dominated by rare lucky draws.

Latent models you already know
  • A Gaussian mixture: zz is which cluster (discrete, KK values), so the "integral" is a sum of KK terms and is easy. Its posterior is the cluster responsibilities of EM.
  • A VAE: zz is a continuous vector (2 to a few hundred dimensions); decoder a network. Intractable.
  • A diffusion model: the latents are 1,000 progressively noisier copies of the image, x1,…,xTx_1, \dots, x_T, each as large as the image (section 12). Intractable.
  • Contrast: the next-token model of Topic: math has no latent: p(word|context)p(\text{word} \mid \text{context}) is a softmax, one sum over the vocabulary, computed exactly. That is why language models train on plain cross-entropy and need nothing on this page.
What it is used for in ML
Any model that generates data through hidden structure: VAEs, the autoencoders inside latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion's first stage), diffusion and flow models themselves, topic models, probabilistic PCA, and Bayesian neural networks (where zz is the weights).

2. The posterior, and why we approximate it

The question. Given an image, which hidden causes could have produced it? That distribution is the posterior. Why does everything hinge on it?

The posterior is the distribution of zz after seeing xx. Bayes' rule (Probability for ML, section 2) gives it:

pθ(z|x)=pθ(x|z)p(z)pθ(x).p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)}{p_\theta(x)} .

The numerator is easy (run the decoder, evaluate the prior). The denominator is the evidence from section 1, the intractable integral. So the posterior is intractable for exactly the same reason. Two jobs need it:

  • Inference: "what is this image's latent code?" (for representation learning, compression, editing).
  • Learning: the gradient of log⁡pθ(x)\log p_\theta(x) is an average under the posterior, ∇θlog⁡pθ(x)=𝔼pθ(z|x)[∇θlog⁡pθ(x,z)]\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(z \mid x)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x, z)\big] (Fisher's identity: differentiate log⁡∫pθ(x,z)dz\log\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz, then use ∇p=p∇log⁡p\nabla p = p\, \nabla \log p). The EM algorithm alternates exactly these two: compute the posterior (E-step), then maximise the expected log-joint (M-step). With an intractable posterior, EM cannot run, which is the second intractability Kingma and Welling list.
Model A's posterior, exactly

Multiply prior and likelihood and complete the square in zz: −z22−(1.5−z)22⋅0.25-\tfrac{z^2}{2} - \tfrac{(1.5 - z)^2}{2 \cdot 0.25} is a quadratic with leading coefficient −12(1+4)=−52-\tfrac12(1 + 4) = -\tfrac{5}{2}, so the posterior variance is 1/5=0.21/5 = 0.2; its peak is where the derivative −z+4(1.5−z)-z + 4(1.5 - z) vanishes, z=6/5=1.2z = 6/5 = 1.2. So p(z|x=1.5)=𝒩(1.2, 0.2)p(z \mid x{=}1.5) = \mathcal{N}(1.2,\ 0.2), standard deviation 0.447. In general 𝒩(x1+s2,s21+s2)\mathcal{N}\!\big(\tfrac{x}{1+s^2}, \tfrac{s^2}{1+s^2}\big): the observation pulls the prior's mean 0 most of the way to 1.5 and shrinks its spread five-fold.

3. Variational inference: turn inference into optimisation

The question. If the posterior cannot be computed, what is the next best thing, and how do we find it?

The idea. Choose a family 𝒬\mathcal{Q} of distributions simple enough to sample from and to evaluate (say, all Gaussians, each fixed by a mean and a variance), and pick the member closest to the true posterior. "Closest" is measured by the KL divergence (Information theory for ML, section 6), in this direction:

q∗=arg⁡minq∈𝒬⁡DKL(q(z)‖p(z|x)),DKL(q‖p)=𝔼z∼q[log⁡q(z)p(z|x)].q^{*} = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z \mid x)\big), \qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\left[\log \frac{q(z)}{p(z \mid x)}\right].

This is equation (10) of Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe's review. The search over qq is ordinary optimisation over the numbers that define qq, the variational parameters (written ϕ\phi, or μ,σ\mu, \sigma for a Gaussian). The word "variational" comes from the calculus of variations, optimising over functions; here the functions are indexed by ϕ\phi, so it is just gradient ascent on ϕ\phi.

Why this direction of KL (reverse KL). It is an average under qq, which we can sample. The other direction, DKL(p(z|x)‖q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(z\mid x) \Vert q), is an average under the posterior we cannot sample. The price: reverse KL is mode-seeking. It punishes qq for putting mass where the posterior has none, but not for missing some of the posterior's mass, so on a two-humped posterior a Gaussian qq picks one hump. The Information theory page, section 7 animates exactly this (its reverse fit lands on one mode with a KL of 0.692, about ln⁡2\ln 2); model B in the next section shows the same number appearing as an ELBO gap.

But the KL contains the posterior we cannot compute. Expand it with p(z|x)=p(x,z)/p(x)p(z \mid x) = p(x, z)/p(x) (Blei et al. eq. 11 to 12):

DKL(q‖p(z|x))=𝔼q[log⁡q(z)]−𝔼q[log⁡p(x,z)]+log⁡p(x).D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(z\mid x)\big) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] + \log p(x).

The unknown part, log⁡p(x)\log p(x), does not depend on qq at all. So minimising the KL over qq is the same as maximising what is left, the computable part with its sign flipped. That quantity is the ELBO.

What it is used for in ML
Every VAE encoder, Bayesian neural networks (Bayes by Backprop), topic models (LDA's original inference), Gaussian processes with inducing points, and the training objective of diffusion models (section 13).

4. The ELBO, derived three ways

The question. What exactly is the evidence lower bound, why is it a lower bound, and how tight is it? Three derivations, because each one answers a different follow-up question.

The definition (Kingma and Welling eq. 2; Blei et al. eq. 13):

ELBO(q)=𝔼z∼q[log⁡p(x,z)−log⁡q(z)].\text{ELBO}(q) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\big[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)\big].
log⁡p(x,z)\log p(x,z)
the log-joint: prior plus decoder, log⁡p(z)+log⁡p(x|z)\log p(z) + \log p(x\mid z); computable for any zz.
log⁡q(z)\log q(z)
the approximation's own log-density at the same zz.
𝔼z∼q\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q}
average over samples from qq: estimate it by drawing a few zz and averaging.

Way 1: Jensen's inequality (why it is a lower bound)

  1. Multiply and divide inside the integral by q(z)q(z) (allowed wherever q(z)>0q(z) > 0):
    log⁡p(x)=log⁡∫p(x,z)dz=log⁡∫q(z)p(x,z)q(z)dz=log⁡𝔼q[p(x,z)q(z)]\log p(x) = \log \int p(x, z)\, dz = \log \int q(z) \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\, dz = \log \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\right]
    .
  2. Jensen's inequality: for a concave function such as log⁡\log, the log of an average is at least the average of the log, log⁡𝔼[Y]≥𝔼[log⁡Y]\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log Y] (a chord of a concave curve lies below the curve).
  3. Apply it with Y=p(x,z)/q(z)Y = p(x,z)/q(z): log⁡p(x)≥𝔼q[log⁡p(x,z)−log⁡q(z)]=ELBO(q)\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)\big] = \text{ELBO}(q).

It is a bound for every qq, however bad. Equality needs YY to be constant, p(x,z)/q(z)=p(x,z)/q(z) = the same number for every zz, which happens only when q(z)∝p(x,z)q(z) \propto p(x, z), that is qq equals the posterior.

Way 2: the KL decomposition (how far below)

  1. Start from the definition and write p(x,z)=p(z|x)p(x)p(x, z) = p(z\mid x)\, p(x): ELBO=𝔼q[log⁡p(z|x)+log⁡p(x)−log⁡q(z)]\text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p(z \mid x) + \log p(x) - \log q(z)\big].
  2. log⁡p(x)\log p(x) is a constant under 𝔼q\mathbb{E}_q:
    ELBO=log⁡p(x)−𝔼q[log⁡q(z)p(z|x)]=log⁡p(x)−DKL(q‖p(z|x))\text{ELBO} = \log p(x) - \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log \frac{q(z)}{p(z\mid x)}\right] = \log p(x) - D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p(z\mid x))
    .
  3. Rearranged, this is Kingma and Welling's eq. (1) and Blei et al.'s eq. (14):
log⁡p(x)=ELBO(q)+DKL(q(z)‖p(z|x)).\log p(x) = \text{ELBO}(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z \mid x)\big).

The evidence splits into the bound plus the gap, and the gap is exactly the reverse KL of section 3. Because log⁡p(x)\log p(x) is fixed, every nat the ELBO gains is a nat the KL loses. When the model's parameters θ\theta also change, raising the ELBO either raises log⁡pθ(x)\log p_\theta(x) (better model) or shrinks the gap (better qq); you cannot tell which from the ELBO alone.

Model A, with q=𝒩(0.5, 0.52)q = \mathcal{N}(0.5,\ 0.5^2) (too far left and too wide; the posterior is 𝒩(1.2, 0.2)\mathcal{N}(1.2,\ 0.2))

Split the ELBO into three averages, each in closed form for Gaussians (using 𝔼q[(a−z)2]=(a−m)2+v\mathbb{E}_q[(a - z)^2] = (a - m)^2 + v for q=𝒩(m,v)q = \mathcal{N}(m, v)):

  • 𝔼q[log⁡p(x|z)]=−12log⁡(2π⋅0.25)−(1.5−0.5)2+0.252⋅0.25=−0.2258−2.5=−2.7258\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi \cdot 0.25) - \frac{(1.5 - 0.5)^2 + 0.25}{2 \cdot 0.25} = -0.2258 - 2.5 = -2.7258
  • 𝔼q[log⁡p(z)]=−12log⁡2π−0.52+0.252=−0.9189−0.25=−1.1689\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(z)] = -\tfrac12 \log 2\pi - \frac{0.5^2 + 0.25}{2} = -0.9189 - 0.25 = -1.1689
  • Entropy of qq: −𝔼q[log⁡q]=12log⁡(2πe⋅0.25)=0.7258-\mathbb{E}_q[\log q] = \tfrac12 \log(2\pi e \cdot 0.25) = 0.7258

Way 3: importance sampling (a family of tighter bounds)

  1. Way 1's first line says p(x)=𝔼q[w]p(x) = \mathbb{E}_q[w] with the importance weight w=p(x,z)/q(z)w = p(x, z)/q(z): draw zz from qq, reweight by how much more likely the model finds it than qq does. This is importance sampling, and it is unbiased for p(x)p(x).
  2. Average KK weights: pˆK=1K∑k=1Kwk\hat p_K = \frac1K \sum_{k=1}^K w_k, still unbiased for p(x)p(x), with variance falling like 1/K1/K.
  3. Take the log and apply Jensen again: 𝔼[log⁡pˆK]≤log⁡𝔼[pˆK]=log⁡p(x)\mathbb{E}[\log \hat p_K] \le \log \mathbb{E}[\hat p_K] = \log p(x). So
ℒK=𝔼z1,…,zK∼q[log⁡1K∑k=1Kp(x,zk)q(zk)]≤log⁡p(x),\mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}_{z_1, \dots, z_K \sim q}\left[\log \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{p(x, z_k)}{q(z_k)}\right] \le \log p(x),

the importance-weighted bound of Burda, Grosse and Salakhutdinov (IWAE), Theorem 1: ℒ1\mathcal{L}_1 is the ELBO, ℒK\mathcal{L}_K never decreases as KK grows, and it tends to log⁡p(x)\log p(x) when the weights are bounded.

Model B: when the family cannot reach the posterior

With the two-humped posterior of section 2 (x=2x = 2, log⁡p(x)=−2.1693\log p(x) = -2.1693 by quadrature), the best Gaussian qq sits on one hump (mean 1.33, standard deviation 0.19) and reaches an ELBO of −2.8811-2.8811: a gap of 0.712 nats that no optimiser can close, close to ln⁡2=0.693\ln 2 = 0.693, the cost of covering only one of two equal humps. Two more forms you will meet. Split log⁡p(x,z)=log⁡p(x|z)+log⁡p(z)\log p(x,z) = \log p(x \mid z) + \log p(z) and group the prior with qq (Kingma and Welling eq. 3):

ELBO=𝔼q[log⁡p(x|z)]⏟reconstruction−DKL(q(z)‖p(z))⏟stay near the prior.\text{ELBO} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p(x\mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{stay near the prior}} .

For model A's qq: −2.7258−0.4431=−3.1689-2.7258 - 0.4431 = -3.1689, the same number. This is the form a VAE implements (section 7). Engineers often minimise the negative ELBO and call it the loss; papers on images report it in bits per dimension (divide by Dln⁡2D \ln 2, DD the number of pixels).

5. Mean-field VI: coordinate ascent on a tiny example

The question. Before neural networks, how was the ELBO maximised, and what does the simplest family, "every latent independent", get wrong?

The mean-field family assumes qq factorises over groups of latents, q(z)=∏jqj(zj)q(z) = \prod_j q_j(z_j) (Blei et al. eq. 15). It cannot represent correlations between groups, but it makes each factor's best update computable. Holding all other factors fixed, the optimal qjq_j is (Blei et al. eq. 18; Bishop 2006, eq. 10.9):

qj∗(zj)∝exp⁡(𝔼−j[log⁡p(zj,z−j,x)]),q_j^{*}(z_j) \propto \exp\!\Big(\mathbb{E}_{-j}\big[\log p(z_j, z_{-j}, x)\big]\Big),

where 𝔼−j\mathbb{E}_{-j} averages over every factor except qjq_j. Why: with the others fixed, the ELBO as a function of qjq_j equals −DKL(qj‖p~j)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_j \,\Vert\, \tilde p_j\big) plus a constant, where p~j∝exp⁡(𝔼−j[log⁡p])\tilde p_j \propto \exp(\mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p]) (Blei et al. eq. 19); a KL is smallest, zero, when the two match. Cycling through the factors is coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI); each update can only raise the ELBO, so it converges (to a local optimum).

The example: a Gaussian with unknown mean and precision

Bishop's Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning, section 10.1.3. Data x1,…,xN∼𝒩(μ,τ−1)x_1, \dots, x_N \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^{-1}) (τ\tau is the precision, one over the variance). Priors μ|τ∼𝒩(μ0,(λ0τ)−1)\mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, (\lambda_0 \tau)^{-1}) and τ∼Gamma(a0,b0)\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a_0, b_0). The exact posterior is known (a Normal-Gamma), so we can see exactly what mean-field loses. Mean field: q(μ,τ)=q(μ)q(τ)q(\mu, \tau) = q(\mu)\, q(\tau).

  1. Write the log-joint, keeping only terms with μ\mu or τ\tau:
    log⁡p=N2log⁡τ−τ2∑n(xn−μ)2+12log⁡τ−λ0τ2(μ−μ0)2+(a0−1)log⁡τ−b0τ+const\log p = \tfrac{N}{2}\log\tau - \tfrac{\tau}{2}\sum_n (x_n - \mu)^2 + \tfrac12 \log \tau - \tfrac{\lambda_0 \tau}{2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2 + (a_0 - 1)\log \tau - b_0 \tau + \text{const}
    .
  2. Update q(μ)q(\mu): average over q(τ)q(\tau), which only replaces τ\tau by 𝔼[τ]\mathbb{E}[\tau] in the μ\mu terms. 6. Gradients through samples: score function against reparameterisation

The question. With a neural qϕq_\phi there is no closed-form update. We need ∇ϕ\nabla_\phi of the ELBO, an expectation under qϕq_\phi itself. How do you differentiate an average whose sampling distribution moves with the parameters?

Write the ELBO as 𝔼z∼qϕ[fϕ(z)]\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\phi}[f_\phi(z)] with fϕ(z)=log⁡p(x,z)−log⁡qϕ(z)f_\phi(z) = \log p(x,z) - \log q_\phi(z). Probability for ML, section 15 introduces both estimators on f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2; here they are derived for the ELBO and pushed to the case that decides between them: many latent dimensions.

The score-function estimator (REINFORCE, likelihood ratio)

  1. Write the expectation as an integral and differentiate under it:
    ∇ϕ∫qϕ(z)fϕ(z)dz=∫∇ϕqϕ(z)fϕ(z)dz+∫qϕ(z)∇ϕfϕ(z)dz\nabla_\phi \int q_\phi(z) f_\phi(z)\, dz = \int \nabla_\phi q_\phi(z)\, f_\phi(z)\, dz + \int q_\phi(z)\, \nabla_\phi f_\phi(z)\, dz
    .
  2. Use ∇q=q∇log⁡q\nabla q = q\, \nabla \log q (the derivative of a log): the first integral is 𝔼q[fϕ(z)∇ϕlog⁡qϕ(z)]\mathbb{E}_q\big[f_\phi(z)\, \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z)\big]. The function ∇ϕlog⁡qϕ(z)\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z) is called the score of qq.
  3. The second integral: ∇ϕfϕ=−∇ϕlog⁡qϕ\nabla_\phi f_\phi = -\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi, and the score averages to zero, 𝔼q[∇ϕlog⁡qϕ]=∫∇ϕqϕ=∇ϕ∫qϕ=∇ϕ1=0\mathbb{E}_q[\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi] = \int \nabla_\phi q_\phi = \nabla_\phi \int q_\phi = \nabla_\phi 1 = 0. It drops out.
∇ϕELBO=𝔼z∼qϕ[fϕ(z)∇ϕlog⁡qϕ(z)]≈1S∑sfϕ(zs)∇ϕlog⁡qϕ(zs).\nabla_\phi \text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\phi}\big[f_\phi(z)\,\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z)\big] \approx \frac1S \sum_{s} f_\phi(z_s)\, \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z_s).

It treats ff as a black box: try a zz, see how good it was, push qq towards it in proportion. It needs no derivative of ff, so it works for discrete zz and non-differentiable rewards (Williams 1992), which is why policy gradients use it.

The reparameterisation (pathwise) estimator

  1. Write the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and of noise that does not depend on them: z=gϕ(ε)=μ+σεz = g_\phi(\varepsilon) = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon, ε∼𝒩(0,1)\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) (for a diagonal Gaussian, element-wise).   2. Now the average is over ε\varepsilon, whose distribution has no ϕ\phi in it, so the gradient moves inside: ∇ϕ𝔼ε[fϕ(gϕ(ε))]=𝔼ε[∇ϕfϕ(gϕ(ε))]\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[f_\phi(g_\phi(\varepsilon))] = \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[\nabla_\phi f_\phi(g_\phi(\varepsilon))].

It uses the slope of ff at the sample, not only its value. One sample already says which way is uphill.

7. The VAE: encoder, decoder, and the two terms of its loss

The question. Put sections 1 to 6 together with neural networks. What exactly does a variational autoencoder compute, and where does each line of its loss come from?

A variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling 2013; Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra 2014, independently) has three parts:

  • Prior p(z)=𝒩(0,I)p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I), fixed.
  • Decoder pθ(x|z)p_\theta(x \mid z): a network maps zz to the parameters of a distribution over xx. For black-and-white pixels, 784 Bernoulli probabilities (one per pixel of a 28 by 28 image); for real values, Gaussian means.
  • Encoder qϕ(z|x)q_\phi(z \mid x): a network maps xx to a mean μϕ(x)\mu_\phi(x) and a log-variance log⁡σϕ2(x)\log\sigma^2_\phi(x), one per latent dimension, defining a diagonal Gaussian. It replaces solving for a separate qq per image (section 9).

The training objective is the ELBO in its "reconstruction minus KL" form (Kingma and Welling eq. 3 and 7), averaged over the data:

ℒ(θ,ϕ;x)=𝔼qϕ(z|x)[log⁡pθ(x|z)]−DKL(qϕ(z|x)‖𝒩(0,I)).\mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; x) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x) \,\Vert\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big).
  • Reconstruction: encode xx, sample z=μ+σ⊙εz = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon (section 6), decode, score the original xx under the decoder's distribution. For Bernoulli pixels log⁡pθ(x|z)\log p_\theta(x\mid z) is minus the binary cross-entropy summed over pixels; for a Gaussian decoder with fixed variance it is minus a scaled squared error plus a constant (Probability for ML, section 8). One sample per image is enough in practice (Kingma and Welling, section 2.3).
  • KL to the prior: keeps each image's code distribution from drifting away from 𝒩(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0,I) or shrinking to a point. Without it, the encoder would put every image at a distinct, infinitely precise point (a plain autoencoder), and sampling z∼𝒩(0,I)z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) would decode to nonsense.

The Gaussian KL, derived (Kingma and Welling, Appendix B)

One dimension first: q=𝒩(μ,σ2)q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2), p=𝒩(0,1)p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1).

  1. Definition: DKL(q‖p)=𝔼q[log⁡q(z)−log⁡p(z)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z) - \log p(z)].
  2. Write both log-densities: log⁡q(z)=−12log⁡(2πσ2)−(z−μ)22σ2\log q(z) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} and log⁡p(z)=−12log⁡2π−z22\log p(z) = -\tfrac12 \log 2\pi - \frac{z^2}{2}.
  3. Subtract; the log⁡2π\log 2\pi cancels: log⁡q−log⁡p=−12log⁡σ2−(z−μ)22σ2+z22\log q - \log p = -\tfrac12 \log \sigma^2 - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{z^2}{2}.
  4. Average under qq, using 𝔼q[(z−μ)2]=σ2\mathbb{E}_q[(z - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2 (definition of variance) and 𝔼q[z2]=μ2+σ2\mathbb{E}_q[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2 (variance plus squared mean): −12log⁡σ2−12+12(μ2+σ2)-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2).
  5. Collect, and sum over the JJ independent dimensions of a diagonal Gaussian (the KL of independent factors adds):
DKL(𝒩(μ,diag⁡σ2)‖𝒩(0,I))=12∑j=1J(μj2+σj2−1−log⁡σj2).D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2) \,\Vert\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big) = \frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2\big).

Each dimension pays for its mean being away from 0 (μ2\mu^2) and for its spread being anything but 1 (σ2−1−log⁡σ2\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2, zero at σ=1\sigma = 1, growing as σ\sigma shrinks or grows). Kingma and Welling print it as 12∑j(1+log⁡σj2−μj2−σj2)\frac12\sum_j(1 + \log\sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2), the negative KL, because they maximise.

Worked and checked

μ=(1,−0.5)\mu = (1, -0.5), σ=(0.5,2)\sigma = (0.5, 2): dimension 1 gives 12(1+0.25−1−log⁡0.25)=0.818\tfrac12(1 + 0.25 - 1 - \log 0.25) = 0.818 (the same number as the Information theory page's example), dimension 2 gives 12(0.25+4−1−log⁡4)=0.932\tfrac12(0.25 + 4 - 1 - \log 4) = 0.932; total 1.750 nats. A two-million-sample Monte Carlo average of log⁡q−log⁡p\log q - \log p gives 1.749 (standard error 0.002).

The whole loss, in code

This is the loss the page's VAE was trained with (from src/data/vae.py, PyTorch 2; log⁡σ2\log\sigma^2 is predicted rather than σ\sigma so it can take any real value):

mu, logvar = encoder(x)                      # [batch, J] each
z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)   # reparameterised sample
logits = decoder(z)                          # [batch, 784]
rec = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x, reduction='none').sum(-1)  # -log p(x|z)
kl = 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(-1)                          # closed form
loss = (rec + beta * kl).mean()              # beta = 1: minus the ELBO
Two common bugs in this snippet's neighbours
Averaging the reconstruction over pixels (reduction='mean') while summing the KL over dimensions silently multiplies the KL weight by 784: the model then behaves like a heavily over-weighted beta-VAE (section 10) and its samples blur. And the BCE loss on grey-level pixels is not the likelihood of any normalised distribution over grey levels (Loaiza-Ganem and Cunningham 2019, the continuous Bernoulli), so a "log-likelihood" computed that way is not comparable across papers; binarise the data, as here, or use a proper continuous likelihood.

9. Amortised inference, and where the ELBO's gap comes from

The question. The encoder answers "what is qq for this image?" in one forward pass. What does that shortcut cost?

Classical VI (section 5) optimises separate variational parameters for each data point. A VAE instead trains one network qϕ(z|x)q_\phi(z \mid x) for all of them: amortised inference (the cost of inference is paid once, in training, and spread over every future query). It is fast and works on new data, but the network may not output the best qq for each image. Cremer, Li and Duvenaud 2018 split the gap accordingly:

log⁡p(x)−ℒ[qϕ(z|x)]⏟total gap=log⁡p(x)−ℒ[q∗]⏟approximation gap+ℒ[q∗]−ℒ[qϕ(z|x)]⏟amortisation gap,\underbrace{\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]}_{\text{total gap}} = \underbrace{\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}[q^{*}]}_{\text{approximation gap}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}[q^{*}] - \mathcal{L}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]}_{\text{amortisation gap}},

where q∗q^{*} is the best member of the family for that one image, found by optimising its mean and variance directly.

Measured on the 2-D MNIST VAE (src/data/vae_gap.py, first 100 test images)

With a 2-D latent, log⁡p(x)\log p(x) can be computed almost exactly by brute-force quadrature: 161 by 161 decoder evaluations on a grid around each image's posterior. Then:

  • log⁡p(x)=−136.22\log p(x) = -136.22 nats (average).
  • ELBO with the encoder's qq: −144.46-144.46. Total gap 8.24 nats.
  • ELBO with a Gaussian optimised separately for each image (2,000 Adam steps): −136.31-136.31. Approximation gap 0.10: a diagonal Gaussian fits this model's posteriors almost perfectly.
  • Amortisation gap 8.15: nearly the whole gap is the encoder being a poor per-image optimiser on test images.

The importance-weighted estimates of section 4 with the encoder as proposal reach only −144.6-144.6 at K=1,000K = 1{,}000 (on 1,000 test images): when qq is off-centre, importance sampling converges slowly, which is why the quadrature is used as the truth here.

Cremer et al. found the same pattern on larger models: the amortisation gap often dominates, and fixes include bigger encoders, iterative refinement of the encoder's output at test time, and semi-amortised VI. Diffusion models avoid the question entirely: their "encoder" is fixed noise, not learned (section 12).

10. Beta-VAE and posterior collapse

The question. What happens when you change the weight on the KL term, and why do some VAEs ignore their latent altogether?

The beta-VAE (Higgins et al. 2017) multiplies the KL by β\beta: 𝔼q[log⁡p(x|z)]−βDKL(q(z|x)‖p(z))\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z\mid x)\Vert p(z)). With β>1\beta > 1 each nat of information in the code costs more, so the model keeps fewer, more independent factors (the paper's motivation: disentangled representations); with β<1\beta < 1 it keeps more and reconstructs better. Only β=1\beta = 1 is a bound on log⁡p(x)\log p(x); otherwise it is a different objective, the rate-distortion trade-off of Alemi et al. 2018: rate is the KL, distortion is the reconstruction term.

Five VAEs with a 16-D latent, measured (same data and network, 15 epochs each; test set; rec. is the reconstruction term). Bars: the KL carried by each of the 16 latent dimensions, sorted. "Active": how many of the 16 dimensions have a posterior mean that varies across images (variance above 0.01, the criterion of Burda et al. 2015, section 5.2). log p: importance-weighted estimate, K=1,000K = 1{,}000, first 1,000 test images.

Posterior collapse. At β=8\beta = 8 half the dimensions carry no information: their q(zj|x)q(z_j\mid x) equals the prior for every image, KL exactly 0, and the decoder ignores them. That is posterior collapse, partial here. It becomes total when the decoder is powerful enough to model xx without any code: Bowman et al. 2015 found an LSTM text decoder drove the KL to zero, so the VAE became a plain language model. Their fixes, still in use: KL annealing (start with the KL weight at 0 and raise it to 1) and word dropout (weaken the decoder so it must use zz). Free bits (Kingma et al. 2016, IAF) stops penalising the KL below a floor per dimension group; VQ-VAE replaces the Gaussian latent by a discrete codebook partly to avoid collapse.

Why it happens, in one line: the KL term is minimised (to 0) by q(z|x)=p(z)q(z\mid x) = p(z), and if the decoder can already reach a good reconstruction term without zz, nothing pays for moving away from that optimum.

11. Normalising flows, in one section

The question. Section 4's model B showed a Gaussian qq cannot fit a two-humped posterior. How do you build a distribution that is flexible but still has a density you can evaluate exactly?

Idea: start from a simple random variable zz and push it through an invertible function x=f(z)x = f(z). Where ff stretches space, probability thins out; where it squeezes, it piles up. The change-of-variables formula keeps the books.

  1. One dimension, ff increasing: the probability in a small interval is preserved, px(x)|dx|=pz(z)|dz|p_x(x)\,|dx| = p_z(z)\,|dz|.
  2. So px(x)=pz(z)|dzdx|=pz(f−1(x))|dfdz|−1p_x(x) = p_z(z) \left|\frac{dz}{dx}\right| = p_z\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)\, \left|\frac{df}{dz}\right|^{-1}.
  3. In DD dimensions the stretch factor of a small volume is the absolute determinant of the Jacobian matrix ∂f/∂z\partial f/\partial z (Matrix calculus and backprop, section 2; the determinant is the volume scaling of a linear map, Linear algebra for ML). In logs:
log⁡px(x)=log⁡pz(z)−log⁡|det⁡∂f∂z|,z=f−1(x).\log p_x(x) = \log p_z(z) - \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|, \qquad z = f^{-1}(x).
Two checks

z∼𝒩(0,1)z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1), x=2z+1x = 2z + 1: at x=3x = 3, z=1z = 1, df/dz=2df/dz = 2, so px(3)=𝒩(1;0,1)/2=0.2420/2=0.121p_x(3) = \mathcal{N}(1; 0, 1)/2 = 0.2420/2 = 0.121; the log-determinant is log⁡2=0.693\log 2 = 0.693. x=ezx = e^{z} (the log-normal): at x=2x = 2, z=ln⁡2z = \ln 2, df/dz=ez=2df/dz = e^z = 2, so px(2)=𝒩(0.693)/2=0.1569p_x(2) = \mathcal{N}(0.693)/2 = 0.1569; counting four million samples falling in [1.99,2.01][1.99, 2.01] gives 0.158.

A normalising flow composes KK such maps, zK=fK∘⋯∘f1(z0)z_K = f_K \circ \dots \circ f_1(z_0), and the log-densities add up (Rezende and Mohamed "Variational Inference with Normalizing Flows", eq. 6 to 7): log⁡qK(zK)=log⁡q0(z0)−∑k=1Klog⁡|det⁡∂fk∂zk−1|\log q_K(z_K) = \log q_0(z_0) - \sum_{k=1}^K \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f_k}{\partial z_{k-1}}\right|. The whole design problem is making that determinant cheap: a general D×DD \times D determinant costs O(D3)O(D^3).

  • Coupling layers (RealNVP, Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein and Bengio 2016): split zz into halves (a,b)(a, b); keep aa; set b′=b⊙es(a)+t(a)b' = b \odot e^{s(a)} + t(a) with any networks s,ts, t. The Jacobian is triangular, so its determinant is the product of the diagonal, exp⁡(∑s(a))\exp(\sum s(a)): one sum. Inverting needs no inverse of ss or tt.
  • Autoregressive flows (IAF, Kingma et al. 2016; MAF): each coordinate depends only on earlier ones, again a triangular Jacobian. IAF used as the VAE's qq is what fixes model B's problem at scale: the approximate posterior can bend and split.
  • Continuous flows: let K→∞K \to \infty with tiny steps, dz/dt=v(z,t)dz/dt = v(z, t). The log-density then changes at the rate −tr⁡(∂v/∂z)-\operatorname{tr}(\partial v/\partial z) (the instantaneous change of variables, Chen et al. 2018, Neural ODEs). What it is used for in ML

12. Diffusion: the forward process and its closed form

The question. A diffusion model's latents are the data with more and more noise added. What exactly is added, and why can we jump straight to any noise level?

Take a data point x0x_0 (an image; here a 2-D point). The forward process adds a little Gaussian noise TT times (Ho et al. 2020 eq. 2; Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015 introduced the idea):

q(xt|xt−1)=𝒩(xt; 1−βtxt−1, βtI),t=1,…,T.q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big), \qquad t = 1, \dots, T.
βt\beta_t
the noise variance added at step tt, from a fixed schedule; Ho et al. use T=1000T = 1000 and βt\beta_t rising linearly from 10−410^{-4} to 0.02.
1−βt\sqrt{1-\beta_t}
a slight shrink of the previous value, so the total variance stays bounded instead of growing.
αt, α‾t\alpha_t,\ \bar\alpha_t
shorthand: αt=1−βt\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t, and α‾t=∏s=1tαs\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s, the signal fraction left after tt steps (the bar is a product, not an average).

Read it as "xt=αtxt−1+1−αtεtx_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_t" with fresh noise εt∼𝒩(0,I)\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) each step: in the VAE language of section 7, this is a reparameterised encoder with no trainable parameters.

14. Watch it: the same points noised, then generated back

The question. What do the forward and reverse processes look like on actual points, and does the reverse process give each point back?

The models here were trained for this page (src/data/diffusion.py): a 3-hidden-layer network of width 64 (9,858 weights, shipped as 8-bit integers with one scale per row), trained for 30,000 Adam steps on two-moons data with LsimpleL_{\text{simple}}. A second network of the same size is trained with flow matching (section 18). What to notice. With DDPM the forward process is a random walk and the reverse process draws fresh noise at every step, so the points come back to the moons but not to their own places: the colours end up mixed across both moons. The model learned the distribution, not a code for each point. With DDIM (σt=0\sigma_t = 0, section 16) and with flow matching, both directions are deterministic ODE steps, so running the encoder and then the decoder returns each point close to where it started: these samplers define an invertible map between noise and data, which is what image editing by inversion relies on.

15. Score matching: predicting the noise is estimating a gradient

The question. Song and Ermon's score-based models and Ho et al.'s DDPM arrived separately and turned out to be the same thing. What is a score, and why is the noise prediction one?

The score of a distribution is the gradient of its log-density with respect to the data, s(x)=∇xlog⁡p(x)s(x) = \nabla_x \log p(x): an arrow at every point, pointing towards higher density. (Not the score of section 6, which was a gradient with respect to parameters; statistics uses the word for both.) Two facts make it useful:

  • It ignores normalisation. If p=p~/Zp = \tilde p/Z, then ∇xlog⁡p=∇xlog⁡p~\nabla_x \log p = \nabla_x \log \tilde p: the intractable constant disappears.
  • It is enough to sample. Langevin dynamics repeats x←x+η2s(x)+ηzx \leftarrow x + \tfrac{\eta}{2}\, s(x) + \sqrt{\eta}\, z with z∼𝒩(0,I)z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I); for small steps and long runs its samples follow pp (Song and Ermon 2019, eq. 4).

Learning a score without knowing it. Minimising 𝔼‖sθ(x)−∇log⁡p(x)‖2\mathbb{E}\|s_\theta(x) - \nabla\log p(x)\|^2 needs the unknown true score. Two tricks remove it. Hyvärinen 2005 integrated by parts to get an objective with only sθs_\theta and its divergence. Vincent 2011 showed that for noised data the target can be the score of the noising kernel, which is known: denoising score matching. For our kernel q(xt|x0)=𝒩(α‾tx0,(1−α‾t)I)q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I):

∇xtlog⁡q(xt|x0)=−xt−α‾tx01−α‾t=−ε1−α‾t.\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} .

So regressing onto ε\varepsilon is regressing onto the kernel's score times −1−α‾t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}, and the minimiser of a squared loss is a conditional mean: εθ∗(xt)=𝔼[ε|xt]\varepsilon_\theta^*(x_t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t], which Vincent's identity turns into the score of the noised marginal:

sθ(xt,t)=−εθ(xt,t)1−α‾t≈∇xtlog⁡qt(xt).s_\theta(x_t, t) = -\frac{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} \approx \nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t).
A case you can check by hand (Tweedie's formula)

Let the data be x0∼𝒩(0,1)x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). Then every xt∼𝒩(0,1)x_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) (variance preserving, section 12), with score −xt-x_t. The formula says the ideal noise prediction is ε∗=−1−α‾t⋅(−xt)=1−α‾txt\varepsilon^* = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\cdot(-x_t) = \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t. At t=200t = 200: slope 0.5839. A million simulated (x0,ε)(x_0, \varepsilon) pairs, regressing ε\varepsilon on xtx_t by least squares: slope 0.5843.

That is why Ho et al. say their sampler "resembles Langevin dynamics with εθ\varepsilon_\theta as a learned gradient of the data density" (section 3.2): DDPM's reverse step is a scaled score step plus noise. Song et al. 2021 unified the two families in continuous time: the forward process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) dx=f(x,t)dt+g(t)dwdx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw; its exact time reversal is another SDE that needs only the score, dx=[f−g2∇xlog⁡qt(x)]dt+gdw‾dx = [f - g^2 \nabla_x \log q_t(x)]\,dt + g\, d\bar w (their eq. 6, from Anderson 1982); and a deterministic probability flow ODE, dx=[f−12g2∇xlog⁡qt(x)]dtdx = [f - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla_x \log q_t(x)]\,dt (their eq. 13), has the same marginals at every tt. DDPM discretises the reverse SDE; DDIM, next, is a discretisation of the ODE.

16. Faster sampling: DDIM

The question. DDPM needs 1,000 network calls per sample. Can the same trained network sample in 20?

Song, Meng and Ermon 2020 noticed that LsimpleL_{\text{simple}} only ever uses the marginals q(xt|x0)q(x_t\mid x_0), never the step-to-step chain. Many chains share those marginals, including non-Markovian ones, so a network trained once can be sampled with any of them. Their update (eq. 12), in DDPM's notation:

xt−1=α‾t−1xt−1−α‾tεθ(xt)α‾t⏟predicted x0+1−α‾t−1−σt2εθ(xt)⏟direction pointing to xt+σtεt.x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\underbrace{\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}}_{\text{predicted } x_0} + \underbrace{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}_{\text{direction pointing to } x_t} + \sigma_t\,\varepsilon_t .

Guess the clean point from the current one; re-noise that guess to the next, lower noise level, reusing the predicted noise as the direction; optionally add fresh noise. With σt\sigma_t equal to DDPM's posterior spread it is DDPM; with σt=0\sigma_t = 0 it is deterministic, the DDIM sampler, and its steps can skip: use only a subsequence of noise levels (α‾\bar\alpha taken at, say, 20 evenly spaced tt).

17. Classifier-free guidance, as a formula

The question. A text-to-image model asked for "a red bicycle" should stick to the prompt more than plain conditional sampling does. What is the one line that achieves it, and where does it come from?

  1. Bayes' rule for scores. p(x|c)∝p(c|x)p(x)p(x \mid c) \propto p(c\mid x)\,p(x), so ∇xlog⁡p(x|c)=∇xlog⁡p(x)+∇xlog⁡p(c|x)\nabla_x \log p(x\mid c) = \nabla_x\log p(x) + \nabla_x \log p(c \mid x): the conditional score is the unconditional one plus a classifier's gradient (the normalising constant p(c)p(c) has no xx in it and vanishes).
  2. Turn up the classifier. Sampling with ∇log⁡p(x)+(1+w)∇log⁡p(c|x)\nabla\log p(x) + (1+w)\,\nabla\log p(c\mid x) targets p(x)p(c|x)1+wp(x)\,p(c\mid x)^{1+w}: images the classifier is more sure about. That is classifier guidance (Dhariwal and Nichol 2021), which needs a separate classifier trained on noisy images.
  3. Remove the classifier. Bayes again: ∇log⁡p(c|x)=∇log⁡p(x|c)−∇log⁡p(x)\nabla\log p(c\mid x) = \nabla\log p(x\mid c) - \nabla\log p(x). Substitute:
    ∇log⁡p(x)+(1+w)(∇log⁡p(x|c)−∇log⁡p(x))=(1+w)∇log⁡p(x|c)−w∇log⁡p(x)\nabla\log p(x) + (1+w)\big(\nabla\log p(x\mid c) - \nabla\log p(x)\big) = (1+w)\nabla\log p(x\mid c) - w\,\nabla\log p(x)
    .
  4. Write it with noise predictions (s=−ε/1−α‾s = -\varepsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}, section 15) and you have Ho and Salimans 2022, eq. 6:
ε~θ(xt,c)=(1+w)εθ(xt,c)−wεθ(xt,⌀).\tilde\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) = (1+w)\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) - w\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing).

Run the network twice, with the condition and with an empty condition ⌀\varnothing, and extrapolate past the conditional prediction, away from the unconditional one. One network does both: during training the condition is replaced by ⌀\varnothing with some probability (15% for this page's toy). w=0w = 0 is plain conditional sampling.

Libraries usually write ε⌀+s(εc−ε⌀)\varepsilon_\varnothing + s\,(\varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_\varnothing), so s=1+ws = 1 + w. Hugging Face diffusers' Stable Diffusion pipeline defaults to guidance_scale = 7.5, which is w=6.5w = 6.5 in the paper's notation (pipeline_stable_diffusion.py, v0.35.1).

18. Flow matching and rectified flow

The question. If generation is integrating an ODE from noise to data (sections 15, 16), why not learn the ODE's velocity directly, with the simplest possible path?

The path. Pair a data point x0x_0 with a noise sample ε\varepsilon and join them by a straight line: xt=(1−t)x0+tεx_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\varepsilon, t∈[0,1]t \in [0, 1] (Esser et al. 2024 (Stable Diffusion 3), eq. 13; Liu, Gong and Liu 2022, rectified flow). Along that line the velocity is constant, dxtdt=ε−x0\frac{dx_t}{dt} = \varepsilon - x_0.

The loss (conditional flow matching, Lipman et al. 2023):

ℒCFM(θ)=𝔼t∼U[0,1], x0, ε‖vθ(xt,t)−(ε−x0)‖2.\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim U[0,1],\ x_0,\ \varepsilon}\,\big\|v_\theta(x_t, t) - (\varepsilon - x_0)\big\|^2 .
  1. Many lines pass through the same point xx at time tt. The best single answer for a squared loss is their average direction, the marginal velocity v∗(x,t)=𝔼[ε−x0|xt=x]v^*(x,t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon - x_0 \mid x_t = x].
  2. Lipman et al.'s Theorem 2: the conditional loss above and the (intractable) loss against v∗v^* differ by a constant, so they have the same gradients. We regress on single lines and learn the average.
  3. The ODE dx/dt=v∗(x,t)dx/dt = v^*(x, t) moves the whole noise distribution at t=1t = 1 onto the data distribution at t=0t = 0 (the marginals of the straight-line paths are preserved). Generate by integrating backwards from t=1t = 1: Euler steps x←x−1Svθ(x,t)x \leftarrow x - \tfrac1S v_\theta(x, t).

It is the same family as diffusion: xtx_t is a scaled data point plus scaled Gaussian noise, so a velocity prediction converts to a noise prediction (εˆ=xt+(1−t)v\hat\varepsilon = x_t + (1-t)\,v) or a clean-data prediction (xˆ0=xt−tv\hat x_0 = x_t - t\,v). What changes is the path (straight in tt rather than variance-preserving) and the target (velocity rather than noise), and with them which errors are cheap.

Trajectories of 40 samples from the same starting noise, deterministic in both cases (50 steps, unconditional). Left: DDIM with the noise-prediction network. Right: Euler on the flow-matching network. Straightness = straight-line distance from start to end divided by the path length (1 = perfectly straight); the lines bend where they must choose a moon.

What the measurement says. On the 40 paths drawn, the flow-matching paths are less straight than DDIM's (0.55 against 0.73; recomputed in src/check_js.mjs). Each training line is straight, but lines from different (data, noise) pairs cross, and the network learns their average direction, which bends as a path has to commit to one moon; on two well-separated modes that bend is large. So the claim "flow matching gives straight paths" holds for each conditional path, not automatically for the learned ones. The flow model's good 5-step result in section 16 comes from where its steps fall in noise level, not from straightness. Rectified flow's reflow step retrains on the model's own (noise, sample) pairs, which no longer cross, to straighten the learned paths (Liu et al. 2022, section 2.1: "This reflow procedure not only decreases transport cost, but also has the important effect of straightening paths"); distillation then needs very few steps. Conventions differ: Lipman et al. put noise at t=0t = 0 and data at t=1t = 1 (their eq. 22 to 23, with a small σmin⁡\sigma_{\min}); Stable Diffusion 3 and this page put data at t=0t = 0.

20. Common mistakes

Using the wrong direction of KL for variational inference
VI minimises DKL(q‖p(z|x))D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p(z\mid x)), the average under qq. The other direction would need samples from the posterior. The direction is why VI underestimates spread (section 5) and picks one mode (section 4, model B).
Reading the ELBO as the log-likelihood
It is a lower bound with an unknown gap. On the MNIST VAE the encoder's ELBO was 8.2 nats below the true log⁡p(x)\log p(x) (section 9). Compare models with an importance-weighted estimate at stated KK (or exact quadrature when the latent is tiny), on the same data and binarisation.
Mixing sum and mean between the two loss terms
Mean over pixels for reconstruction and sum over latent dimensions for the KL is a hidden beta of 784 on MNIST. Sum both over dimensions, then average over the batch (section 7).
The KL formula with log⁡σ\log\sigma where it wants log⁡σ2\log\sigma^2
12(μ2+σ2−1−log⁡σ2)\tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2): if the network outputs log⁡σ\log\sigma, the last term is 2log⁡σ2\log\sigma and σ2=e2log⁡σ\sigma^2 = e^{2\log\sigma}. Getting it wrong still trains, to the wrong objective. Check against a Monte Carlo estimate, as section 7 does.
Diagnosing posterior collapse from the total loss
Look at the KL per latent dimension: a dimension with KL near 0 for every input is unused (section 10's beta = 8 model: 8 of 16). The total loss can look fine.
α\alpha means α‾\bar\alpha in some papers
DDPM: αt=1−βt\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t, α‾t\bar\alpha_t the product. DDIM: αt\alpha_t is the product. VDM and others use αt,σt\alpha_t, \sigma_t for the signal and noise scales. Read the definitions first.
Time running the other way
Diffusion and Stable Diffusion 3 put data at t=0t = 0; Lipman et al.'s flow matching puts noise at t=0t = 0. The velocity's sign flips with it.
Two guidance scales with the same name
The paper's ww and the libraries' s=1+ws = 1 + w (section 17). "Guidance 7.5" is w=6.5w = 6.5.
Dividing by α‾t\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} near the end of the cosine schedule
With βt\beta_t clipped at 0.999 the cosine schedule ends at α‾1000=2.4×10−9\bar\alpha_{1000} = 2.4\times10^{-9}, and the predicted x0=(xt−1−α‾εˆ)/α‾x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\hat\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha} multiplies the network's error by about 20,000. On this page's toy, 20-step DDIM on that schedule put 1.2% of 600 samples on the data until the predicted x0x_0 was clipped to the data range ([−3,3][-3, 3]) and the noise re-derived from the clipped value; with that fix, 95.3%. Image samplers clip to [−1,1][-1, 1] for the same reason.
Treating LsimpleL_{\text{simple}} as the ELBO
It drops the per-step weights (section 13): the true bound weighs t=1t = 1 about 80 times more than t=200t = 200. Likelihood numbers need the weighted bound.
Mixing up the two "scores"
Section 6's score is ∇ϕlog⁡qϕ(z)\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z), a gradient with respect to parameters, averaging to zero. Section 15's score is ∇xlog⁡p(x)\nabla_x \log p(x), a gradient with respect to the data. The link to noise prediction is s=−ε/1−α‾ts = -\varepsilon/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}, with that minus sign.

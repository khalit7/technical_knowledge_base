The idea in one screen

1. Latent-variable models, and the integral you cannot do

A latent-variable model tells a two-step story: draw a hidden value zz (the latent variable) from a simple prior p(z)p(z), then draw the data from the likelihood or decoder pθ(x|z)p_\theta(x\mid z), with θ\theta the parameters we train (in a VAE, a network's weights). We only ever see xx, so the probability of an observation is the marginal likelihood or evidence, the joint added up over every zz:

pθ(x)=∫p(z)pθ(x|z)dz.p_\theta(x) = \int p(z)\, p_\theta(x \mid z)\, dz .

Maximum likelihood (Probability for ML, section 7) needs ∑ilog⁡pθ(xi)\sum_i \log p_\theta(x_i) and its gradient, so it needs this integral for every example.

Model A, the running example (sections 1 to 5)

Prior z∼𝒩(0,1)z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1); decoder x|z∼𝒩(z,s2)x \mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(z, s^2) with s=0.5s = 0.5; we observe x=1.5x = 1.5. Here the integral has a closed form: xx is a sum of two independent Gaussians (variances 1 and 0.25), so x∼𝒩(0,1.25)x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1.25) and

log⁡p(x=1.5)=−12log⁡(2π⋅1.25)−1.522⋅1.25=−1.0305−0.9000=−1.9305,\log p(x{=}1.5) = -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi \cdot 1.25) - \frac{1.5^2}{2 \cdot 1.25} = -1.0305 - 0.9000 = -1.9305,

that is p(x)=0.1451p(x) = 0.1451. Every number in sections 2 to 5 is measured against this −1.9305-1.9305 (src/recompute.py recomputes each one).

Why it is usually impossible. Model A is solvable only because a Gaussian pushed through a straight line stays Gaussian. Make the mean a network's output, x|z∼Bernoulli(decoderθ(z))x \mid z \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\text{decoder}_\theta(z)), and nothing cancels: Kingma and Welling name exactly this case, intractable "in cases of moderately complicated likelihood functions pθ(x|z)p_\theta(x \mid z), e.g. a neural network with a nonlinear hidden layer" (Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes, section 2.1). A grid of 100 points per axis costs 10410^4 decoder calls for a 2-D latent and 103210^{32} for a 16-D one. Naive Monte Carlo (draw zz from the prior, average p(x|z)p(x\mid z)) is unbiased, but almost every prior sample explains a given image terribly, so the average is dominated by rare lucky draws.

Where latents appear. A Gaussian mixture (zz is the cluster; the integral is a sum of KK terms, easy, and its posterior is EM's responsibilities); a VAE (zz a continuous vector of 2 to a few hundred numbers, decoder a network: intractable); a diffusion model (the latents are 1,000 progressively noisier copies of the image, x1,…,xTx_1, \dots, x_T, each as large as the image: intractable); also topic models, probabilistic PCA and Bayesian neural networks (where zz is the weights). Contrast: a next-token model has no latent; p(word|context)p(\text{word}\mid\text{context}) is a softmax, one exact sum over the vocabulary, which is why language models train on plain cross-entropy and need nothing on this page.

2. The posterior, and turning inference into optimisation

The posterior is the distribution of zz after seeing xx, by Bayes' rule (Probability for ML, section 5):

pθ(z|x)=pθ(x|z)p(z)pθ(x).p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)}{p_\theta(x)} .

The numerator is easy (run the decoder, evaluate the prior); the denominator is the evidence of section 1. So the posterior is intractable for exactly the same reason, and two jobs need it: inference ("what is this image's latent code?", for representations, compression, editing) and learning, because the gradient of log⁡pθ(x)\log p_\theta(x) is an average under the posterior (Fisher's identity, below), which is also why EM cannot run when the posterior is intractable, the second intractability Kingma and Welling list.

Model A's posterior, exactly

Multiply prior and likelihood and complete the square in zz: −z22−(1.5−z)22⋅0.25-\tfrac{z^2}{2} - \tfrac{(1.5 - z)^2}{2 \cdot 0.25} has leading coefficient −12(1+4)=−52-\tfrac12(1 + 4) = -\tfrac{5}{2}, so the posterior variance is 1/5=0.21/5 = 0.2; the peak is where −z+4(1.5−z)-z + 4(1.5 - z) vanishes, z=6/5=1.2z = 6/5 = 1.2. So p(z|x=1.5)=𝒩(1.2, 0.2)p(z \mid x{=}1.5) = \mathcal{N}(1.2,\ 0.2), standard deviation 0.447. In general 𝒩(x1+s2,s21+s2)\mathcal{N}\!\big(\tfrac{x}{1+s^2}, \tfrac{s^2}{1+s^2}\big): the observation pulls the prior's mean 0 most of the way to 1.5 and shrinks its spread five-fold. Variational inference: pick the closest simple distribution

Choose a family 𝒬\mathcal{Q} simple enough to sample and evaluate (say all Gaussians, each fixed by a mean and a variance) and take the member closest to the posterior in KL (Information theory for ML, section 3), in this direction (equation (10) of Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe's review):

q∗=arg⁡minq∈𝒬⁡DKL(q(z)‖p(z|x)),DKL(q‖p)=𝔼z∼q[log⁡q(z)p(z|x)].q^{*} = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z \mid x)\big), \qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\left[\log \frac{q(z)}{p(z \mid x)}\right].

The search is ordinary optimisation over the numbers that define qq, the variational parameters ϕ\phi (μ,σ\mu, \sigma for a Gaussian). ("Variational" comes from the calculus of variations, optimising over functions; here they are indexed by ϕ\phi, so it is gradient ascent on ϕ\phi.)

  • Why this direction (reverse KL). It is an average under qq, which we can sample; DKL(p(z|x)‖q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(z\mid x) \Vert q) would average under the posterior we cannot sample. The price: reverse KL is mode-seeking. It punishes qq for mass where the posterior has none, not for missing some of the posterior's mass, so on a two-humped posterior a Gaussian qq picks one hump. Information theory for ML, section 4 animates it (its reverse fit lands on one mode with a KL of 0.692, about ln⁡2\ln 2); model B shows the same number as an ELBO gap.
  • The KL still contains the posterior. Expand it with p(z|x)=p(x,z)/p(x)p(z \mid x) = p(x, z)/p(x) (Blei et al. eq. 11 to 12):
    DKL(q‖p(z|x))=𝔼q[log⁡q(z)]−𝔼q[log⁡p(x,z)]+log⁡p(x)D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(z\mid x)\big) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] + \log p(x)
    The unknown log⁡p(x)\log p(x) does not depend on qq, so minimising the KL over qq is maximising the computable rest with its sign flipped. That quantity is the ELBO.
Fisher's identity, EM, and the sampling road (depth)

Fisher's identity: ∇θlog⁡pθ(x)=𝔼pθ(z|x)[∇θlog⁡pθ(x,z)]\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(z \mid x)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x, z)\big] (differentiate log⁡∫pθ(x,z)dz\log\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz, then use ∇p=p∇log⁡p\nabla p = p\, \nabla \log p). The EM algorithm alternates exactly the two jobs: compute the posterior (E-step), then maximise the expected log-joint (M-step).

The full Bayesian view (keep every answer, weighted) is Probability for ML, section 9. Sampling methods that target the exact posterior instead of approximating it (MCMC: Metropolis-Hastings, HMC) are the other road; Murphy's Probabilistic Machine Learning: Advanced Topics has a part on each (Further reading). Variational inference is used for every VAE encoder, Bayesian neural networks (Bayes by Backprop), topic models (LDA's original inference), Gaussian processes with inducing points, and the training objective of diffusion models (section 6).

3. The ELBO, derived three ways, and the gap drawn

The definition (Kingma and Welling eq. 2; Blei et al. eq. 13), with log⁡p(x,z)=log⁡p(z)+log⁡p(x|z)\log p(x,z) = \log p(z) + \log p(x\mid z) computable for any zz and 𝔼z∼q\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q} estimated by drawing a few zz and averaging:

ELBO(q)=𝔼z∼q[log⁡p(x,z)−log⁡q(z)].\text{ELBO}(q) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\big[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)\big].

Three derivations, because each answers a different question: why it is a bound, how far below it sits, and how to tighten it.

Way 1: Jensen's inequality (why it is a lower bound)

  1. Multiply and divide inside the integral by q(z)q(z) (allowed wherever q(z)>0q(z) > 0):
    log⁡p(x)=log⁡∫q(z)p(x,z)q(z)dz=log⁡𝔼q[p(x,z)q(z)]\log p(x) = \log \int q(z) \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\, dz = \log \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\right]
    .
  2. Jensen's inequality: for a concave function such as log⁡\log, log⁡𝔼[Y]≥𝔼[log⁡Y]\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log Y] (a chord of a concave curve lies below the curve). With Y=p(x,z)/q(z)Y = p(x,z)/q(z): log⁡p(x)≥𝔼q[log⁡p(x,z)−log⁡q(z)]=ELBO(q)\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)\big] = \text{ELBO}(q).

A bound for every qq, however bad. Equality needs p(x,z)/q(z)p(x,z)/q(z) constant in zz, which happens only when q(z)∝p(x,z)q(z) \propto p(x, z), that is qq equals the posterior.

Way 2: the KL decomposition (how far below)

Write p(x,z)=p(z|x)p(x)p(x, z) = p(z\mid x)\, p(x) inside the definition; log⁡p(x)\log p(x) is a constant under 𝔼q\mathbb{E}_q, and what remains is a KL (Kingma and Welling eq. 1; Blei et al. eq. 14):

log⁡p(x)=ELBO(q)+DKL(q(z)‖p(z|x))⏟the gap.\log p(x) = \text{ELBO}(q) + \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z \mid x)\big)}_{\text{the gap}}.

The evidence is the bound plus the gap, and the gap is exactly the reverse KL of section 2: every nat the ELBO gains is a nat the KL loses. When the model's θ\theta also moves, raising the ELBO either raises log⁡pθ(x)\log p_\theta(x) (a better model) or shrinks the gap (a better qq); the ELBO alone cannot tell you which.

Way 3: importance sampling (a family of tighter bounds)

Way 1's first line says p(x)=𝔼q[w]p(x) = \mathbb{E}_q[w] with the importance weight w=p(x,z)/q(z)w = p(x, z)/q(z): draw zz from qq, reweight by how much more likely the model finds it than qq does (importance sampling, unbiased for p(x)p(x)). Average KK weights (still unbiased, variance falling like 1/K1/K), take the log and apply Jensen again:

ℒK=𝔼z1,…,zK∼q[log⁡1K∑k=1Kp(x,zk)q(zk)]≤log⁡p(x),\mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}_{z_1, \dots, z_K \sim q}\left[\log \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{p(x, z_k)}{q(z_k)}\right] \le \log p(x),

The form a VAE implements

Split log⁡p(x,z)=log⁡p(x|z)+log⁡p(z)\log p(x,z) = \log p(x \mid z) + \log p(z) and group the prior with qq (Kingma and Welling eq. 3):

ELBO=𝔼q[log⁡p(x|z)]⏟reconstruction−DKL(q(z)‖p(z))⏟stay near the prior.\text{ELBO} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p(x\mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{stay near the prior}} .

For model A's qq: −2.7258−0.4431=−3.1689-2.7258 - 0.4431 = -3.1689, the same number. Engineers usually minimise the negative ELBO and call it the loss; image papers report it in bits per dimension (divide by Dln⁡2D \ln 2, DD the number of pixels).

4. Choosing the family: mean field, amortised, or richer

Every choice of family trades how close qq can get to the posterior against how cheap it is to fit. Three choices matter.

Mean field, fitted by coordinate ascent

The mean-field family assumes qq factorises over groups of latents, q(z)=∏jqj(zj)q(z) = \prod_j q_j(z_j) (Blei et al. eq. 15). It cannot represent correlations between groups, but each factor's best update is computable: holding the others fixed (Blei et al. eq. 18; Bishop 2006, eq. 10.9),

qj∗(zj)∝exp⁡(𝔼−j[log⁡p(zj,z−j,x)]),q_j^{*}(z_j) \propto \exp\!\Big(\mathbb{E}_{-j}\big[\log p(z_j, z_{-j}, x)\big]\Big),

with 𝔼−j\mathbb{E}_{-j} averaging over every factor except qjq_j. Why: with the others fixed, the ELBO as a function of qjq_j is −DKL(qj‖p~j)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_j \,\Vert\, \tilde p_j\big) plus a constant, p~j∝exp⁡(𝔼−j[log⁡p])\tilde p_j \propto \exp(\mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p]) (Blei et al. eq. 19), smallest (zero) when they match. Cycling through the factors is coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI); each update can only raise the ELBO, so it converges, to a local optimum.

The example is Bishop's (Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning, section 10.1.3): data x1,…,xN∼𝒩(μ,τ−1)x_1, \dots, x_N \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^{-1}) (τ\tau is the precision, one over the variance), priors μ|τ∼𝒩(μ0,(λ0τ)−1)\mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, (\lambda_0 \tau)^{-1}) and τ∼Gamma(a0,b0)\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a_0, b_0). The exact posterior is a known Normal-Gamma, so we can see exactly what q(μ,τ)=q(μ)q(τ)q(\mu, \tau) = q(\mu)\, q(\tau) loses.

Amortised: one network outputs qq for every input

Classical VI optimises separate variational parameters for each data point. A VAE trains one network qϕ(z|x)q_\phi(z \mid x) for all of them: amortised inference (the cost of inference is paid once, in training, and spread over every future query). It is fast and works on new data, but the network may not output the best qq for each input. Cremer, Li and Duvenaud 2018 split the gap accordingly, with q∗q^{*} the best member of the family for that one input:

log⁡p(x)−ℒ[qϕ(z|x)]⏟total gap=log⁡p(x)−ℒ[q∗]⏟approximation gap+ℒ[q∗]−ℒ[qϕ(z|x)]⏟amortisation gap.\underbrace{\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]}_{\text{total gap}} = \underbrace{\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}[q^{*}]}_{\text{approximation gap}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}[q^{*}] - \mathcal{L}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]}_{\text{amortisation gap}} .

Cremer et al. found the same pattern on larger models: the amortisation gap often dominates; fixes include bigger encoders, iterative refinement of the encoder's output at test time, and semi-amortised VI. Diffusion models avoid the question: their "encoder" is fixed noise, not learned (section 6).

5. Gradients through samples: score function against reparameterisation

6. Where the ELBO reappears: VAEs and diffusion

The VAE (Kingma and Welling 2013; Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra 2014) is sections 3 to 5 with networks: an encoder outputs a diagonal Gaussian qϕ(z|x)q_\phi(z\mid x) (amortised, section 4), a decoder outputs pθ(x|z)p_\theta(x\mid z), the prior is 𝒩(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0, I), and the loss is minus the "reconstruction minus KL" ELBO of section 3 (Kingma and Welling eq. 3 and 7) with one reparameterised sample per image (section 5; their section 2.3). The KL has a closed form, 12∑j(μj2+σj2−1−log⁡σj2)\frac12 \sum_{j} (\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2) (their Appendix B, derived in section 7; 1.750 nats for μ=(1,−0.5)\mu = (1, -0.5), σ=(0.5,2)\sigma = (0.5, 2)). Two consequences are pure ELBO maths: only β=1\beta = 1 on the KL is a bound on log⁡p(x)\log p(x) (a β\beta-VAE trains a rate-distortion objective, Alemi et al. 2018), and the KL is minimised, to 0, by q(z|x)=p(z)q(z\mid x) = p(z), so a decoder that reconstructs well without zz gives nothing a reason to move away: posterior collapse. The model itself (architectures, the units of the loss, the blur, collapse and its fixes, VQ-VAE, the latent scaling factor of latent diffusion) is taught on Autoencoders and VAEs; the MNIST VAE trained for this page, its training curves, latent map and a β\beta sweep, are measured there (sections 3 and 5, closed blocks).

D1. The Gaussian KL in closed form (Kingma and Welling, Appendix B), and the loss in code (derivation)

One dimension first: q=𝒩(μ,σ2)q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2), p=𝒩(0,1)p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1).

  1. Definition: DKL(q‖p)=𝔼q[log⁡q(z)−log⁡p(z)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z) - \log p(z)].
  2. Write both log-densities: log⁡q(z)=−12log⁡(2πσ2)−(z−μ)22σ2\log q(z) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} and log⁡p(z)=−12log⁡2π−z22\log p(z) = -\tfrac12 \log 2\pi - \frac{z^2}{2}.
  3. Subtract; the log⁡2π\log 2\pi cancels: log⁡q−log⁡p=−12log⁡σ2−(z−μ)22σ2+z22\log q - \log p = -\tfrac12 \log \sigma^2 - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{z^2}{2}.
  4. Average under qq, using 𝔼q[(z−μ)2]=σ2\mathbb{E}_q[(z - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2 (definition of variance) and 𝔼q[z2]=μ2+σ2\mathbb{E}_q[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2 (variance plus squared mean): −12log⁡σ2−12+12(μ2+σ2)-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2).
  5. Collect, and sum over the JJ independent dimensions of a diagonal Gaussian (the KL of independent factors adds):
DKL(𝒩(μ,diag⁡σ2)‖𝒩(0,I))=12∑j=1J(μj2+σj2−1−log⁡σj2).D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2) \,\Vert\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big) = \frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2\big).

Each dimension pays for its mean being away from 0 (μ2\mu^2) and for its spread being anything but 1 (σ2−1−log⁡σ2\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2, zero at σ=1\sigma = 1, growing as σ\sigma shrinks or grows). The whole loss, in code

This is the loss the page's VAE was trained with (from src/data/vae.py, PyTorch 2; log⁡σ2\log\sigma^2 is predicted rather than σ\sigma so it can take any real value):

mu, logvar = encoder(x)                      # [batch, J] each
z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)   # reparameterised sample
logits = decoder(z)                          # [batch, 784]
rec = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x, reduction='none').sum(-1)  # -log p(x|z)
kl = 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(-1)                          # closed form
loss = (rec + beta * kl).mean()              # beta = 1: minus the ELBO
Two common bugs in this snippet's neighbours
Averaging the reconstruction over pixels (reduction='mean') while summing the KL over dimensions silently multiplies the KL weight by 784: the model then behaves like a heavily over-weighted beta-VAE (see Autoencoders and VAEs) and its samples blur. And the BCE loss on grey-level pixels is not the likelihood of any normalised distribution over grey levels (Loaiza-Ganem and Cunningham 2019, the continuous Bernoulli), so a "log-likelihood" computed that way is not comparable across papers; binarise the data, as here, or use a proper continuous likelihood.
D2. Normalising flows: the change-of-variables formula (derivation)

Idea: start from a simple random variable zz and push it through an invertible function x=f(z)x = f(z). Where ff stretches space, probability thins out; where it squeezes, it piles up. The change-of-variables formula keeps the books.

  1. One dimension, ff increasing: the probability in a small interval is preserved, px(x)|dx|=pz(z)|dz|p_x(x)\,|dx| = p_z(z)\,|dz|.
  2. So px(x)=pz(z)|dzdx|=pz(f−1(x))|dfdz|−1p_x(x) = p_z(z) \left|\frac{dz}{dx}\right| = p_z\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)\, \left|\frac{df}{dz}\right|^{-1}.
  3. In DD dimensions the stretch factor of a small volume is the absolute determinant of the Jacobian matrix ∂f/∂z\partial f/\partial z (Matrix calculus and backprop, section 1; the determinant is the volume scaling of a linear map, Linear algebra for ML). In logs:
log⁡px(x)=log⁡pz(z)−log⁡|det⁡∂f∂z|,z=f−1(x).\log p_x(x) = \log p_z(z) - \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|, \qquad z = f^{-1}(x).

  • Coupling layers (RealNVP, Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein and Bengio 2016): split zz into halves (a,b)(a, b); keep aa; set b′=b⊙es(a)+t(a)b' = b \odot e^{s(a)} + t(a) with any networks s,ts, t.   • Autoregressive flows (IAF, Kingma et al. 2016; MAF): each coordinate depends only on earlier ones, again a triangular Jacobian. IAF used as the VAE's qq is what fixes model B's problem at scale: the approximate posterior can bend and split.
  • Continuous flows: let K→∞K \to \infty with tiny steps, dz/dt=v(z,t)dz/dt = v(z, t). The log-density then changes at the rate −tr⁡(∂v/∂z)-\operatorname{tr}(\partial v/\partial z) (the instantaneous change of variables, Chen et al. 2018, Neural ODEs). D3. Diffusion: the forward process, its closed form, and two schedules (derivation)

D5. Score matching: predicting the noise is estimating a gradient (Tweedie, the SDE and the probability flow ODE) (derivation)

The score of a distribution is the gradient of its log-density with respect to the data, s(x)=∇xlog⁡p(x)s(x) = \nabla_x \log p(x): an arrow at every point, pointing towards higher density. (Not the score of section 5, which was a gradient with respect to parameters; statistics uses the word for both.) Two facts make it useful:

  • It ignores normalisation. If p=p~/Zp = \tilde p/Z, then ∇xlog⁡p=∇xlog⁡p~\nabla_x \log p = \nabla_x \log \tilde p: the intractable constant disappears.
  • It is enough to sample. Langevin dynamics repeats x←x+η2s(x)+ηzx \leftarrow x + \tfrac{\eta}{2}\, s(x) + \sqrt{\eta}\, z with z∼𝒩(0,I)z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I); for small steps and long runs its samples follow pp (Song and Ermon 2019, eq. 4).

Learning a score without knowing it. Minimising 𝔼‖sθ(x)−∇log⁡p(x)‖2\mathbb{E}\|s_\theta(x) - \nabla\log p(x)\|^2 needs the unknown true score. Two tricks remove it. Hyvärinen 2005 integrated by parts to get an objective with only sθs_\theta and its divergence. Vincent 2011 showed that for noised data the target can be the score of the noising kernel, which is known: denoising score matching. For our kernel q(xt|x0)=𝒩(α‾tx0,(1−α‾t)I)q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I):

∇xtlog⁡q(xt|x0)=−xt−α‾tx01−α‾t=−ε1−α‾t.\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} .

So regressing onto ε\varepsilon is regressing onto the kernel's score times −1−α‾t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}, and the minimiser of a squared loss is a conditional mean: εθ∗(xt)=𝔼[ε|xt]\varepsilon_\theta^*(x_t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t], which Vincent's identity turns into the score of the noised marginal:

sθ(xt,t)=−εθ(xt,t)1−α‾t≈∇xtlog⁡qt(xt).s_\theta(x_t, t) = -\frac{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} \approx \nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t).
A case you can check by hand (Tweedie's formula)

Let the data be x0∼𝒩(0,1)x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). Then every xt∼𝒩(0,1)x_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) (variance preserving, D3), with score −xt-x_t. The formula says the ideal noise prediction is ε∗=−1−α‾t⋅(−xt)=1−α‾txt\varepsilon^* = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\cdot(-x_t) = \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t. At t=200t = 200: slope 0.5839. A million simulated (x0,ε)(x_0, \varepsilon) pairs, regressing ε\varepsilon on xtx_t by least squares: slope 0.5843.

That is why Ho et al. say their sampler "resembles Langevin dynamics with εθ\varepsilon_\theta as a learned gradient of the data density" (section 3.2): DDPM's reverse step is a scaled score step plus noise. Song et al. 2021 unified the two families in continuous time: the forward process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) dx=f(x,t)dt+g(t)dwdx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw; its exact time reversal is another SDE that needs only the score, dx=[f−g2∇xlog⁡qt(x)]dt+gdw‾dx = [f - g^2 \nabla_x \log q_t(x)]\,dt + g\, d\bar w (their eq. 6, from Anderson 1982); and a deterministic probability flow ODE, dx=[f−12g2∇xlog⁡qt(x)]dtdx = [f - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla_x \log q_t(x)]\,dt (their eq. 13), has the same marginals at every tt. DDPM discretises the reverse SDE; DDIM, next, is a discretisation of the ODE.

D6. DDIM: the same network, a deterministic sampler that skips steps (derivation)

Song, Meng and Ermon 2020 noticed that LsimpleL_{\text{simple}} only ever uses the marginals q(xt|x0)q(x_t\mid x_0), never the step-to-step chain. Many chains share those marginals, including non-Markovian ones, so a network trained once can be sampled with any of them. Their update (eq. 12), in DDPM's notation:

xt−1=α‾t−1xt−1−α‾tεθ(xt)α‾t⏟predicted x0+1−α‾t−1−σt2εθ(xt)⏟direction pointing to xt+σtεt.x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\underbrace{\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}}_{\text{predicted } x_0} + \underbrace{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}_{\text{direction pointing to } x_t} + \sigma_t\,\varepsilon_t .

Guess the clean point from the current one; re-noise that guess to the next, lower noise level, reusing the predicted noise as the direction; optionally add fresh noise. With σt\sigma_t equal to DDPM's posterior spread it is DDPM; with σt=0\sigma_t = 0 it is deterministic, the DDIM sampler, and its steps can skip: use only a subsequence of noise levels (α‾\bar\alpha taken at, say, 20 evenly spaced tt).

D7. Classifier-free guidance, as a formula (derivation)

  2. Turn up the classifier. That is classifier guidance (Dhariwal and Nichol 2021), which needs a separate classifier trained on noisy images.
  3. Remove the classifier. Bayes again: ∇log⁡p(c|x)=∇log⁡p(x|c)−∇log⁡p(x)\nabla\log p(c\mid x) = \nabla\log p(x\mid c) - \nabla\log p(x). Substitute:
    ∇log⁡p(x)+(1+w)(∇log⁡p(x|c)−∇log⁡p(x))=(1+w)∇log⁡p(x|c)−w∇log⁡p(x)\nabla\log p(x) + (1+w)\big(\nabla\log p(x\mid c) - \nabla\log p(x)\big) = (1+w)\nabla\log p(x\mid c) - w\,\nabla\log p(x)
    .
  4. Write it with noise predictions (s=−ε/1−α‾s = -\varepsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}, D5) and you have Ho and Salimans 2022, eq. 6:
ε~θ(xt,c)=(1+w)εθ(xt,c)−wεθ(xt,⌀).\tilde\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) = (1+w)\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) - w\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing).

Run the network twice, with the condition and with an empty condition ⌀\varnothing, and extrapolate past the conditional prediction, away from the unconditional one. One network does both: during training the condition is replaced by ⌀\varnothing with some probability (15% for the two-moons toy on Diffusion from zero). w=0w = 0 is plain conditional sampling.

Two conventions for the same knob
Libraries usually write ε⌀+s(εc−ε⌀)\varepsilon_\varnothing + s\,(\varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_\varnothing), so s=1+ws = 1 + w. Hugging Face diffusers' Stable Diffusion pipeline defaults to guidance_scale = 7.5, which is w=6.5w = 6.5 in the paper's notation (pipeline_stable_diffusion.py, v0.35.1).
D8. Flow matching: the conditional loss and why it learns the marginal velocity (Lipman et al., Theorem 2) (derivation)

The path. Pair a data point x0x_0 with a noise sample ε\varepsilon and join them by a straight line: xt=(1−t)x0+tεx_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\varepsilon, t∈[0,1]t \in [0, 1] (Esser et al. 2024 (Stable Diffusion 3), eq. 13; Liu, Gong and Liu 2022, rectified flow). Along that line the velocity is constant, dxtdt=ε−x0\frac{dx_t}{dt} = \varepsilon - x_0.

The loss (conditional flow matching, Lipman et al. 2023):

ℒCFM(θ)=𝔼t∼U[0,1], x0, ε‖vθ(xt,t)−(ε−x0)‖2.\mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim U[0,1],\ x_0,\ \varepsilon}\,\big\|v_\theta(x_t, t) - (\varepsilon - x_0)\big\|^2 .
  1. Many lines pass through the same point xx at time tt. The best single answer for a squared loss is their average direction, the marginal velocity v∗(x,t)=𝔼[ε−x0|xt=x]v^*(x,t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon - x_0 \mid x_t = x].
  2. Lipman et al.'s Theorem 2: the conditional loss above and the (intractable) loss against v∗v^* differ by a constant, so they have the same gradients. We regress on single lines and learn the average.
  3. The ODE dx/dt=v∗(x,t)dx/dt = v^*(x, t) moves the whole noise distribution at t=1t = 1 onto the data distribution at t=0t = 0 (the marginals of the straight-line paths are preserved). Generate by integrating backwards from t=1t = 1: Euler steps x←x−1Svθ(x,t)x \leftarrow x - \tfrac1S v_\theta(x, t).

It is the same family as diffusion: xtx_t is a scaled data point plus scaled Gaussian noise, so a velocity prediction converts to a noise prediction (εˆ=xt+(1−t)v\hat\varepsilon = x_t + (1-t)\,v) or a clean-data prediction (xˆ0=xt−tv\hat x_0 = x_t - t\,v). What changes is the path (straight in tt rather than variance-preserving) and the target (velocity rather than noise), and with them which errors are cheap.

Conventions differ: Lipman et al. put noise at t=0t = 0 and data at t=1t = 1 (their eq. 22 to 23, with a small σmin⁡\sigma_{\min}); Stable Diffusion 3 and this page put data at t=0t = 0. Couplings, reflow and solvers are on Flow matching and rectified flow.

9. Common mistakes

Each one opens; the first five are about the ELBO itself, the rest about the diffusion and flow derivations of section 7.

Using the wrong direction of KL for variational inference
VI minimises DKL(q‖p(z|x))D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p(z\mid x)), the average under qq. The other direction would need samples from the posterior. The direction is why VI underestimates spread (section 4) and picks one mode (section 3, model B).
Reading the ELBO as the log-likelihood
It is a lower bound with an unknown gap. On the MNIST VAE the encoder's ELBO was 8.2 nats below the true log⁡p(x)\log p(x) (section 4). Compare models with an importance-weighted estimate at stated KK (or exact quadrature when the latent is tiny), on the same data and binarisation.
Mixing sum and mean between the two loss terms
Mean over pixels for reconstruction and sum over latent dimensions for the KL is a hidden beta of 784 on MNIST. Sum both over dimensions, then average over the batch (D1).
The KL formula with log⁡σ\log\sigma where it wants log⁡σ2\log\sigma^2
12(μ2+σ2−1−log⁡σ2)\tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2): if the network outputs log⁡σ\log\sigma, the last term is 2log⁡σ2\log\sigma and σ2=e2log⁡σ\sigma^2 = e^{2\log\sigma}. Getting it wrong still trains, to the wrong objective. Check against a Monte Carlo estimate, as D1 does.
Diagnosing posterior collapse from the total loss
Look at the KL per latent dimension: a dimension with KL near 0 for every input is unused (the MNIST β\beta sweep on Autoencoders and VAEs, section 5: 8 of 16 at β=8\beta = 8). The total loss can look fine.
α\alpha means α‾\bar\alpha in some papers
DDPM: αt=1−βt\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t, α‾t\bar\alpha_t the product. DDIM: αt\alpha_t is the product. VDM and others use αt,σt\alpha_t, \sigma_t for the signal and noise scales. Read the definitions first.
Time running the other way
Diffusion and Stable Diffusion 3 put data at t=0t = 0; Lipman et al.'s flow matching puts noise at t=0t = 0. The velocity's sign flips with it.
Two guidance scales with the same name
The paper's ww and the libraries' s=1+ws = 1 + w (D7). "Guidance 7.5" is w=6.5w = 6.5.
Dividing by α‾t\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} near the end of the cosine schedule
With βt\beta_t clipped at 0.999 the cosine schedule ends at α‾1000=2.4×10−9\bar\alpha_{1000} = 2.4\times10^{-9}, and the predicted x0=(xt−1−α‾εˆ)/α‾x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\hat\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha} multiplies the network's error by about 20,000. On the two-moons toy of Diffusion from zero (section 5), 20-step DDIM on that schedule put 1.2% of 600 samples on the data until the predicted x0x_0 was clipped to the data range ([−3,3][-3, 3]) and the noise re-derived from the clipped value; with that fix, 95.3%. Image samplers clip to [−1,1][-1, 1] for the same reason.
Treating LsimpleL_{\text{simple}} as the ELBO
It drops the per-step weights (D4): the true bound weighs t=1t = 1 about 80 times more than t=200t = 200. Likelihood numbers need the weighted bound.
Mixing up the two "scores"
Section 5's score is ∇ϕlog⁡qϕ(z)\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z), a gradient with respect to parameters, averaging to zero. D5's score is ∇xlog⁡p(x)\nabla_x \log p(x), a gradient with respect to the data. The link to noise prediction is s=−ε/1−α‾ts = -\varepsilon/\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}, with that minus sign.

1. Latent-variable models, and the integral you cannot do

The question. A model that generates images has to decide, somewhere, "this will be a 7, slanted, thick-stroked" before it decides any pixel. How do we write that down as probability, and why does it make training hard?

The story a latent-variable model tells. First draw a hidden value \(z\) (the latent variable, Latin latere, to lie hidden) from a simple distribution, the prior \(p(z)\). Then draw the data \(x\) from a distribution that depends on \(z\), the likelihood or decoder \(p_\theta(x \mid z)\), where \(\theta\) are the parameters we train (in a VAE, a neural network's weights). Together they define a joint distribution:

\[ p_\theta(x, z) = p(z)\, p_\theta(x \mid z). \]

We only ever see \(x\). The probability the model gives to an observed \(x\) is therefore the marginal likelihood, also called the evidence: add up the joint over every value \(z\) could have taken.

\[ p_\theta(x) = \int p(z)\, p_\theta(x \mid z)\, dz . \]

In plain words: the chance of seeing this image is the average, over all possible hidden causes drawn from the prior, of how likely each cause makes the image.

Training by maximum likelihood ({{Probability for ML, section 10|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}) means maximising \(\sum_i \log p_\theta(x_i)\) over the training set. So we need this integral, and its gradient with respect to \(\theta\), for every example.

The running example: a model you can do by hand

Model A (used in sections 1 to 6 and in the ELBO lab)

Prior \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). Decoder \(x \mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(z, s^2)\) with \(s = 0.5\): the data is the hidden value plus a little noise. We observe \(x = 1.5\).

Here the integral has a closed form. \(x\) is a sum of two independent Gaussians, \(z\) (variance 1) and the noise (variance \(s^2 = 0.25\)), so \(x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1.25)\) and

\[ \log p(x{=}1.5) = -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi \cdot 1.25) - \frac{1.5^2}{2 \cdot 1.25} = -1.0305 - 0.9000 = -1.9305, \]

that is \(p(x) = 0.1451\). Every number in sections 2 to 6 is measured against this \(-1.9305\) (src/recompute.py recomputes each one).

Why the integral is usually impossible. Model A is solvable only because a Gaussian pushed through a straight line stays Gaussian. Replace the mean \(z\) by a neural network's output, \(x \mid z \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\text{decoder}_\theta(z))\), and nothing cancels. Kingma and Welling name exactly this case: the integral is intractable "in cases of moderately complicated likelihood functions \(p_\theta(x \mid z)\), e.g. a neural network with a nonlinear hidden layer" ({{Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes, section 2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114}}). You could approximate it numerically, but the cost grows exponentially with the dimension of \(z\): a grid of 100 points per axis is \(10^4\) decoder calls for a 2-D latent and \(10^{32}\) for a 16-D one. Naive Monte Carlo (draw \(z\) from the prior, average \(p(x \mid z)\)) is unbiased but almost every prior sample explains a given image terribly, so the average is dominated by rare lucky draws.

Latent models you already know
  • A Gaussian mixture: \(z\) is which cluster (discrete, \(K\) values), so the "integral" is a sum of \(K\) terms and is easy. Its posterior is the cluster responsibilities of EM.
  • A VAE: \(z\) is a continuous vector (2 to a few hundred dimensions); decoder a network. Intractable.
  • A diffusion model: the latents are 1,000 progressively noisier copies of the image, \(x_1, \dots, x_T\), each as large as the image (section 12). Intractable.
  • Contrast: the next-token model of {{Topic: math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} has no latent: \(p(\text{word} \mid \text{context})\) is a softmax, one sum over the vocabulary, computed exactly. That is why language models train on plain cross-entropy and need nothing on this page.
What it is used for in ML
Any model that generates data through hidden structure: VAEs, the autoencoders inside latent diffusion (Stable Diffusion's first stage), diffusion and flow models themselves, topic models, probabilistic PCA, and Bayesian neural networks (where \(z\) is the weights).

2. The posterior, and why we approximate it

The question. Given an image, which hidden causes could have produced it? That distribution is the posterior. Why does everything hinge on it?

The posterior is the distribution of \(z\) after seeing \(x\). Bayes' rule ({{Probability for ML, section 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}) gives it:

\[ p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)}{p_\theta(x)} . \]

The numerator is easy (run the decoder, evaluate the prior). The denominator is the evidence from section 1, the intractable integral. So the posterior is intractable for exactly the same reason. Two jobs need it:

  • Inference: "what is this image's latent code?" (for representation learning, compression, editing).
  • Learning: the gradient of \(\log p_\theta(x)\) is an average under the posterior, \(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(z \mid x)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x, z)\big]\) (Fisher's identity: differentiate \(\log\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz\), then use \(\nabla p = p\, \nabla \log p\)). The EM algorithm alternates exactly these two: compute the posterior (E-step), then maximise the expected log-joint (M-step). With an intractable posterior, EM cannot run, which is the second intractability Kingma and Welling list.
Model A's posterior, exactly

Multiply prior and likelihood and complete the square in \(z\): \(-\tfrac{z^2}{2} - \tfrac{(1.5 - z)^2}{2 \cdot 0.25}\) is a quadratic with leading coefficient \(-\tfrac12(1 + 4) = -\tfrac{5}{2}\), so the posterior variance is \(1/5 = 0.2\); its peak is where the derivative \(-z + 4(1.5 - z)\) vanishes, \(z = 6/5 = 1.2\). So \(p(z \mid x{=}1.5) = \mathcal{N}(1.2,\ 0.2)\), standard deviation 0.447. In general \(\mathcal{N}\!\big(\tfrac{x}{1+s^2}, \tfrac{s^2}{1+s^2}\big)\): the observation pulls the prior's mean 0 most of the way to 1.5 and shrinks its spread five-fold.

Model B, where no Gaussian fits. Change one thing: \(x \mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(z^2, 0.25)\), the data is the square of the hidden value plus noise, and observe \(x = 2\). Now \(z \approx +1.37\) and \(z \approx -1.37\) explain it equally well: the posterior has two humps. Section 4 shows what that costs a Gaussian \(q\); the ELBO lab draws both models.

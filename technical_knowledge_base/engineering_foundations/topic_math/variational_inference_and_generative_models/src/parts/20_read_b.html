2. The posterior, and turning inference into optimisation

The posterior is the distribution of \(z\) after seeing \(x\), by Bayes' rule ({{Probability for ML, section 5|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}):

\[ p_\theta(z \mid x) = \frac{p_\theta(x \mid z)\, p(z)}{p_\theta(x)} . \]

The numerator is easy (run the decoder, evaluate the prior); the denominator is the evidence of section 1. So the posterior is intractable for exactly the same reason, and two jobs need it: inference ("what is this image's latent code?", for representations, compression, editing) and learning, because the gradient of \(\log p_\theta(x)\) is an average under the posterior (Fisher's identity, below), which is also why EM cannot run when the posterior is intractable, the second intractability Kingma and Welling list.

Model A's posterior, exactly

Multiply prior and likelihood and complete the square in \(z\): \(-\tfrac{z^2}{2} - \tfrac{(1.5 - z)^2}{2 \cdot 0.25}\) has leading coefficient \(-\tfrac12(1 + 4) = -\tfrac{5}{2}\), so the posterior variance is \(1/5 = 0.2\); the peak is where \(-z + 4(1.5 - z)\) vanishes, \(z = 6/5 = 1.2\). So \(p(z \mid x{=}1.5) = \mathcal{N}(1.2,\ 0.2)\), standard deviation 0.447. In general \(\mathcal{N}\!\big(\tfrac{x}{1+s^2}, \tfrac{s^2}{1+s^2}\big)\): the observation pulls the prior's mean 0 most of the way to 1.5 and shrinks its spread five-fold. This is the shaded curve in the hero figure.

Model B, where no Gaussian fits. Change one thing: \(x \mid z \sim \mathcal{N}(z^2, 0.25)\), the data is the square of the hidden value plus noise, and observe \(x = 2\). Now \(z \approx +1.37\) and \(z \approx -1.37\) explain it equally well: the posterior has two humps. Section 3 measures what that costs a Gaussian \(q\).

Variational inference: pick the closest simple distribution

Choose a family \(\mathcal{Q}\) simple enough to sample and evaluate (say all Gaussians, each fixed by a mean and a variance) and take the member closest to the posterior in KL ({{Information theory for ML, section 3|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}), in this direction (equation (10) of {{Blei, Kucukelbir and McAuliffe's review|https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.00670}}):

\[ q^{*} = \arg\min_{q \in \mathcal{Q}} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z \mid x)\big), \qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\left[\log \frac{q(z)}{p(z \mid x)}\right]. \]

The search is ordinary optimisation over the numbers that define \(q\), the variational parameters \(\phi\) (\(\mu, \sigma\) for a Gaussian). ("Variational" comes from the calculus of variations, optimising over functions; here they are indexed by \(\phi\), so it is gradient ascent on \(\phi\).)

  • Why this direction (reverse KL). It is an average under \(q\), which we can sample; \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(z\mid x) \Vert q)\) would average under the posterior we cannot sample. The price: reverse KL is mode-seeking. It punishes \(q\) for mass where the posterior has none, not for missing some of the posterior's mass, so on a two-humped posterior a Gaussian \(q\) picks one hump. {{Information theory for ML, section 4|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}} animates it (its reverse fit lands on one mode with a KL of 0.692, about \(\ln 2\)); model B shows the same number as an ELBO gap.
  • The KL still contains the posterior. Expand it with \(p(z \mid x) = p(x, z)/p(x)\) (Blei et al. eq. 11 to 12): \[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(z\mid x)\big) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] + \log p(x)\] The unknown \(\log p(x)\) does not depend on \(q\), so minimising the KL over \(q\) is maximising the computable rest with its sign flipped. That quantity is the ELBO.
Fisher's identity, EM, and the sampling road (depth)

Fisher's identity: \(\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta(z \mid x)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x, z)\big]\) (differentiate \(\log\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz\), then use \(\nabla p = p\, \nabla \log p\)). The EM algorithm alternates exactly the two jobs: compute the posterior (E-step), then maximise the expected log-joint (M-step).

The full Bayesian view (keep every answer, weighted) is {{Probability for ML, section 9|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}. Sampling methods that target the exact posterior instead of approximating it (MCMC: Metropolis-Hastings, HMC) are the other road; Murphy's Probabilistic Machine Learning: Advanced Topics has a part on each (Further reading). Variational inference is used for every VAE encoder, Bayesian neural networks (Bayes by Backprop), topic models (LDA's original inference), Gaussian processes with inducing points, and the training objective of diffusion models (section 6).

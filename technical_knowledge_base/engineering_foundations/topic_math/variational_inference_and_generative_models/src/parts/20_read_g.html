7. Derivations the generative pages use

Each block derives one result that the model pages cite rather than re-derive; all are optional on a first read. The closed forms are recomputed and checked by Monte Carlo or an independent route in src/recompute.py. In order: the Gaussian KL of every VAE loss; normalising flows (the richer family of section 4); the diffusion forward process; the diffusion ELBO down to "predict the noise"; scores and the SDE view; DDIM; classifier-free guidance; flow matching.

D1. The Gaussian KL in closed form (Kingma and Welling, Appendix B), and the loss in code (derivation)

One dimension first: \(q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), \(p = \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\).

  1. Definition: \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z) - \log p(z)]\).
  2. Write both log-densities: \(\log q(z) = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\) and \(\log p(z) = -\tfrac12 \log 2\pi - \frac{z^2}{2}\).
  3. Subtract; the \(\log 2\pi\) cancels: \(\log q - \log p = -\tfrac12 \log \sigma^2 - \frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{z^2}{2}\).
  4. Average under \(q\), using \(\mathbb{E}_q[(z - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2\) (definition of variance) and \(\mathbb{E}_q[z^2] = \mu^2 + \sigma^2\) (variance plus squared mean): \(-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2 - \tfrac12 + \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2)\).
  5. Collect, and sum over the \(J\) independent dimensions of a diagonal Gaussian (the KL of independent factors adds):
\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}\sigma^2) \,\Vert\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big) = \frac12 \sum_{j=1}^{J} \big(\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2\big). \]

Each dimension pays for its mean being away from 0 (\(\mu^2\)) and for its spread being anything but 1 (\(\sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2\), zero at \(\sigma = 1\), growing as \(\sigma\) shrinks or grows). Kingma and Welling print it as \(\frac12\sum_j(1 + \log\sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2)\), the negative KL, because they maximise.

Worked and checked

\(\mu = (1, -0.5)\), \(\sigma = (0.5, 2)\): dimension 1 gives \(\tfrac12(1 + 0.25 - 1 - \log 0.25) = 0.818\) (the same number as the {{Information theory page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}'s example), dimension 2 gives \(\tfrac12(0.25 + 4 - 1 - \log 4) = 0.932\); total 1.750 nats. A two-million-sample Monte Carlo average of \(\log q - \log p\) gives 1.749 (standard error 0.002).

The whole loss, in code

This is the loss the page's VAE was trained with (from src/data/vae.py, PyTorch 2; \(\log\sigma^2\) is predicted rather than \(\sigma\) so it can take any real value):

mu, logvar = encoder(x)                      # [batch, J] each
z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)   # reparameterised sample
logits = decoder(z)                          # [batch, 784]
rec = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, x, reduction='none').sum(-1)  # -log p(x|z)
kl = 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(-1)                          # closed form
loss = (rec + beta * kl).mean()              # beta = 1: minus the ELBO
Two common bugs in this snippet's neighbours
Averaging the reconstruction over pixels (reduction='mean') while summing the KL over dimensions silently multiplies the KL weight by 784: the model then behaves like a heavily over-weighted beta-VAE (see Autoencoders and VAEs) and its samples blur. And the BCE loss on grey-level pixels is not the likelihood of any normalised distribution over grey levels ({{Loaiza-Ganem and Cunningham 2019, the continuous Bernoulli|https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.06845}}), so a "log-likelihood" computed that way is not comparable across papers; binarise the data, as here, or use a proper continuous likelihood.
D2. Normalising flows: the change-of-variables formula (derivation)

Idea: start from a simple random variable \(z\) and push it through an invertible function \(x = f(z)\). Where \(f\) stretches space, probability thins out; where it squeezes, it piles up. The change-of-variables formula keeps the books.

  1. One dimension, \(f\) increasing: the probability in a small interval is preserved, \(p_x(x)\,|dx| = p_z(z)\,|dz|\).
  2. So \(p_x(x) = p_z(z) \left|\frac{dz}{dx}\right| = p_z\big(f^{-1}(x)\big)\, \left|\frac{df}{dz}\right|^{-1}\).
  3. In \(D\) dimensions the stretch factor of a small volume is the absolute determinant of the Jacobian matrix \(\partial f/\partial z\) ({{Matrix calculus and backprop, section 1|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}}; the determinant is the volume scaling of a linear map, {{Linear algebra for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d89c26e1dab2fcb701}}). In logs:
\[ \log p_x(x) = \log p_z(z) - \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|, \qquad z = f^{-1}(x). \]
Two checks

\(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), \(x = 2z + 1\): at \(x = 3\), \(z = 1\), \(df/dz = 2\), so \(p_x(3) = \mathcal{N}(1; 0, 1)/2 = 0.2420/2 = 0.121\); the log-determinant is \(\log 2 = 0.693\). \(x = e^{z}\) (the log-normal): at \(x = 2\), \(z = \ln 2\), \(df/dz = e^z = 2\), so \(p_x(2) = \mathcal{N}(0.693)/2 = 0.1569\); counting four million samples falling in \([1.99, 2.01]\) gives 0.158.

A normalising flow composes \(K\) such maps, \(z_K = f_K \circ \dots \circ f_1(z_0)\), and the log-densities add up (Rezende and Mohamed {{"Variational Inference with Normalizing Flows", eq. 6 to 7|https://arxiv.org/abs/1505.05770}}): \(\log q_K(z_K) = \log q_0(z_0) - \sum_{k=1}^K \log\left|\det \frac{\partial f_k}{\partial z_{k-1}}\right|\). The whole design problem is making that determinant cheap: a general \(D \times D\) determinant costs \(O(D^3)\).

  • Coupling layers (RealNVP, {{Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein and Bengio 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1605.08803}}): split \(z\) into halves \((a, b)\); keep \(a\); set \(b' = b \odot e^{s(a)} + t(a)\) with any networks \(s, t\). The Jacobian is triangular, so its determinant is the product of the diagonal, \(\exp(\sum s(a))\): one sum. Inverting needs no inverse of \(s\) or \(t\).
  • Autoregressive flows (IAF, {{Kingma et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04934}}; MAF): each coordinate depends only on earlier ones, again a triangular Jacobian. IAF used as the VAE's \(q\) is what fixes model B's problem at scale: the approximate posterior can bend and split.
  • Continuous flows: let \(K \to \infty\) with tiny steps, \(dz/dt = v(z, t)\). The log-density then changes at the rate \(-\operatorname{tr}(\partial v/\partial z)\) (the instantaneous change of variables, {{Chen et al. 2018, Neural ODEs|https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07366}}). Flow matching (D8) trains exactly such a \(v\), without ever computing the trace during training.
D3. Diffusion: the forward process, its closed form, and two schedules (derivation)

Take a data point \(x_0\) (an image; here a 2-D point). The forward process adds a little Gaussian noise \(T\) times (Ho et al. 2020 eq. 2; {{Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585}} introduced the idea):

\[ q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big), \qquad t = 1, \dots, T. \]
\(\beta_t\)
the noise variance added at step \(t\), from a fixed schedule; Ho et al. use \(T = 1000\) and \(\beta_t\) rising linearly from \(10^{-4}\) to 0.02.
\(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\)
a slight shrink of the previous value, so the total variance stays bounded instead of growing.
\(\alpha_t,\ \bar\alpha_t\)
shorthand: \(\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t\), and \(\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s\), the signal fraction left after \(t\) steps (the bar is a product, not an average).

Read it as "\(x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_t\)" with fresh noise \(\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) each step: in the VAE language of section 6, this is a reparameterised encoder with no trainable parameters.

The closed form, derived by induction (Ho et al. eq. 4)

  1. Claim: \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \bar\varepsilon\) for a standard Gaussian \(\bar\varepsilon\). True at \(t = 1\), since \(\bar\alpha_1 = \alpha_1\).
  2. Assume it for \(t - 1\) and substitute: \[x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, \bar\varepsilon'\big) + \sqrt{1 - \alpha_t}\, \varepsilon_t\].
  3. The signal part is \(\sqrt{\alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1}}\, x_0 = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0\).
  4. The two noise parts are independent zero-mean Gaussians; independent variances add: \(\alpha_t(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1}) + (1 - \alpha_t) = 1 - \alpha_t \bar\alpha_{t-1} = 1 - \bar\alpha_t\). A Gaussian with that variance is \(\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\bar\varepsilon\). Done:
\[ q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0,\ (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\big). \]

Any noise level in one jump: training never runs the chain, it draws \(t\) and noise and computes \(x_t\) in one line. If the data has unit variance, so does every \(x_t\) (\(\bar\alpha_t + 1 - \bar\alpha_t = 1\)): the "variance-preserving" process.

Worked, and checked by running the chain

The root's example ({{Topic: math, Notation decoder|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}}, "Diffusion training loss"): \(x_0 = (0.5, -0.2)\), \(\varepsilon = (1, -0.5)\), \(t = 200\) on the linear schedule. \(\bar\alpha_{200} = \prod_{s \le 200}(1 - \beta_s) = 0.6590\), so \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha} = 0.8118\), \(\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha} = 0.5839\), and \(x_{200} = 0.8118\,x_0 + 0.5839\,\varepsilon = (0.9898, -0.4543)\).

Monte Carlo: 200,000 copies of \(x_0\) pushed through all 200 single steps, fresh noise each step. Mean \((0.4049, -0.1627)\) against \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,x_0 = (0.4059, -0.1624)\) (standard error 0.0013); variance 0.342 against \(1 - \bar\alpha = 0.341\). After all 1,000 steps: variance 1.001 and 1.007 against \(1 - 4.0 \times 10^{-5}\); the data is gone.

Two schedules. Left: \(\bar\alpha_t\), the signal fraction left, for the linear schedule (Ho et al.) and the cosine schedule (Nichol and Dhariwal 2021, eq. 17: \(\bar\alpha_t = f(t)/f(0)\), \(f(t) = \cos^2\!\big(\frac{t/T + 0.008}{1.008}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\big)\), \(\beta_t\) clipped at 0.999). Right: 400 two-moons points at the chosen step, \(x_t\) drawn from the closed form.

Why a second schedule. On the linear schedule \(\bar\alpha_{500} = 0.079\): by the halfway point only 8% of the signal variance is left, and "the latents in the last quarter of the linear schedule are almost purely noise" ({{Nichol and Dhariwal 2021, section 3.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09672}}), so those steps teach little. The cosine schedule keeps \(\bar\alpha_{500} = 0.494\) and drops roughly linearly in the middle. A useful single number per step is the signal-to-noise ratio \(\text{SNR}(t) = \bar\alpha_t/(1-\bar\alpha_t)\), usually as its log \(\lambda_t\): linear runs from \(\lambda = 9.2\) to \(-10.1\), cosine from 10.1 to \(-12.9\) at \(t = 999\) and \(-19.8\) at \(t = 1000\), where the 0.999 clip leaves \(\bar\alpha = 2.4\times 10^{-9}\). {{Kingma et al. 2021 (Variational Diffusion Models)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00630}} show that in continuous time the ELBO depends on the schedule only through its two end values of SNR; schedules change which noise levels training spends its effort on.

D4. The reverse process and the diffusion ELBO, down to "predict the noise" (eight steps) (derivation)

The model. Generation runs the chain backwards: start from pure noise \(p(x_T) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and apply learned Gaussian steps (Ho et al. eq. 1)

\[ p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_{t-1};\ \mu_\theta(x_t, t),\ \sigma_t^2 I\big), \]

with \(\sigma_t^2\) fixed (Ho et al. use \(\beta_t\) or \(\tilde\beta_t\), below). This is a latent-variable model with latents \(z = x_{1:T}\), and the forward process of D3 is its \(q(z \mid x_0)\). Reverse steps can be Gaussian because, for small \(\beta_t\), the true reverse of a small Gaussian step is itself close to Gaussian (Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015, citing Feller).

  1. The ELBO, as in section 3 with \(z = x_{1:T}\) (Ho et al. eq. 3): \[-\log p_\theta(x_0) \le \mathbb{E}_q\Big[-\log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\Big] = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[-\log p(x_T) - \sum_{t \ge 1} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})}\Big] =: L\]. Both chains factorise step by step, so the log of the ratio is a sum.
  2. Turn each forward step around with Bayes' rule. The forward chain is Markov, so \(q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0)\), and Bayes' rule gives \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}, x_0) = \frac{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\, q(x_t \mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}\). Use it for every \(t \ge 2\).
  3. Telescope. The extra factors \(\frac{q(x_t\mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}\) for \(t = 2..T\) multiply to \(\frac{q(x_T\mid x_0)}{q(x_1\mid x_0)}\): every middle term cancels.
  4. Regroup (Ho et al. eq. 5, derived in their Appendix A):
\[ L = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\Vert\, p(x_T)\big)}_{L_T} + \sum_{t>1} \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\,\Vert\, p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\big)}_{L_{t-1}} \underbrace{-\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)}_{L_0}\Big]. \]

\(L_T\): how far the fully noised data is from pure noise; no parameters, and tiny (for \(x_0 = 1\) on the linear schedule, \(\tfrac12(\bar\alpha_T x_0^2 - \bar\alpha_T - \log(1 - \bar\alpha_T)) \approx 2\times10^{-5}\) nats per dimension). \(L_{t-1}\): at every step, the learned reverse step against the true reverse step given the clean data. \(L_0\): the final decoder. Every term compares Gaussians, so it has a closed form: no high-variance sampling of the whole chain.

  1. The true reverse step given \(x_0\) (Ho et al. eq. 6 to 7). As a function of \(x_{t-1}\), \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) \propto q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\, q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)\), a product of two Gaussians in \(x_{t-1}\). Its exponent is \(-\tfrac12\Big[\frac{(x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1})^2}{\beta_t} + \frac{(x_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0)^2}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\Big]\). Complete the square: the coefficient of \(x_{t-1}^2\) gives the precision \(\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}} = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}\) (using \(\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t\)); the linear terms give the mean. Result:
\[ \begin{gathered} q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\tilde\mu_t, \tilde\beta_t I), \\ \tilde\mu_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, x_t, \\ \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t . \end{gathered} \]
At \(t = 200\), linear schedule

\(\tilde\mu_{200} = 0.009696\, x_0 + 0.990095\, x_{200}\) and \(\tilde\beta_{200} = 0.004032\) (against \(\beta_{200} = 0.004064\)). The same three numbers come out of conditioning the joint Gaussian of \((x_{199}, x_{200})\) given \(x_0\) by the textbook formula (\(\mu_{1|2} = \mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2 - \mu_2)\)), in src/recompute.py. Given the noisy point, the clean data barely moves the next step: one more reason each step is easy to learn.

  1. KL between Gaussians with the same variance is a squared distance between means: with \(p_\theta = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta, \sigma_t^2 I)\), \(L_{t-1} = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\big\|\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) - \mu_\theta(x_t, t)\big\|^2\Big] + C\) (Ho et al. eq. 8; \(C\) collects the variance terms, which do not depend on \(\theta\)). So the network should predict \(\tilde\mu_t\).
  2. Write \(\tilde\mu_t\) in terms of the noise. From D3, \(x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\). Substitute into \(\tilde\mu_t\). Using \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\), the coefficient of \(x_t\) becomes \(\frac{\beta_t + \alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{(1-\bar\alpha_t)\sqrt{\alpha_t}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\), and the coefficient of \(\varepsilon\) becomes \(-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\). So (Ho et al. eq. 10) \(\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon\Big)\).
  3. Parameterise the network the same way (Ho et al. eq. 11): \(\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big)\). The \(x_t\) parts cancel in the difference of means, leaving only the noise:
\[ \begin{aligned} L_{t-1} - C = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[&\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)} \\ &\times\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\big)\big\|^2\Big] \end{aligned} \]

(Ho et al. eq. 12)

The last step is a choice, not a derivation. Ho et al. drop the weight in front and sample \(t\) uniformly: \(L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\cdot, t)\big\|^2\) (eq. 14). With \(\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t\) the dropped weight is \(\frac{\beta_t}{2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\):

The true ELBO puts about 80 times more weight on \(t = 1\) than on \(t = 200\). In Ho et al.'s words, the simplified objective "down-weight[s] loss terms corresponding to small t. These terms train the network to denoise data with very small amounts of noise, so it is beneficial to down-weight them so that the network can focus on more difficult denoising tasks at larger t terms" (section 3.4). So \(L_{\text{simple}}\) is a reweighted ELBO: better samples, slightly worse likelihood (their Table 1: FID 3.17 against 13.51, test bound 3.75 against 3.70 bits per dimension on CIFAR-10). Later work treats the weighting as a design knob: {{Kingma and Gao 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00848}} show that with any monotonic weighting the diffusion objective equals the ELBO of the data with Gaussian noise augmentation.

D5. Score matching: predicting the noise is estimating a gradient (Tweedie, the SDE and the probability flow ODE) (derivation)

The score of a distribution is the gradient of its log-density with respect to the data, \(s(x) = \nabla_x \log p(x)\): an arrow at every point, pointing towards higher density. (Not the score of section 5, which was a gradient with respect to parameters; statistics uses the word for both.) Two facts make it useful:

  • It ignores normalisation. If \(p = \tilde p/Z\), then \(\nabla_x \log p = \nabla_x \log \tilde p\): the intractable constant disappears.
  • It is enough to sample. Langevin dynamics repeats \(x \leftarrow x + \tfrac{\eta}{2}\, s(x) + \sqrt{\eta}\, z\) with \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\); for small steps and long runs its samples follow \(p\) ({{Song and Ermon 2019, eq. 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05600}}).

Learning a score without knowing it. Minimising \(\mathbb{E}\|s_\theta(x) - \nabla\log p(x)\|^2\) needs the unknown true score. Two tricks remove it. {{Hyvärinen 2005|https://jmlr.org/papers/v6/hyvarinen05a.html}} integrated by parts to get an objective with only \(s_\theta\) and its divergence. {{Vincent 2011|https://doi.org/10.1162/NECO_a_00142}} showed that for noised data the target can be the score of the noising kernel, which is known: denoising score matching. For our kernel \(q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I)\):

\[ \nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} . \]

So regressing onto \(\varepsilon\) is regressing onto the kernel's score times \(-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\), and the minimiser of a squared loss is a conditional mean: \(\varepsilon_\theta^*(x_t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]\), which Vincent's identity turns into the score of the noised marginal:

\[ s_\theta(x_t, t) = -\frac{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} \approx \nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t). \]
A case you can check by hand (Tweedie's formula)

Let the data be \(x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). Then every \(x_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) (variance preserving, D3), with score \(-x_t\). The formula says the ideal noise prediction is \(\varepsilon^* = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\cdot(-x_t) = \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t\). At \(t = 200\): slope 0.5839. A million simulated \((x_0, \varepsilon)\) pairs, regressing \(\varepsilon\) on \(x_t\) by least squares: slope 0.5843.

That is why Ho et al. say their sampler "resembles Langevin dynamics with \(\varepsilon_\theta\) as a learned gradient of the data density" (section 3.2): DDPM's reverse step is a scaled score step plus noise. {{Song et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456}} unified the two families in continuous time: the forward process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) \(dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw\); its exact time reversal is another SDE that needs only the score, \(dx = [f - g^2 \nabla_x \log q_t(x)]\,dt + g\, d\bar w\) (their eq. 6, from Anderson 1982); and a deterministic probability flow ODE, \(dx = [f - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla_x \log q_t(x)]\,dt\) (their eq. 13), has the same marginals at every \(t\). DDPM discretises the reverse SDE; DDIM, next, is a discretisation of the ODE.

D6. DDIM: the same network, a deterministic sampler that skips steps (derivation)

{{Song, Meng and Ermon 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02502}} noticed that \(L_{\text{simple}}\) only ever uses the marginals \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), never the step-to-step chain. Many chains share those marginals, including non-Markovian ones, so a network trained once can be sampled with any of them. Their update (eq. 12), in DDPM's notation:

\[ x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\underbrace{\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}}_{\text{predicted } x_0} + \underbrace{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}_{\text{direction pointing to } x_t} + \sigma_t\,\varepsilon_t . \]

Guess the clean point from the current one; re-noise that guess to the next, lower noise level, reusing the predicted noise as the direction; optionally add fresh noise. With \(\sigma_t\) equal to DDPM's posterior spread it is DDPM; with \(\sigma_t = 0\) it is deterministic, the DDIM sampler, and its steps can skip: use only a subsequence of noise levels (\(\bar\alpha\) taken at, say, 20 evenly spaced \(t\)).

Notation trap
The DDIM paper writes \(\alpha_t\) for what DDPM calls \(\bar\alpha_t\) (the cumulative product). Read any diffusion paper's definition of \(\alpha\) before its equations; the {{Notation decoder|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} lists more such clashes.
D7. Classifier-free guidance, as a formula (derivation)
  1. Bayes' rule for scores. \(p(x \mid c) \propto p(c\mid x)\,p(x)\), so \(\nabla_x \log p(x\mid c) = \nabla_x\log p(x) + \nabla_x \log p(c \mid x)\): the conditional score is the unconditional one plus a classifier's gradient (the normalising constant \(p(c)\) has no \(x\) in it and vanishes).
  2. Turn up the classifier. Sampling with \(\nabla\log p(x) + (1+w)\,\nabla\log p(c\mid x)\) targets \(p(x)\,p(c\mid x)^{1+w}\): images the classifier is more sure about. That is classifier guidance ({{Dhariwal and Nichol 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05233}}), which needs a separate classifier trained on noisy images.
  3. Remove the classifier. Bayes again: \(\nabla\log p(c\mid x) = \nabla\log p(x\mid c) - \nabla\log p(x)\). Substitute: \[\nabla\log p(x) + (1+w)\big(\nabla\log p(x\mid c) - \nabla\log p(x)\big) = (1+w)\nabla\log p(x\mid c) - w\,\nabla\log p(x)\].
  4. Write it with noise predictions (\(s = -\varepsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\), D5) and you have {{Ho and Salimans 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12598}}, eq. 6:
\[ \tilde\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) = (1+w)\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c) - w\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing). \]

Run the network twice, with the condition and with an empty condition \(\varnothing\), and extrapolate past the conditional prediction, away from the unconditional one. One network does both: during training the condition is replaced by \(\varnothing\) with some probability (15% for the two-moons toy on {{Diffusion from zero|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cf9bf0d94e57ce9164}}). \(w = 0\) is plain conditional sampling.

Two conventions for the same knob
Libraries usually write \(\varepsilon_\varnothing + s\,(\varepsilon_c - \varepsilon_\varnothing)\), so \(s = 1 + w\). Hugging Face diffusers' Stable Diffusion pipeline defaults to guidance_scale = 7.5, which is \(w = 6.5\) in the paper's notation ({{pipeline_stable_diffusion.py, v0.35.1|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.35.1/src/diffusers/pipelines/stable_diffusion/pipeline_stable_diffusion.py}}).
D8. Flow matching: the conditional loss and why it learns the marginal velocity (Lipman et al., Theorem 2) (derivation)

The path. Pair a data point \(x_0\) with a noise sample \(\varepsilon\) and join them by a straight line: \(x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\varepsilon\), \(t \in [0, 1]\) ({{Esser et al. 2024 (Stable Diffusion 3), eq. 13|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03206}}; {{Liu, Gong and Liu 2022, rectified flow|https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03003}}). Along that line the velocity is constant, \(\frac{dx_t}{dt} = \varepsilon - x_0\).

The loss (conditional flow matching, {{Lipman et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02747}}):

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim U[0,1],\ x_0,\ \varepsilon}\,\big\|v_\theta(x_t, t) - (\varepsilon - x_0)\big\|^2 . \]
  1. Many lines pass through the same point \(x\) at time \(t\). The best single answer for a squared loss is their average direction, the marginal velocity \(v^*(x,t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon - x_0 \mid x_t = x]\).
  2. Lipman et al.'s Theorem 2: the conditional loss above and the (intractable) loss against \(v^*\) differ by a constant, so they have the same gradients. We regress on single lines and learn the average.
  3. The ODE \(dx/dt = v^*(x, t)\) moves the whole noise distribution at \(t = 1\) onto the data distribution at \(t = 0\) (the marginals of the straight-line paths are preserved). Generate by integrating backwards from \(t = 1\): Euler steps \(x \leftarrow x - \tfrac1S v_\theta(x, t)\).

It is the same family as diffusion: \(x_t\) is a scaled data point plus scaled Gaussian noise, so a velocity prediction converts to a noise prediction (\(\hat\varepsilon = x_t + (1-t)\,v\)) or a clean-data prediction (\(\hat x_0 = x_t - t\,v\)). What changes is the path (straight in \(t\) rather than variance-preserving) and the target (velocity rather than noise), and with them which errors are cheap.

Conventions differ: Lipman et al. put noise at \(t = 0\) and data at \(t = 1\) (their eq. 22 to 23, with a small \(\sigma_{\min}\)); Stable Diffusion 3 and this page put data at \(t = 0\). Couplings, reflow and solvers are on {{Flow matching and rectified flow|n:3fc5c17b0d0d8136a1efc73aaa446ceb}}.

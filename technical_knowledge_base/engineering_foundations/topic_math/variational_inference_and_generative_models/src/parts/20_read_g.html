8. A VAE on MNIST, trained for this page

The question. What do the two terms actually do during training, and what does the latent space look like?

Setup (src/data/vae.py, PyTorch 2.11 on 2 CPU threads): {{MNIST|https://ossci-datasets.s3.amazonaws.com/mnist/}} handwritten digits, 60,000 for training and 10,000 for testing, binarised (pixel above 127 becomes 1); encoder 784 to 400 (ReLU) to a 2-D mean and log-variance; decoder 2 to 400 (ReLU) to 784 Bernoulli logits; Adam at \(10^{-3}\), batches of 128, 30 epochs (14,070 steps, about 1.5 to 4 seconds per epoch). A 2-D latent is chosen so it can be drawn; it reconstructs worse than a larger one (section 10).

The two terms during training (training batches, averaged over each 100 steps; nats per image). Left axis: reconstruction, \(-\mathbb{E}_q\log p(x\mid z)\). Right axis: \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z\mid x)\Vert p(z))\).

What the curves say. Reconstruction falls fast (222.5 nats at step 100, 159 by step 700, 138 at the end on training batches). The KL first falls (7.1 to 5.4 nats by step 700) and then slowly rises to about 6.3: early on the cheapest way to raise the ELBO is to keep codes near the prior; once the decoder can use precise codes, buying information about each image in the latent pays for its KL. On the test set after 30 epochs: reconstruction 143.98 and KL 6.22, so the negative ELBO is 150.20 nats per image. The KL is the number of nats the code carries about each image (at most; it is an upper bound on that mutual information, {{Information theory for ML, section 8|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}): about 6.2 nats, 9 bits, enough to say which digit and roughly how it is drawn, not the 144 nats of pixel detail the decoder still has to guess.

The posterior widths are tiny. Averaged over test images, the encoder's standard deviations are 0.066 and 0.086 against the prior's 1: each image owns a small patch of the latent plane. The tab A VAE on MNIST draws 1,500 test images' codes coloured by digit, a 10 by 10 grid of decoded latent points (Kingma and Welling's Figure 4 recipe: grid points at evenly spaced quantiles of the prior), and ten reconstructions.

These numbers are not comparable with papers
Published MNIST log-likelihoods (around \(-80\) to \(-90\) nats) use dynamically binarised MNIST (each pixel resampled as Bernoulli of its grey level every epoch) and larger latents. Thresholding at 127, as here, gives a different, less noisy dataset, so the same model scores differently. Compare likelihoods only on the same data, binarisation and test split.

9. Amortised inference, and where the ELBO's gap comes from

The question. The encoder answers "what is \(q\) for this image?" in one forward pass. What does that shortcut cost?

Classical VI (section 5) optimises separate variational parameters for each data point. A VAE instead trains one network \(q_\phi(z \mid x)\) for all of them: amortised inference (the cost of inference is paid once, in training, and spread over every future query). It is fast and works on new data, but the network may not output the best \(q\) for each image. {{Cremer, Li and Duvenaud 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.03558}} split the gap accordingly:

\[ \underbrace{\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]}_{\text{total gap}} = \underbrace{\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}[q^{*}]}_{\text{approximation gap}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}[q^{*}] - \mathcal{L}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]}_{\text{amortisation gap}}, \]

where \(q^{*}\) is the best member of the family for that one image, found by optimising its mean and variance directly.

Measured on the 2-D MNIST VAE (src/data/vae_gap.py, first 100 test images)

With a 2-D latent, \(\log p(x)\) can be computed almost exactly by brute-force quadrature: 161 by 161 decoder evaluations on a grid around each image's posterior. Then:

  • \(\log p(x) = -136.22\) nats (average).
  • ELBO with the encoder's \(q\): \(-144.46\). Total gap 8.24 nats.
  • ELBO with a Gaussian optimised separately for each image (2,000 Adam steps): \(-136.31\). Approximation gap 0.10: a diagonal Gaussian fits this model's posteriors almost perfectly.
  • Amortisation gap 8.15: nearly the whole gap is the encoder being a poor per-image optimiser on test images.

The importance-weighted estimates of section 4 with the encoder as proposal reach only \(-144.6\) at \(K = 1{,}000\) (on 1,000 test images): when \(q\) is off-centre, importance sampling converges slowly, which is why the quadrature is used as the truth here.

Cremer et al. found the same pattern on larger models: the amortisation gap often dominates, and fixes include bigger encoders, iterative refinement of the encoder's output at test time, and semi-amortised VI. Diffusion models avoid the question entirely: their "encoder" is fixed noise, not learned (section 12).

10. Beta-VAE and posterior collapse

The question. What happens when you change the weight on the KL term, and why do some VAEs ignore their latent altogether?

The beta-VAE ({{Higgins et al. 2017|https://openreview.net/forum?id=Sy2fzU9gl}}) multiplies the KL by \(\beta\): \(\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z\mid x)\Vert p(z))\). With \(\beta > 1\) each nat of information in the code costs more, so the model keeps fewer, more independent factors (the paper's motivation: disentangled representations); with \(\beta < 1\) it keeps more and reconstructs better. Only \(\beta = 1\) is a bound on \(\log p(x)\); otherwise it is a different objective, the rate-distortion trade-off of {{Alemi et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00464}}: rate is the KL, distortion is the reconstruction term.

Five VAEs with a 16-D latent, measured (same data and network, 15 epochs each; test set; rec. is the reconstruction term). Bars: the KL carried by each of the 16 latent dimensions, sorted. "Active": how many of the 16 dimensions have a posterior mean that varies across images (variance above 0.01, the criterion of {{Burda et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.00519}}, section 5.2). log p: importance-weighted estimate, \(K = 1{,}000\), first 1,000 test images.

Posterior collapse. At \(\beta = 8\) half the dimensions carry no information: their \(q(z_j\mid x)\) equals the prior for every image, KL exactly 0, and the decoder ignores them. That is posterior collapse, partial here. It becomes total when the decoder is powerful enough to model \(x\) without any code: {{Bowman et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06349}} found an LSTM text decoder drove the KL to zero, so the VAE became a plain language model. Their fixes, still in use: KL annealing (start with the KL weight at 0 and raise it to 1) and word dropout (weaken the decoder so it must use \(z\)). Free bits ({{Kingma et al. 2016, IAF|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.04934}}) stops penalising the KL below a floor per dimension group; VQ-VAE replaces the Gaussian latent by a discrete codebook partly to avoid collapse.

Why it happens, in one line: the KL term is minimised (to 0) by \(q(z\mid x) = p(z)\), and if the decoder can already reach a good reconstruction term without \(z\), nothing pays for moving away from that optimum.

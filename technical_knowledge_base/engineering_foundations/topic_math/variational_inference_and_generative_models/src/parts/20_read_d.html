5. Mean-field VI: coordinate ascent on a tiny example

The question. Before neural networks, how was the ELBO maximised, and what does the simplest family, "every latent independent", get wrong?

The mean-field family assumes \(q\) factorises over groups of latents, \(q(z) = \prod_j q_j(z_j)\) (Blei et al. eq. 15). It cannot represent correlations between groups, but it makes each factor's best update computable. Holding all other factors fixed, the optimal \(q_j\) is (Blei et al. eq. 18; Bishop 2006, eq. 10.9):

\[ q_j^{*}(z_j) \propto \exp\!\Big(\mathbb{E}_{-j}\big[\log p(z_j, z_{-j}, x)\big]\Big), \]

where \(\mathbb{E}_{-j}\) averages over every factor except \(q_j\). Why: with the others fixed, the ELBO as a function of \(q_j\) equals \(-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_j \,\Vert\, \tilde p_j\big)\) plus a constant, where \(\tilde p_j \propto \exp(\mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p])\) (Blei et al. eq. 19); a KL is smallest, zero, when the two match. Cycling through the factors is coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI); each update can only raise the ELBO, so it converges (to a local optimum).

The example: a Gaussian with unknown mean and precision

Bishop's {{Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/pattern-recognition-machine-learning/}}, section 10.1.3. Data \(x_1, \dots, x_N \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^{-1})\) (\(\tau\) is the precision, one over the variance). Priors \(\mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, (\lambda_0 \tau)^{-1})\) and \(\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)\). The exact posterior is known (a Normal-Gamma), so we can see exactly what mean-field loses. Mean field: \(q(\mu, \tau) = q(\mu)\, q(\tau)\).

  1. Write the log-joint, keeping only terms with \(\mu\) or \(\tau\): \[\log p = \tfrac{N}{2}\log\tau - \tfrac{\tau}{2}\sum_n (x_n - \mu)^2 + \tfrac12 \log \tau - \tfrac{\lambda_0 \tau}{2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2 + (a_0 - 1)\log \tau - b_0 \tau + \text{const}\].
  2. Update \(q(\mu)\): average over \(q(\tau)\), which only replaces \(\tau\) by \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) in the \(\mu\) terms. What is left is \(-\tfrac{\mathbb{E}[\tau]}{2}\big[\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu-\mu_0)^2\big]\), a quadratic in \(\mu\), so \(q(\mu) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_N, \lambda_N^{-1})\) with \(\mu_N = \frac{\lambda_0 \mu_0 + N\bar x}{\lambda_0 + N}\) and \(\lambda_N = (\lambda_0 + N)\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]\).
  3. Update \(q(\tau)\): average over \(q(\mu)\). The \(\log\tau\) terms have coefficient \(\tfrac{N}{2} + \tfrac12 + a_0 - 1\) and the \(\tau\) terms coefficient \(-b_0 - \tfrac12 \mathbb{E}_\mu\big[\sum_n (x_n - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\big]\): the log of a Gamma density, so \(q(\tau) = \text{Gamma}(a_N, b_N)\) with \(a_N = a_0 + \tfrac{N+1}{2}\) and \(b_N\) that bracket, which needs only \(\mathbb{E}[\mu] = \mu_N\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\mu^2] = \mu_N^2 + 1/\lambda_N\).
  4. Each update uses the other's current moments; alternate until nothing moves.
CAVI, step by step. Shading: the exact posterior over \((\mu, \tau)\). Ellipses: the mean-field \(q\) at 1 and 2 standard deviations. Ten observations (illustrative, listed below), priors \(\mu_0 = 0, \lambda_0 = 1, a_0 = b_0 = 1\); start from \(\mathbb{E}[\tau] = 1\).
What mean field gets right and wrong here (all from src/recompute.py)

Data: 1.00, 1.15, 0.86, 0.55, 0.77, 0.50, 1.03, 1.67, 0.75, 0.69 (mean 0.897). CAVI converges in about five rounds to \(\mu_N = 0.8155\), \(\lambda_N = 34.91\), \(a_N = 6.5\), \(b_N = 2.048\). The posterior mean of \(\tau\) comes out right (3.1735 both ways), but the spreads are too small: \(\operatorname{Var}(\mu)\) is 0.0287 under \(q\) against 0.0344 exactly (17% too small), and \(\operatorname{Var}(\tau)\) 1.549 against 1.679. The exact posterior's \(\mu\) spread depends on \(\tau\) (a funnel: low precision, wide \(\mu\)); an axis-aligned product cannot express that, and reverse KL prefers to be too narrow rather than put mass where the posterior has little. Blei et al. (section 1 of the review): "variational inference generally underestimates the variance of the posterior density".

What it is used for in ML
CAVI and its stochastic version (stochastic variational inference, Hoffman et al. 2013) scaled topic models to millions of documents. In deep learning the factors are replaced by a network that outputs \(q\) (section 9), but "mean field" still names the diagonal-Gaussian \(q\) of every standard VAE: its latents are independent given \(x\).

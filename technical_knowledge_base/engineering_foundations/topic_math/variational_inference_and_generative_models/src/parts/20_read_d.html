4. Choosing the family: mean field, amortised, or richer

Every choice of family trades how close \(q\) can get to the posterior against how cheap it is to fit. Three choices matter.

Mean field, fitted by coordinate ascent

The mean-field family assumes \(q\) factorises over groups of latents, \(q(z) = \prod_j q_j(z_j)\) (Blei et al. eq. 15). It cannot represent correlations between groups, but each factor's best update is computable: holding the others fixed (Blei et al. eq. 18; Bishop 2006, eq. 10.9),

\[ q_j^{*}(z_j) \propto \exp\!\Big(\mathbb{E}_{-j}\big[\log p(z_j, z_{-j}, x)\big]\Big), \]

with \(\mathbb{E}_{-j}\) averaging over every factor except \(q_j\). Why: with the others fixed, the ELBO as a function of \(q_j\) is \(-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_j \,\Vert\, \tilde p_j\big)\) plus a constant, \(\tilde p_j \propto \exp(\mathbb{E}_{-j}[\log p])\) (Blei et al. eq. 19), smallest (zero) when they match. Cycling through the factors is coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI); each update can only raise the ELBO, so it converges, to a local optimum.

The example is Bishop's ({{Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/pattern-recognition-machine-learning/}}, section 10.1.3): data \(x_1, \dots, x_N \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^{-1})\) (\(\tau\) is the precision, one over the variance), priors \(\mu \mid \tau \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, (\lambda_0 \tau)^{-1})\) and \(\tau \sim \text{Gamma}(a_0, b_0)\). The exact posterior is a known Normal-Gamma, so we can see exactly what \(q(\mu, \tau) = q(\mu)\, q(\tau)\) loses.

Data: 1.00, 1.15, 0.86, 0.55, 0.77, 0.50, 1.03, 1.67, 0.75, 0.69 (mean 0.897). CAVI converges in about five rounds to \(\mu_N = 0.8155\), \(\lambda_N = 34.91\), \(a_N = 6.5\), \(b_N = 2.048\). The posterior mean of \(\tau\) comes out right (3.1735 both ways), but the spreads are too small: \(\operatorname{Var}(\mu)\) is 0.0287 under \(q\) against 0.0344 exactly (17% too small), and \(\operatorname{Var}(\tau)\) 1.549 against 1.679. An axis-aligned product cannot express the funnel, and reverse KL prefers too narrow to too wide. Blei et al. (section 1): "variational inference generally underestimates the variance of the posterior density".

The two CAVI updates for the Normal-Gamma, derived (depth)
  1. Write the log-joint, keeping only terms with \(\mu\) or \(\tau\): \[\log p = \tfrac{N}{2}\log\tau - \tfrac{\tau}{2}\sum_n (x_n - \mu)^2 + \tfrac12 \log \tau - \tfrac{\lambda_0 \tau}{2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2 + (a_0 - 1)\log \tau - b_0 \tau + \text{const}\].
  2. Update \(q(\mu)\): average over \(q(\tau)\), which only replaces \(\tau\) by \(\mathbb{E}[\tau]\) in the \(\mu\) terms. What is left is \(-\tfrac{\mathbb{E}[\tau]}{2}\big[\sum_n (x_n-\mu)^2 + \lambda_0(\mu-\mu_0)^2\big]\), a quadratic in \(\mu\), so \(q(\mu) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_N, \lambda_N^{-1})\) with \(\mu_N = \frac{\lambda_0 \mu_0 + N\bar x}{\lambda_0 + N}\) and \(\lambda_N = (\lambda_0 + N)\,\mathbb{E}[\tau]\).
  3. Update \(q(\tau)\): average over \(q(\mu)\). The \(\log\tau\) terms have coefficient \(\tfrac{N}{2} + \tfrac12 + a_0 - 1\) and the \(\tau\) terms coefficient \(-b_0 - \tfrac12 \mathbb{E}_\mu\big[\sum_n (x_n - \mu)^2 + \lambda_0 (\mu - \mu_0)^2\big]\): the log of a Gamma density, so \(q(\tau) = \text{Gamma}(a_N, b_N)\) with \(a_N = a_0 + \tfrac{N+1}{2}\) and \(b_N\) that bracket, which needs only \(\mathbb{E}[\mu] = \mu_N\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\mu^2] = \mu_N^2 + 1/\lambda_N\).
  4. Each update uses the other's current moments; alternate until nothing moves.

CAVI and its stochastic version (stochastic variational inference, Hoffman et al. 2013) scaled topic models to millions of documents. In deep learning the factors are replaced by a network that outputs \(q\) (next), but "mean field" still names the diagonal-Gaussian \(q\) of every standard VAE: its latents are independent given \(x\).

Amortised: one network outputs \(q\) for every input

Classical VI optimises separate variational parameters for each data point. A VAE trains one network \(q_\phi(z \mid x)\) for all of them: amortised inference (the cost of inference is paid once, in training, and spread over every future query). It is fast and works on new data, but the network may not output the best \(q\) for each input. {{Cremer, Li and Duvenaud 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.03558}} split the gap accordingly, with \(q^{*}\) the best member of the family for that one input:

\[ \underbrace{\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]}_{\text{total gap}} = \underbrace{\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}[q^{*}]}_{\text{approximation gap}} + \underbrace{\mathcal{L}[q^{*}] - \mathcal{L}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]}_{\text{amortisation gap}} . \]

On a trained MNIST VAE, almost the whole gap is the encoder, not the Gaussian family

The 2-D-latent VAE trained for this page (section 8), first 100 test images (src/data/vae_gap.py). With a 2-D latent, \(\log p(x)\) is computed almost exactly by quadrature, 161 by 161 decoder evaluations on a grid around each image's posterior: \(\log p(x) = -136.22\) nats on average. ELBO with the encoder's \(q\): \(-144.46\), a total gap of 8.24 nats. ELBO with a Gaussian optimised separately for each image (2,000 Adam steps): \(-136.31\). So the approximation gap is 0.10 (a diagonal Gaussian fits this model's posteriors almost perfectly) and the amortisation gap 8.15: the encoder is a poor per-image optimiser on test images. Importance weighting with the encoder as proposal reaches only \(-144.6\) at \(K = 1{,}000\) (on 1,000 test images), which is why the quadrature is the truth here.

Cremer et al. found the same pattern on larger models: the amortisation gap often dominates; fixes include bigger encoders, iterative refinement of the encoder's output at test time, and semi-amortised VI. Diffusion models avoid the question: their "encoder" is fixed noise, not learned (section 6).

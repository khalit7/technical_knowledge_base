6. Gradients through samples: score function against reparameterisation

The question. With a neural \(q_\phi\) there is no closed-form update. We need \(\nabla_\phi\) of the ELBO, an expectation under \(q_\phi\) itself. How do you differentiate an average whose sampling distribution moves with the parameters?

Write the ELBO as \(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\phi}[f_\phi(z)]\) with \(f_\phi(z) = \log p(x,z) - \log q_\phi(z)\). {{Probability for ML, section 15|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}} introduces both estimators on \(f(x) = x^2\); here they are derived for the ELBO and pushed to the case that decides between them: many latent dimensions.

The score-function estimator (REINFORCE, likelihood ratio)

  1. Write the expectation as an integral and differentiate under it: \[\nabla_\phi \int q_\phi(z) f_\phi(z)\, dz = \int \nabla_\phi q_\phi(z)\, f_\phi(z)\, dz + \int q_\phi(z)\, \nabla_\phi f_\phi(z)\, dz\].
  2. Use \(\nabla q = q\, \nabla \log q\) (the derivative of a log): the first integral is \(\mathbb{E}_q\big[f_\phi(z)\, \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z)\big]\). The function \(\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z)\) is called the score of \(q\).
  3. The second integral: \(\nabla_\phi f_\phi = -\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi\), and the score averages to zero, \(\mathbb{E}_q[\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi] = \int \nabla_\phi q_\phi = \nabla_\phi \int q_\phi = \nabla_\phi 1 = 0\). It drops out.
\[ \nabla_\phi \text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\phi}\big[f_\phi(z)\,\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z)\big] \approx \frac1S \sum_{s} f_\phi(z_s)\, \nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(z_s). \]

It treats \(f\) as a black box: try a \(z\), see how good it was, push \(q\) towards it in proportion. It needs no derivative of \(f\), so it works for discrete \(z\) and non-differentiable rewards ({{Williams 1992|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00992696}}), which is why policy gradients use it.

The reparameterisation (pathwise) estimator

  1. Write the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and of noise that does not depend on them: \(z = g_\phi(\varepsilon) = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) (for a diagonal Gaussian, element-wise). This is Kingma and Welling's eq. (5) and (10).
  2. Now the average is over \(\varepsilon\), whose distribution has no \(\phi\) in it, so the gradient moves inside: \(\nabla_\phi \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[f_\phi(g_\phi(\varepsilon))] = \mathbb{E}_\varepsilon[\nabla_\phi f_\phi(g_\phi(\varepsilon))]\).
  3. The chain rule does the rest, with \(\partial z/\partial\mu = 1\) and \(\partial z/\partial \sigma = \varepsilon\). Autograd computes it: the sample is just another node in the graph.

It uses the slope of \(f\) at the sample, not only its value. One sample already says which way is uphill.

Model A: the gradient of the ELBO with respect to \(q\)'s mean, at \(q = \mathcal{N}(0.5, 0.5^2)\)

True value: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial m}\text{ELBO} = \frac{x - m}{s^2} - m = \frac{1.0}{0.25} - 0.5 = 3.5\). Pathwise, one sample gives \(\frac{x - z}{s^2} - z\) (the \(\log q\) term has no \(m\) in it once \(z = m + \sigma\varepsilon\): \(\log q = -\tfrac12\log 2\pi\sigma^2 - \varepsilon^2/2\)). Its variance is exact: \(z\) enters linearly with slope \(-(1/s^2 + 1) = -5\), so the variance is \(25 \cdot \sigma^2 = 6.25\). Score function, one sample: \(f(z)\,(z - m)/\sigma^2\), variance 71 by simulation, 25 with a baseline (the average of \(f\), the ELBO itself) subtracted from \(f\). Both average to 3.5.

Now let the latent have \(d\) dimensions, \(d\) independent copies of model A, and ask for the gradient with respect to the first mean only. The pathwise estimator only looks at coordinate 1: its variance stays 6.25. The score-function estimator multiplies the whole \(f\), a sum over all \(d\) coordinates each adding its own noise, by coordinate 1's score:

Variance of one-sample gradient estimates (log scale), 400,000 draws each, model A copied \(d\) times. Same mean, different noise.

At \(d = 100\) the score-function estimator needs about 65,000 times as many samples as the pathwise one for the same precision (variance 403,466 against 6.25), and still 200 times as many with a baseline. A VAE latent has 16 to a few hundred dimensions. That gap is the reason the reparameterisation trick made VAEs trainable, and why the score function lives on in RL with heavy variance reduction (baselines, advantages: {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}).

When you cannot reparameterise
Discrete latents (a category, a token) have no smooth \(g_\phi\). Options: the score function with baselines (control variates); the Gumbel-softmax relaxation, a differentiable approximation to a categorical sample ({{Probability for ML, section 13|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}); or straight-through estimators, which VQ-VAE uses to pass gradients through its codebook lookup ({{van den Oord et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00937}}).

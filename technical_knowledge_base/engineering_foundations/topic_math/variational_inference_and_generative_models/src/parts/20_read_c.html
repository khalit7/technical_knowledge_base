3. The ELBO, derived three ways, and the gap drawn

The definition (Kingma and Welling eq. 2; Blei et al. eq. 13), with \(\log p(x,z) = \log p(z) + \log p(x\mid z)\) computable for any \(z\) and \(\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q}\) estimated by drawing a few \(z\) and averaging:

\[ \text{ELBO}(q) = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q}\big[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)\big]. \]

Three derivations, because each answers a different question: why it is a bound, how far below it sits, and how to tighten it.

Way 1: Jensen's inequality (why it is a lower bound)

  1. Multiply and divide inside the integral by \(q(z)\) (allowed wherever \(q(z) > 0\)): \[\log p(x) = \log \int q(z) \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\, dz = \log \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\right]\].
  2. Jensen's inequality: for a concave function such as \(\log\), \(\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log Y]\) (a chord of a concave curve lies below the curve). With \(Y = p(x,z)/q(z)\): \(\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)\big] = \text{ELBO}(q)\).

A bound for every \(q\), however bad. Equality needs \(p(x,z)/q(z)\) constant in \(z\), which happens only when \(q(z) \propto p(x, z)\), that is \(q\) equals the posterior.

Way 2: the KL decomposition (how far below)

Write \(p(x, z) = p(z\mid x)\, p(x)\) inside the definition; \(\log p(x)\) is a constant under \(\mathbb{E}_q\), and what remains is a KL (Kingma and Welling eq. 1; Blei et al. eq. 14):

\[ \log p(x) = \text{ELBO}(q) + \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z \mid x)\big)}_{\text{the gap}}. \]

The evidence is the bound plus the gap, and the gap is exactly the reverse KL of section 2: every nat the ELBO gains is a nat the KL loses. When the model's \(\theta\) also moves, raising the ELBO either raises \(\log p_\theta(x)\) (a better model) or shrinks the gap (a better \(q\)); the ELBO alone cannot tell you which.

Model A, with \(q = \mathcal{N}(0.5,\ 0.5^2)\) (too far left and too wide)

Three averages, each in closed form for Gaussians (using \(\mathbb{E}_q[(a - z)^2] = (a - m)^2 + v\) for \(q = \mathcal{N}(m, v)\)):

\[\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] = -\tfrac12\log(2\pi \cdot 0.25) - \frac{(1.5 - 0.5)^2 + 0.25}{2 \cdot 0.25} = -0.2258 - 2.5 = -2.7258\] \[\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(z)] = -\tfrac12 \log 2\pi - \frac{0.5^2 + 0.25}{2} = -0.9189 - 0.25 = -1.1689\]

and the entropy of \(q\), \(-\mathbb{E}_q[\log q] = \tfrac12 \log(2\pi e \cdot 0.25) = 0.7258\).

ELBO \(= -2.7258 - 1.1689 + 0.7258 = -3.1689\). The Gaussian KL from \(q\) to the posterior is \(1.2384\). Sum: \(-3.1689 + 1.2384 = -1.9305 = \log p(x)\), to every printed digit. A million-sample Monte Carlo estimate of the ELBO gives \(-3.1692\). At the posterior the ELBO is \(-1.9305\): gap zero. (A two-valued latent to do by hand is the drill "Jensen's inequality and the ELBO in one line" in the Practice drills tab of {{Topic: math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}}.)

Model B: when the family cannot reach the posterior

With the two-humped posterior (\(x = 2\), \(\log p(x) = -2.1693\) by quadrature), the best Gaussian \(q\) sits on one hump (mean 1.33, standard deviation 0.19) and reaches an ELBO of \(-2.8811\): a gap of 0.712 nats that no optimiser can close, close to \(\ln 2 = 0.693\), the cost of covering only one of two equal humps. Only a richer family closes it (a mixture, or a normalising flow, section 4).

Way 3: importance sampling (a family of tighter bounds)

Way 1's first line says \(p(x) = \mathbb{E}_q[w]\) with the importance weight \(w = p(x, z)/q(z)\): draw \(z\) from \(q\), reweight by how much more likely the model finds it than \(q\) does (importance sampling, unbiased for \(p(x)\)). Average \(K\) weights (still unbiased, variance falling like \(1/K\)), take the log and apply Jensen again:

\[ \mathcal{L}_K = \mathbb{E}_{z_1, \dots, z_K \sim q}\left[\log \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{p(x, z_k)}{q(z_k)}\right] \le \log p(x), \]

the importance-weighted bound of {{Burda, Grosse and Salakhutdinov (IWAE), Theorem 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.00519}}: \(\mathcal{L}_1\) is the ELBO, \(\mathcal{L}_K\) never decreases as \(K\) grows, and it tends to \(\log p(x)\) when the weights are bounded. It is computed in logs, with log-sum-exp ({{Probability for ML, section 12|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}).

The same \(q\), a better estimator: the bound climbs to \(\log p(x)\) as \(K\) grows

Model A, same \(q\): \(\mathcal{L}_1 = -3.09\) (a Monte Carlo average over 2,000 repeats; the exact ELBO is \(-3.1689\)), \(\mathcal{L}_{10} = -2.13\), \(\mathcal{L}_{100} = -1.954\), \(\mathcal{L}_{1000} = -1.931\), against \(\log p(x) = -1.9305\). On the MNIST VAE of section 4 the same experiment goes from \(-150.1\) to \(-144.6\) nats as \(K\) runs from 1 to 1,000, still far from the truth there: a poor \(q\) makes importance sampling converge slowly.

The form a VAE implements

Split \(\log p(x,z) = \log p(x \mid z) + \log p(z)\) and group the prior with \(q\) (Kingma and Welling eq. 3):

\[ \text{ELBO} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\big[\log p(x\mid z)\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(z) \,\Vert\, p(z)\big)}_{\text{stay near the prior}} . \]

For model A's \(q\): \(-2.7258 - 0.4431 = -3.1689\), the same number. Engineers usually minimise the negative ELBO and call it the loss; image papers report it in bits per dimension (divide by \(D \ln 2\), \(D\) the number of pixels).

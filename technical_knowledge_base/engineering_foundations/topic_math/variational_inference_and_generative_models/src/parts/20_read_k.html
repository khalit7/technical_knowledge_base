14. Watch it: the same points noised, then generated back

The question. What do the forward and reverse processes look like on actual points, and does the reverse process give each point back?

The models here were trained for this page (src/data/diffusion.py): a 3-hidden-layer network of width 64 (9,858 weights, shipped as 8-bit integers with one scale per row), trained for 30,000 Adam steps on two-moons data with \(L_{\text{simple}}\). Two choices differ from Ho et al. and are said here: the network is told the noise level as the log-SNR \(\lambda_t\) instead of the step index, so one network serves both schedules; and it also gets the moon label, dropped 15% of the time, for guidance (section 17). A second network of the same size is trained with flow matching (section 18). Both samplers clip their guess of the clean point to the data range, as Ho et al.'s released code does (section 20 shows why). Everything below runs live in your browser.

started on the upper moonstarted on the lower moon

What to notice. With DDPM the forward process is a random walk and the reverse process draws fresh noise at every step, so the points come back to the moons but not to their own places: the colours end up mixed across both moons. The model learned the distribution, not a code for each point. With DDIM (\(\sigma_t = 0\), section 16) and with flow matching, both directions are deterministic ODE steps, so running the encoder and then the decoder returns each point close to where it started: these samplers define an invertible map between noise and data, which is what image editing by inversion relies on.

15. Score matching: predicting the noise is estimating a gradient

The question. Song and Ermon's score-based models and Ho et al.'s DDPM arrived separately and turned out to be the same thing. What is a score, and why is the noise prediction one?

The score of a distribution is the gradient of its log-density with respect to the data, \(s(x) = \nabla_x \log p(x)\): an arrow at every point, pointing towards higher density. (Not the score of section 6, which was a gradient with respect to parameters; statistics uses the word for both.) Two facts make it useful:

  • It ignores normalisation. If \(p = \tilde p/Z\), then \(\nabla_x \log p = \nabla_x \log \tilde p\): the intractable constant disappears.
  • It is enough to sample. Langevin dynamics repeats \(x \leftarrow x + \tfrac{\eta}{2}\, s(x) + \sqrt{\eta}\, z\) with \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\); for small steps and long runs its samples follow \(p\) ({{Song and Ermon 2019, eq. 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05600}}).

Learning a score without knowing it. Minimising \(\mathbb{E}\|s_\theta(x) - \nabla\log p(x)\|^2\) needs the unknown true score. Two tricks remove it. {{Hyvärinen 2005|https://jmlr.org/papers/v6/hyvarinen05a.html}} integrated by parts to get an objective with only \(s_\theta\) and its divergence. {{Vincent 2011|https://doi.org/10.1162/NECO_a_00142}} showed that for noised data the target can be the score of the noising kernel, which is known: denoising score matching. For our kernel \(q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I)\):

\[ \nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} . \]

So regressing onto \(\varepsilon\) is regressing onto the kernel's score times \(-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\), and the minimiser of a squared loss is a conditional mean: \(\varepsilon_\theta^*(x_t) = \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t]\), which Vincent's identity turns into the score of the noised marginal:

\[ s_\theta(x_t, t) = -\frac{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}} \approx \nabla_{x_t}\log q_t(x_t). \]
A case you can check by hand (Tweedie's formula)

Let the data be \(x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). Then every \(x_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) (variance preserving, section 12), with score \(-x_t\). The formula says the ideal noise prediction is \(\varepsilon^* = -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\cdot(-x_t) = \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t\). At \(t = 200\): slope 0.5839. A million simulated \((x_0, \varepsilon)\) pairs, regressing \(\varepsilon\) on \(x_t\) by least squares: slope 0.5843.

That is why Ho et al. say their sampler "resembles Langevin dynamics with \(\varepsilon_\theta\) as a learned gradient of the data density" (section 3.2): DDPM's reverse step is a scaled score step plus noise. {{Song et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456}} unified the two families in continuous time: the forward process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) \(dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw\); its exact time reversal is another SDE that needs only the score, \(dx = [f - g^2 \nabla_x \log q_t(x)]\,dt + g\, d\bar w\) (their eq. 6, from Anderson 1982); and a deterministic probability flow ODE, \(dx = [f - \tfrac12 g^2\nabla_x \log q_t(x)]\,dt\) (their eq. 13), has the same marginals at every \(t\). DDPM discretises the reverse SDE; DDIM, next, is a discretisation of the ODE.

16. Faster sampling: DDIM

The question. DDPM needs 1,000 network calls per sample. Can the same trained network sample in 20?

{{Song, Meng and Ermon 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02502}} noticed that \(L_{\text{simple}}\) only ever uses the marginals \(q(x_t\mid x_0)\), never the step-to-step chain. Many chains share those marginals, including non-Markovian ones, so a network trained once can be sampled with any of them. Their update (eq. 12), in DDPM's notation:

\[ x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\underbrace{\frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}}_{\text{predicted } x_0} + \underbrace{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma_t^2}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t)}_{\text{direction pointing to } x_t} + \sigma_t\,\varepsilon_t . \]

Guess the clean point from the current one; re-noise that guess to the next, lower noise level, reusing the predicted noise as the direction; optionally add fresh noise. With \(\sigma_t\) equal to DDPM's posterior spread it is DDPM; with \(\sigma_t = 0\) it is deterministic, the DDIM sampler, and its steps can skip: use only a subsequence of noise levels (\(\bar\alpha\) taken at, say, 20 evenly spaced \(t\)).

Notation trap
The DDIM paper writes \(\alpha_t\) for what DDPM calls \(\bar\alpha_t\) (the cumulative product). Read any diffusion paper's definition of \(\alpha\) before its equations; the {{Notation decoder|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} lists more such clashes.

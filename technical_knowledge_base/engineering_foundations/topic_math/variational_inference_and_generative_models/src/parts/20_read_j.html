13. The reverse process and the diffusion ELBO, down to "predict the noise"

The question. The root page decodes Ho et al.'s one-line loss, \(\mathbb{E}\,\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\varepsilon, t)\|^2\). Where does it come from? From the ELBO of section 4, in eight steps, none skipped.

The model. Generation runs the chain backwards: start from pure noise \(p(x_T) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and apply learned Gaussian steps (Ho et al. eq. 1)

\[ p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_{t-1};\ \mu_\theta(x_t, t),\ \sigma_t^2 I\big), \]

with \(\sigma_t^2\) fixed (Ho et al. use \(\beta_t\) or \(\tilde\beta_t\), below). This is a latent-variable model with latents \(z = x_{1:T}\), and the forward process of section 12 is its \(q(z \mid x_0)\). Reverse steps can be Gaussian because, for small \(\beta_t\), the true reverse of a small Gaussian step is itself close to Gaussian (Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015, citing Feller).

  1. The ELBO, as in section 4 with \(z = x_{1:T}\) (Ho et al. eq. 3): \[-\log p_\theta(x_0) \le \mathbb{E}_q\Big[-\log \frac{p_\theta(x_{0:T})}{q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)}\Big] = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[-\log p(x_T) - \sum_{t \ge 1} \log \frac{p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)}{q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})}\Big] =: L\]. Both chains factorise step by step, so the log of the ratio is a sum.
  2. Turn each forward step around with Bayes' rule. The forward chain is Markov, so \(q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, x_0)\), and Bayes' rule gives \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1}, x_0) = \frac{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\, q(x_t \mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}\). Use it for every \(t \ge 2\).
  3. Telescope. The extra factors \(\frac{q(x_t\mid x_0)}{q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)}\) for \(t = 2..T\) multiply to \(\frac{q(x_T\mid x_0)}{q(x_1\mid x_0)}\): every middle term cancels.
  4. Regroup (Ho et al. eq. 5, derived in their Appendix A):
\[ L = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_T\mid x_0)\,\Vert\, p(x_T)\big)}_{L_T} + \sum_{t>1} \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0)\,\Vert\, p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\big)}_{L_{t-1}} \underbrace{-\log p_\theta(x_0 \mid x_1)}_{L_0}\Big]. \]

\(L_T\): how far the fully noised data is from pure noise; no parameters, and tiny (for \(x_0 = 1\) on the linear schedule, \(\tfrac12(\bar\alpha_T x_0^2 - \bar\alpha_T - \log(1 - \bar\alpha_T)) \approx 2\times10^{-5}\) nats per dimension). \(L_{t-1}\): at every step, the learned reverse step against the true reverse step given the clean data. \(L_0\): the final decoder. Every term compares Gaussians, so it has a closed form: no high-variance sampling of the whole chain.

  1. The true reverse step given \(x_0\) (Ho et al. eq. 6 to 7). As a function of \(x_{t-1}\), \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) \propto q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\, q(x_{t-1} \mid x_0)\), a product of two Gaussians in \(x_{t-1}\). Its exponent is \(-\tfrac12\Big[\frac{(x_t - \sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1})^2}{\beta_t} + \frac{(x_{t-1} - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0)^2}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\Big]\). Complete the square: the coefficient of \(x_{t-1}^2\) gives the precision \(\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t} + \frac{1}{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}} = \frac{1 - \bar\alpha_t}{\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}\) (using \(\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + \beta_t = 1 - \bar\alpha_t\)); the linear terms give the mean. Result:
\[ \begin{gathered} q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\tilde\mu_t, \tilde\beta_t I), \\ \tilde\mu_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1 - \bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\, x_t, \\ \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t . \end{gathered} \]
At \(t = 200\), linear schedule

\(\tilde\mu_{200} = 0.009696\, x_0 + 0.990095\, x_{200}\) and \(\tilde\beta_{200} = 0.004032\) (against \(\beta_{200} = 0.004064\)). The same three numbers come out of conditioning the joint Gaussian of \((x_{199}, x_{200})\) given \(x_0\) by the textbook formula (\(\mu_{1|2} = \mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2 - \mu_2)\)), in src/recompute.py. Given the noisy point, the clean data barely moves the next step: one more reason each step is easy to learn.

  1. KL between Gaussians with the same variance is a squared distance between means: with \(p_\theta = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta, \sigma_t^2 I)\), \(L_{t-1} = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\frac{1}{2\sigma_t^2}\big\|\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0) - \mu_\theta(x_t, t)\big\|^2\Big] + C\) (Ho et al. eq. 8; \(C\) collects the variance terms, which do not depend on \(\theta\)). So the network should predict \(\tilde\mu_t\).
  2. Write \(\tilde\mu_t\) in terms of the noise. From section 12, \(x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\). Substitute into \(\tilde\mu_t\). Using \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\), the coefficient of \(x_t\) becomes \(\frac{\beta_t + \alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{(1-\bar\alpha_t)\sqrt{\alpha_t}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\), and the coefficient of \(\varepsilon\) becomes \(-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\). So (Ho et al. eq. 10) \(\tilde\mu_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon\Big)\).
  3. Parameterise the network the same way (Ho et al. eq. 11): \(\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big)\). The \(x_t\) parts cancel in the difference of means, leaving only the noise:
\[ \begin{aligned} L_{t-1} - C = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, \varepsilon}\Big[&\frac{\beta_t^2}{2\sigma_t^2\,\alpha_t\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)} \\ &\times\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\big)\big\|^2\Big] \end{aligned} \]

(Ho et al. eq. 12)

The last step is a choice, not a derivation. Ho et al. drop the weight in front and sample \(t\) uniformly: \(L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, \varepsilon}\big\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(\cdot, t)\big\|^2\) (eq. 14). With \(\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t\) the dropped weight is \(\frac{\beta_t}{2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t)}\):

The true ELBO puts about 80 times more weight on \(t = 1\) than on \(t = 200\). In Ho et al.'s words, the simplified objective "down-weight[s] loss terms corresponding to small t. These terms train the network to denoise data with very small amounts of noise, so it is beneficial to down-weight them so that the network can focus on more difficult denoising tasks at larger t terms" (section 3.4). So \(L_{\text{simple}}\) is a reweighted ELBO: better samples, slightly worse likelihood (their Table 1: FID 3.17 against 13.51, test bound 3.75 against 3.70 bits per dimension on CIFAR-10). Later work treats the weighting as a design knob: {{Kingma and Gao 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00848}} show that with any monotonic weighting the diffusion objective equals the ELBO of the data with Gaussian noise augmentation.

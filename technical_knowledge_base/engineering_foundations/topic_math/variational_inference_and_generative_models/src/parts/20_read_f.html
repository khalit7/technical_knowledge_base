6. Where the ELBO reappears: VAEs and diffusion

The VAE ({{Kingma and Welling 2013|https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.6114}}; {{Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra 2014|https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.4082}}) is sections 3 to 5 with networks: an encoder outputs a diagonal Gaussian \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\) (amortised, section 4), a decoder outputs \(p_\theta(x\mid z)\), the prior is \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\), and the loss is minus the "reconstruction minus KL" ELBO of section 3 (Kingma and Welling eq. 3 and 7) with one reparameterised sample per image (section 5; their section 2.3). The KL has a closed form, \(\frac12 \sum_{j} (\mu_j^2 + \sigma_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2)\) (their Appendix B, derived in section 7; 1.750 nats for \(\mu = (1, -0.5)\), \(\sigma = (0.5, 2)\)). Two consequences are pure ELBO maths: only \(\beta = 1\) on the KL is a bound on \(\log p(x)\) (a \(\beta\)-VAE trains a rate-distortion objective, {{Alemi et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00464}}), and the KL is minimised, to 0, by \(q(z\mid x) = p(z)\), so a decoder that reconstructs well without \(z\) gives nothing a reason to move away: posterior collapse. The model itself (architectures, the units of the loss, the blur, collapse and its fixes, VQ-VAE, the latent scaling factor of latent diffusion) is taught on {{Autoencoders and VAEs|n:3c65c17b0d0d814da93bdb159b7c1e11}}; the MNIST VAE trained for this page, its training curves, latent map and a \(\beta\) sweep, are measured there (sections 3 and 5, closed blocks).

Fig. 5 and Fig. 6 are the same bound with two choices of \(q\): learned and one-step (VAE), fixed and many-step (diffusion).

  1. Encoder. One image goes through the encoder, which outputs a diagonal Gaussian \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\): a mean and a standard deviation per latent dimension. The figure's two-dimensional latent uses D1's worked numbers, \(\mu = (1, -0.5)\), \(\sigma = (0.5, 2)\) (illustrative; the plates stand for networks and carry no weights).
  2. Noise from the side. \(z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) drawn outside the network (section 5). The dots are 40 seeded draws: an ellipse of samples on the latent plane, against the prior's rings.
  3. Decoder. Each \(z\) goes through the decoder, which outputs \(p_\theta(x\mid z)\); the reconstruction term \(\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]\) is estimated with one sample per image.
  4. The KL to the prior. \(q\)'s ellipses (1 and 2 sd) against the rings of \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\): \(\tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \log\sigma^2)\) per dimension, 0.818 and 0.932, so 1.750 nats. The loss is minus the ELBO: reconstruction minus this KL.
  5. Gradients come back. Autograd runs back through the decoder and through \(z = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon\) (\(\partial z/\partial\mu = 1\), \(\partial z/\partial\sigma = \varepsilon\)) into the encoder; \(\varepsilon\) has no parameters and gets no gradient. A VAE learns both arrows.

Diffusion is a latent-variable model with latents \(x_{1:T}\), each a noisier copy of the data, whose \(q(x_{1:T}\mid x_0)\) is fixed Gaussian noise rather than learned, so there is no amortisation gap to train away. Its ELBO (Ho et al. eq. 3 and 5) telescopes into \(L_T + \sum_{t>1} L_{t-1} + L_0\), each \(L_{t-1}\) a KL between two Gaussians with a closed form; writing the true reverse step's mean in terms of the added noise turns each KL into a weighted squared error on the noise, and dropping the weights gives Ho et al.'s one-line loss \(L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}\,\|\varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\|^2\). That drop is a choice: the true bound weighs \(t = 1\) about 80 times more than \(t = 200\), so \(L_{\text{simple}}\) is a reweighted ELBO, better samples for slightly worse likelihood. The eight steps, the score view, DDIM, guidance and flow matching are derived in section 7; the models, schedules, samplers and guidance in practice are {{Diffusion from zero|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cf9bf0d94e57ce9164}}, {{Flow matching and rectified flow|n:3fc5c17b0d0d8136a1efc73aaa446ceb}} and {{Latent diffusion and diffusion transformers|n:3fc5c17b0d0d814fbcb5e0c8df5289f3}}.

  1. Data at \(t = 0\). 200 of D3's two-moons points, and the worked point \(x_0 = (0.5, -0.2)\) (highlighted).
  2. Forward: add noise, fixed. Each \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\) adds Gaussian noise and has no parameters. On the linear schedule the signal fraction left, \(\bar\alpha_t\), is 0.897 at \(t = 100\), 0.659 at 200, 0.0786 at 500 and \(4.0\times10^{-5}\) at 1000: the data is gone.
  3. Any noise level in one jump. \(q(x_t\mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)I)\) (D3). With \(\varepsilon = (1, -0.5)\): \(x_{200} = 0.8118\,x_0 + 0.5839\,\varepsilon = (0.9898, -0.4543)\). Training draws \(t\) and the noise; it never runs the chain.
  4. Reverse: learned denoisers. Only the reverse steps \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) are learned. The ELBO is two end terms plus one KL between Gaussians per step, each against the true reverse step given \(x_0\) (D4).
  5. The weight of each step in the bound. Written in terms of the noise, step \(t\)'s KL carries the weight \(\beta_t/(2\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_t))\): 0.50 at \(t = 1\), 0.0101 at 100, 0.0060 at 200, 0.0055 at 500, 0.0102 at 1000.
  6. \(L_{\text{simple}}\) drops the weights. Ho et al. weigh every step the same and predict the noise: a reweighted ELBO, which gives up a little likelihood for better samples (D4).

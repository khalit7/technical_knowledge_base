8. Measured on this page's own models

The evidence behind sections 3 to 7, run for this page: a VAE on MNIST and two tiny diffusion networks on 2-D data. The models themselves are taught on {{Autoencoders and VAEs|n:3c65c17b0d0d814da93bdb159b7c1e11}}, {{Diffusion from zero|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cf9bf0d94e57ce9164}} and {{Flow matching and rectified flow|n:3fc5c17b0d0d8136a1efc73aaa446ceb}}; these blocks are optional.

M1. A VAE on MNIST: training curves, test numbers, latent map (measured here)

Setup (src/data/vae.py, PyTorch 2.11 on 2 CPU threads): {{MNIST|https://ossci-datasets.s3.amazonaws.com/mnist/}} handwritten digits, 60,000 for training and 10,000 for testing, binarised (pixel above 127 becomes 1); encoder 784 to 400 (ReLU) to a 2-D mean and log-variance; decoder 2 to 400 (ReLU) to 784 Bernoulli logits; Adam at \(10^{-3}\), batches of 128, 30 epochs (14,070 steps, about 1.5 to 4 seconds per epoch). A 2-D latent is chosen so it can be drawn; it reconstructs worse than a larger one (block M2).

The two terms during training (training batches, averaged over each 100 steps; nats per image). Left axis: reconstruction, \(-\mathbb{E}_q\log p(x\mid z)\). Right axis: \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z\mid x)\Vert p(z))\).

What the curves say. Reconstruction falls fast (222.5 nats at step 100, 159 by step 700, 138 at the end on training batches). The KL first falls (7.1 to 5.4 nats by step 700) and then slowly rises to about 6.3: early on the cheapest way to raise the ELBO is to keep codes near the prior; once the decoder can use precise codes, buying information about each image in the latent pays for its KL. On the test set after 30 epochs: reconstruction 143.98 and KL 6.22, so the negative ELBO is 150.20 nats per image. The KL is the number of nats the code carries about each image (at most; it is an upper bound on that mutual information, {{Information theory for ML, section 8|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}): about 6.2 nats, 9 bits, enough to say which digit and roughly how it is drawn, not the 144 nats of pixel detail the decoder still has to guess.

The posterior widths are tiny. Averaged over test images, the encoder's standard deviations are 0.066 and 0.086 against the prior's 1: each image owns a small patch of the latent plane. Below: 1,500 test images' codes coloured by digit, a 10 by 10 grid of decoded latent points (Kingma and Welling's Figure 4 recipe: grid points at evenly spaced quantiles of the prior), and ten reconstructions.

These numbers are not comparable with papers
Published MNIST log-likelihoods (around \(-80\) to \(-90\) nats) use dynamically binarised MNIST (each pixel resampled as Bernoulli of its grey level every epoch) and larger latents. Thresholding at 127, as here, gives a different, less noisy dataset, so the same model scores differently. Compare likelihoods only on the same data, binarisation and test split.

The same 2-D VAE, trained for this page on binarised MNIST (784-400-2 encoder, 2-400-784 Bernoulli decoder, 30 epochs). Left: where the encoder puts 1,500 test images (the mean of each \(q(z\mid x)\)), coloured by the digit's true label, which the model never saw. Right: what the decoder draws at a 10 by 10 grid of latent points. Click a grid cell to mark its \(z\) on the map; click a digit to highlight it. The grid covers the prior's central 92% (quantiles 0.04 to 0.96 per axis); the codes themselves spread wider than the prior (standard deviations 1.55 and 1.08 across these images), because the KL term pulls each image's \(q\) towards \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) only on average, against the reconstruction term.

M2. A β sweep: where the bound is, and partial posterior collapse (measured here)
Five VAEs with a 16-D latent: the best log-likelihood near β = 1, and half the dimensions dead at β = 8 (same data and network, 15 epochs each; test set; rec. is the reconstruction term). Bars: the KL carried by each of the 16 latent dimensions, sorted. "Active": how many of the 16 dimensions have a posterior mean that varies across images (variance above 0.01, the criterion of {{Burda et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.00519}}, section 5.2). log p: importance-weighted estimate, \(K = 1{,}000\), first 1,000 test images.

The five rows (rec., KL, active dimensions, log p): \(\beta = 0.5\): 55.1, 34.4, 16, \(-83.9\); \(\beta = 1\): 60.5, 27.1, 16, \(-81.7\); \(\beta = 2\): 71.1, 19.6, 16, \(-82.9\); \(\beta = 4\): 93.7, 11.4, 14, \(-91.3\); \(\beta = 8\): 125.1, 5.5, 8, \(-111.3\).

At \(\beta = 8\) half the dimensions carry no information: their \(q(z_j\mid x)\) equals the prior for every image, KL exactly 0, and the decoder ignores them, partial posterior collapse. It becomes total when the decoder can model \(x\) without any code: {{Bowman et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06349}} found an LSTM text decoder drove the KL to zero, so the VAE became a plain language model; their fixes were KL annealing and word dropout (weaken the decoder so it must use \(z\)). The fixes measured, with free bits and VQ-VAE's codebook, are on {{Autoencoders and VAEs|n:3c65c17b0d0d814da93bdb159b7c1e11}}.

M3. Two tiny diffusion networks on 2-D data: noised then generated back, samplers, guidance, straightness, a sampler lab (measured here)

The models here were trained for this page (src/data/diffusion.py): a 3-hidden-layer network of width 64 (9,858 weights, shipped as 8-bit integers with one scale per row), trained for 30,000 Adam steps on two-moons data with \(L_{\text{simple}}\). Two choices differ from Ho et al. and are said here: the network is told the noise level as the log-SNR \(\lambda_t\) instead of the step index, so one network serves both schedules; and it also gets the moon label, dropped 15% of the time, for guidance (D7). A second network of the same size is trained with flow matching (D8). Both samplers clip their guess of the clean point to the data range, as Ho et al.'s released code does (section 9 shows why). Everything below runs live in your browser.

started on the upper moonstarted on the lower moon

What to notice. With DDPM the forward process is a random walk and the reverse process draws fresh noise at every step, so the points come back to the moons but not to their own places: the colours end up mixed across both moons. The model learned the distribution, not a code for each point. With DDIM (\(\sigma_t = 0\), D6) and with flow matching, both directions are deterministic ODE steps, so running the encoder and then the decoder returns each point close to where it started: these samplers define an invertible map between noise and data, which is what image editing by inversion relies on.

Guidance trades diversity for fidelity: with DDPM, \(w = 4\) keeps every sample on the right moon but squeezes them towards its middle (spread 0.70); the deterministic DDIM sampler squeezes harder (0.55) and starts to push samples off the data (91% on it), the toy version of the over-saturated, low-variety images that large guidance scales produce. And a little guidance rescues a too-short sampler (DDIM, 5 steps: 37.5% to 98%), by pulling every sample towards one mode.

Are flow-matching paths straight?

Trajectories of 40 samples from the same starting noise, deterministic in both cases (50 steps, unconditional). Left: DDIM with the noise-prediction network. Right: Euler on the flow-matching network. Straightness = straight-line distance from start to end divided by the path length (1 = perfectly straight); the lines bend where they must choose a moon.

What the measurement says. On the 40 paths drawn, the flow-matching paths are less straight than DDIM's (0.55 against 0.73; recomputed in src/check_js.mjs). Each training line is straight, but lines from different (data, noise) pairs cross, and the network learns their average direction, which bends as a path has to commit to one moon; on two well-separated modes that bend is large. So the claim "flow matching gives straight paths" holds for each conditional path, not automatically for the learned ones. The flow model's good 5-step result in the table above comes from where its steps fall in noise level, not from straightness. Rectified flow's reflow step retrains on the model's own (noise, sample) pairs, which no longer cross, to straighten the learned paths (Liu et al. 2022, section 2.1: "This reflow procedure not only decreases transport cost, but also has the important effect of straightening paths"); distillation then needs very few steps.

Both diffusion samplers clip the predicted \(x_0\) to \([-3, 3]\) (the data range), as Ho et al.'s code does with \([-1, 1]\) for images: DDPM then takes the posterior mean \(\tilde\mu_t(x_t, \hat x_0)\) of D4, and DDIM re-derives the noise from the clipped \(\hat x_0\), as GLIDE and diffusers do. The pattern: the VAE survives as the compressor in front of nearly every image and video generator; the generator itself moved from DDPM's noise prediction to flow-matching velocity with transformer backbones; and diffusion is being tried for text, where the latents are masked tokens rather than Gaussian noise. {{Topic: generative-and-multimodal|n:3c65c17b0d0d817ab6ade318917bff55}} compares these systems (its Model map); this page owns the maths they share.

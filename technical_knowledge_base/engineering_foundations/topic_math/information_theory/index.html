Information theory measures uncertainty in bits. For machine learning it does one job above all: it says what a log-loss number means. The loss of a classifier or a language model is a cross-entropy: the average length of a code built from the model's predictions. The part of it a better model could remove is a KL divergence; the part no model can remove is the data's own entropy. The same few quantities explain why perplexity cannot be compared across tokenizers while bits per byte can, why maximum likelihood spreads probability while an RLHF-style KL penalty narrows it, and why contrastive losses such as CLIP's want huge batches.

1. Surprise, and why the logarithm

The question. The tiny model gave the right word probability 0.090 and the loss came out as 2.408. Why take a logarithm of a probability at all, and what is the unit?

The picture first

Think of how surprised you are when something happens. A thing you were sure of (probability 1) surprises you not at all. A thing you thought had a 1 in 2 chance surprises you a little; 1 in 1,000, a lot. And if two unrelated things both happen, your surprise at the pair should be the surprise at the first plus the surprise at the second. Those three wishes fix the formula.

Surprise −log2⁡p-\log_2 p, to scale (10 bits is the full width). Each halving of the probability adds exactly one bit.

The words, then the formula

The surprise (also called self-information or information content) of an outcome xx that had probability p(x)p(x) is

s(x)=−log⁡p(x)=log⁡1p(x)s(x) = -\log p(x) = \log \frac{1}{p(x)}
p(x)p(x)
the probability you gave outcome xx before seeing it, a number between 0 and 1.
log⁡\log
a logarithm; its base sets the unit. Base 2 gives bits, base ee (the natural log ln⁡\ln) gives nats.
minus sign
logs of numbers below 1 are negative; the minus makes surprise positive.

In plain words: the surprise of an outcome is the number of halvings it takes to get from certainty down to its probability. A probability of 1/8 is three halvings, so 3 bits.

Why the log, exactly. If xx and yy are independent, the probability that both happen is p(x)p(y)p(x)\,p(y). We want the surprise of the pair to be the sum of the two surprises: s(x and y)=s(x)+s(y)s(x \text{ and } y) = s(x) + s(y). The only continuous, decreasing functions that turn products into sums are −log⁡-\log to some base: −log⁡(p(x)p(y))=−log⁡p(x)−log⁡p(y)-\log\big(p(x)p(y)\big) = -\log p(x) - \log p(y). Shannon put the same requirement on the average (his third axiom: "if a choice be broken down into two successive choices, the original HH should be the weighted sum of the individual values of HH") and proved that −K∑ipilog⁡pi-K\sum_i p_i \log p_i is the only form satisfying it, the constant KK "merely" choosing the unit (Shannon 1948, Theorem 2 and Appendix 2).

Shannon's own example, checked
Choose among three outcomes with probabilities 12,13,16\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16 at once, or first choose between two halves and then, half the time, between 23\tfrac23 and 13\tfrac13. The final probabilities are the same (12×23=13\tfrac12 \times \tfrac23 = \tfrac13, 12×13=16\tfrac12 \times \tfrac13 = \tfrac16), so the uncertainty must be too:
H(12,13,16)=H(12,12)+12H(23,13)H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16\right) = H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right) + \tfrac12\, H\!\left(\tfrac23, \tfrac13\right)
In bits: left side 12×1+13×1.585+16×2.585=1.459\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac13 \times 1.585 + \tfrac16 \times 2.585 = 1.459; right side 1+12×0.918=1.4591 + \tfrac12 \times 0.918 = 1.459. The 12\tfrac12 is there because the second choice happens only half the time.

Bits and nats

Changing the base of a log multiplies it by a constant: log2⁡a=ln⁡a/ln⁡2\log_2 a = \ln a / \ln 2. So 1 nat =1/ln⁡2=1.443= 1/\ln 2 = 1.443 bits and 1 bit =ln⁡2=0.693= \ln 2 = 0.693 nats. Nothing else changes. PyTorch, JAX and NumPy use ln⁡\ln, so a loss printed during training is in nats; papers about compression and evaluation often quote bits.

2. Entropy: the average surprise

The question (you flagged it on the old page). What is entropy, in one sentence, and why does it show up in places as different as decision trees, RL and language-model losses?

The words, then the formula

Before you look, you do not know which outcome will happen, but you know how surprised you would be by each. The entropy of a distribution is that surprise averaged over the outcomes, each weighted by how often it happens:

H(p)=𝔼x∼p[−log⁡p(x)]=−∑xp(x)log⁡p(x)H(p) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[-\log p(x)\big] = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log p(x)
xx
one possible outcome; the sum runs over all of them.
p(x)p(x)
its probability; the weights add up to 1.
−log⁡p(x)-\log p(x)
its surprise (section 1).
x∼px \sim p
read "xx drawn from pp"; the expectation 𝔼\mathbb{E} is the weighted average.

In plain words: entropy is how uncertain you are before you look, measured as the surprise you expect. Three readings of the same number: (1) the average surprise; (2) the shortest possible average length, in bits, of a message that tells someone which outcome happened (section 3); (3) the spread of the distribution: 0 for a certain outcome, largest when every outcome is equally likely.

Worked examples

Weather: sun 1/2, cloud 1/4, rain 1/4. Surprises 1, 2 and 2 bits; H=12×1+14×2+14×2=1.5H = \tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac14 \times 2 + \tfrac14 \times 2 = 1.5 bits.

A fair coin: H=12×1+12×1=1H = \tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac12 \times 1 = 1 bit. A coin that lands heads 90% of the time: H=−0.9log2⁡0.9−0.1log2⁡0.1H = -0.9 \log_2 0.9 - 0.1 \log_2 0.1 =0.9×0.152+0.1×3.322= 0.9 \times 0.152 + 0.1 \times 3.322 =0.137+0.332=0.469= 0.137 + 0.332 = 0.469 bits. The rare outcome is very surprising (3.32 bits) but rare, so the average is low.

The tiny model's bet (0.665,0.245,0.090)(0.665, 0.245, 0.090): 0.665×0.588+0.245×2.031+0.090×3.473=1.2010.665 \times 0.588 + 0.245 \times 2.031 + 0.090 \times 3.473 = 1.201 bits (0.832 nats). A uniform bet over 3 words would give the maximum, log2⁡3=1.585\log_2 3 = 1.585 bits.

The entropy of a coin that lands heads with probability θ\theta: H(θ)=−θlog2⁡θ−(1−θ)log2⁡(1−θ)H(\theta) = -\theta \log_2 \theta - (1-\theta)\log_2(1-\theta), the binary entropy function. It is 0 at θ=0\theta = 0 or 1, peaks at 1 bit at θ=1/2\theta = 1/2, and is steep near the ends: going from 99% to 95% certain adds more entropy (0.081 to 0.286 bits) than going from 70% to 60% (0.881 to 0.971).

Three facts worth knowing by heart

  1. 0≤H(p)≤log⁡K0 \le H(p) \le \log K for KK outcomes. Zero exactly when one outcome is certain; log⁡K\log K exactly when all are equally likely. The upper bound follows from KL being non-negative (section 6): DKL(p‖u)=log⁡K−H(p)≥0D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert u) = \log K - H(p) \ge 0 where uu is uniform.
  2. Entropy depends only on the probabilities, not on the names or values of the outcomes. Relabel the words and the entropy is unchanged. That is why it measures uncertainty and not, say, the size of an error.
  3. Maximum entropy. Among all distributions on the real line with a given variance σ2\sigma^2, the Gaussian has the largest (differential) entropy, 12ln⁡(2πeσ2)\tfrac12 \ln(2\pi e \sigma^2), which is 1.419 nats at σ=1\sigma = 1; among all distributions over KK classes with a given expected score, the softmax (Boltzmann) distribution qi∝eziq_i \propto e^{z_i} has the largest entropy (Cover and Thomas, ch. 12). Choosing a Gaussian or a softmax is choosing the least committal distribution that matches what you know.
Optional: entropy of a continuous variable is different

For a density f(x)f(x), the differential entropy is h(f)=−∫f(x)ln⁡f(x)dxh(f) = -\int f(x) \ln f(x)\, dx. It is not the limit of the discrete entropy (that limit is infinite: pinning a real number down exactly takes infinitely many bits), it can be negative (a Gaussian with σ=0.1\sigma = 0.1 has h=1.419+ln⁡0.1=−0.884h = 1.419 + \ln 0.1 = -0.884 nats), and it changes if you rescale xx. Differences of differential entropies, such as KL divergences and mutual information, do not have these problems: they are finite, non-negative and unchanged by rescaling. That is why ML on continuous data works with KL and mutual information rather than with hh itself.

What it is used for in ML
Exploration in RL: an entropy bonus +αH(π(⋅|s))+\alpha H(\pi(\cdot \mid s)) keeps a policy from collapsing too early (SAC maximises reward plus entropy; see Policy gradients and actor-critic). Confidence: the entropy of a predicted distribution is 0 for a certain prediction and log⁡K\log K for a uniform one; it is a cheap uncertainty signal. Decision trees: the information gain of a split is the parent's label entropy minus the weighted entropy of the children. Ten examples, 5 yes and 5 no (1 bit), split into 4 yes / 1 no and 1 yes / 4 no: each child has H(0.8)=0.722H(0.8) = 0.722 bits, so the gain is 1−0.722=0.2781 - 0.722 = 0.278 bits, which is exactly the mutual information between the split and the label (section 8). Sampling: temperature changes the entropy of the next-token distribution (the Sampling and decoding page measures it on real logits).

3. Codes: entropy is the shortest average message

The question. "Entropy is the minimum average number of bits" is quoted everywhere. Minimum over what, and how do you build a code that reaches it?

The picture: a code that matches the probabilities

You want to tell a friend, in 0s and 1s, what the weather was each day. Sun happens half the time, so give it the shortest word; cloud and rain share the rest. The code sun = 0, cloud = 10, rain = 11 works: no codeword is the beginning of another (a prefix code), so the stream 0100110 splits one way only: 0 10 0 11 0, sun, cloud, sun, rain, sun. Its average length is 12×1+14×2+14×2=1.5\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac14 \times 2 + \tfrac14 \times 2 = 1.5 bits per day, exactly the entropy, because each codeword is exactly −log2⁡p-\log_2 p bits long.

The rule behind it: lengths are a budget

A codeword of length ℓ\ell uses up a fraction 2−ℓ2^{-\ell} of all possible bit strings (every longer string that starts with it is no longer available). A prefix code with lengths ℓ1,…,ℓK\ell_1, \dots, \ell_K exists if and only if these fractions fit in the budget:

∑i=1K2−ℓi≤1\sum_{i=1}^{K} 2^{-\ell_i} \le 1

This is the Kraft inequality (MacKay, ITILA, §5.2). Short codewords are expensive: a 1-bit word spends half the budget. So you can only afford short words for a few outcomes, and you should spend them on the frequent ones. Treat qi=2−ℓiq_i = 2^{-\ell_i} as the probability the code "assumes" for outcome ii; a code is a probability distribution in disguise, and a distribution is a code.

Why entropy is the floor. Average length L=∑ipiℓiL = \sum_i p_i \ell_i. Write ℓi=−log2⁡qi\ell_i = -\log_2 q_i with qi=2−ℓiq_i = 2^{-\ell_i}, so L=−∑ipilog2⁡qiL = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 q_i, the cross-entropy H(p,q)H(p, q) of section 5. Section 6 proves H(p,q)≥H(p)H(p, q) \ge H(p) for any distribution qq (and a sub-normalised qq from Kraft only makes LL larger). So no prefix code beats H(p)H(p) on average, and the code meets it exactly when ℓi=−log2⁡pi\ell_i = -\log_2 p_i. MacKay writes this as his equation 5.23: with lengths implying qq, "the average length is L=H(X)+∑ipilog⁡pi/qiL = H(X) + \sum_i p_i \log p_i/q_i", "it exceeds the entropy by the relative entropy DKL(p‖q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q)". That single equation is the bridge from codes to losses.

Whole numbers of bits: Huffman's code, built on the tiny model's bet

The ideal lengths for the tiny model's bet are 0.588, 2.031 and 3.473 bits, not whole numbers. Huffman's algorithm finds the best whole-number lengths: repeatedly merge the two least likely items into one node, then read each codeword off the branches. Step through it:

The numbers

H(p)≤LHuffman<H(p)+1H(p) \le L_{\text{Huffman}} < H(p) + 1

Shannon's source coding theorem, stated plainly

Coding longer and longer blocks is the idea behind the theorem. In MacKay's words (§4.4): "N i.i.d. random variables each with entropy H(X) can be compressed into more than N H(X) bits with negligible risk of information loss, as N → ∞; conversely if they are compressed into fewer than N H(X) bits it is virtually certain that information will be lost." Shannon's original is Theorem 9 of the 1948 paper, stated for transmitting over a channel. Two things to keep: entropy is a limit on the average, not on every message; and reaching it needs long blocks, or a method that does not spend whole bits per symbol.

That method is arithmetic coding: it codes a whole message as one number and spends, in total, within two bits of the message's information content ∑t−log2⁡q(xt|x<t)\sum_t -\log_2 q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) (MacKay §6.2). So with arithmetic coding, the length of the compressed file is the model's total surprise, to within two bits. Section 11 animates it and uses it to turn language models into compressors.

On real text
Count the bytes of the 10,376-byte Darwin passage: 58 distinct byte values, with an entropy (ignoring all context) of 4.269 bits per byte; a Huffman code built on those counts spends 4.295 bits per byte, within 0.03 of the entropy, against 8 bits raw. Getting lower needs context: what came before predicts what comes next. That is conditional entropy (section 4) and, in the limit, a language model (section 11).
What it is used for in ML
The code-length reading is what makes a loss interpretable: 2.408 nats means the model would need 3.47 bits to transmit "sat". It is also why tokenizers are compressors (a BPE merge table shortens frequent strings, see Tokenizers), and why "minimum description length" arguments treat a model plus its residual code as one message.

4. Joint and conditional entropy: what context buys you

The question. A language model predicts the next token given the previous ones. How much uncertainty is left once you know the context, and how does that add up over a whole text?

The words, then the formulas

For two random variables XX and YY with joint distribution p(x,y)p(x, y), the joint entropy is the entropy of the pair, and the conditional entropy is the uncertainty left in XX once YY is known, averaged over the values of YY:

H(X,Y)=−∑x,yp(x,y)log⁡p(x,y),H(X|Y)=∑yp(y)H(X|Y=y)=−∑x,yp(x,y)log⁡p(x|y)H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y) \log p(x, y), \qquad H(X \mid Y) = \sum_y p(y)\, H(X \mid Y = y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y) \log p(x \mid y)
p(x,y)p(x, y)
the probability that X=xX = x and Y=yY = y together.
p(y)p(y)
the marginal: ∑xp(x,y)\sum_x p(x, y).
p(x|y)p(x \mid y)
the conditional, p(x,y)/p(y)p(x, y) / p(y): the distribution of XX among the cases where Y=yY = y.
H(X|Y=y)H(X \mid Y = y)
the entropy of that conditional distribution, for one value yy.

The chain rule, derived without skipping a step

  1. Definition of the conditional: p(x,y)=p(y)p(x|y)p(x, y) = p(y)\, p(x \mid y).
  2. Take −log⁡-\log of both sides; the log of a product is a sum: −log⁡p(x,y)=−log⁡p(y)−log⁡p(x|y)-\log p(x, y) = -\log p(y) - \log p(x \mid y).
  3. Average both sides over (x,y)∼p(x,y)(x, y) \sim p(x, y). The left is H(X,Y)H(X, Y). The first term on the right only depends on yy, so its average is −∑yp(y)log⁡p(y)=H(Y)-\sum_y p(y) \log p(y) = H(Y). The second is H(X|Y)H(X \mid Y) by definition.
  4. Result: H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X|Y)H(X, Y) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y), and by the same steps with the roles swapped, =H(X)+H(Y|X)= H(X) + H(Y \mid X).

In plain words: the uncertainty of the pair is the uncertainty of one, plus what is left of the other once the first is known.

Worked example: two signals that agree 80% of the time
p(0,0)=p(1,1)=0.4p(0,0) = p(1,1) = 0.4 and p(0,1)=p(1,0)=0.1p(0,1) = p(1,0) = 0.1. Each marginal is (0.5,0.5)(0.5, 0.5), so H(X)=H(Y)=1H(X) = H(Y) = 1 bit.
  • Joint: H(X,Y)=2×0.4×1.322+2×0.1×3.322H(X, Y) = 2 \times 0.4 \times 1.322 + 2 \times 0.1 \times 3.322 =1.058+0.664=1.722= 1.058 + 0.664 = 1.722 bits.

  • Directly: given Y=0Y = 0, XX is 0 with probability 0.4/0.5=0.80.4 / 0.5 = 0.8, so H(X|Y=0)=H(0.8,0.2)=0.722H(X \mid Y = 0) = H(0.8, 0.2) = 0.722; the same for Y=1Y = 1; the average is 0.722 bits. Both routes agree.
Knowing YY cuts the uncertainty about XX from 1 bit to 0.722 bits. The 0.278 bits saved is the mutual information of section 8.

Conditioning never increases entropy on average: H(X|Y)≤H(X)H(X \mid Y) \le H(X), with equality exactly when XX and YY are independent (proof in section 8: the difference is the mutual information, which is a KL divergence and so non-negative). A single value can still raise it: learning that a usually reliable test came back strange can make you less sure. Only the average is guaranteed to fall.

For a sequence: the language-model factorisation

Apply the chain rule repeatedly to a sequence X1,…,XnX_1, \dots, X_n:

H(X1,…,Xn)=∑t=1nH(Xt|X1,…,Xt−1)H(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} H(X_t \mid X_1, \dots, X_{t-1})

In plain words: the uncertainty of a whole text is the sum, over positions, of the uncertainty of each token given everything before it. This is exactly the shape of a language model's loss: it predicts q(xt|x<t)q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) and pays −log⁡q(xt|x<t)-\log q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) at each position. Averaged per token over a long text it estimates the entropy rate of the source, the uncertainty per symbol once all the past is known.

Shannon measured this ladder for English in 1951 ("Prediction and Entropy of Printed English"). Writing FNF_N for the entropy of the next letter given the previous N−1N - 1: with a 26-letter alphabet and no context, F0=log2⁡26=4.70F_0 = \log_2 26 = 4.70 bits; using letter frequencies, F1=4.14F_1 = 4.14; using pairs, F2=3.56F_2 = 3.56. From experiments where people guessed the next letter with 100 letters of context (27 symbols, counting the space), he bounded the entropy between about 0.6 and 1.3 bits per letter, "of the order of one bit per letter". Context is worth a lot.

What it is used for in ML
The loss floor of a classifier is the conditional entropy of the label given the input, H(Y|X)H(Y \mid X): zero only when every input has exactly one correct label, above zero when labels are ambiguous or noisy (each one-hot target has entropy 0, but the population of targets for one input need not). The loss floor of a language model is the entropy rate of text under its tokenizer (section 11). Longer context lowers loss because it lowers the conditional entropy the model can reach.

5. Cross-entropy: the length of the wrong code, and the training loss

The question. Why is the loss of almost every classifier and language model called "cross-entropy", and what is it the cross of?

The words, then the formula

Outcomes come from the truth pp, but you built your code (or placed your bets) for qq. Each outcome xx costs you −log⁡q(x)-\log q(x), the surprise your model assigns to what really happened. Average that over what really happens:

H(p,q)=𝔼x∼p[−log⁡q(x)]=−∑xp(x)log⁡q(x)H(p, q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[-\log q(x)\big] = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x)
pp
the truth: the distribution outcomes are really drawn from (for training data, the empirical distribution of the labels).
qq
the model: the distribution the code, or the loss, is built from.
𝔼x∼p\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}
the average is taken under the truth, because that is how often each outcome actually happens.

In plain words: cross-entropy is the average message length when you use the model's code on the real data. It is the "cross" of two distributions: the frequencies come from one, the code lengths from the other. H(p,p)=H(p)H(p, p) = H(p), and H(p,q)≥H(p)H(p, q) \ge H(p) for every qq (section 6).

Worked example: the forecaster who swaps sun and cloud
Truth pp = (sun 0.5, cloud 0.25, rain 0.25); forecast qq = (sun 0.25, cloud 0.5, rain 0.25). The code built for qq is cloud 0, sun 10, rain 11. The weather still follows pp, so the average length is
H(p,q)=0.5×2+0.25×1+0.25×2=1.75 bits,H(p, q) = 0.5 \times 2 + 0.25 \times 1 + 0.25 \times 2 = 1.75 \text{ bits,}
0.25 bits per day worse than the 1.5 bits of the code built for the truth.

From cross-entropy to the training loss, step by step

  1. We do not know the true pp; we have nn examples (xi,yi)(x_i, y_i). Use the empirical distribution: each observed example gets probability 1/n1/n.
  2. The model gives a distribution over labels for each input, qθ(y|x)q_\theta(y \mid x), where θ\theta are the parameters.
  3. Cross-entropy of the empirical distribution against the model: H(pˆ,qθ)=−∑i=1n1nlog⁡qθ(yi|xi)H(\hat p, q_\theta) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \tfrac{1}{n} \log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i). With a one-hot target, every term of −∑yp(y)log⁡q(y)-\sum_y p(y) \log q(y) except the true label is multiplied by 0.
  4. So the loss is the average negative log-probability of the correct answer, the negative log-likelihood (NLL):
    ℒ(θ)=−1n∑i=1nlog⁡qθ(yi|xi)\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i)
  5. Minimising it maximises ∏iqθ(yi|xi)\prod_i q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i), the probability the model gives the whole dataset: cross-entropy minimisation is maximum-likelihood estimation. The Probability page starts from the likelihood side and arrives at the same loss.
A classifier in nats
Correct-class probability 0.7: loss −ln⁡0.7=0.357-\ln 0.7 = 0.357 nats (0.515 bits). At 0.99: 0.010 nats. At 0.01: 4.61 nats. The loss grows without bound as the correct-class probability goes to 0, so a few confidently wrong examples can dominate a batch. The tiny model on sat: −ln⁡0.090=2.408-\ln 0.090 = 2.408 nats, the root page's loss.

Soft targets. When the target is itself a distribution (a teacher's probabilities in distillation, or label smoothing), the loss is the full sum −∑yp(y)log⁡q(y)-\sum_y p(y) \log q(y), and it no longer reaches 0 at its best: its minimum is H(p)H(p). Say the data after "the cat" were sat 0.8, dog 0.2 illustrative: against the tiny model, H(p,q)=−0.2ln⁡0.245−0.8ln⁡0.090=2.207H(p, q) = -0.2 \ln 0.245 - 0.8 \ln 0.090 = 2.207 nats, of which H(p)=0.500H(p) = 0.500 is the target's own entropy and 1.707 is removable. The gradient with respect to the scores is still "prediction minus target", q−pq - p (derived on the root page, section 4; try it in its Gradient lab tab).

Binary cross-entropy is the same thing
For a yes/no label y∈{0,1}y \in \{0, 1\} and a predicted probability yˆ\hat y of yes, the two-outcome cross-entropy is −yln⁡yˆ−(1−y)ln⁡(1−yˆ)-y \ln \hat y - (1 - y)\ln(1 - \hat y): one term survives for each label. PyTorch's BCEWithLogitsLoss and CrossEntropyLoss take scores (logits) rather than probabilities, so that they can compute log⁡\log of a softmax stably (the Numerical computing page shows why).
What it is used for in ML
Every classification and next-token loss; distillation (cross-entropy against a teacher's soft probabilities, Distillation and Small Language Models); calibration (cross-entropy is a proper scoring rule: its expected value is minimised only by reporting the true probabilities, so a model trained on it is pushed towards calibrated probabilities, not just correct rankings; see Evaluation metrics).

6. KL divergence: the bits you waste

The question. Cross-entropy mixes two things: how unpredictable the data is, and how wrong the model is. How do you separate them, and why can the second never be negative?

The picture: one stream, two codes

Sixteen symbols drawn from pp = (A 1/2, B 1/4, C 1/8, D 1/8), in exactly those proportions. The top strip encodes them with the code built for pp (A 0, B 10, C 110, D 111); the bottom strip encodes the same symbols with a code built for a wrong guess qq = (A 1/8, B 1/2, C 1/4, D 1/8) (A 110, B 0, C 10, D 111). Every bit is drawn to the same scale. Watch the gap per symbol settle.

The numbers
Own code: 8×1+4×2+2×3+2×3=288 \times 1 + 4 \times 2 + 2 \times 3 + 2 \times 3 = 28 bits, 1.75 bits per symbol =H(p)= H(p). Wrong code: 8×3+4×1+2×2+2×3=388 \times 3 + 4 \times 1 + 2 \times 2 + 2 \times 3 = 38 bits, 2.375 bits per symbol =H(p,q)= H(p, q). The difference, 0.6250.625 bits per symbol, is exactly DKL(p‖q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q). Every probability here is a power of one half, so the code lengths are exactly −log2⁡-\log_2 of them and the equality is exact, not approximate.

The words, then the formula

The Kullback-Leibler divergence (or relative entropy) from qq to pp is the average extra surprise, or extra code length, paid for using qq when the truth is pp:

DKL(p‖q)=∑xp(x)log⁡p(x)q(x)=𝔼x∼p[log⁡p(x)q(x)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\Vert\, q) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right]
p(x)q(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
the likelihood ratio: how many times more probable the truth makes xx than the model does.
‖\Vert
a separator, read "from qq to pp" or "of pp from qq". The order matters.
the sum
runs over outcomes with p(x)>0p(x) > 0; if q(x)=0q(x) = 0 for any of them, the divergence is infinite.
Cross-entropy = entropy + KL, in three lines.
  1. Split the log of a ratio: log⁡p(x)q(x)=−log⁡q(x)−(−log⁡p(x))\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = -\log q(x) - \big(-\log p(x)\big).
  2. Average both sides under pp: DKL(p‖q)=𝔼p[−log⁡q(x)]−𝔼p[−log⁡p(x)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = \mathbb{E}_p[-\log q(x)] - \mathbb{E}_p[-\log p(x)] =H(p,q)−H(p)= H(p, q) - H(p).
  3. Rearranged: H(p,q)=H(p)+DKL(p‖q)H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q). Weather: 1.75=1.5+0.251.75 = 1.5 + 0.25. Stream above: 2.375=1.75+0.6252.375 = 1.75 + 0.625.

Why KL is never negative: Gibbs' inequality, via Jensen

Jensen's inequality says: for a convex function ff (curving upwards, like −log⁡u-\log u), the average of ff is at least ff of the average, 𝔼[f(U)]≥f(𝔼[U])\mathbb{E}[f(U)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[U]). Picture two points on a bowl-shaped curve: the straight chord between them lies above the curve, and the average of the two heights is a point on the chord. Equality needs UU to be constant (or ff to be straight there).

Proof that DKL(p‖q)≥0D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) \ge 0 (the same proof as MacKay's solution to exercise 2.26):
  1. Let U=q(x)/p(x)U = q(x)/p(x) with x∼px \sim p, and f(u)=−log⁡uf(u) = -\log u, which is convex.
  2. Then DKL(p‖q)=𝔼p[−log⁡q(x)p(x)]=𝔼[f(U)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = \mathbb{E}_p\big[-\log \tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}\big] = \mathbb{E}[f(U)].
  3. Jensen: 𝔼[f(U)]≥f(𝔼[U])=−log⁡𝔼p[q(x)p(x)]\mathbb{E}[f(U)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[U]) = -\log \mathbb{E}_p\big[\tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}\big].
  4. The average inside: 𝔼p[q(x)p(x)]=∑x:p(x)>0p(x)q(x)p(x)\mathbb{E}_p\big[\tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}\big] = \sum_{x: p(x) > 0} p(x) \tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)} =∑x:p(x)>0q(x)≤1= \sum_{x: p(x) > 0} q(x) \le 1.
  5. So DKL(p‖q)≥−log⁡(something≤1)≥0D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) \ge -\log(\text{something} \le 1) \ge 0. Equality needs q(x)/p(x)q(x)/p(x) constant and ∑q=1\sum q = 1 over the support of pp: that is, q=pq = p.

In plain words: you cannot, on average, beat the truth's own code. MacKay calls this "probably the most important inequality in this book" (§2.6, eq. 2.46). Every "≥\ge" on this page (entropy below log⁡K\log K, conditioning reduces entropy, mutual information is non-negative, the data processing inequality, the ELBO) comes from it.

Not a distance: the order matters

Worked examples
  • Weather: DKL(p‖q)=0.5log2⁡0.50.25+0.25log2⁡0.250.5+0.25log2⁡1D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = 0.5 \log_2 \tfrac{0.5}{0.25} + 0.25 \log_2 \tfrac{0.25}{0.5} + 0.25 \log_2 1 =0.5−0.25+0=0.25= 0.5 - 0.25 + 0 = 0.25 bits.   • A fair coin p=(0.5,0.5)p = (0.5, 0.5) against a model q=(0.9,0.1)q = (0.9, 0.1): DKL(p‖q)=0.737D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = 0.737 bits but DKL(q‖p)=0.531D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p) = 0.531 bits.

KL is not symmetric and has no triangle inequality, so it is not a metric, despite the occasional name "KL distance" (MacKay notes it "is not strictly a distance"). Section 7 shows that the direction you minimise changes the answer, and section 12 gives symmetric relatives.

Zero probability means infinite cost
If the model gives probability exactly 0 to something that happens, −log⁡0=∞-\log 0 = \infty: infinite cross-entropy and infinite forward KL. A softmax never outputs an exact 0 in exact arithmetic, but log⁡(softmax(z))\log(\mathrm{softmax}(z)) computed in floating point can underflow to log⁡0\log 0. That is why frameworks compute log_softmax directly as zi−log⁡∑jezjz_i - \log \sum_j e^{z_j} with the max subtracted (Numerical computing).

The loss floor, in one line

Write the training loss as H(p,qθ)=H(p)+DKL(p‖qθ)H(p, q_\theta) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q_\theta). H(p)H(p) does not depend on θ\theta. So minimising cross-entropy over the parameters is exactly minimising DKL(data‖model)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\text{data} \Vert \text{model}), and the loss can never go below the data's own entropy. For the tiny model with the one-hot target sat, H(p)=0H(p) = 0 and all 2.408 nats are KL: all of it could be removed. On real text it cannot (section 11).

What it is used for in ML
The removable part of every log-loss; KL penalties that keep a fine-tuned model near its reference (section 13); the ELBO of VAEs and diffusion models (section 13 and Variational inference and generative-model maths); trust regions in RL (TRPO constrains a KL between old and new policies); knowledge distillation (KL from teacher to student). 7. Forward against reverse KL: covering every mode or choosing one

The question. If KL is not symmetric, which way round should you minimise it, and what does each choice do to the model you get?

The picture: fit one bell curve to a two-humped truth

The truth pp has two humps, 12𝒩(−2,0.62)+12𝒩(2,0.62)\tfrac12 \mathcal{N}(-2, 0.6^2) + \tfrac12 \mathcal{N}(2, 0.6^2). The model qq is a single Gaussian with mean μ\mu and standard deviation σ\sigma. Start both fits from the same place (μ=1,σ=1\mu = 1, \sigma = 1) and run gradient descent on each objective: the top run minimises the forward KL DKL(p‖q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q), the bottom run the reverse KL DKL(q‖p)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p).

Why they differ: where each one takes its average

  • Forward, DKL(p‖q)=𝔼x∼p[log⁡p(x)q(x)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\big], averages over the truth. Wherever the truth has mass and the model has almost none, log⁡pq\log \frac{p}{q} is huge. So the model is forced to put some probability everywhere the data goes: mode-covering (also called mean-seeking, or zero-avoiding). Its weakness: to cover both humps with one bell, it also covers the valley between them, where the truth has almost nothing.
  • Reverse, DKL(q‖p)=𝔼x∼q[log⁡q(x)p(x)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\big[\log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\big], averages over the model. Wherever the model puts mass and the truth has almost none, it pays heavily; where the truth has mass the model ignores, it pays nothing, because those points are never averaged over. So the model picks one hump and fits it tightly: mode-seeking (or zero-forcing).

Why the forward fit matches moments (for a Gaussian qq):
  1. DKL(p‖q)=−H(p)−𝔼p[ln⁡q(x)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = -H(p) - \mathbb{E}_p[\ln q(x)], and H(p)H(p) does not depend on μ,σ\mu, \sigma.
  2. ln⁡q(x)=−12ln⁡(2πσ2)−(x−μ)22σ2\ln q(x) = -\tfrac12 \ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}, so −𝔼p[ln⁡q]=12ln⁡(2πσ2)+𝔼p[(x−μ)2]2σ2-\mathbb{E}_p[\ln q] = \tfrac12 \ln(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{\mathbb{E}_p[(x-\mu)^2]}{2\sigma^2}.
  3. Derivative in μ\mu: −𝔼p[x−μ]σ2=0-\frac{\mathbb{E}_p[x - \mu]}{\sigma^2} = 0, so μ=𝔼p[x]\mu = \mathbb{E}_p[x].
  4. Derivative in σ\sigma: 1σ−𝔼p[(x−μ)2]σ3=0\frac{1}{\sigma} - \frac{\mathbb{E}_p[(x-\mu)^2]}{\sigma^3} = 0, so σ2=Varp(x)\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}_p(x).
This is maximum likelihood for a Gaussian: the fitted mean is the data mean and the fitted variance the data variance, whatever shape the data has.

Where each direction shows up

  • Maximum likelihood is forward KL. Training a language model on cross-entropy minimises DKL(data‖model)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\text{data} \Vert \text{model}), so it learns to put some probability on everything the data does. That is good for coverage; it is also the usual explanation (an argument, not a measurement) for why pure sampling from a pretrained model wanders into low-probability text, and why decoding tricks such as top-p cut the tail (Sampling and Decoding).
  • Variational inference is reverse KL. The evidence lower bound (ELBO) is raised by lowering DKL(q(z)‖p(z|x))D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z) \Vert p(z \mid x)) (section 13): the approximate posterior hugs one mode and underestimates uncertainty. MacKay's Figure 33.6 shows the same effect for a correlated two-dimensional Gaussian: the reverse-KL fit is too narrow (ITILA ch. 33).
  • RLHF's KL penalty is reverse KL, DKL(π‖πref)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}}), averaged over the policy's own samples. It punishes the policy for saying things the reference finds unlikely, and does not punish it for dropping things the reference would say: one mechanism behind the narrower, less diverse outputs of aligned models (section 13).
Choosing a direction
Forward KL when missing a mode is the worse failure (a density model, a speech recogniser that must not rule out a word). Reverse KL when putting mass where the truth has none is the worse failure (a policy that must not say unacceptable things), or when you can sample from your approximation and evaluate the target only up to a constant (the variational-inference case, where p(z|x)p(z \mid x) is known only up to p(x)p(x)).

8. Mutual information, and why processing cannot add information

The question. How much does knowing one thing tell you about another, in bits, including when the relationship is not a straight line?

The words, then the formulas

The mutual information between XX and YY is the drop in uncertainty about XX when you learn YY. Three equal ways to write it:

I(X;Y)=H(X)−H(X|Y)=H(X)+H(Y)−H(X,Y)=DKL(p(x,y)‖p(x)p(y))I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(x, y) \,\Vert\, p(x)\,p(y)\big)
H(X)−H(X|Y)H(X) - H(X \mid Y)
uncertainty before, minus uncertainty left after seeing YY.
p(x)p(y)p(x)\,p(y)
what the joint distribution would be if XX and YY were independent.
the KL form
how far the real joint is from independence.
From the first form to the KL form:
  1. H(X)=−∑x,yp(x,y)log⁡p(x)H(X) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x, y) \log p(x) (summing p(x,y)p(x,y) over yy gives back p(x)p(x)).
  2. H(X|Y)=−∑x,yp(x,y)log⁡p(x|y)H(X \mid Y) = -\sum_{x, y} p(x, y) \log p(x \mid y) (section 4).
  3. Subtract: I=∑x,yp(x,y)[log⁡p(x|y)−log⁡p(x)]I = \sum_{x, y} p(x, y) \big[\log p(x \mid y) - \log p(x)\big] =∑x,yp(x,y)log⁡p(x|y)p(x)= \sum_{x,y} p(x, y) \log \frac{p(x \mid y)}{p(x)}.
  4. Multiply top and bottom by p(y)p(y): p(x|y)p(x)=p(x,y)p(x)p(y)\frac{p(x \mid y)}{p(x)} = \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}. So I(X;Y)=DKL(p(x,y)‖p(x)p(y))I(X; Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(p(x,y) \Vert p(x)p(y)\big).

Let XX be −1-1, 0 or 1 with equal probability and Y=X2Y = X^2. The correlation is 0 (𝔼[XY]=𝔼[X3]=0\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = 0 and 𝔼[X]=0\mathbb{E}[X] = 0), yet YY is a function of XX. The data processing inequality

Suppose ZZ is computed from YY alone, and YY from XX: a Markov chain X→Y→ZX \to Y \to Z, meaning p(x,y,z)=p(x)p(y|x)p(z|y)p(x, y, z) = p(x)\,p(y \mid x)\,p(z \mid y). Then

I(X;Z)≤I(X;Y)I(X; Z) \le I(X; Y)

In plain words: no processing of YY, however clever, can tell you more about XX than YY itself does. MacKay: "data processing can only destroy information" (§8.3, exercise 8.9).

Proof, using the chain rule for mutual information, I(X;Y,Z)=I(X;Z)+I(X;Y|Z)=I(X;Y)+I(X;Z|Y)I(X; Y, Z) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z) = I(X; Y) + I(X; Z \mid Y) (the same chain rule as section 4, applied to H(X)−H(X|⋅)H(X) - H(X \mid \cdot)):
  1. The conditional mutual information I(X;Z|Y)I(X; Z \mid Y) is what ZZ tells about XX once YY is known. In a Markov chain ZZ depends on XX only through YY, so it is 0.
  2. So I(X;Y)=I(X;Z)+I(X;Y|Z)I(X; Y) = I(X; Z) + I(X; Y \mid Z).
  3. I(X;Y|Z)≥0I(X; Y \mid Z) \ge 0 (it is an average of KL divergences), so I(X;Y)≥I(X;Z)I(X; Y) \ge I(X; Z).
Worked example: a message copied twice with errors

What it is used for in ML
Representations: the layers of a network form a Markov chain from input to output, so a hidden layer cannot carry more information about the label than the input does. Training does not add information; it makes the useful information easier to extract by a simple final layer, which Shannon information does not measure. Feature selection: rank features by their mutual information with the label (information gain in trees is exactly this, section 2). Contrastive learning: maximise a lower bound on the mutual information between two views (section 9). The information bottleneck asks for a representation that keeps as little as possible about the input while keeping as much as possible about the label, trading I(input;representation)I(\text{input}; \text{representation}) against I(representation;label)I(\text{representation}; \text{label}) (Tishby, Pereira and Bialek 1999).

The question. Contrastive models (CPC, SimCLR, CLIP) are trained to pick the matching pair out of a batch. What does that have to do with mutual information, and why do they want batches of tens of thousands?

What it is used for in ML

10. Perplexity, bits per byte, bits per character

The question. One model reports perplexity 18, another 30. Is the first better? Only if they cut the text into the same tokens. Why, and what should you compare instead?

Perplexity: the loss as a number of choices

PPL=exp⁡(CE in nats per token)=2CE in bits per token\mathrm{PPL} = \exp\big(\text{CE in nats per token}\big) = 2^{\,\text{CE in bits per token}}

In plain words: perplexity is the number of equally likely options that would be as confusing as the model is. A model with perplexity 30 is, on average, as unsure as if it were choosing uniformly among 30 tokens. Perplexity 1 is perfect prediction; a uniform guess over KK tokens has perplexity KK. The tiny model on sat: e2.408=11.1e^{2.408} = 11.1, worse than a uniform guess over its 3 words (perplexity 3).

Why perplexity depends on the tokenizer

The total surprise of a text, ∑t−ln⁡q(xt|x<t)\sum_t -\ln q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}), is the model's compressed length of that text (section 3). Perplexity divides it by the number of tokens before exponentiating. A tokenizer that cuts the same text into fewer, longer tokens divides by a smaller number, so the per-token loss goes up even though nothing about the model's knowledge changed.

11. The loss floor, and compression as prediction

The question. A language model's loss levels off around 2 nats per token and never approaches 0. Is that a failure? And in what exact sense is a language model a compressor?

The floor is the data's entropy

Section 6 showed H(p,qθ)=H(p)+DKL(p‖qθ)H(p, q_\theta) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q_\theta). For text, pp is the true distribution of the next token given its context, and H(p)H(p) is the entropy rate of text (section 4): what is left unpredictable however good the model. A loss that levels off is the KL shrinking towards a floor that is not zero. Nobody can measure that floor directly; two estimates:

  • Shannon (1951), from people guessing the next letter: about 0.6 to 1.3 bits per letter with 100 letters of context (section 4).
  • Chinchilla's fit: the scaling law Lˆ(N,D)=E+A/Nα+B/Dβ\hat L(N, D) = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta} has a constant EE that the paper calls "the entropy of natural text" (Hoffmann et al. 2022, §D.2), fitted as 1.69 nats per token (2.44 bits; perplexity 5.4) on its own data and tokenizer. A refit of the same form by Besiroglu et al. (2024) gives 1.82 (2.63 bits). Both are extrapolations of a fitted curve to infinite model and data, not measurements, and both are per token under one tokenizer. The Chinchilla page discusses the fit.

In plain words: the terms A/NαA/N^\alpha and B/DβB/D^\beta of a scaling law are the KL part, the part more parameters and more data remove; EE is the entropy part, which nothing removes.

Compression is prediction: arithmetic coding, animated

Arithmetic coding turns any predictor into a lossless compressor. Start with the interval [0,1)[0, 1). For each symbol, split the current interval in proportion to the model's probabilities and keep the slice of the symbol that actually occurred. At the end, send just enough bits to name a number inside the final slice. Its width is the product of the probabilities, so the number of bits is about −log2⁡-\log_2 of that product: the total surprise. A language model plugs in q(xt|x<t)q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) at each step instead of a fixed bet; the decoder runs the same model, so it can rebuild the same intervals. So a model's total loss on a text, in bits, is the size of the file it would compress that text to.

Counting the model. The decoder needs the model too. In plain words: how far each distribution is from their average. Because mm is never 0 where pp or qq is positive, JS is always finite; it is symmetric; and it is at most ln⁡2\ln 2 nats (1 bit), reached when pp and qq do not overlap at all (for p=(1,0)p = (1, 0), q=(0,1)q = (0, 1): 12ln⁡2+12ln⁡2=0.693\tfrac12 \ln 2 + \tfrac12 \ln 2 = 0.693). That cap is a weakness as well as a strength: once two distributions stop overlapping, JS stays at ln⁡2\ln 2 however far apart they move, so it gives no gradient telling them to come closer.

Pinsker's inequality, TV(p,q)≤DKL(p‖q)/2\mathrm{TV}(p, q) \le \sqrt{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q)/2} (KL in nats), says a small KL forces a small total variation, the largest possible difference in the probability of any event. Divide by −β-\beta to turn the maximisation into a minimisation: minπ⁡𝔼π[log⁡π(y)πref(y)−r(y)β]=minπ⁡𝔼π[log⁡π(y)πref(y)er(y)/β]\min_\pi \mathbb{E}_{\pi}\big[\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} - \frac{r(y)}{\beta}\big] = \min_\pi \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta}}\big].
  3. Normalise the denominator into a distribution: let Z=∑yπref(y)er(y)/βZ = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y) e^{r(y)/\beta} and π∗(y)=πref(y)er(y)/β/Z\pi^*(y) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta} / Z. The objective becomes 𝔼π[log⁡π(y)π∗(y)]−log⁡Z=DKL(π‖π∗)−log⁡Z\mathbb{E}_\pi\big[\log \frac{\pi(y)}{\pi^*(y)}\big] - \log Z = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \Vert \pi^*) - \log Z.
  4. ZZ does not depend on π\pi, and KL is 0 only when π=π∗\pi = \pi^* (Gibbs, section 6). So the optimum is
    π∗(y)=1Zπref(y)er(y)/β\pi^*(y) = \frac{1}{Z}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\, e^{r(y)/\beta}
    and the best achievable objective is βlog⁡Z\beta \log Z.

In plain words: the ideal fine-tuned model is the reference model, reweighted by ereward/βe^{\text{reward}/\beta}. Small β\beta: rewards dominate and the policy concentrates on the best response. Large β\beta: it barely moves. Nothing is ever given probability the reference does not give: reverse KL forbids it.

Estimating the KL from samples

In practice the KL over all possible responses cannot be summed; it is estimated from sampled tokens.   1. For any zz: log⁡p(x)=log⁡p(x,z)−log⁡p(z|x)\log p(x) = \log p(x, z) - \log p(z \mid x) (definition of the conditional, logged).

14. Estimating entropy and mutual information from samples, and the traps

The question. Everything so far assumed you know the distribution. In practice you have samples. Can you just count and plug the frequencies into the formula?

The obvious estimator does exactly that: count, divide by NN, and compute −∑pˆlog⁡pˆ-\sum \hat p \log \hat p. It is called the plug-in (or maximum-likelihood) estimator. It is biased downwards: with finite samples, outcomes you have not seen yet count as impossible, and the ones you have seen look more concentrated than they are. For mm possible outcomes and NN samples, the bias is about

𝔼[Hˆplug-in]−H≈−m−12N nats\mathbb{E}\big[\hat H_{\text{plug-in}}\big] - H \approx -\frac{m - 1}{2N} \text{ nats}

(Paninski 2003, eq. 4.6, completing Miller's 1955 result; valid when NN is large compared with mm.) The Miller-Madow correction adds (mˆ−1)/(2N)(\hat m - 1)/(2N) back, with mˆ\hat m the number of outcomes actually seen. The same bias, as a case of estimator bias in general (bias, variance and mean squared error of an estimator, with an exact table for the plug-in and Miller-Madow on the tiny model's three words), is section 2 of Statistics for ML: estimation, bias and variance, bootstrap, hypothesis tests.

Mutual information inherits the bias, with a twist

Plugging counts into I=H(X)+H(Y)−H(X,Y)I = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y) combines three biased estimates. The joint has many more cells (mXmYm_X m_Y) than the marginals, so it is underestimated the most, and the plug-in mutual information comes out too high: by about (mX−1)(mY−1)2N\frac{(m_X - 1)(m_Y - 1)}{2N} nats (the Miller-Madow terms combined; derived here, not quoted). Independent variables look dependent.

Two independent variables, 10 values each (true I=0I = 0)
With 100 samples, two variables that have nothing to do with each other show 0.48 nats (0.69 bits) of "shared information". Reading a plug-in mutual information without its bias is a classic way to find structure in noise.

The text example: memorisation looks like knowledge

The conditional-entropy chart of section 4 is the same trap at scale. Counting which byte follows each context of N−1N - 1 bytes in the 10,376-byte passage, the plug-in estimate falls to 0.18 bits per byte at N=8N = 8, far below the 0.85 that a 0.5B-parameter language model achieves. The counts have not learned English; with about ten thousand positions, most 7-byte contexts occur once, so the "next byte" is always certain in the sample. On the whole book (953,697 bytes) the same estimate falls more slowly (0.81 bits at N=8N = 8), and it would keep falling with longer contexts. Shannon's 1951 lower bound of about 0.6 bits per letter is the sanity check: a count-based estimate below it on a short sample is the estimator, not the text.

High dimensions: bounds instead of counts

For images, sentences or embeddings, counting is hopeless: every sample is unique. Methods switch to variational bounds learned by a network: InfoNCE (section 9), and others that trade bias against variance (Poole et al. 2019: "the existing variational lower bounds degrade when the MI is large, exhibiting either high bias or high variance"). And the general limit of section 9 applies to all of them: without assumptions, no lower bound from NN samples can certify much more than ln⁡N\ln N (McAllester and Stratos 2020).

A checklist for any reported entropy or mutual information
  1. How many samples per possible outcome (or per cell of the joint)? Below a few, plug-in numbers are mostly bias.
  2. Was a bias correction or a shuffled-data baseline used? Shuffle YY against XX and re-estimate: the shuffled value is the noise floor of "independent".
  3. For continuous data, how was it binned? The answer changes with the bins.
  4. For a variational bound: what was NN (the batch size)? Is the reported value near ln⁡N\ln N?
  5. In bits or nats?

15. Common mistakes, and which quantity to use when

Mistakes and misconceptions

Mixing units. Frameworks report nats; papers often quote bits.
A loss of 2.0 nats is 2.885 bits. A perplexity computed as 2loss2^{\text{loss}} from a loss in nats is wrong: use elosse^{\text{loss}}.
Comparing perplexities across tokenizers.
A tokenizer with fewer, longer tokens raises the per-token loss without changing how well the text is compressed, and one with many cheap tokens (whitespace in code) lowers it. Compare in bits per byte (section 10).
Treating KL as a distance.
It is not symmetric and has no triangle inequality; which direction you minimise changes the answer (section 7). If you need a symmetric, bounded quantity, use Jensen-Shannon or total variation (section 12).
Expecting the loss to reach 0.
The floor is the data's own (conditional) entropy; for text, and for noisy labels, that is above 0 (sections 4, 11).
Assigning probability exactly 0.
A model that gives 0 to something that happens has infinite cross-entropy and infinite forward KL. Compute log-probabilities with log_softmax, never as the log of a softmax output that can underflow (section 6).
Reading the InfoNCE bound as the mutual information.
It is a lower bound capped at ln⁡N\ln N; a large true mutual information with a small batch reads as at most ln⁡N\ln N (section 9).
Reading zero correlation as independence.
Only zero mutual information means independence; Y=X2Y = X^2 has zero correlation and 0.918 bits of mutual information (section 8).
Trusting a plug-in estimate from few samples.
Entropy comes out too low and mutual information too high; independent variables look dependent (section 14).
Quoting the old "compression" headline numbers.
Delétang et al.'s abstract says 43.4% and 16.4%; their Table 1 says 48.0% and 21.0% for the same model; quote the table, and say whether the rate counts the model's size (section 11).
Calling cross-entropy and KL "the same loss".
Their gradients with respect to the model are the same, because they differ by H(p)H(p), which does not depend on the model. Their values are not: with soft targets, cross-entropy cannot reach 0, KL can. Report KL when you want "distance from the target", cross-entropy when you want "compressed length".

Which quantity to use when

  • Training a predictor of discrete outcomes (classes, tokens): minimise cross-entropy. It is the negative log-likelihood, and the only part you can change is the KL to the data.
  • Reporting a language model's quality: perplexity only within one tokenizer and one evaluation set; bits per byte (or per character, saying which) across tokenizers.
  • Keeping a fine-tuned model close to its starting point: a KL penalty to the reference, in the reverse direction DKL(π‖πref)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}}); expect it to trade some diversity for staying on the reference's high-probability outputs (section 13).
  • Fitting an approximate distribution: forward KL (maximum likelihood) when missing a mode is the worse failure; reverse KL (variational inference) when putting mass where the target has none is the worse failure, or when the target can be evaluated only up to a constant and only the approximation can be sampled (section 7).
  • Measuring dependence between variables: mutual information when it may be nonlinear, with a bias check (section 14); correlation only for linear relationships. For high-dimensional variables, a bound such as InfoNCE, remembering it cannot exceed ln⁡N\ln N.
  • Measuring how confident a prediction is: the entropy of the predicted distribution, 0 for a certain prediction and log⁡K\log K for a uniform one over KK outcomes.
  • Comparing two distributions symmetrically, or with a bounded number: Jensen-Shannon (at most ln⁡2\ln 2) or total variation (at most 1).

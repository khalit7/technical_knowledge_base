The idea in one screen

Every log-loss is a code length: the cross-entropy a model pays is the data's own entropy, which no model can remove, plus a KL divergence, which is the part training removes.

The loss is a floor plus a removable gap, in two codes and in two real models

Top: the same 16 symbols coded with the code built for the truth pp and with one built for a wrong guess qq (section 3): 1.75 against 2.375 bits per symbol, and the extra 0.625 is exactly the KL. Bottom: GPT-2 and Qwen2.5-0.5B on the same 10,376-byte Darwin passage (measured, section 6). Nobody knows the passage's floor, but it is the same for both models, so the whole 0.153 bits per byte between them is a difference in KL.

H(p,q)⏟the loss=H(p)⏟floor+DKL(p‖q)⏟removable\underbrace{H(p, q)}_{\text{the loss}} \;=\; \underbrace{H(p)}_{\text{floor}} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\Vert\, q)}_{\text{removable}}

This one line (derived in section 3) is why minimising cross-entropy is maximum likelihood, why a language model's loss levels off above zero, and why the answer to "is 2.0 a good loss?" is "compared with what entropy?". The same few quantities explain why perplexity cannot be compared across tokenizers while bits per byte can, why maximum likelihood spreads probability while an RLHF-style KL penalty narrows it, and why contrastive losses such as CLIP's want huge batches.

A certain outcome surprises you not at all; and for two independent outcomes the probability multiplies, p(x)p(y)p(x)p(y), while the surprise should add. Only −log⁡-\log turns products into sums (details below), and the base only sets the unit: base 2 gives bits, base ee gives nats, and 1 nat =1.443= 1.443 bits, 1 bit =ln⁡2=0.693= \ln 2 = 0.693 nats. PyTorch, JAX and NumPy use ln⁡\ln, so a loss printed during training is in nats: 2.0 nats is 2.885 bits, and its perplexity is e2.0e^{2.0}, not 22.02^{2.0}. Per-token surprise is also the first thing to read in a loss spike: a token with loss 10 nats is one the model gave about e−10=4.5×10−5e^{-10} = 4.5 \times 10^{-5}, and a few of those can dominate a batch's average.

Why the logarithm, as Shannon proved it (with his own example checked)

Shannon put the additivity requirement on the average (his third axiom: "if a choice be broken down into two successive choices, the original HH should be the weighted sum of the individual values of HH") and proved that −K∑ipilog⁡pi-K\sum_i p_i \log p_i is the only form satisfying it, the constant KK "merely" choosing the unit (Shannon 1948, Theorem 2 and Appendix 2).

His example: choose among 12,13,16\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16 at once, or first between two halves and then, half the time, between 23\tfrac23 and 13\tfrac13. The final probabilities agree (12×23=13\tfrac12 \times \tfrac23 = \tfrac13), so H(12,13,16)=H(12,12)+12H(23,13)H(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16) = H(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) + \tfrac12 H(\tfrac23, \tfrac13). In bits: left 12×1+13×1.585+16×2.585=1.459\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac13 \times 1.585 + \tfrac16 \times 2.585 = 1.459; right 1+12×0.918=1.4591 + \tfrac12 \times 0.918 = 1.459. The 12\tfrac12 is there because the second choice happens only half the time.

Entropy: the surprise you expect before you look

Weather that is sun half the time and cloud or rain a quarter each surprises you by 1, 2 and 2 bits, so on average by 12×1+14×2+14×2=1.5\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac14 \times 2 + \tfrac14 \times 2 = 1.5 bits. The tiny model's bet: 0.665×0.588+0.245×2.031+0.090×3.473=1.2010.665 \times 0.588 + 0.245 \times 2.031 + 0.090 \times 3.473 = 1.201 bits (0.832 nats), below the 1.585 bits (log2⁡3\log_2 3) of a uniform bet over three words. A coin that lands heads 90% of the time: 0.137+0.332=0.4690.137 + 0.332 = 0.469 bits; the rare tail is very surprising (3.32 bits) but rare.

Entropy is steep near certainty and flat near a fair coin

The binary entropy H(θ)=−θlog2⁡θ−(1−θ)log2⁡(1−θ)H(\theta) = -\theta \log_2 \theta - (1-\theta)\log_2(1-\theta) of a coin with heads probability θ\theta: 0 at the ends, 1 bit at θ=1/2\theta = 1/2. Going from 99% to 95% certain adds more (0.081 to 0.286 bits) than going from 70% to 60% (0.881 to 0.971).

H(p)=𝔼x∼p[−log⁡p(x)]=−∑xp(x)log⁡p(x)H(p) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[-\log p(x)\big] = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log p(x)

Three readings of one number: the average surprise; the shortest possible average length of a message saying which outcome happened (section 2); and the spread of the distribution. 0≤H(p)≤log⁡K0 \le H(p) \le \log K for KK outcomes, 0 exactly when one outcome is certain and log⁡K\log K exactly when all are equally likely (because DKL(p‖u)=log⁡K−H(p)≥0D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert u) = \log K - H(p) \ge 0 for the uniform uu, section 3). It depends only on the probabilities, not on the names or values of the outcomes, which is why it measures uncertainty and not the size of an error. Choosing a Gaussian or a softmax is choosing the maximum-entropy, least committal distribution that matches what you know (details below).

Where entropy shows up in ML
Exploration in RL: an entropy bonus +αH(π(⋅|s))+\alpha H(\pi(\cdot \mid s)) keeps a policy from collapsing too early (SAC maximises reward plus entropy; Policy gradients and actor-critic). Confidence: the entropy of a predicted distribution is a cheap uncertainty signal, 0 for a certain prediction and log⁡K\log K for a uniform one. Decision trees: the information gain of a split is the parent's label entropy minus the children's weighted entropy; ten examples, 5 yes and 5 no (1 bit), split into 4 yes / 1 no and 1 yes / 4 no gives children of H(0.8)=0.722H(0.8) = 0.722 bits, a gain of 0.278 bits, exactly the mutual information between split and label (section 8). Sampling: temperature moves the entropy of the next-token distribution (section 5).
Maximum entropy, and why the entropy of a continuous variable is different

Among distributions on the real line with variance σ2\sigma^2, the Gaussian has the largest (differential) entropy, 12ln⁡(2πeσ2)\tfrac12 \ln(2\pi e \sigma^2), 1.419 nats at σ=1\sigma = 1; among distributions over KK classes with a given expected score, the softmax qi∝eziq_i \propto e^{z_i} has the largest (Cover and Thomas, ch. 12).

For a density f(x)f(x), the differential entropy is h(f)=−∫f(x)ln⁡f(x)dxh(f) = -\int f(x) \ln f(x)\, dx. It is not the limit of the discrete entropy (that limit is infinite: pinning a real number down exactly takes infinitely many bits), it can be negative (a Gaussian with σ=0.1\sigma = 0.1 has h=1.419+ln⁡0.1=−0.884h = 1.419 + \ln 0.1 = -0.884 nats), and it changes if you rescale xx. Differences of differential entropies, such as KL divergences and mutual information, are finite, non-negative and unchanged by rescaling, which is why ML on continuous data works with them rather than with hh.

2. Codes: entropy is the shortest average message

Tell a friend the weather each day in 0s and 1s with sun = 0, cloud = 10, rain = 11. No codeword is the start of another (a prefix code), so 0100110 splits one way only: sun, cloud, sun, rain, sun. The average length is 12×1+14×2+14×2=1.5\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac14 \times 2 + \tfrac14 \times 2 = 1.5 bits per day, exactly the entropy, because each codeword is exactly −log2⁡p-\log_2 p bits long.

Lengths are a budget. A codeword of length ℓ\ell uses up a fraction 2−ℓ2^{-\ell} of all bit strings, and a prefix code with lengths ℓ1,…,ℓK\ell_1, \dots, \ell_K exists if and only if

∑i=1K2−ℓi≤1\sum_{i=1}^{K} 2^{-\ell_i} \le 1

(the Kraft inequality, MacKay, ITILA, §5.2). A 1-bit word spends half the budget, so short words can go to only a few outcomes, and they should go to the frequent ones. Read qi=2−ℓiq_i = 2^{-\ell_i} as the probability the code assumes: a code is a probability distribution in disguise, and a distribution is a code. The average length is then L=∑ipiℓi=−∑ipilog2⁡qiL = \sum_i p_i \ell_i = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 q_i, the cross-entropy H(p,q)H(p, q), and section 3 proves H(p,q)≥H(p)H(p, q) \ge H(p). So no prefix code beats the entropy on average, and it meets it exactly when ℓi=−log2⁡pi\ell_i = -\log_2 p_i. MacKay's equation 5.23: "the average length is L=H(X)+∑ipilog⁡pi/qiL = H(X) + \sum_i p_i \log p_i/q_i", which "exceeds the entropy by the relative entropy DKL(p‖q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q)". That equation is the bridge from codes to losses.

Whole numbers of bits: Huffman's code on the tiny model's bet

The ideal lengths for the bet are 0.588, 2.031 and 3.473 bits. Huffman's algorithm finds the best whole-number lengths: merge the two least likely items, repeat, then read the codewords off the branches.

Huffman pays 0.134 bits per word for rounding to whole bits

Cat 0, dog 10, sat 11 average 1.335 bits per word against the entropy 1.201; the guarantee is H(p)≤LHuffman<H(p)+1H(p) \le L_{\text{Huffman}} \lt H(p) + 1 (MacKay, Theorem 5.1, eq. 5.19). Coding longer blocks shrinks the "+1", and arithmetic coding removes it: it codes a whole message as one number and spends, in total, within two bits of the message's surprise ∑t−log2⁡q(xt|x<t)\sum_t -\log_2 q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) (MacKay §6.2). So the length of a compressed file is the model's total surprise, to within two bits; section 7 animates it.

On real text: the Darwin passage uses 58 distinct byte values with an entropy, ignoring all context, of 4.269 bits per byte; a Huffman code on those counts spends 4.295, against 8 raw. Going lower needs context, which is a language model (section 7). The code-length reading is also why tokenizers are compressors (a BPE merge table shortens frequent strings, Tokenizers), and why minimum-description-length arguments treat a model plus its residual code as one message.

The Shannon code, block coding, and the source coding theorem as stated

The cruder rule "round each ideal length up" (a Shannon code, lengths 1, 3, 4) gives 1.760 bits on the bet. Huffman on pairs of words (9 outcomes) costs 1.2145 bits per word, on triples 1.2107, on blocks of four 1.2094, closing in on 1.2009.

The theorem, in MacKay's words (§4.4): "N i.i.d. random variables each with entropy H(X) can be compressed into more than N H(X) bits with negligible risk of information loss, as N → ∞; conversely if they are compressed into fewer than N H(X) bits it is virtually certain that information will be lost." Shannon's original is Theorem 9 of the 1948 paper, stated for transmitting over a channel. Entropy limits the average, not every message, and reaching it needs long blocks or a method such as arithmetic coding that does not spend whole bits per symbol.

3. Cross-entropy and KL: the wrong code, and the bits it wastes

Sixteen symbols drawn from pp = (A 1/2, B 1/4, C 1/8, D 1/8), in exactly those proportions, go through two codes: the code built for pp (A 0, B 10, C 110, D 111) and a code built for a wrong guess qq = (A 1/8, B 1/2, C 1/4, D 1/8) (A 110, B 0, C 10, D 111). Predict before you step: the wrong code is shorter on B, so does it lose overall, and by how much per symbol?

The same symbols cost 28 bits with the right code and 38 with the wrong one

Own code: 8×1+4×2+2×3+2×3=288 \times 1 + 4 \times 2 + 2 \times 3 + 2 \times 3 = 28 bits, 1.75 per symbol =H(p)= H(p). Wrong code: 8×3+4×1+2×2+2×3=388 \times 3 + 4 \times 1 + 2 \times 2 + 2 \times 3 = 38 bits, 2.375 per symbol, the cross-entropy H(p,q)H(p, q): frequencies from one distribution, code lengths from the other. The difference, 0.625 bits per symbol, is the Kullback-Leibler divergence (or relative entropy) DKL(p‖q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q). Every probability here is a power of one half, so the equality is exact.

H(p,q)=−∑xp(x)log⁡q(x),DKL(p‖q)=∑xp(x)log⁡p(x)q(x)=H(p,q)−H(p)H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x), \qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\Vert\, q) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = H(p, q) - H(p)

The identity is one line: split log⁡pq=−log⁡q+log⁡p\log \frac{p}{q} = -\log q + \log p and average under pp. The weather agrees: a forecaster who swaps sun and cloud builds cloud 0, sun 10, rain 11, and the real weather costs 1.75 bits a day, =1.5+0.25= 1.5 + 0.25. The ratio p(x)/q(x)p(x)/q(x) is the likelihood ratio; the sum runs over outcomes with p(x)>0p(x) \gt 0, and if q(x)=0q(x) = 0 for any of them the divergence is infinite.

The training loss is a cross-entropy, so training minimises a KL

With nn examples (xi,yi)(x_i, y_i), use the empirical distribution (each example gets 1/n1/n) as pp and the model qθ(y|x)q_\theta(y \mid x) as qq. A one-hot target multiplies every term of −∑yp(y)log⁡q(y)-\sum_y p(y) \log q(y) by 0 except the true label, leaving the negative log-likelihood

ℒ(θ)=−1n∑i=1nlog⁡qθ(yi|xi)=H(pˆ)+DKL(pˆ‖qθ)\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i) = H(\hat p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\hat p \,\Vert\, q_\theta)

Minimising it maximises ∏iqθ(yi|xi)\prod_i q_\theta(y_i \mid x_i): cross-entropy minimisation is maximum-likelihood estimation (the Probability page arrives at the same loss from the likelihood side), and because H(pˆ)H(\hat p) does not depend on θ\theta, it is exactly minimising DKL(data‖model)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\text{data} \Vert \text{model}); the loss can never go below the data's own entropy. For the tiny model with the one-hot target sat, H(p)=0H(p) = 0 and all 2.408 nats are KL. In nats, a correct-class probability of 0.7 costs 0.357 (0.515 bits), 0.99 costs 0.010, and 0.01 costs 4.61: the loss grows without bound, so a few confidently wrong examples can dominate a batch.

Soft targets (a teacher's probabilities, label smoothing) keep the full sum, and its minimum is H(p)H(p), not 0. Say the data after "the cat" were sat 0.8, dog 0.2 illustrative: against the tiny model H(p,q)=2.207H(p, q) = 2.207 nats, of which H(p)=0.500H(p) = 0.500 is the target's own entropy and 1.707 is removable. Cross-entropy and KL have the same gradient in the model (they differ by H(p)H(p)), "prediction minus target" q−pq - p on the scores (derived on the Calculus and optimisation for ML, section 2), but not the same value: report KL for "distance from the target", cross-entropy for "compressed length".

Binary cross-entropy, logits, and why cross-entropy rewards calibration
Binary cross-entropy, −yln⁡yˆ−(1−y)ln⁡(1−yˆ)-y \ln \hat y - (1 - y)\ln(1 - \hat y), is the two-outcome case; PyTorch's BCEWithLogitsLoss and CrossEntropyLoss take logits so they can compute the log of a softmax stably. Cross-entropy is also a proper scoring rule: its expected value is minimised only by reporting the true probabilities, which pushes a model towards calibration, not just correct rankings (Evaluation metrics). Cross-entropy among the other losses, with label smoothing and focal loss, is on Loss functions.

Never negative, and not a distance

DKL(p‖q)≥0D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) \ge 0, with equality only when q=pq = p (Gibbs' inequality): on average you cannot beat the truth's own code. MacKay calls it "probably the most important inequality in this book" (§2.6, eq. 2.46); every "≥\ge" on this page (entropy below log⁡K\log K, conditioning reduces entropy, mutual information is non-negative, data processing, the ELBO) comes from it. It is not symmetric: a fair coin p=(0.5,0.5)p = (0.5, 0.5) against a model q=(0.9,0.1)q = (0.9, 0.1) gives DKL(p‖q)=0.737D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = 0.737 bits but DKL(q‖p)=0.531D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p) = 0.531; the stream above gives 0.625 one way and 0.5 the other; the weather gives 0.25 both ways only because qq is pp with two outcomes swapped. With no symmetry and no triangle inequality it is not a metric, despite the name "KL distance" (MacKay: it "is not strictly a distance"). Section 4 shows the direction changes the answer. Zero probability means infinite cost
A model that gives probability exactly 0 to something that happens has −log⁡0=∞-\log 0 = \infty: infinite cross-entropy and infinite forward KL. A softmax never outputs an exact 0 in exact arithmetic, but log⁡(softmax(z))\log(\mathrm{softmax}(z)) in floating point can underflow to log⁡0\log 0, which is why frameworks compute log_softmax directly as zi−log⁡∑jezjz_i - \log \sum_j e^{z_j} with the maximum subtracted (Numerical computing).
Proof that KL is never negative (Jensen's inequality)

Jensen: for a convex function ff (curving upwards, like −log⁡u-\log u), 𝔼[f(U)]≥f(𝔼[U])\mathbb{E}[f(U)] \ge f(\mathbb{E}[U]); the chord between two points of a bowl lies above the curve, and equality needs UU constant. The same proof as MacKay's solution to exercise 2.26:

  1. Let U=q(x)/p(x)U = q(x)/p(x) with x∼px \sim p, and f(u)=−log⁡uf(u) = -\log u. Then DKL(p‖q)=𝔼[f(U)]D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = \mathbb{E}[f(U)].
  2. Jensen: 𝔼[f(U)]≥−log⁡𝔼p[q(x)p(x)]\mathbb{E}[f(U)] \ge -\log \mathbb{E}_p\big[\tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}\big].
  3. 𝔼p[q(x)p(x)]=∑x:p(x)>0q(x)≤1\mathbb{E}_p\big[\tfrac{q(x)}{p(x)}\big] = \sum_{x: p(x) \gt 0} q(x) \le 1, so DKL≥−log⁡(something≤1)≥0D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge -\log(\text{something} \le 1) \ge 0.
  4. Equality needs q/pq/p constant and ∑q=1\sum q = 1 on the support of pp: q=pq = p.

4. Forward against reverse KL: covering every mode or choosing one

The truth pp has two humps, 12𝒩(−2,0.62)+12𝒩(2,0.62)\tfrac12 \mathcal{N}(-2, 0.6^2) + \tfrac12 \mathcal{N}(2, 0.6^2); the model qq is one Gaussian with mean μ\mu and standard deviation σ\sigma. Both fits start at μ=1,σ=1\mu = 1, \sigma = 1 and run gradient descent, the top on the forward KL DKL(p‖q)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q), the bottom on the reverse KL DKL(q‖p)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p). Predict: which one ends up sitting in the valley between the humps?

Forward KL spreads over both humps; reverse KL picks one

A grid of 801 points from −8-8 to 8; gradients by central differences on (μ,ln⁡σ)(\mu, \ln\sigma), step size 0.1, 200 steps; src/recompute.py gives the same end points.

Forward, 𝔼x∼p[log⁡p(x)q(x)]\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\big], averages over the truth: wherever the data has mass and the model has almost none, log⁡pq\log \frac{p}{q} is huge, so the model must put some probability everywhere the data goes, mode-covering (mean-seeking, zero-avoiding). The fit converges to μ=0.009,σ=2.088\mu = 0.009, \sigma = 2.088, which is moment matching: the mean and variance of pp, σ=0.62+22=2.088\sigma = \sqrt{0.6^2 + 2^2} = 2.088. The price is 18.6% of its mass in the valley |x|<0.5|x| \lt 0.5, where the truth has 0.6%; forward KL 0.555 nats, reverse 1.267.

Reverse, 𝔼x∼q[log⁡q(x)p(x)]\mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\big[\log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\big], averages over the model: mass the model puts where the truth has none is punished, and truth the model ignores costs nothing because it is never averaged over. So it picks one hump, mode-seeking (zero-forcing): μ=1.997,σ=0.605\mu = 1.997, \sigma = 0.605, reverse KL 0.692 nats, almost exactly ln⁡2=0.693\ln 2 = 0.693 because on its hump q≈2pq \approx 2p (the hump holds half of pp). Its forward KL is 10.23 nats: it ignores half the data. Started at μ=−1\mu = -1 it lands on the left hump; the reverse objective has a local minimum at each.

Why the forward fit matches moments (for a Gaussian model)

This is maximum likelihood for a Gaussian: the fitted mean and variance are the data's, whatever shape the data has.

Where each direction shows up

  • Maximum likelihood is forward KL. A language model trained on cross-entropy minimises DKL(data‖model)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\text{data} \Vert \text{model}), so it learns to put some probability on everything the data does. That is good for coverage; it is also the usual explanation (an argument, not a measurement) for why pure sampling from a pretrained model wanders into low-probability text, and why decoding tricks such as top-p cut the tail (Sampling and Decoding).
  • Variational inference is reverse KL. Raising the ELBO lowers DKL(q(z)‖p(z|x))D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z) \Vert p(z \mid x)) (section 10), so the approximate posterior hugs one mode and underestimates uncertainty; MacKay's Figure 33.6 shows a reverse-KL fit to a correlated two-dimensional Gaussian coming out too narrow (ITILA ch. 33).
  • RLHF's KL penalty is reverse KL, DKL(π‖πref)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}}), averaged over the policy's own samples: it punishes saying what the reference finds unlikely and does not punish dropping what the reference would say, one mechanism behind the narrower, less diverse outputs of aligned models (section 10).
  • Distillation uses both: forward KL on the teacher's distribution, reverse KL on the student's own samples (section 5).
Choosing a direction
Forward KL when missing a mode is the worse failure (a density model, a speech recogniser that must not rule out a word). Reverse KL when putting mass where the truth has none is the worse failure (a policy that must not say unacceptable things), or when you can sample from your approximation and evaluate the target only up to a constant (variational inference, where p(z|x)p(z \mid x) is known only up to p(x)p(x)).

Distillation read as information theory

A teacher's soft probabilities carry more bits than a one-hot label: the relative sizes of the wrong answers. Distillation trains the student on cross-entropy against the teacher's distribution at a raised temperature, which is the teacher's entropy (fixed) plus the forward KL from teacher to student (removable), exactly the split of section 3. Raising TT raises the teacher's entropy and so how much of that wrong-answer structure the target exposes. 7. The loss floor, and compression as prediction

A language model predicts each token given the ones before it, and the chain rule of entropy (section 8) says the uncertainty of a whole text is exactly the sum of those conditional uncertainties:

H(X1,…,Xn)=∑t=1nH(Xt|X1,…,Xt−1)H(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} H(X_t \mid X_1, \dots, X_{t-1})

That is the shape of the loss: the model pays −log⁡q(xt|x<t)-\log q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}) at each position, and the per-token average over a long text estimates the entropy rate, the uncertainty per symbol once all the past is known. That rate is the floor H(p)H(p) of section 3; a loss that levels off is the KL shrinking towards a floor that is not zero. Context is worth a lot: Shannon measured, for a 26-letter alphabet, F0=log2⁡26=4.70F_0 = \log_2 26 = 4.70 bits per letter with no context, F1=4.14F_1 = 4.14 with letter frequencies and F2=3.56F_2 = 3.56 with pairs, and from people guessing the next letter with 100 letters of context (27 symbols, counting the space) bounded the entropy between about 0.6 and 1.3 bits per letter, "of the order of one bit per letter" ("Prediction and Entropy of Printed English", 1951). Longer context lowers loss because it lowers the conditional entropy a model can reach.

Chinchilla's fit puts a number on the floor: in Lˆ(N,D)=E+A/Nα+B/Dβ\hat L(N, D) = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}, the constant EE, which the paper calls "the entropy of natural text" (Hoffmann et al. 2022, §D.2), is fitted as 1.69 nats per token (2.44 bits; perplexity 5.4) on its own data and tokenizer; a refit of the same form by Besiroglu et al. (2024) gives 1.82 (2.63 bits). Both are extrapolations of a fitted curve to infinite model and data, not measurements, and both are per token under one tokenizer (Chinchilla page). Read the terms as this page's split: A/NαA/N^\alpha and B/DβB/D^\beta are the KL that more parameters and more data remove; EE is the entropy, which nothing removes. The floor of a classifier is the same idea, H(Y|X)H(Y \mid X): zero only when every input has exactly one correct label.

The data processing inequality

A message XX (a fair bit) is copied with a 10% chance of a flip to give YY, and YY copied again the same way to give ZZ. I(X;Y)=1−H(0.1)=1−0.469=0.531I(X; Y) = 1 - H(0.1) = 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 bits; ZZ differs from XX when exactly one flip happens, 2×0.1×0.9=0.182 \times 0.1 \times 0.9 = 0.18, so I(X;Z)=1−H(0.18)=0.320I(X; Z) = 1 - H(0.18) = 0.320 bits. Each copy loses information and none gains it. In general, for a Markov chain X→Y→ZX \to Y \to Z (ZZ computed from YY alone, p(x,y,z)=p(x)p(y|x)p(z|y)p(x, y, z) = p(x)\,p(y \mid x)\,p(z \mid y)):

I(X;Z)≤I(X;Y)I(X; Z) \le I(X; Y)

MacKay: "data processing can only destroy information" (§8.3, exercise 8.9). The layers of a network form such a chain, so a hidden layer cannot carry more information about the label than the input does. Training does not add information; it makes the useful information easier to extract by a simple final layer, which Shannon information does not measure. The same quantity ranks features (information gain in trees is exactly mutual information, section 1), and the information bottleneck asks for a representation that keeps as little as possible about the input while keeping as much as possible about the label (Tishby, Pereira and Bialek 1999).

The ceiling is not a quirk of InfoNCE: McAllester and Stratos (2020) prove that "any distribution-free high-confidence lower bound on mutual information estimated from N samples cannot be larger than O(ln N)". CLIP trains with 32,768 image-text pairs per batch, so each direction is a 32,768-way classification with a ceiling of ln⁡32,768=10.4\ln 32{,}768 = 10.4 nats (15 bits) (Radford et al. 2021). Reading any reported "mutual information" estimate: check the batch size first; a value near ln⁡N\ln N is the ceiling talking. InfoNCE as a training method (SimCLR, MoCo, SimCSE, temperature, collapse) is on Contrastive and self-supervised learning; the CLIP page trains a toy one.

  1. How many samples per possible outcome (or per cell of the joint)? Below a few, plug-in numbers are mostly bias.

  3. For continuous data, how was it binned? The answer changes with the bins.
  4. For a variational bound: what was NN (the batch size)? Is the reported value near ln⁡N\ln N?
  5. In bits or nats?

Which quantity to use when
  • Training a predictor of discrete outcomes (classes, tokens): minimise cross-entropy; it is the negative log-likelihood, and the only part you can change is the KL to the data.
  • Reporting a language model's quality: perplexity only within one tokenizer and one evaluation set; bits per byte (or per character, saying which) across tokenizers.
  • Keeping a fine-tuned model close to its starting point: a reverse KL penalty DKL(π‖πref)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}}); expect it to trade some diversity for staying on the reference's high-probability outputs.
  • Fitting an approximate distribution: forward KL when missing a mode is the worse failure; reverse KL when putting mass where the target has none is, or when only the approximation can be sampled.
  • Measuring dependence: mutual information when it may be nonlinear, with a bias check; correlation only for linear relationships; for high-dimensional variables a bound such as InfoNCE, remembering it cannot exceed ln⁡N\ln N.
  • How confident a prediction is: the entropy of the predicted distribution, 0 for certain and log⁡K\log K for uniform.
  • Comparing two distributions symmetrically, or with a bounded number: Jensen-Shannon (at most ln⁡2\ln 2) or total variation (at most 1).

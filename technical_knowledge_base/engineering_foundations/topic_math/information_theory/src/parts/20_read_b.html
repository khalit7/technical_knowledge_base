Information theory in one screen

About 50 minutes for the whole Reading tab (sections 1 to 6 and 10, the core, about 25); every section can be read on its own. Each formula comes with a plain-words reading and a worked example; every number is recomputed by src/recompute.py.

Information theory measures uncertainty in bits. For machine learning it does one job above all: it says what a log-loss number means. The loss of a classifier or a language model is a cross-entropy: the average length of a code built from the model's predictions. The part of it a better model could remove is a KL divergence; the part no model can remove is the data's own entropy. The same few quantities explain why perplexity cannot be compared across tokenizers while bits per byte can, why maximum likelihood spreads probability while an RLHF-style KL penalty narrows it, and why contrastive losses such as CLIP's want huge batches.

Notation on this page (and how it differs from the root)
This page uses the textbook convention of Cover and Thomas and of MacKay, which most papers follow: \(p\) is the truth (the data distribution) and \(q\) is the model. So cross-entropy is \(H(p, q)\) and the KL the loss reduces is \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\Vert\, q)\). The {{Topic: math root page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} writes the model's softmax output as \(p\) and the target as \(q\), so its \(H(q, p)\) is this page's \(H(p, q)\). The tiny model's bet keeps its numbers, \((0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\); here it plays the role of \(q\) when it is a prediction and of \(p\) when we ask how uncertain it is. When reading a paper, find which letter is the data before reading anything else.

Surprise \(-\log p(x)\)

How surprised you are by an outcome you gave probability \(p(x)\). The tiny model gave the true word "sat" 0.090: \(-\log_2 0.090 = 3.473\) bits (section 1).

Entropy \(H(p) = \mathbb{E}_p[-\log p(x)]\)

Average surprise; also the shortest possible average code length. The model's bet: 1.201 bits. Weather (sun 1/2, cloud 1/4, rain 1/4): 1.5 bits (sections 2, 3).

Cross-entropy \(H(p, q) = \mathbb{E}_p[-\log q(x)]\)

Average surprise when the world follows \(p\) but you bet \(q\); the training loss. Weather coded for the wrong forecast: 1.75 bits (section 5).

KL \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = H(p, q) - H(p)\)

The extra bits paid for betting \(q\); never negative; not symmetric. Weather: \(1.75 - 1.5 = 0.25\) bits (sections 6, 7).

Mutual information \(I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)\)

Bits that knowing \(Y\) saves about \(X\). Two signals that agree 80% of the time: 0.278 bits (section 8). InfoNCE lower-bounds it, but never above \(\ln N\) (section 9).

Perplexity \(e^{\text{CE}}\), bits per byte

The loss as a branching factor (depends on the tokenizer) or as a compression rate (does not). GPT-2 on a Darwin passage: perplexity 30.2 per token, 1.003 bits per byte (section 10).
\[ \underbrace{H(p, q)}_{\text{the loss}} \;=\; \underbrace{H(p)}_{\text{floor}} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\Vert\, q)}_{\text{removable}} \]

In plain words (the floor is the data's own entropy; the removable part is what training can shrink): the loss you see is the unpredictability of the data plus the model's mistakes. Training can only shrink the second term. This one line (derived in section 6) is why minimising cross-entropy is maximum likelihood, why the loss of a language model levels off above zero, and why the answer to "is 2.0 a good loss?" is "compared with what entropy?".

The running examples

  • The tiny model from the {{root page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}}: scores \(z = (2, 1, 0)\) for the words cat, dog, sat; softmax bet \((0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\); the true word is sat, so the loss is \(-\ln 0.090 = 2.408\) nats.
  • The weather (from the old version of this page): truth sun 1/2, cloud 1/4, rain 1/4; a forecaster who swaps sun and cloud.
  • A four-letter source A, B, C, D with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8, encoded with its own code and with a wrong one (the animation in section 6).
  • Real text: a 10,376-byte passage of Darwin's Origin of Species (chapter III, public domain), scored by four small open models and five compressors ({{Real text, measured|#t-real}}).

Symbols: \(\log_2\) gives bits, \(\ln\) gives nats, and 1 nat \(= 1/\ln 2 = 1.443\) bits. \(\mathbb{E}_p[\cdot]\) means "average, weighting each outcome by its probability under \(p\)". \(\sum_x\) runs over every outcome. By convention \(0 \log 0 = 0\).

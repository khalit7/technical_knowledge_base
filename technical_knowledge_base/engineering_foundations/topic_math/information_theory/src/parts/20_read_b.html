1. Surprise and entropy

The tiny model gave the true word "sat" probability 0.090, and the loss came out as 2.408. That number is a surprise (also called self-information): \(-\ln 0.090 = 2.408\) nats, or \(-\log_2 0.090 = 3.473\) bits, as surprised as you would be by three and a half fair coins all landing heads.

\[ s(x) = -\log p(x) \]

A certain outcome surprises you not at all; and for two independent outcomes the probability multiplies, \(p(x)p(y)\), while the surprise should add. Only \(-\log\) turns products into sums (details below), and the base only sets the unit: base 2 gives bits, base \(e\) gives nats, and 1 nat \(= 1.443\) bits, 1 bit \(= \ln 2 = 0.693\) nats. PyTorch, JAX and NumPy use \(\ln\), so a loss printed during training is in nats: 2.0 nats is 2.885 bits, and its perplexity is \(e^{2.0}\), not \(2^{2.0}\). Per-token surprise is also the first thing to read in a loss spike: a token with loss 10 nats is one the model gave about \(e^{-10} = 4.5 \times 10^{-5}\), and a few of those can dominate a batch's average.

Why the logarithm, as Shannon proved it (with his own example checked)

Shannon put the additivity requirement on the average (his third axiom: "if a choice be broken down into two successive choices, the original \(H\) should be the weighted sum of the individual values of \(H\)") and proved that \(-K\sum_i p_i \log p_i\) is the only form satisfying it, the constant \(K\) "merely" choosing the unit ({{Shannon 1948, Theorem 2 and Appendix 2|https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf}}).

His example: choose among \(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16\) at once, or first between two halves and then, half the time, between \(\tfrac23\) and \(\tfrac13\). The final probabilities agree (\(\tfrac12 \times \tfrac23 = \tfrac13\)), so \(H(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16) = H(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) + \tfrac12 H(\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\). In bits: left \(\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac13 \times 1.585 + \tfrac16 \times 2.585 = 1.459\); right \(1 + \tfrac12 \times 0.918 = 1.459\). The \(\tfrac12\) is there because the second choice happens only half the time.

Entropy: the surprise you expect before you look

Weather that is sun half the time and cloud or rain a quarter each surprises you by 1, 2 and 2 bits, so on average by \(\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac14 \times 2 + \tfrac14 \times 2 = 1.5\) bits. The tiny model's bet: \(0.665 \times 0.588 + 0.245 \times 2.031 + 0.090 \times 3.473 = 1.201\) bits (0.832 nats), below the 1.585 bits (\(\log_2 3\)) of a uniform bet over three words. A coin that lands heads 90% of the time: \(0.137 + 0.332 = 0.469\) bits; the rare tail is very surprising (3.32 bits) but rare.

Entropy is steep near certainty and flat near a fair coin

The binary entropy \(H(\theta) = -\theta \log_2 \theta - (1-\theta)\log_2(1-\theta)\) of a coin with heads probability \(\theta\): 0 at the ends, 1 bit at \(\theta = 1/2\). Going from 99% to 95% certain adds more (0.081 to 0.286 bits) than going from 70% to 60% (0.881 to 0.971).

\[ H(p) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[-\log p(x)\big] = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log p(x) \]

Three readings of one number: the average surprise; the shortest possible average length of a message saying which outcome happened (section 2); and the spread of the distribution. \(0 \le H(p) \le \log K\) for \(K\) outcomes, 0 exactly when one outcome is certain and \(\log K\) exactly when all are equally likely (because \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert u) = \log K - H(p) \ge 0\) for the uniform \(u\), section 3). It depends only on the probabilities, not on the names or values of the outcomes, which is why it measures uncertainty and not the size of an error. Choosing a Gaussian or a softmax is choosing the maximum-entropy, least committal distribution that matches what you know (details below).

Where entropy shows up in ML
Exploration in RL: an entropy bonus \(+\alpha H(\pi(\cdot \mid s))\) keeps a policy from collapsing too early (SAC maximises reward plus entropy; {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}). Confidence: the entropy of a predicted distribution is a cheap uncertainty signal, 0 for a certain prediction and \(\log K\) for a uniform one. Decision trees: the information gain of a split is the parent's label entropy minus the children's weighted entropy; ten examples, 5 yes and 5 no (1 bit), split into 4 yes / 1 no and 1 yes / 4 no gives children of \(H(0.8) = 0.722\) bits, a gain of 0.278 bits, exactly the mutual information between split and label (section 8). Sampling: temperature moves the entropy of the next-token distribution (section 5).
Maximum entropy, and why the entropy of a continuous variable is different

Among distributions on the real line with variance \(\sigma^2\), the Gaussian has the largest (differential) entropy, \(\tfrac12 \ln(2\pi e \sigma^2)\), 1.419 nats at \(\sigma = 1\); among distributions over \(K\) classes with a given expected score, the softmax \(q_i \propto e^{z_i}\) has the largest ({{Cover and Thomas, ch. 12|https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Elements+of+Information+Theory%2C+2nd+Edition-p-9780471241959}}).

For a density \(f(x)\), the differential entropy is \(h(f) = -\int f(x) \ln f(x)\, dx\). It is not the limit of the discrete entropy (that limit is infinite: pinning a real number down exactly takes infinitely many bits), it can be negative (a Gaussian with \(\sigma = 0.1\) has \(h = 1.419 + \ln 0.1 = -0.884\) nats), and it changes if you rescale \(x\). Differences of differential entropies, such as KL divergences and mutual information, are finite, non-negative and unchanged by rescaling, which is why ML on continuous data works with them rather than with \(h\).

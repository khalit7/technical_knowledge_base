2. Entropy: the average surprise

The question (you flagged it on the old page). What is entropy, in one sentence, and why does it show up in places as different as decision trees, RL and language-model losses?

The words, then the formula

Before you look, you do not know which outcome will happen, but you know how surprised you would be by each. The entropy of a distribution is that surprise averaged over the outcomes, each weighted by how often it happens:

\[ H(p) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[-\log p(x)\big] = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log p(x) \]
\(x\)
one possible outcome; the sum runs over all of them.
\(p(x)\)
its probability; the weights add up to 1.
\(-\log p(x)\)
its surprise (section 1).
\(x \sim p\)
read "\(x\) drawn from \(p\)"; the expectation \(\mathbb{E}\) is the weighted average.

In plain words: entropy is how uncertain you are before you look, measured as the surprise you expect. Three readings of the same number: (1) the average surprise; (2) the shortest possible average length, in bits, of a message that tells someone which outcome happened (section 3); (3) the spread of the distribution: 0 for a certain outcome, largest when every outcome is equally likely.

Worked examples

Weather: sun 1/2, cloud 1/4, rain 1/4. Surprises 1, 2 and 2 bits; \(H = \tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac14 \times 2 + \tfrac14 \times 2 = 1.5\) bits.

A fair coin: \(H = \tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac12 \times 1 = 1\) bit. A coin that lands heads 90% of the time: \(H = -0.9 \log_2 0.9 - 0.1 \log_2 0.1\) \(= 0.9 \times 0.152 + 0.1 \times 3.322\) \(= 0.137 + 0.332 = 0.469\) bits. The rare outcome is very surprising (3.32 bits) but rare, so the average is low.

The tiny model's bet \((0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\): \(0.665 \times 0.588 + 0.245 \times 2.031 + 0.090 \times 3.473 = 1.201\) bits (0.832 nats). A uniform bet over 3 words would give the maximum, \(\log_2 3 = 1.585\) bits.

The entropy of a coin that lands heads with probability \(\theta\): \(H(\theta) = -\theta \log_2 \theta - (1-\theta)\log_2(1-\theta)\), the binary entropy function. It is 0 at \(\theta = 0\) or 1, peaks at 1 bit at \(\theta = 1/2\), and is steep near the ends: going from 99% to 95% certain adds more entropy (0.081 to 0.286 bits) than going from 70% to 60% (0.881 to 0.971).

Three facts worth knowing by heart

  1. \(0 \le H(p) \le \log K\) for \(K\) outcomes. Zero exactly when one outcome is certain; \(\log K\) exactly when all are equally likely. The upper bound follows from KL being non-negative (section 6): \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert u) = \log K - H(p) \ge 0\) where \(u\) is uniform.
  2. Entropy depends only on the probabilities, not on the names or values of the outcomes. Relabel the words and the entropy is unchanged. That is why it measures uncertainty and not, say, the size of an error.
  3. Maximum entropy. Among all distributions on the real line with a given variance \(\sigma^2\), the Gaussian has the largest (differential) entropy, \(\tfrac12 \ln(2\pi e \sigma^2)\), which is 1.419 nats at \(\sigma = 1\); among all distributions over \(K\) classes with a given expected score, the softmax (Boltzmann) distribution \(q_i \propto e^{z_i}\) has the largest entropy ({{Cover and Thomas, ch. 12|https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Elements+of+Information+Theory%2C+2nd+Edition-p-9780471241959}}). Choosing a Gaussian or a softmax is choosing the least committal distribution that matches what you know.
Optional: entropy of a continuous variable is different

For a density \(f(x)\), the differential entropy is \(h(f) = -\int f(x) \ln f(x)\, dx\). It is not the limit of the discrete entropy (that limit is infinite: pinning a real number down exactly takes infinitely many bits), it can be negative (a Gaussian with \(\sigma = 0.1\) has \(h = 1.419 + \ln 0.1 = -0.884\) nats), and it changes if you rescale \(x\). Differences of differential entropies, such as KL divergences and mutual information, do not have these problems: they are finite, non-negative and unchanged by rescaling. That is why ML on continuous data works with KL and mutual information rather than with \(h\) itself.

What it is used for in ML
Exploration in RL: an entropy bonus \(+\alpha H(\pi(\cdot \mid s))\) keeps a policy from collapsing too early (SAC maximises reward plus entropy; see {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}). Confidence: the entropy of a predicted distribution is 0 for a certain prediction and \(\log K\) for a uniform one; it is a cheap uncertainty signal. Decision trees: the information gain of a split is the parent's label entropy minus the weighted entropy of the children. Ten examples, 5 yes and 5 no (1 bit), split into 4 yes / 1 no and 1 yes / 4 no: each child has \(H(0.8) = 0.722\) bits, so the gain is \(1 - 0.722 = 0.278\) bits, which is exactly the mutual information between the split and the label (section 8). Sampling: temperature changes the entropy of the next-token distribution (the {{Sampling and decoding|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bafcda29a2c7086e}} page measures it on real logits).

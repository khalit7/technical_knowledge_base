12. f-divergences and Jensen-Shannon: KL's family

The question. GAN papers talk about Jensen-Shannon, some RL papers about chi-squared, robustness papers about total variation. Are these different ideas or one?

One idea. Pick any convex function \(f\) with \(f(1) = 0\). The f-divergence of \(p\) from \(q\) averages \(f\) of the likelihood ratio under \(q\):

\[ D_f(p \,\Vert\, q) = \sum_x q(x)\, f\!\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) \]

In plain words: look at how many times more likely each outcome is under \(p\) than under \(q\), push that ratio through a bowl-shaped penalty \(f\) that is 0 when the ratio is 1, and average. Jensen's inequality (section 6) gives \(D_f \ge f\big(\sum_x q \tfrac{p}{q}\big) = f(1) = 0\) every time: all of them are non-negative, and 0 when \(p = q\). This is the definition in {{Nowozin, Cseke and Tomioka 2016 (f-GAN), eq. 2 and Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.00709}}.

Jensen-Shannon in particular

Let \(m = \tfrac12(p + q)\), the half-and-half mixture. Then

\[ \mathrm{JS}(p, q) = \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert m) + \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert m) \]

In plain words: how far each distribution is from their average. Because \(m\) is never 0 where \(p\) or \(q\) is positive, JS is always finite; it is symmetric; and it is at most \(\ln 2\) nats (1 bit), reached when \(p\) and \(q\) do not overlap at all (for \(p = (1, 0)\), \(q = (0, 1)\): \(\tfrac12 \ln 2 + \tfrac12 \ln 2 = 0.693\)). That cap is a weakness as well as a strength: once two distributions stop overlapping, JS stays at \(\ln 2\) however far apart they move, so it gives no gradient telling them to come closer.

GANs. The original GAN objective, at the best discriminator, equals \(2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}, p_{\text{model}}) - \ln 4\) (Goodfellow et al. 2014, as restated in f-GAN's Table 1), so the generator is minimising JS. f-GAN generalises this: any f-divergence can be minimised the same way, using its variational lower bound \(D_f(p \Vert q) \ge \sup_T \big(\mathbb{E}_p[T(x)] - \mathbb{E}_q[f^*(T(x))]\big)\) (their eq. 4), where \(f^*\) is the convex conjugate of \(f\) and the discriminator plays \(T\).

Why they behave alike near agreement. All smooth f-divergences look like the same quadratic when \(q\) is close to \(p\): for a model \(p_\theta\), \(D_f(p_0, p_\theta) \approx \tfrac{f''(1)}{2}\, \theta^\top F \theta\), where \(F\) is the Fisher information ({{Schulman, "Approximating KL Divergence", 2020|http://joschu.net/blog/kl-approx.html}}). For KL, \(f''(1) = 1\). This is why trust regions measured in KL (TRPO, PPO's KL variants) behave like Fisher-weighted step limits, the connection the root's {{"Curvature is read three ways"|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} box makes.

An inequality worth knowing
Pinsker's inequality, \(\mathrm{TV}(p, q) \le \sqrt{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q)/2}\) (KL in nats), says a small KL forces a small total variation, the largest possible difference in the probability of any event. Weather: \(0.25 \le \sqrt{0.173/2} = 0.294\). This is how a KL penalty in RLHF becomes a guarantee about behaviour: no event's probability can move by more than \(\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}/2}\).

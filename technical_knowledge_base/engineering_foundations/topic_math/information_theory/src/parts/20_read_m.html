12. More depth: KL's family, and which quantity to use when

f-divergences and Jensen-Shannon: GAN papers' Jensen-Shannon, chi-squared and total variation are one idea

Pick any convex \(f\) with \(f(1) = 0\). The f-divergence of \(p\) from \(q\) averages \(f\) of the likelihood ratio under \(q\), \(D_f(p \Vert q) = \sum_x q(x)\, f\big(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\big)\), and Jensen gives \(D_f \ge f\big(\sum_x q \tfrac{p}{q}\big) = f(1) = 0\) every time ({{Nowozin, Cseke and Tomioka 2016 (f-GAN), eq. 2 and Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.00709}}).

Weather: \(p\) = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25), \(q\) = (0.25, 0.5, 0.25), recomputed in src/recompute.py from the generators. f-GAN's Table 1 defines Jensen-Shannon with the \(\tfrac12\) but lists the generator \(u \ln u - (u+1)\ln\tfrac{1+u}{2}\) without it, which gives twice the value (0.085 here); the generator above includes the \(\tfrac12\).

Jensen-Shannon, \(\mathrm{JS}(p, q) = \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert m) + \tfrac12 D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert m)\) with \(m = \tfrac12(p + q)\), is how far each distribution is from their average. It is always finite, symmetric and at most \(\ln 2\) nats (1 bit), reached when \(p\) and \(q\) do not overlap (\(p = (1, 0)\), \(q = (0, 1)\): \(\tfrac12 \ln 2 + \tfrac12 \ln 2 = 0.693\)). The cap is also a weakness: once two distributions stop overlapping, JS stays at \(\ln 2\) however far apart they move, so it gives no gradient telling them to come closer.

GANs. The original GAN objective at the best discriminator equals \(2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}, p_{\text{model}}) - \ln 4\) (Goodfellow et al. 2014, as restated in f-GAN's Table 1), so the generator minimises JS; f-GAN minimises any f-divergence through its variational lower bound \(D_f(p \Vert q) \ge \sup_T \big(\mathbb{E}_p[T(x)] - \mathbb{E}_q[f^*(T(x))]\big)\) (eq. 4), with \(f^*\) the convex conjugate and the discriminator playing \(T\).

Near agreement they all look alike: \(D_f(p_0, p_\theta) \approx \tfrac{f''(1)}{2}\, \theta^\top F \theta\), with \(F\) the Fisher information ({{Schulman, "Approximating KL Divergence", 2020|http://joschu.net/blog/kl-approx.html}}); for KL, \(f''(1) = 1\). This is why trust regions measured in KL (TRPO, PPO's KL variants) behave like Fisher-weighted step limits, the connection the root's {{"Curvature is read three ways"|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} box makes. Pinsker's inequality (section 10) on the weather: \(0.25 \le \sqrt{0.173/2} = 0.294\).

Which quantity to use when
  • Training a predictor of discrete outcomes (classes, tokens): minimise cross-entropy; it is the negative log-likelihood, and the only part you can change is the KL to the data.
  • Reporting a language model's quality: perplexity only within one tokenizer and one evaluation set; bits per byte (or per character, saying which) across tokenizers.
  • Keeping a fine-tuned model close to its starting point: a reverse KL penalty \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}})\); expect it to trade some diversity for staying on the reference's high-probability outputs.
  • Fitting an approximate distribution: forward KL when missing a mode is the worse failure; reverse KL when putting mass where the target has none is, or when only the approximation can be sampled.
  • Measuring dependence: mutual information when it may be nonlinear, with a bias check; correlation only for linear relationships; for high-dimensional variables a bound such as InfoNCE, remembering it cannot exceed \(\ln N\).
  • How confident a prediction is: the entropy of the predicted distribution, 0 for certain and \(\log K\) for uniform.
  • Comparing two distributions symmetrically, or with a bounded number: Jensen-Shannon (at most \(\ln 2\)) or total variation (at most 1).

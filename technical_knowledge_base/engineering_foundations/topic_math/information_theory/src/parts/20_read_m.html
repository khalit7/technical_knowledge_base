11. The loss floor, and compression as prediction

The question. A language model's loss levels off around 2 nats per token and never approaches 0. Is that a failure? And in what exact sense is a language model a compressor?

The floor is the data's entropy

Section 6 showed \(H(p, q_\theta) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q_\theta)\). For text, \(p\) is the true distribution of the next token given its context, and \(H(p)\) is the entropy rate of text (section 4): what is left unpredictable however good the model. A loss that levels off is the KL shrinking towards a floor that is not zero. Nobody can measure that floor directly; two estimates:

  • Shannon (1951), from people guessing the next letter: about 0.6 to 1.3 bits per letter with 100 letters of context (section 4).
  • Chinchilla's fit: the scaling law \(\hat L(N, D) = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}\) has a constant \(E\) that the paper calls "the entropy of natural text" (Hoffmann et al. 2022, §D.2), fitted as 1.69 nats per token (2.44 bits; perplexity 5.4) on its own data and tokenizer. A refit of the same form by Besiroglu et al. (2024) gives 1.82 (2.63 bits). Both are extrapolations of a fitted curve to infinite model and data, not measurements, and both are per token under one tokenizer. The {{Chinchilla page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8116b7ddffba5c599f3f}} discusses the fit.

In plain words: the terms \(A/N^\alpha\) and \(B/D^\beta\) of a scaling law are the KL part, the part more parameters and more data remove; \(E\) is the entropy part, which nothing removes.

Compression is prediction: arithmetic coding, animated

Arithmetic coding turns any predictor into a lossless compressor. Start with the interval \([0, 1)\). For each symbol, split the current interval in proportion to the model's probabilities and keep the slice of the symbol that actually occurred. At the end, send just enough bits to name a number inside the final slice. Its width is the product of the probabilities, so the number of bits is about \(-\log_2\) of that product: the total surprise. Here the tiny model's bet, used as a fixed predictor, encodes the message "sat cat cat":

The numbers
Final width \(0.090 \times 0.665 \times 0.665 = 0.0398\); \(-\log_2 0.0398 = 4.65\) bits \(= 3.473 + 0.588 + 0.588\), the sum of the three surprises. A real coder adds at most about two bits for the whole message (MacKay §6.2). A language model plugs in \(q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\) at each step instead of a fixed bet; the decoder runs the same model, so it can rebuild the same intervals. So a model's total loss on a text, in bits, is the size of the file it would compress that text to.

Real text: models against compressors

Bits per byte on the 10,376-byte Darwin passage (lower is better). Compressors are real runs (src/data/comp.py: Apple gzip 487.0.1, bzip2 1.0.8, xz 5.8.2, zstd 1.5.7, brotli 1.1.0, each at its strongest setting); models are total loss in bits divided by bytes. Order-0 Huffman codes each byte on its own, with no context.

The language models compress the passage to about 1 bit per byte or less; the best general-purpose compressor, brotli, needs 2.57, and brotli is already cheating a little: it ships a built-in dictionary of 122,784 bytes of common words and phrases ({{RFC 7932, Appendix A|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7932}}), a tiny frozen prior, the same idea as a pretrained model. Classical compressors learn only from the text in front of them, so they improve with length: on the whole 953,697-byte book, bzip2 reaches 1.92 bits per byte and xz 2.09, still about twice what the models achieve on a 10 KB excerpt. The {{Real text, measured|#t-real}} tab plots this against length.

"Language Modeling Is Compression" (Delétang et al. 2023), checked

{{Delétang et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10668}} (ICLR 2024) feed models' predictions to an arithmetic coder and measure compression on 1 GB of text (enwik9), image patches (ImageNet) and audio (LibriSpeech), each cut into 2,048-byte chunks. Their Table 1, raw rates (compressed size over raw size): Chinchilla 70B compresses enwik9 to 8.3% (0.66 bits per byte), against 32.3% for gzip on the whole file (2.58 bits per byte). On images and audio, which it was not trained on, Chinchilla 70B reaches 48.0% and 21.0%, against PNG's 58.5% (whole file) and FLAC's 30.3% (in chunks).

Correction: the headline numbers are not in the paper's table
The abstract and introduction say Chinchilla 70B "compresses ImageNet patches to 43.4% and LibriSpeech samples to 16.4% of their raw size". Table 1 of the same paper, in both arXiv versions (v1 of 19 September 2023 and v2 of 18 March 2024), gives 48.0% and 21.0% for Chinchilla 70B; we found 43.4% nowhere else in the paper, and 16.4% only as an unrelated cell of Table 2 (a 6.4M-parameter model with a 20K BPE vocabulary on enwik9). The comparison in the abstract also sets the model's chunked rate against PNG's whole-file rate (58.5%; PNG in chunks is 61.7%) and FLAC's chunked rate (30.3%; whole-file 30.9%). The conclusion survives either way: the model beats both domain compressors. The old version of this page quoted the abstract's numbers.

Counting the model. The decoder needs the model too. Delétang et al.'s "adjusted" rate adds the parameters to the compressed size: Chinchilla 70B's enwik9 rate becomes 14,008.3%, and their Figure 2 shows that for each dataset size there is an optimal model size, past which a bigger model compresses worse once its own size is counted. The same arithmetic here: GPT-2 has about 124 million parameters, roughly 250 MB in 16-bit floats, some 24,000 times the size of the 10 KB passage it compresses so well derived. Prediction and compression are the same thing; whether a model is a practical compressor depends on how much data it is amortised over.

Tokenization is compression too. The same paper's Table 2 trains small Transformers on enwik8 with different tokenizers and measures raw rates on enwik9. The best choice depends on model size: at 200K parameters a 20K-token BPE vocabulary wins (19.3% against 22.9% with plain ASCII bytes), at 38M parameters ASCII wins (6.4% against 9.0%). The paper's reading: a bigger vocabulary packs more text into the context but makes each prediction harder.

What it is used for in ML
Reading a loss curve against a floor; the "compression" view of language modelling and of generalisation; minimum description length arguments, where a model plus its residual code is one message; and evaluating models in bits per byte so that tokenizers do not matter (see {{Pretraining|n:3c65c17b0d0d814f82cffd0d5c0dd5ba}}).

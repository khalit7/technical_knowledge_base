4. Forward against reverse KL: covering every mode or choosing one

The truth \(p\) has two humps, \(\tfrac12 \mathcal{N}(-2, 0.6^2) + \tfrac12 \mathcal{N}(2, 0.6^2)\); the model \(q\) is one Gaussian with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\). Both fits start at \(\mu = 1, \sigma = 1\) and run gradient descent, the top on the forward KL \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q)\), the bottom on the reverse KL \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p)\). Predict: which one ends up sitting in the valley between the humps?

Forward KL spreads over both humps; reverse KL picks one

A grid of 801 points from \(-8\) to 8; gradients by central differences on \((\mu, \ln\sigma)\), step size 0.1, 200 steps; src/recompute.py gives the same end points.

Forward, \(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\big]\), averages over the truth: wherever the data has mass and the model has almost none, \(\log \frac{p}{q}\) is huge, so the model must put some probability everywhere the data goes, mode-covering (mean-seeking, zero-avoiding). The fit converges to \(\mu = 0.009, \sigma = 2.088\), which is moment matching: the mean and variance of \(p\), \(\sigma = \sqrt{0.6^2 + 2^2} = 2.088\). The price is 18.6% of its mass in the valley \(|x| \lt 0.5\), where the truth has 0.6%; forward KL 0.555 nats, reverse 1.267.

Reverse, \(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\big[\log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\big]\), averages over the model: mass the model puts where the truth has none is punished, and truth the model ignores costs nothing because it is never averaged over. So it picks one hump, mode-seeking (zero-forcing): \(\mu = 1.997, \sigma = 0.605\), reverse KL 0.692 nats, almost exactly \(\ln 2 = 0.693\) because on its hump \(q \approx 2p\) (the hump holds half of \(p\)). Its forward KL is 10.23 nats: it ignores half the data. Started at \(\mu = -1\) it lands on the left hump; the reverse objective has a local minimum at each.

Why the forward fit matches moments (for a Gaussian model)
  1. \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \Vert q) = -H(p) - \mathbb{E}_p[\ln q(x)]\), and \(H(p)\) does not depend on \(\mu, \sigma\).
  2. \(-\mathbb{E}_p[\ln q] = \tfrac12 \ln(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{\mathbb{E}_p[(x-\mu)^2]}{2\sigma^2}\).
  3. In \(\mu\): \(-\mathbb{E}_p[x - \mu]/\sigma^2 = 0\), so \(\mu = \mathbb{E}_p[x]\). In \(\sigma\): \(\frac{1}{\sigma} - \frac{\mathbb{E}_p[(x-\mu)^2]}{\sigma^3} = 0\), so \(\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}_p(x)\).
This is maximum likelihood for a Gaussian: the fitted mean and variance are the data's, whatever shape the data has.

Where each direction shows up

  • Maximum likelihood is forward KL. A language model trained on cross-entropy minimises \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\text{data} \Vert \text{model})\), so it learns to put some probability on everything the data does. That is good for coverage; it is also the usual explanation (an argument, not a measurement) for why pure sampling from a pretrained model wanders into low-probability text, and why decoding tricks such as top-p cut the tail ({{Sampling and Decoding|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bafcda29a2c7086e}}).
  • Variational inference is reverse KL. Raising the ELBO lowers \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q(z) \Vert p(z \mid x))\) (section 10), so the approximate posterior hugs one mode and underestimates uncertainty; MacKay's Figure 33.6 shows a reverse-KL fit to a correlated two-dimensional Gaussian coming out too narrow ({{ITILA ch. 33|https://www.inference.org.uk/mackay/itila/}}).
  • RLHF's KL penalty is reverse KL, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}})\), averaged over the policy's own samples: it punishes saying what the reference finds unlikely and does not punish dropping what the reference would say, one mechanism behind the narrower, less diverse outputs of aligned models (section 10).
  • Distillation uses both: forward KL on the teacher's distribution, reverse KL on the student's own samples (section 5).
Choosing a direction
Forward KL when missing a mode is the worse failure (a density model, a speech recogniser that must not rule out a word). Reverse KL when putting mass where the truth has none is the worse failure (a policy that must not say unacceptable things), or when you can sample from your approximation and evaluate the target only up to a constant (variational inference, where \(p(z \mid x)\) is known only up to \(p(x)\)).

14. Estimating entropy and mutual information from samples, and the traps

The question. Everything so far assumed you know the distribution. In practice you have samples. Can you just count and plug the frequencies into the formula?

The obvious estimator does exactly that: count, divide by \(N\), and compute \(-\sum \hat p \log \hat p\). It is called the plug-in (or maximum-likelihood) estimator. It is biased downwards: with finite samples, outcomes you have not seen yet count as impossible, and the ones you have seen look more concentrated than they are. For \(m\) possible outcomes and \(N\) samples, the bias is about

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[\hat H_{\text{plug-in}}\big] - H \approx -\frac{m - 1}{2N} \text{ nats} \]

({{Paninski 2003, eq. 4.6|https://www.stat.columbia.edu/~liam/research/pubs/info_est-nc.pdf}}, completing Miller's 1955 result; valid when \(N\) is large compared with \(m\).) The Miller-Madow correction adds \((\hat m - 1)/(2N)\) back, with \(\hat m\) the number of outcomes actually seen.

At \(N = 100\) samples of a 100-outcome distribution the plug-in estimate averages 4.04 nats, 0.56 below the truth (the formula predicts 0.50); at \(N = 25\) it is 1.54 below. The correction helps but cannot invent the outcomes it has not seen.

Mutual information inherits the bias, with a twist

Plugging counts into \(I = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y)\) combines three biased estimates. The joint has many more cells (\(m_X m_Y\)) than the marginals, so it is underestimated the most, and the plug-in mutual information comes out too high: by about \(\frac{(m_X - 1)(m_Y - 1)}{2N}\) nats (the Miller-Madow terms combined; derived here, not quoted). Independent variables look dependent.

Two independent variables, 10 values each (true \(I = 0\))
With 100 samples, two variables that have nothing to do with each other show 0.48 nats (0.69 bits) of "shared information". Reading a plug-in mutual information without its bias is a classic way to find structure in noise.

The text example: memorisation looks like knowledge

The conditional-entropy chart of section 4 is the same trap at scale. Counting which byte follows each context of \(N - 1\) bytes in the 10,376-byte passage, the plug-in estimate falls to 0.18 bits per byte at \(N = 8\), far below the 0.85 that a 0.5B-parameter language model achieves. The counts have not learned English; with about ten thousand positions, most 7-byte contexts occur once, so the "next byte" is always certain in the sample. On the whole book (953,697 bytes) the same estimate falls more slowly (0.81 bits at \(N = 8\)), and it would keep falling with longer contexts. Shannon's 1951 lower bound of about 0.6 bits per letter is the sanity check: a count-based estimate below it on a short sample is the estimator, not the text.

High dimensions: bounds instead of counts

For images, sentences or embeddings, counting is hopeless: every sample is unique. Methods switch to variational bounds learned by a network: InfoNCE (section 9), and others that trade bias against variance ({{Poole et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06922}}: "the existing variational lower bounds degrade when the MI is large, exhibiting either high bias or high variance"). And the general limit of section 9 applies to all of them: without assumptions, no lower bound from \(N\) samples can certify much more than \(\ln N\) ({{McAllester and Stratos 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04251}}).

A checklist for any reported entropy or mutual information
  1. How many samples per possible outcome (or per cell of the joint)? Below a few, plug-in numbers are mostly bias.
  2. Was a bias correction or a shuffled-data baseline used? Shuffle \(Y\) against \(X\) and re-estimate: the shuffled value is the noise floor of "independent".
  3. For continuous data, how was it binned? The answer changes with the bins.
  4. For a variational bound: what was \(N\) (the batch size)? Is the reported value near \(\ln N\)?
  5. In bits or nats?

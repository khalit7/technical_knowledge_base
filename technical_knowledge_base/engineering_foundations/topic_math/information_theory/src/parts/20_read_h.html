7. The loss floor, and compression as prediction

A language model predicts each token given the ones before it, and the chain rule of entropy (section 8) says the uncertainty of a whole text is exactly the sum of those conditional uncertainties:

\[ H(X_1, \dots, X_n) = \sum_{t=1}^{n} H(X_t \mid X_1, \dots, X_{t-1}) \]

That is the shape of the loss: the model pays \(-\log q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\) at each position, and the per-token average over a long text estimates the entropy rate, the uncertainty per symbol once all the past is known. That rate is the floor \(H(p)\) of section 3; a loss that levels off is the KL shrinking towards a floor that is not zero. Context is worth a lot: Shannon measured, for a 26-letter alphabet, \(F_0 = \log_2 26 = 4.70\) bits per letter with no context, \(F_1 = 4.14\) with letter frequencies and \(F_2 = 3.56\) with pairs, and from people guessing the next letter with 100 letters of context (27 symbols, counting the space) bounded the entropy between about 0.6 and 1.3 bits per letter, "of the order of one bit per letter" ({{"Prediction and Entropy of Printed English", 1951|https://www.princeton.edu/~wbialek/rome/refs/shannon_51.pdf}}). Longer context lowers loss because it lowers the conditional entropy a model can reach.

Chinchilla's fit puts a number on the floor: in \(\hat L(N, D) = E + A/N^{\alpha} + B/D^{\beta}\), the constant \(E\), which the paper calls "the entropy of natural text" (Hoffmann et al. 2022, §D.2), is fitted as 1.69 nats per token (2.44 bits; perplexity 5.4) on its own data and tokenizer; a refit of the same form by Besiroglu et al. (2024) gives 1.82 (2.63 bits). Both are extrapolations of a fitted curve to infinite model and data, not measurements, and both are per token under one tokenizer ({{Chinchilla page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8116b7ddffba5c599f3f}}). Read the terms as this page's split: \(A/N^\alpha\) and \(B/D^\beta\) are the KL that more parameters and more data remove; \(E\) is the entropy, which nothing removes. The floor of a classifier is the same idea, \(H(Y \mid X)\): zero only when every input has exactly one correct label.

Compression is prediction: arithmetic coding

Start with \([0, 1)\). For each symbol, split the current interval in proportion to the model's probabilities and keep the slice of the symbol that occurred; at the end, send just enough bits to name a number inside the final slice. Here the tiny model's bet, used as a fixed predictor, encodes "sat cat cat":

The final interval's width is the product of the probabilities, so its bits are the total surprise

Final width \(0.090 \times 0.665 \times 0.665 = 0.0398\), and \(-\log_2 0.0398 = 4.65\) bits \(= 3.473 + 0.588 + 0.588\); a real coder adds at most about two bits for the whole message (MacKay §6.2). A language model plugs in \(q(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\) at each step instead of a fixed bet, and the decoder runs the same model to rebuild the same intervals. So a model's total loss on a text, in bits, is the size of the file it would compress that text to.

Four small language models need about 1 bit per byte or less; the best general compressor needs 2.57

Bits per byte on the 10,376-byte Darwin passage (lower is better). Compressors are real runs (src/data/comp.py: Apple gzip 487.0.1, bzip2 1.0.8, xz 5.8.2, zstd 1.5.7, brotli 1.1.0, each at its strongest setting); models are total loss in bits over bytes. Order-0 Huffman codes each byte on its own, with no context.

The models reach about 1 bit per byte or less; the best general-purpose compressor, brotli, needs 2.57, and brotli already ships a built-in dictionary of 122,784 bytes of common words and phrases ({{RFC 7932, Appendix A|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7932}}), a tiny frozen prior, the same idea as a pretrained model. Classical compressors learn only from the text in front of them, so they improve with length: on the whole 953,697-byte book bzip2 reaches 1.92 bits per byte and xz 2.09, still about twice what the models achieve on a 10 KB excerpt ({{Real text, measured|#t-real}} plots it against length).

"Language Modeling Is Compression" ({{Delétang et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10668}}, ICLR 2024) feeds models' predictions to an arithmetic coder on 1 GB of text (enwik9), image patches (ImageNet) and audio (LibriSpeech), each in 2,048-byte chunks. Table 1, raw rates (compressed over raw size): Chinchilla 70B compresses enwik9 to 8.3% (0.66 bits per byte), against 32.3% for gzip on the whole file (2.58 bits per byte); on images and audio, which it was not trained on, it reaches 48.0% and 21.0%, against PNG's 58.5% (whole file) and FLAC's 30.3% (in chunks).

Counting the model. The decoder needs the model too. Delétang et al.'s "adjusted" rate adds the parameters to the compressed size: Chinchilla 70B's enwik9 rate becomes 14,008.3%, and their Figure 2 shows an optimal model size for each dataset size, past which a bigger model compresses worse once its own size is counted. Here, GPT-2's about 124 million parameters are roughly 250 MB in 16-bit floats, some 24,000 times the 10 KB passage it compresses so well derived. Prediction and compression are the same thing; whether a model is a practical compressor depends on how much data it is amortised over (see also {{Pretraining|n:3c65c17b0d0d814f82cffd0d5c0dd5ba}} for loss curves against data).

Tokenization is compression too (Delétang et al., Table 2)
The paper trains small Transformers on enwik8 with different tokenizers and measures raw rates on enwik9. The best choice depends on model size: at 200K parameters a 20K-token BPE vocabulary wins (19.3% against 22.9% with plain ASCII bytes), at 38M parameters ASCII wins (6.4% against 9.0%). The paper's reading: a bigger vocabulary packs more text into the context but makes each prediction harder.

1. Surprise, and why the logarithm

The question. The tiny model gave the right word probability 0.090 and the loss came out as 2.408. Why take a logarithm of a probability at all, and what is the unit?

The picture first

Think of how surprised you are when something happens. A thing you were sure of (probability 1) surprises you not at all. A thing you thought had a 1 in 2 chance surprises you a little; 1 in 1,000, a lot. And if two unrelated things both happen, your surprise at the pair should be the surprise at the first plus the surprise at the second. Those three wishes fix the formula.

The words, then the formula

The surprise (also called self-information or information content) of an outcome \(x\) that had probability \(p(x)\) is

\[ s(x) = -\log p(x) = \log \frac{1}{p(x)} \]
\(p(x)\)
the probability you gave outcome \(x\) before seeing it, a number between 0 and 1.
\(\log\)
a logarithm; its base sets the unit. Base 2 gives bits, base \(e\) (the natural log \(\ln\)) gives nats.
minus sign
logs of numbers below 1 are negative; the minus makes surprise positive.

In plain words: the surprise of an outcome is the number of halvings it takes to get from certainty down to its probability. A probability of 1/8 is three halvings, so 3 bits.

Why the log, exactly. If \(x\) and \(y\) are independent, the probability that both happen is \(p(x)\,p(y)\). We want the surprise of the pair to be the sum of the two surprises: \(s(x \text{ and } y) = s(x) + s(y)\). The only continuous, decreasing functions that turn products into sums are \(-\log\) to some base: \(-\log\big(p(x)p(y)\big) = -\log p(x) - \log p(y)\). Shannon put the same requirement on the average (his third axiom: "if a choice be broken down into two successive choices, the original \(H\) should be the weighted sum of the individual values of \(H\)") and proved that \(-K\sum_i p_i \log p_i\) is the only form satisfying it, the constant \(K\) "merely" choosing the unit ({{Shannon 1948, Theorem 2 and Appendix 2|https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf}}).

Shannon's own example, checked
Choose among three outcomes with probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16\) at once, or first choose between two halves and then, half the time, between \(\tfrac23\) and \(\tfrac13\). The final probabilities are the same (\(\tfrac12 \times \tfrac23 = \tfrac13\), \(\tfrac12 \times \tfrac13 = \tfrac16\)), so the uncertainty must be too: \[ H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac13, \tfrac16\right) = H\!\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac12\right) + \tfrac12\, H\!\left(\tfrac23, \tfrac13\right) \] In bits: left side \(\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac13 \times 1.585 + \tfrac16 \times 2.585 = 1.459\); right side \(1 + \tfrac12 \times 0.918 = 1.459\). The \(\tfrac12\) is there because the second choice happens only half the time.

Bits and nats

Changing the base of a log multiplies it by a constant: \(\log_2 a = \ln a / \ln 2\). So 1 nat \(= 1/\ln 2 = 1.443\) bits and 1 bit \(= \ln 2 = 0.693\) nats. Nothing else changes. PyTorch, JAX and NumPy use \(\ln\), so a loss printed during training is in nats; papers about compression and evaluation often quote bits.

The loss of 2.408 nats is the surprise at the true word: \(2.408 \times 1.443 = 3.473\) bits, as surprised as you would be by three and a half fair coins all landing heads. Notice the nats column goes up by exactly 1 per row: the scores \(z = (2, 1, 0)\) drop by 1 each, and \(-\ln \mathrm{softmax}(z)_i = \ln \sum_j e^{z_j} - z_i\).
What it is used for in ML
The per-token loss of a language model is the surprise at the true token. A token with loss 10 nats is one the model gave about \(e^{-10} = 4.5 \times 10^{-5}\); a few such tokens can dominate a batch's average. Reading per-token surprise is the first step of debugging a loss spike: find which tokens carry it.

11. Bias and variance of a model, and double descent

The question. Section 2 split an estimator's error into bias and variance. A trained model is an estimator too (of the true function). What does the split say about model size, and why do huge models not overfit the way the split seems to predict?

The training set is itself a random sample, so the fitted model \(\hat f\) is a random function: retrain on a fresh sample and you get a slightly different one. Bias-variance splits the expected error at a point into what is wrong on average and what wobbles from sample to sample. For squared error, with the target \(y = f(x) + \varepsilon\) where the noise \(\varepsilon\) has mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\):

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{f}(x) - y)^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{f}(x) - \mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)])^2\big]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}. \]
\(f(x)\)
the true function at \(x\); \(\hat{f}(x)\) the model's prediction there.
\(\mathbb{E}\)
the average over resampled training sets (and the noise in \(y\)).
\(\text{bias}^2\)
how far the average prediction sits from the truth: the error of the model family's systematic assumptions.
\(\text{variance}\)
how much the prediction scatters around its own average from one training set to another.
\(\sigma^2\)
the noise in the target itself, which no model can remove.
Derivation, every step
  1. Write \(\bar f = \mathbb{E}[\hat f(x)]\) and split: \(\hat f - y = (\hat f - \bar f) + (\bar f - f) - \varepsilon\).
  2. Square: three squares plus three cross terms, \(2(\hat f - \bar f)(\bar f - f)\), \(-2(\hat f - \bar f)\varepsilon\) and \(-2(\bar f - f)\varepsilon\).
  3. Each cross term has a factor with mean zero: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat f - \bar f] = 0\) by definition of \(\bar f\), and \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon] = 0\), with the test noise \(\varepsilon\) independent of the training set that made \(\hat f\). \((\bar f - f)\) is a fixed number. So all three vanish in expectation.
  4. The squares give variance, bias² and \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^2] = \sigma^2\).

The classic picture. As capacity grows, bias falls and variance rises, so test error traces a U. Ensembling attacks variance: averaging \(M\) models whose errors have variance \(\sigma^2\) and pairwise correlation \(\rho\) gives variance \(\rho\sigma^2 + (1 - \rho)\sigma^2/M\) (Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning, 2nd ed., eq. 15.1). With \(M = 10\): \(0.1\sigma^2\) for independent errors, but \(0.55\sigma^2\) at \(\rho = 0.5\); correlation sets the floor, which is why random forests decorrelate their trees. Boosting attacks bias: each new weak learner fits what the ensemble still gets wrong.

Double descent: a real run, decomposed

Belkin et al. (PNAS 2019, arXiv 1812.11118) showed the U is only half the story: "This 'double descent' curve subsumes the textbook U-shaped bias-variance trade-off curve by showing how increasing model capacity beyond the point of interpolation results in improved performance." Test error rises as capacity approaches the interpolation threshold (the size at which the model can first fit the training set exactly, with zero training error), then falls again past it, because among the many models that interpolate, training finds smooth ones.

The smallest setting where this can be watched and checked against an exact formula is Belkin, Hsu and Xu's weak-features regression (SIAM J. Math. Data Science 2020, arXiv 1903.07571): inputs are 100 independent Gaussian features, the target is a linear function of all 100 plus noise (\(\sigma = 0.2\)), but the model sees only the first \(p\) of them and is fitted by least squares on \(n = 40\) examples (the minimum-norm solution once \(p > 40\), which is what gradient descent from zero finds). We trained it on 1,000 independent training sets for every \(p\) from 1 to 100 and split the test error into bias² and variance simulated. The paper's Theorem 1 gives the expected error in closed form (excerpt saved in src/inputs/); our averages reproduce it independently to within 3.2% everywhere at least 5 features from the threshold. At \(p = 39, 40, 41\) the theorem says the expectation is infinite, and our average there is dominated by a few near-singular training sets.

What the run shows. Variance, not bias, makes the peak: at \(p = 40\) the 40-by-40 training matrix is square and its smallest singular value collapses (median 0.086, against 3.55 at \(p = 10\) and 3.95 at \(p = 100\)), so the fit divides noise by almost zero. This is one of the three factors Schaeffer et al. identify in "Double Descent Demystified" (arXiv 2303.14151). Past the threshold, the minimum-norm solution spreads the weight over many directions, and variance falls again. A little ridge (\(\lambda = 0.1\)) cuts the error at \(p = 40\) from about 285,600 to 6.8; Nakkiran et al. (arXiv 2003.01897) prove that "optimally-tuned \(\ell_2\) regularization achieves monotonic test performance as we grow either the sample size or the model size" in this kind of setting. And whether the second descent beats the first depends on the problem: with the signal spread evenly over all 100 features the best error is at \(p = 100\) (0.667, against 1.057 at best below the threshold); when the first features carry most of the signal, the classic sweet spot wins (0.119 at \(p = 14\) against 0.440). The {{Double descent lab|#t-dd}} has all three signals, four ridge strengths, and the singular values.

More data can hurt. The same formula, read along \(n\) at fixed \(p = 60\) with the signal spread evenly: 30 examples give an expected error of 1.195, 55 give 6.54 (now near the threshold \(n = p\)), 80 give 1.83, and only 200 bring it down to 0.63. Nakkiran et al. ("Deep Double Descent", arXiv 1912.02292) found the same in deep networks: "increasing (even quadrupling) the number of train samples actually hurts test performance" in some regimes, and "double descent occurs not just as a function of model size, but also as a function of the number of training epochs".

Checked against the papers
The old notes said "the decomposition still holds; the variance term just behaves non-monotonically". The decomposition is an identity, so it always holds, and in this linear run the variance is indeed what spikes. But that sentence is a simplification as an explanation of double descent in deep networks: Yang et al. (ICML 2020, arXiv 2002.11328) measure that "the bias is monotonically decreasing as in the classical theory, the variance is unimodal or bell-shaped" with width, Nakkiran et al. show epoch-wise and sample-wise versions and propose an "effective model complexity", and no single mechanism is agreed for deep networks (unconfirmed as a single cause).
What it is used for in ML
Reasoning about model size, data size and regularisation together; why ensembles and dropout help (variance); why a model that fits its training set perfectly can still generalise; why weight decay smooths the peak (on {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}, which reproduces the random-features version on real digits).

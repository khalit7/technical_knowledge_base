10. Bayesian estimation: priors, posteriors, conjugacy

What most people want to say is "a 95% probability the true accuracy is in this range" or "B is better with probability 0.9". That needs the unknown to be a random variable with a prior, updated by Bayes' rule into a posterior ({{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}, section 12, derives the coin case). A 95% credible interval holds 95% of the posterior probability: the statement a confidence interval is often misread as making, now true by construction, at the price of choosing a prior.

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior has the same form, so updating is arithmetic. For a heads probability with a Beta prior and \(k\) heads in \(n\) flips,

\[ \text{Beta}(a, b) \;\xrightarrow{\;k \text{ of } n\;}\; \text{Beta}(a + k,\ b + n - k), \qquad \text{posterior mean} = \frac{a + k}{a + b + n}, \]

so the prior acts like \(a\) heads and \(b\) tails seen before the data (Gamma for Poisson rates and normal for normal means are the other common pairs).

With 10 flips the prior still moves the answer; the data pull every prior towards 0.9

Dashed: the prior; solid: the posterior after 9 heads in 10; shaded: its 95% equal-tailed credible interval. exact Uniform: Beta(10, 2), mean \(10/12 = 0.833\), interval (0.587, 0.977), \(P(q > 0.5 \mid \text{data}) = 0.994\). Sceptical Beta(5, 5), as if 5 heads and 5 tails were already seen: Beta(14, 6), mean 0.700, interval (0.488, 0.874), \(P(q > 0.5) = 0.968\). Jeffreys: Beta(9.5, 1.5), mean 0.864, interval (0.619, 0.989).

Bayes factors compare two hypotheses by how well each predicted the data, \(\mathrm{BF}_{10} = P(\text{data} \mid H_1)/P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\), each likelihood averaged over that hypothesis's prior. Fair coin: \(P(9 \text{ of } 10) = 10/1024 = 0.00977\); any bias, uniform: \(\int_0^1 10\,q^9(1-q)\,dq = 10 \times \frac{9!\,1!}{11!} = 1/11 = 0.0909\). So \(\mathrm{BF}_{10} = 9.31\), and with even prior odds the posterior probability of a fair coin is \(1/(1 + 9.31) = 0.097\): about 10%, not the 2.1% a misread p-value suggests. Kass and Raftery (JASA 90, 1995) call factors from 3 to 20 "positive" evidence. exact

Credible intervals from weak priors match confidence intervals closely once \(n\) is large; they differ, usefully, when \(n\) is small or the parameter is near a boundary (accuracy near 0% or 100%). In ML: small evals of tens of items, A/B tests reported as "probability B beats A", Thompson sampling ({{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}) and Bayesian optimisation of hyperparameters. Beta-Bernoulli intervals for small evals and their exact coverage are in section 1 and the Small-n intervals tab of {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}.

Two cautions about Bayes factors (depth)

Bayes factors depend strongly on the prior under \(H_1\) (a very wide prior spreads its bets and predicts the data poorly), and with huge \(n\) a tiny p-value can come with a Bayes factor favouring \(H_0\) (Lindley's paradox, 1957).

11. Regression as statistics: least squares, standard errors, ridge as a prior

Fit \(y = w_0 + w_1 x + \varepsilon\) with Gaussian noise \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). The log-likelihood is \(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - w_0 - w_1 x_i)^2\) plus a constant, so least squares is the MLE. Because the fitted weights are a linear function of the noisy \(y\), their sampling distribution is exact for Gaussian noise:

\[ \hat w = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y, \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(\hat w) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}, \qquad \mathrm{SE}(\hat w_1) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}, \quad s^2 = \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n - 2}, \]

with \(S_{xx} = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2\) and \(n - 2\) because two parameters were fitted (the degrees-of-freedom idea of section 3 again). Worked example illustrative: the eight numbers of section 3 as \(y\) against \(x = 1, \dots, 8\). \(S_{xx} = 42\), \(S_{xy} = 34\), slope \(\hat w_1 = 34/42 = 0.8095\), intercept 1.357, residual sum of squares 4.476, \(s = \sqrt{4.476/6} = 0.864\), \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat w_1) = 0.864/\sqrt{42} = 0.1333\). The t-test of "no slope" gives \(t = 6.07\) on 6 degrees of freedom (\(p = 0.0009\)), and the 95% interval is \(0.8095 \pm 2.447 \times 0.1333 = (0.483, 1.136)\).

Ridge is a Gaussian prior. Give the slope a prior \(w_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\); the MAP estimate minimises \(\sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2 + \lambda w_1^2\) with \(\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2\) (the MAP derivation is {{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}, section 11). With centred \(x\) and the intercept left unpenalised, the slope becomes \(S_{xy}/(S_{xx} + \lambda)\).

A stronger prior pulls the slope towards zero: bias bought for variance

Dots: the eight points; dashed: least squares; solid: ridge. The equivalent prior standard deviation is \(\tau = s/\sqrt{\lambda}\), taking \(s\) as the noise level. \(\lambda = 1\): slope 0.7907 (\(\tau = 0.864\)); \(\lambda = 10\): 0.6538 (\(\tau = 0.273\)); \(\lambda = 42 = S_{xx}\): 0.4048, exactly half, with \(\tau = 0.133\), the size of the slope's own standard error. exact

This is the shrinkage of section 1 in a model: the penalty adds bias and removes variance, and \(\lambda\) is chosen by cross-validation (section 14) or, in the Bayesian reading, by the prior. Weight decay, dropout and early stopping as variance control are on {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}; linear and logistic regression in practice are on {{Classical ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ffb886ff30176bc6ce}}.

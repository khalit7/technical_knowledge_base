The idea in one picture

Every number computed from data is one draw of a random variable. Statistics measures how far it would move on another sample of the same size (its standard error), and asks whether a difference is bigger than that wobble.

One sample's average is one dot from a bell whose width is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\)

1: the population, the tiny model's loss on one sampled word: 0.408, 1.408 or 2.408 nats with probabilities 0.665, 0.245 and 0.090; mean \(\mu\) = 0.832, standard deviation \(\sigma\) = 0.651. 2: the averages of 20,000 samples of 30 words simulated: three spikes have become a bell centred on 0.832 with width 0.651/\(\sqrt{30}\) = 0.119, the standard error. 3: what you actually have: one sample (mean 0.775) and the interval built from it, (0.525, 1.024), which here catches the truth. Every section below is one of these arrows: estimators and the CLT explain panel 2, the bootstrap measures it when no formula does, intervals and tests read panel 3.

The running example is the one from {{Topic: math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}}: a tiny language model whose bet for the word after "the cat" is \(p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\) over cat, dog, sat. Its average loss is its entropy, 0.832 nats, and here (unlike in real life) we know it exactly, so we can count how often each statistical tool recovers it. simulated marks numbers from this page's seeded simulations (src/sims.py); exact marks numbers computed exactly.

You need: random variables, expectation and variance, the normal and Beta distributions, and Bayes' rule, from {{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}} (its sections 4, 6 and 12). This page uses them without re-deriving them.

Estimator: a recipe from sample to guess, \(\hat\theta\)
Standard error: the standard deviation of an estimate over samples
Bias, variance, MSE: off-centre, scatter, and their sum
Fisher information: curvature of the log-likelihood; sets the MLE's error bar
Confidence interval: a procedure that catches the truth 95% of the time
Bootstrap: resample your sample to see the wobble
p-value: how surprising the data are if nothing is going on
Power: the chance a test detects a real effect
FWER, FDR: error rates for many tests at once
Credible interval, conjugate prior: the Bayesian versions

6. The bootstrap: resample your sample

The question. The \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) formula is for means. What is the error bar on a median, an F1 score, a ratio of two losses, a pass@k curve, or the entropy estimate of section 2, where no formula is at hand?

The ideal experiment is to draw many fresh samples from the population and watch the statistic vary. We cannot, because we have only one sample. Efron's idea (Annals of Statistics 7, 1979): treat the sample as if it were the population. Draw \(n\) points with replacement from your \(n\) points (some appear twice, some not at all), recompute the statistic, repeat \(B\) times (a few thousand). The spread of the \(B\) recomputed values estimates the standard error, and their percentiles give an interval.

\[ \hat\theta^{*}_b = s\big(x^{*}_{b,1}, \dots, x^{*}_{b,n}\big), \quad x^{*}_{b,i} \text{ drawn uniformly from } \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\text{boot}} = \operatorname{sd}\big(\hat\theta^{*}_1, \dots, \hat\theta^{*}_B\big). \]
\(s(\cdot)\)
the statistic: any recipe, as long as it is computed the same way on the resample.
\(x^{*}\)
a star marks a bootstrap quantity.

In plain words: the bootstrap asks "if the world looked exactly like my sample, how much would my number wobble?" It works when the sample is a good enough picture of the population for that question.

Why it works. The resample is to the sample what the sample is to the population: the same sampling process, one level down. If the statistic's wobble depends on the population only through features the sample reproduces well (its centre, spread and shape, for smooth statistics like means and their functions), the two wobbles match. Each resample leaves out \((1 - 1/n)^n\) of the points on average: 36.2% at \(n = 30\), tending to \(e^{-1} = 36.8\%\). There are \(\binom{2n-1}{n}\) distinct resamples (10 at \(n = 3\); about \(5.9 \times 10^{16}\) at \(n = 30\)), so we sample them rather than list them.

The figure compares the two worlds simulated. Truth: 20,000 fresh samples from the population (impossible in practice). Bootstrap: 4,000 resamples of one sample. On the tiny model with \(n = 30\) they agree: bootstrap spread 0.122 against the true 0.119. On a lognormal with \(n = 10\) the one sample happened to contain no large value (its \(s\) is 0.70 against a population \(\sigma\) of 2.16), so the bootstrap thinks the spread is 0.21 when it is 0.70. The bootstrap cannot see what the sample never contained.

From resamples to an interval: percentile against BCa

  • Percentile: the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the \(\hat\theta^*\). Simple, never leaves the range of possible values, but inherits any bias and too-narrow spread of the bootstrap distribution.
  • BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated; Efron, JASA 82, 1987): use shifted percentiles \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2\) instead of 2.5% and 97.5%: \[ \alpha_1 = \Phi\!\Big(\hat z_0 + \frac{\hat z_0 + z_{0.025}}{1 - \hat a(\hat z_0 + z_{0.025})}\Big), \] \[ \hat z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{\#\{\hat\theta^*_b < \hat\theta\}}{B}\Big), \] \[ \hat a = \frac{\sum_i (\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^3}{6\big[\sum_i (\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^2\big]^{3/2}}, \] and \(\alpha_2\) the same with \(z_{0.975}\). Here \(\hat z_0\) measures the median bias of the bootstrap distribution (0 if half the resamples fall below \(\hat\theta\)), \(\hat\theta_{(i)}\) is the statistic with point \(i\) left out (the jackknife) and \(\bar\theta_{(\cdot)}\) their average, and \(\hat a\) (the acceleration) measures how the standard error changes with the parameter, which is what skew does. BCa's coverage error shrinks like \(1/n\) rather than the percentile's \(1/\sqrt{n}\) (Efron and Tibshirani, An Introduction to the Bootstrap, 1993, chapters 14 and 22).
The numbers simulated
Tiny model, the \(n = 30\) sample above (22 cat, 5 dog, 3 sat; mean 0.775; truth 0.832): t-interval (0.525, 1.024); percentile (0.541, 1.041); BCa (0.575, 1.108), shifted right because the population's tail is to the right. Lognormal, \(n = 10\) (truth 1.649): t (0.668, 1.665) just catches it, percentile (0.792, 1.598) misses, BCa (0.848, 1.756) catches it. Over 5,000 repetitions on the lognormal at \(n = 30\), coverage is 88.6% (t), 88.3% (percentile) and 89.4% (BCa): BCa helps, but nothing reaches 95% there.

When the bootstrap struggles. Small \(n\): at \(n = 5\) from a normal population the percentile interval covers only 83.0% (t: 94.9%), because a resample's spread is the divide-by-\(n\) spread and there is no t-correction. Heavy symmetric tails: on a t-distribution with 3 degrees of freedom at \(n = 10\), BCa covers 86.4% against t's 95.9%, because one outlier swings the jackknife acceleration. Extremes (the maximum, the minimum) and dependent data (time series, items in clusters) need other schemes: the \(m\)-out-of-\(n\) bootstrap, block or cluster resampling.

Bias, too. The bootstrap estimates bias as the average of \(\hat\theta^*\) minus \(\hat\theta\). On the tiny-model sample the plug-in entropy is 0.756; the resamples average 0.718, so the estimated bias is \(-0.039\) (the exact bias at \(n = 30\) is \(-0.036\), section 2), and the corrected estimate is \(2 \times 0.756 - 0.718 = 0.795\).

The paired bootstrap. To compare models A and B scored on the same examples, keep each example's pair of scores together. Repeat many times (a few thousand is typical): draw \(n\) examples with replacement from the eval set, and compute A's score minus B's on that resample. The spread of those differences is the uncertainty of the delta (its 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a 95% interval), and the fraction of resamples in which A beats B says how often the ordering survives resampling. Resampling examples, not scores, is what keeps the shared difficulty of each example in both columns, so it cancels in the difference. It is the robust default for LLM evals with a few hundred to a few thousand examples (Koehn 2004 popularised it for machine translation).

What it is used for in ML
Intervals for metrics with no formula (F1, BLEU, AUC, pass@k, calibration error); paired comparisons of two models; bagging (bootstrap aggregating: train on resamples and average, which is variance reduction, section 11); out-of-bag error in random forests (the 36.8% left out are a free validation set).

6. The bootstrap: resample your sample

\(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) is for means. What is the error bar on a median, an F1 score, a ratio of losses, a pass@k curve, or the entropy estimate of section 1? The ideal is to draw many fresh samples and watch the statistic vary; we have one sample. Efron's bootstrap (Annals of Statistics 7, 1979) treats the sample as the population: draw \(n\) points with replacement from your \(n\) (some twice, some not at all), recompute the statistic, repeat \(B\) times (a few thousand). The spread of the \(B\) values estimates the standard error; their percentiles give an interval.

\[ \hat\theta^{*}_b = s\big(x^{*}_{b,1}, \dots, x^{*}_{b,n}\big), \quad x^{*}_{b,i} \text{ drawn uniformly from } \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}, \qquad \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}_{\text{boot}} = \operatorname{sd}\big(\hat\theta^{*}_1, \dots, \hat\theta^{*}_B\big), \]

with \(s(\cdot)\) any recipe computed the same way on the resample, and a star marking a bootstrap quantity. It works because the resample is to the sample what the sample is to the population: if the statistic's wobble depends on the population only through features the sample reproduces well (centre, spread and shape, for smooth statistics), the two wobbles match. Each resample leaves out \((1 - 1/n)^n\) of the points on average: 36.2% at \(n = 30\), tending to \(e^{-1} = 36.8\%\).

From resamples to an interval. The percentile interval takes the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the \(\hat\theta^*\): simple, never leaves the range of possible values, but inherits any bias and narrowness of the bootstrap distribution. BCa (bias-corrected and accelerated; Efron, JASA 82, 1987) shifts those percentiles to correct for median bias and for skew, and its coverage error shrinks like \(1/n\) rather than \(1/\sqrt{n}\) (Efron and Tibshirani, An Introduction to the Bootstrap, 1993, chapters 14 and 22). Over 5,000 repetitions on the lognormal at \(n = 30\), coverage is 88.6% (t), 88.3% (percentile) and 89.4% (BCa): BCa helps, but nothing reaches 95% there. simulated

When it struggles. Small \(n\): at \(n = 5\) from a normal population the percentile interval covers only 83.0% (t: 94.9%), because a resample's spread is the divide-by-\(n\) spread with no t-correction. Heavy symmetric tails: on a t-distribution with 3 degrees of freedom at \(n = 10\), BCa covers 86.4% against t's 95.9%, because one outlier swings the jackknife acceleration. Extremes (the maximum) and dependent data (time series, items in clusters) need other schemes: the \(m\)-out-of-\(n\) bootstrap, block or cluster resampling.

The paired bootstrap. To compare models A and B scored on the same examples, resample examples, keeping each example's pair of scores together, and compute A minus B on each resample. The spread of those differences is the uncertainty of the delta, and the fraction of resamples in which A wins says how often the ordering survives. Keeping the pair together keeps each example's shared difficulty in both columns, so it cancels. It is the robust default for LLM evals with a few hundred to a few thousand examples (Koehn 2004 popularised it for machine translation); the paired and cluster bootstrap on real model comparisons are sections 4 and 5 of {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}. Bagging (train on resamples and average: variance reduction; ensembles are on {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}, section 1) and the out-of-bag error of random forests (the 36.8% left out are a free validation set) are the same resampling.

BCa uses shifted percentiles \(\alpha_1, \alpha_2\) instead of 2.5% and 97.5%:

\[ \alpha_1 = \Phi\!\Big(\hat z_0 + \frac{\hat z_0 + z_{0.025}}{1 - \hat a(\hat z_0 + z_{0.025})}\Big), \] \[ \hat z_0 = \Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\frac{\#\{\hat\theta^*_b < \hat\theta\}}{B}\Big), \] \[ \hat a = \frac{\sum_i (\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^3}{6\big[\sum_i (\bar\theta_{(\cdot)} - \hat\theta_{(i)})^2\big]^{3/2}}, \]

and \(\alpha_2\) the same with \(z_{0.975}\). \(\hat z_0\) measures the median bias of the bootstrap distribution (0 if half the resamples fall below \(\hat\theta\)); \(\hat\theta_{(i)}\) is the statistic with point \(i\) left out (the jackknife) and \(\bar\theta_{(\cdot)}\) their average; \(\hat a\), the acceleration, measures how the standard error changes with the parameter, which is what skew does.

Bias. The bootstrap estimates bias as the average of \(\hat\theta^*\) minus \(\hat\theta\). On the tiny-model sample the plug-in entropy is 0.756; the resamples average 0.718, so the estimated bias is \(-0.039\) (the exact bias at \(n = 30\) is \(-0.036\), section 1), and the corrected estimate is \(2 \times 0.756 - 0.718 = 0.795\).

Counting. There are \(\binom{2n-1}{n}\) distinct resamples (10 at \(n = 3\); about \(5.9 \times 10^{16}\) at \(n = 30\)), so we sample them rather than list them.

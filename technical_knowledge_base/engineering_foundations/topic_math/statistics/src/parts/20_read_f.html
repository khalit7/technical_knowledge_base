7. Tests, p-values, power, and A/B tests

A coin lands heads 9 times in 10. Biased, or a fair coin that got lucky? Every "B beats A" is the same question. A test is a proof by near-contradiction: state a null hypothesis \(H_0\) (no effect: the coin is fair), choose a test statistic that grows with evidence against it (heads far from 5), work out its distribution if \(H_0\) were true, and ask how surprising the observed value is. The p-value is the probability, under \(H_0\), of a result at least as extreme as the one observed.

The p-value is the tail mass of the null distribution beyond what you saw

Heads in 10 flips of a fair coin, exact. \(P(\ge 9) = (\binom{10}{9} + \binom{10}{10})/2^{10} = 11/1024 = 0.011\); counting the equally extreme "at most 1 head" too gives the two-sided \(p = 22/1024 = 0.021\). exact

Reject \(H_0\) when \(p < \alpha\), the significance level (conventionally 0.05). Two mistakes are possible: a Type I error (false positive; rate \(\alpha\) when \(H_0\) is true) and a Type II error (false negative; rate \(\beta\) when there is a real effect). Power \(1 - \beta\) is how often a real effect is found; you set \(\alpha\), while power depends on the effect size, the noise and \(n\). A two-sided test at level \(\alpha\) rejects "\(\theta = \theta_0\)" exactly when \(\theta_0\) lies outside the \((1 - \alpha)\) confidence interval built from the same statistic, which is why the {{CI and test lab|#t-lab}} computes power from intervals.

Power and sample size. For a two-sided z-test of a mean shift \(\delta\) with noise \(\sigma\), power \(1 - \beta\) needs

\[ n = \Big(\frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})\,\sigma}{\delta}\Big)^2. \]

Power is the part of the "real effect" curve beyond the rejection line

The test statistic under "no effect" (grey) and under a real shift of half a standard deviation (blue), \(\alpha = 0.05\) two-sided. Predict how large \(n\) must be for 80% power, then drag. With \(z_{0.975} = 1.96\) and \(z_{0.8} = 0.8416\), \(n = ((1.96 + 0.8416)/0.5)^2 = 31.4\), so 32 observations (power 80.7%); with 30 the power is 78.2%, with 10 it is 35.3%. Halving the effect quadruples \(n\). exact

A/B tests. An online A/B test is this test on two proportions with each user randomised to one arm. To detect a lift from a 10% to an 11% conversion rate at \(\alpha = 0.05\) with 80% power, each arm needs \[ n = \frac{(z_{0.975} + z_{0.8})^2\,[p_1(1 - p_1) + p_2(1 - p_2)]}{(p_2 - p_1)^2} = \frac{7.849 \times (0.09 + 0.0979)}{0.01^2}, \] so 14,749 users per arm; a half-point lift needs 57,760. exact (derived from the formula; sample sizes are rounded up). Small lifts on low base rates are expensive. Peeking breaks the test. Testing as the data arrive and stopping at the first "significant" result makes each look another test. Our simulation: 200,000 runs with no real effect, up to 100 observations, a z-test at 5% after every \(k\)-th observation, stopping at the first rejection. simulated

Ten looks almost quadruple the nominal 5% (19.4%); checking after every observation gives 37.6%. Armitage, McPherson and Rowe (JRSS A 132, 1969) worked this out for repeated significance tests (their tables are not re-fetched here). Fixes: decide \(n\) in advance; use a sequential design whose boundaries spend \(\alpha\) across looks (Pocock, O'Brien-Fleming); or use always-valid sequential tests. A dashboard with a live p-value invites exactly this mistake.

What a p-value is not

The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement (Wasserstein and Lazar, The American Statistician 70; principles quoted from the ASA's release, saved in src/inputs/) lists six principles; three are about misreadings: "2. P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone." "5. A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result." "6. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis."

So \(p = 0.021\) is not "a 2.1% chance the coin is fair" (that needs a prior; section 10 computes 9.7% under even prior odds), and \(1 - p\) is not the probability the effect is real. A large \(p\) is not evidence of no effect: a test with low power usually fails to reject a real effect (30 observations find a half-standard-deviation shift only 78% of the time), so report the interval, which shows which effects the data rule out. A tiny \(p\) on a huge eval can come from a trivial effect.

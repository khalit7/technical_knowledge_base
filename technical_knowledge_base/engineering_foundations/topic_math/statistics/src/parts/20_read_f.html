7. Hypothesis testing: the null, the p-value, errors and power

The question. A coin lands heads 9 times in 10. Is it biased, or did a fair coin get lucky? The same question is asked of every "B beats A".

A test is a proof by near-contradiction. State a null hypothesis \(H_0\) (no effect: the coin is fair, the two models are equally accurate) and an alternative \(H_1\). Choose a test statistic that grows with evidence against \(H_0\) (here the number of heads, far from 5). Work out its distribution if \(H_0\) were true, the null distribution. Then ask how surprising the observed value is under it.

p-value: the probability, under \(H_0\), of a result at least as extreme as the one observed.

Worked example exact
Under a fair coin, \(P(\ge 9 \text{ heads}) = \big(\binom{10}{9} + \binom{10}{10}\big)/2^{10} = 11/1024 = 0.011\). Counting the equally extreme "at most 1 head" too gives the two-sided p-value \(22/1024 = 0.021\).

Reject \(H_0\) when \(p < \alpha\), the significance level, conventionally 0.05. Two kinds of mistake are possible:

\(H_0\) true (no effect)\(H_0\) false (real effect)
RejectType I error, false positive; rate \(\alpha\)correct; rate = power \(1 - \beta\)
Do not rejectcorrect; rate \(1 - \alpha\)Type II error, false negative; rate \(\beta\)

In plain words: \(\alpha\) is how often you cry wolf when there is none; power is how often you spot the wolf when it is there. You set \(\alpha\); power depends on the effect size, the noise and \(n\).

Tests and intervals are one idea. A two-sided test at level \(\alpha\) rejects "\(\theta = \theta_0\)" exactly when \(\theta_0\) lies outside the \((1 - \alpha)\) confidence interval built from the same statistic. That is why the {{CI and test lab|#t-lab}} computes power from intervals: the power to detect a shift of \(h\) standard deviations is the fraction of intervals that exclude \(\mu + h\sigma\).

Power and sample size. For a two-sided z-test of a mean shift \(\delta\) with noise \(\sigma\), power \(1 - \beta\) needs

\[ n = \Big(\frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})\,\sigma}{\delta}\Big)^2. \]
The numbers exact
To detect a shift of half a standard deviation (\(\delta = 0.5\sigma\)) with \(\alpha = 0.05\) and 80% power: \(z_{0.975} = 1.96\), \(z_{0.8} = 0.8416\), \(n = ((1.96 + 0.8416)/0.5)^2 = 31.4\), so 32 observations. With 30 the power is 78.2%. Halving the effect quadruples \(n\).
What a p-value is not

The American Statistical Association's 2016 statement (Wasserstein and Lazar, The American Statistician 70; principles quoted from the ASA's release, saved in src/inputs/) lists six principles; three are about misreadings: "2. P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the probability that the data were produced by random chance alone." "5. A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result." "6. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis."

So: \(p = 0.021\) is not "a 2.1% chance the coin is fair" (that needs a prior; section 13 computes it as 9.7% under even prior odds), and \(1 - p\) is not the probability the effect is real. A large \(p\) is not evidence of no effect; it may be a small test. A tiny \(p\) on a huge eval can come from a trivial effect.

What it is used for in ML
Deciding whether a new checkpoint, prompt or feature really helps; A/B tests in production; ablations; planning how large an eval or an experiment must be before running it.

8. The workhorse tests: t-tests, McNemar, permutation

The question. Which test fits which data, and why does scoring two models on the same examples matter so much?

The t-test. To test "mean = \(\mu_0\)", divide the distance by its standard error:

\[ t = \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad \text{compared with Student's } t_{n-1}. \]

The paired t-test is the same test on per-example differences \(d_i = a_i - b_i\) with \(\mu_0 = 0\). For two independent groups, Welch's t-test uses \((\bar x_1 - \bar x_2)/\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}\), which does not assume equal variances.

Why pairing matters. Both models are scored on the same examples, and most of the variation in a score comes from which examples are easy or hard, which both models share. A paired test looks only at per-example differences, so the shared difficulty cancels. Comparing two separate averages carries that difficulty noise twice.

McNemar's test (McNemar 1947) is the paired test for right/wrong scores. It uses only the discordant counts: \(b\) examples where model A is right and B wrong, and \(c\) where B is right and A wrong. Under \(H_0\) each discordant example is equally likely to fall either way, and

\[ \chi^2 = \frac{(b - c)^2}{b + c}, \]

compared with a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom (5% critical value 3.84). Some texts subtract 1 from \(|b - c|\) before squaring (a continuity correction), which is slightly more conservative; with few discordant examples, use the exact version: \(b\) is Binomial\((b + c, \tfrac12)\) under \(H_0\). Examples where both models agree carry no information about which is better and drop out.

Worked example exact
On 500 examples, A beats B on \(b = 30\) and B beats A on \(c = 25\), so A leads by \(5/500 = 1\) point. \(\chi^2 = 25/55 = 0.45\), \(p = 0.50\): no evidence of a difference. The paired standard error of the difference is \(\sqrt{(b + c) - (b - c)^2/n}\,/\,n = \sqrt{54.95}/500 = 0.0148\), a 95% interval of \(\pm 1.96 \times 0.0148 = \pm 0.029\), about \(\pm 3\) points. Unpaired, treating the two scores as independent with accuracies near 0.5, it would be \(\pm 1.96\sqrt{2 \times 0.25/500} = \pm 0.062\), about \(\pm 6.2\) points: pairing halves the error bar, but still cannot rescue a 1-point claim. With \(b = 30\), \(c = 15\) (a 3-point lead), \(\chi^2 = 225/45 = 5.0 > 3.84\), \(p = 0.025\): significant at 0.05 (4.36 and \(p = 0.037\) with the continuity correction; the exact binomial version gives \(p = 0.036\)).

Permutation tests need no distribution at all, only a symmetry of the null (R. A. Fisher, The Design of Experiments, 1935). If A and B are equally good, each per-example difference was as likely to come out \(+d_i\) as \(-d_i\). So flip the signs in every possible way, recompute the mean each time, and count how often it is as extreme as the observed one. The p-value is exact at any \(n\).

Worked example exact
Eight per-example loss improvements \((0.31, -0.12, 0.25, 0.40, 0.05, 0.18, -0.07, 0.22)\) illustrative have mean 0.1525. Of the \(2^8 = 256\) sign patterns, 16 give a mean at least that far from zero, so \(p = 16/256 = 0.0625\). The paired t-test on the same numbers gives \(t = 2.353\), \(p = 0.051\): close, but the t-test leaned on normality that eight numbers cannot confirm. With large \(n\), sample a few thousand random sign patterns instead of listing all.

Rank tests (Wilcoxon's signed-rank for pairs, Mann-Whitney for two groups) are the classic robust choice for ordinal or heavy-tailed scores; they test a shift in location rather than the mean.

What it is used for in ML
Comparing two models on one eval (McNemar, paired t, paired bootstrap, permutation); comparing training runs across seeds (Welch with few seeds, or better, more seeds); checking that an ablation's effect is not noise.

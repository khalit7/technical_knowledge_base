4. Where the central limit theorem holds, and where it fails

{{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}} (section 4) states and simulates the two theorems: the law of large numbers (an average of i.i.d. draws converges to \(\mu\)) and the central limit theorem (for finite variance the average is close to \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)\) whatever the shape of one draw, so four times the samples halve the error). This section keeps what a statistician needs from them: the numbers that set error bars, and where the bell fails. An accuracy is a mean of 0/1 scores; at accuracy 0.5 one score has \(\sigma = 0.5\), so the standard error is \(\sqrt{0.25/500} = 0.022\) on 500 examples and \(\sqrt{0.25/2000} = 0.011\) on 2,000. On the tiny model an average of 30 losses has standard error \(0.651/\sqrt{30} = 0.119\) and a 95% half-width of \(1.96 \times 0.119 = 0.233\), although one draw is skewed (skewness 1.26: a long right tail from the rare "sat"). exact

Two things slow the approach: skew (the skewness of a mean is that of one draw divided by \(\sqrt{n}\), so a lognormal's 6.18 needs \(n\) in the hundreds to fade) and heavy tails. With infinite variance the theorem does not apply: averages of a Pareto with tail index 1.5 never settle into a bell and their spread shrinks more slowly than \(1/\sqrt{n}\). For skewed means use the t multiplier (section 5), the BCa bootstrap (section 6) or a transform; when the variance may be infinite (losses on rare tokens, heavy-tailed rewards, latencies) report medians, quantiles or trimmed means rather than a mean with a normal error bar. The same \(1/\sqrt{B}\) law sets the noise of a minibatch gradient ({{Calculus and optimisation for ML, section 10|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eb8b49ed6a0f897f3b}}) and of Monte Carlo estimates ({{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}, section 4).

The LLN in one line, and how fast the CLT arrives: Berry-Esseen (depth)

With finite variance the LLN is one line: \(\bar X_n\) has variance \(\sigma^2/n\), so by Chebyshev (section 12) \(P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2) \to 0\). Formally the CLT says \((\bar X_n - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})\) converges in distribution to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\): the probability of landing in any interval approaches the normal one, and 95% of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\) lies within \(\pm 1.96\).

The largest gap between the distribution function of the standardised mean and the normal one is at most \(C\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt{n})\), with \(\rho = \mathbb{E}|X - \mu|^3\) and \(C < 0.4748\) (Shevtsova 2011, arXiv 1111.6554). When 0/1 scores are few (an AIME paper has 15 items) the normal approximation fails near 0% and 100%; exact coverage of Wald, Wilson, Clopper-Pearson and Bayes intervals is in section 1 of {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d#rd-se}}.

5. Confidence intervals: what 95% does and does not mean

Turn the CLT around: if \(\bar x\) is within 1.96 standard errors of \(\mu\) in 95% of samples, then \(\bar x \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}\) contains \(\mu\) in 95% of samples. We do not know \(\sigma\), so we plug in \(s\) and pay with a wider multiplier, the 97.5% point of Student's t-distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom, which has heavier tails than the normal and approaches 1.96 as \(n\) grows (exact for normal data; Gosset, writing as "Student", Biometrika 1908):

\[ \bar x \pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad t_{29,\,0.975} = 2.045, \quad t_{4,\,0.975} = 2.776, \quad z_{0.975} = 1.96. \]

For the eight numbers of section 3: SE \(= 2.138/\sqrt{8} = 0.756\), \(t_{7,\,0.975} = 2.365\), interval \(5 \pm 1.788 = (3.21, 6.79)\). For an accuracy near 0.5 on 500 questions: SE 0.022, half-width 0.044 with 1.96 and 0.045 with "2 standard errors"; pick one multiplier and say which. exact

What a 95% confidence interval means, and three things it does not

It means: the procedure catches the true value in 95% of repetitions of the whole experiment.

It does not mean (1) "a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is in this interval": \(\mu\) is fixed and this interval either contains it or not (that is a Bayesian credible interval, section 10); (2) "95% of the data lie inside": that is a much wider prediction interval; (3) "a repeat will land inside with 95% probability": for a normal mean with known \(\sigma\), a replication's mean lands inside the original interval only \(2\Phi(1.96/\sqrt{2}) - 1 = 83.4\%\) of the time on average, because both estimates wobble (Cumming and Maillardet 2006, Psychological Methods 11).

Do not compare intervals by overlap. Two equal-width 95% intervals fail to overlap only when the estimates differ by \(2 \times 1.96 = 3.92\) standard errors, but the difference is significant at 5% already at \(1.96\sqrt{2} = 2.77\) (the difference of independent estimates has standard error \(\sqrt 2\) times one). Overlap does not mean "not significant", and non-overlap corresponds to \(p \approx 0.0056\) (Schenker and Gentleman, The American Statistician 55, 2001). For paired data overlap is even less informative: test the per-example differences (section 8).

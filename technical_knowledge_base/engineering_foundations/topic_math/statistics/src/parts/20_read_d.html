5. Standard errors and confidence intervals: what 95% does and does not mean

The question. You measured an average loss of 0.775 on 30 sampled words. What range of true values is consistent with that, and what exactly does "95% confident" promise?

Turn the CLT around. If \(\bar x\) is within \(1.96\) standard errors of \(\mu\) in 95% of samples, then the interval \(\bar x \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}\) contains \(\mu\) in 95% of samples. We do not know \(\sigma\), so we plug in \(s\), and pay for the plug-in with a slightly wider multiplier:

\[ \bar x \pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad t_{29,\,0.975} = 2.045, \quad t_{4,\,0.975} = 2.776, \quad z_{0.975} = 1.96. \]
\(t_{n-1,\,0.975}\)
the 97.5% point of Student's t-distribution with \(n - 1\) degrees of freedom: the exact multiplier when the data are normal and \(\sigma\) is estimated by \(s\) (W. S. Gosset, writing as "Student", Biometrika 1908). It has heavier tails than the normal and approaches 1.96 as \(n\) grows.
\(s/\sqrt{n}\)
the estimated standard error.
The numbers exact
For the eight numbers of section 3: \(\bar x = 5\), \(s = 2.138\), SE \(= 2.138/\sqrt{8} = 0.756\), \(t_{7,\,0.975} = 2.365\), interval \(5 \pm 1.788 = (3.21, 6.79)\). For an accuracy near 0.5 on 500 questions: SE 0.022; with 1.96 the half-width is 0.044, with the rounder "2 standard errors" it is 0.045. Pick one multiplier and use it throughout (the old notes mixed them; see the box below).
What a 95% confidence interval means, and three things it does not

It means: the procedure that built it catches the true value in 95% of repetitions of the whole experiment. The animation below repeats the experiment 100 times.

It does not mean (1) "there is a 95% probability that \(\mu\) is in this particular interval": \(\mu\) is fixed, and this interval either contains it or not (that statement belongs to a Bayesian credible interval, section 13); (2) "95% of the data lie inside": that is a much wider prediction interval; (3) "a repeat of the experiment will land inside with 95% probability": for a normal mean with known \(\sigma\), a replication's mean lands inside the original 95% interval only \(2\Phi(1.96/\sqrt{2}) - 1 = 83.4\%\) of the time on average, because both estimates wobble (Cumming and Maillardet 2006, Psychological Methods 11).

The same experiment, again and again

Below, each row is one experiment: draw \(n\) values, compute the mean (dot) and its 95% t-interval (line). The vertical line is the true mean. Grey intervals catch it; orange ones miss. Before: bell-shaped data, \(n = 30\): about 95 of 100 catch it, as promised. After: the skewed lognormal with \(n = 5\): the promise breaks, and the misses all fall on one side. simulated, seeded; the long-run rates come from 5,000 repetitions (the {{CI and test lab|#t-lab}} has every combination).

Read the three runs together. Bell-shaped data at \(n = 30\): 95 of 100 (long run 94.8%). The tiny model's loss at \(n = 30\): 99 of 100 in this run, but 92.7% in the long run: 100 intervals is itself a small sample, and the skew of one draw (1.26) still costs about two points at \(n = 30\). The lognormal at \(n = 5\): 82 of 100 (long run 82.4%), and every miss is an interval lying wholly below the truth: small samples from a right-skewed population usually miss the rare large values, so both the mean and \(s\) come out small together. With 1.96 instead of \(t\) the coverage drops further (75 of 100 here).

Corrected from the old notes
The old page gave the unpaired error bar of a 1-point gap on 500 examples as "about \(\pm 6.3\) points" while computing the paired one with 1.96. With 1.96 throughout, the unpaired half-width is \(1.96 \times \sqrt{2 \times 0.25/500} = 1.96 \times 0.0316 = 0.062\), so \(\pm 6.2\) points (6.3 is the 2-standard-error figure). Its "\(\pm 0.045\)" for one score uses 2 standard errors; 1.96 gives 0.044. Both are now stated with their multiplier (section 8 has the paired comparison).

1. Estimators: bias, variance and their sum

You computed a validation loss of 0.81 on 10 examples; a colleague, on 10 other examples from the same source, got 1.11. Both are honest. Ten sampled words, 7 cat, 2 dog and 1 sat, average \((7 \times 0.408 + 2 \times 1.408 + 1 \times 2.408)/10 = 0.808\); ten more, 5 cat, 3 dog and 2 sat, average 1.108. exact The spread of such averages over many samples of 10 is \(\sigma/\sqrt{10} = 0.651/3.162 = 0.206\), so a gap of 0.3 between two samples of 10 is ordinary.

The vocabulary, briefly: the population is everything you could have measured (here every word the model could sample; in ML, the inputs the model will meet in use) and a parameter is a fixed number describing it, such as \(\mu = 0.832\); a sample is the \(n\) values observed, usually assumed i.i.d. (independent draws from the same population); an estimator \(\hat\theta\) is a statistic (any number computed from the sample: its average, its largest value, its median) used as a recipe to turn the sample into a guess of a parameter \(\theta\), and the estimate is what it gives on one sample. Over all the samples you might have drawn, the estimate has a sampling distribution; its standard deviation is the standard error.

When i.i.d. fails (near-duplicate test items, several items cut from one document, leaked test data, users in both train and test), the draws carry less information than their count suggests and every error bar computed as if they were independent is too narrow (clustered standard errors: section 3 of {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}). The test set is a sample from deployment; the trained weights are an estimate; so is every metric you report; keeping train and test samples apart is what makes a test score an honest estimate.

What makes an estimator good. Think of darts at a bullseye \(\theta\), one throw per sample. The throws can be centred off the bullseye (bias) or scattered (variance); the mean squared error counts both:

\[ \operatorname{Bias}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta] - \theta, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\theta - \mathbb{E}\hat\theta)^2\big], \qquad \operatorname{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \mathbb{E}\big[(\hat\theta - \theta)^2\big] = \operatorname{Bias}^2 + \operatorname{Var}. \]

So being unbiased is not the goal; being close is. Estimate \(\mu = 1\) from \(n = 4\) samples of variance 4: the sample mean is unbiased with MSE \(4/4 = 1\); the shrunk estimate \(0.5\,\bar{x}\) has bias \(-0.5\) and variance 0.25, so MSE \(0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5\), half the error. exact When three or more means are estimated together, a data-driven shrinkage beats the plain sample means in total MSE whatever the true means are (James and Stein 1961); weight decay and a prior towards zero ({{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}, section 9) are the same trade.

Derivations: MSE = bias² + variance, and the best shrinkage factor (depth)
  1. Write \(m = \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]\) and split \(\hat\theta - \theta = (\hat\theta - m) + (m - \theta)\). Squaring gives \((\hat\theta - m)^2 + 2(\hat\theta - m)(m - \theta) + (m - \theta)^2\).
  2. In expectation the first term is the variance; in the middle term \(m - \theta\) is a fixed number, so \(\mathbb{E}[2(\hat\theta - m)(m - \theta)] = 2(m - \theta)(m - m) = 0\); the last term is \(\operatorname{Bias}^2\). So \(\operatorname{MSE} = \operatorname{Var} + 0 + \operatorname{Bias}^2\).
  3. Shrinkage: \(\operatorname{MSE}(c\bar{x}) = (c - 1)^2\mu^2 + c^2\sigma^2/n\). Setting the derivative \(2(c - 1)\mu^2 + 2c\,\sigma^2/n\) to zero gives \(c^\ast = \mu^2/(\mu^2 + \sigma^2/n) = 1/(1 + 1) = 0.5\) here, so 0.5 is also the best factor. The catch: \(c^\ast\) depends on the unknown \(\mu\), which is why James-Stein's data-driven version matters. (No noise term appears because the target is the fixed number \(\mu\).)
  4. Consistent means the estimate converges to the truth as \(n \to \infty\) (\(P(|\hat\theta_n - \theta| > \epsilon) \to 0\) for every \(\epsilon > 0\)). If bias and variance both go to 0 then \(\operatorname{MSE} \to 0\), and by Chebyshev's inequality (section 12) \(P(|\hat\theta_n - \theta| > \epsilon) \le \operatorname{MSE}/\epsilon^2\), so the estimator is consistent.

Three estimators of the same entropy

The tiny model's entropy (0.832 nats) can be estimated from \(n\) sampled words in three ways: Monte Carlo averages \(-\ln p(\text{word})\) and needs the model's probabilities; plug-in counts the words and computes the entropy of the frequencies \(\hat p\), needing only samples (how entropy and mutual information of data are usually estimated); Miller-Madow adds \((m - 1)/(2n)\) to the plug-in, with \(m\) the number of distinct words seen, a first-order bias correction (Miller 1955; Paninski 2003, Neural Computation 15, section 3). In ML: when a paper calls a new estimator (of a gradient, a mutual information, a KL, a policy value) "unbiased" or "lower variance", compare MSE at your sample size; REINFORCE against the reparameterised gradient ({{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}, section 11), policy-gradient baselines and importance-sampling corrections are all this trade.

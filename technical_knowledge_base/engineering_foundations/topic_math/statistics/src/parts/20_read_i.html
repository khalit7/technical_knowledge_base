12. Cross-validation and its variance

The question. With little data you cannot afford a large test set. Cross-validation re-uses every example for testing. How much can its number be trusted?

\(K\)-fold cross-validation: split the data into \(K\) folds; for each fold, train on the other \(K - 1\) and record the loss on the held-out fold; average all \(n\) held-out losses. Every example is tested exactly once, by a model that did not see it. Leave-one-out is \(K = n\).

Two facts make its error bar harder than it looks:

  • The held-out losses are not independent. Any two folds' models share most of their training data, so their errors are correlated, and the naive standard error \(s/\sqrt{n}\) of the \(n\) held-out losses is too small. Bengio and Grandvalet (JMLR 5, 2004) prove "there exists no universal (valid under all distributions) unbiased estimator of the variance of K-fold cross-validation".
  • It does not estimate what you think. Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani (JASA 2023, arXiv 2104.00673) show that cross-validation "estimates the average prediction error of models fit on other unseen training sets drawn from the same population", not the error of the model fitted on your data, and that naive intervals "may have coverage far below the desired level". They propose nested cross-validation to estimate the variance.
Our run simulated
Linear regression with 20 Gaussian features, \(n = 100\), noise variance 1, 10-fold CV, repeated on 4,000 independent datasets. The naive 95% interval (mean held-out loss \(\pm 1.96\, s/\sqrt{n}\)) covered the error of the model actually fitted on that dataset in only 85.8% of runs, and the average error over training sets (1.253, exact for this design) in 89.5%. Across datasets the CV estimate's real standard deviation was 0.214, against an average naive standard error of 0.179. And the CV estimate was essentially uncorrelated with the error of the model it was meant to assess (correlation \(-0.047\)): exactly Bates et al.'s point.

In plain words: cross-validation tells you how well this recipe (model family, hyperparameters, training procedure) does on data like yours. Its error bar is wider than the naive one, and it says little about this particular fitted model.

Selection inside CV. If you choose hyperparameters by cross-validation and report the same cross-validated score, the score is optimistic (the best of many noisy numbers, section 9). Use nested CV (an inner loop to choose, an outer loop to score) or a final untouched test set. Preprocessing fitted on all the data (scaling, feature selection) must also go inside the loop, or the held-out folds leak (on {{Classical ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ffb886ff30176bc6ce}}).

What it is used for in ML
Model and hyperparameter selection on small data; comparing learning algorithms (Dietterich 1998, Neural Computation 10, compares the tests for this); out-of-fold predictions for stacking.

13. The Bayesian alternative: credible intervals and Bayes factors

The question. What most people want to say is "there is a 95% probability the true accuracy is in this range" or "B is better with probability 0.9". Can we say that?

Yes, if the unknown is treated as a random variable with a prior distribution, updated by Bayes' rule into a posterior ({{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}, section 12, builds this on a coin). A 95% credible interval is a range holding 95% of the posterior probability: the statement a confidence interval is often misread as making, now true by construction, at the price of choosing a prior.

The coin again exact
9 heads in 10 with a uniform prior gives the posterior Beta(10, 2): mean \(10/12 = 0.833\), 95% equal-tailed credible interval (0.587, 0.977), and \(P(\text{heads probability} > 0.5 \mid \text{data}) = 0.994\).

Bayes factors compare two hypotheses by how well each predicted the data: \(\mathrm{BF}_{10} = P(\text{data} \mid H_1)/P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\), each likelihood averaged over that hypothesis's prior. For the coin, \(H_0\): fair, so \(P(9 \text{ of } 10) = 10/1024 = 0.00977\); \(H_1\): any bias, uniform, so \(P = \int_0^1 10\,q^9(1-q)\,dq = 10 \times \frac{9!\,1!}{11!} = 1/11 = 0.0909\). \(\mathrm{BF}_{10} = 9.31\). With even prior odds the posterior probability of a fair coin is \(1/(1 + 9.31) = 0.097\): about 10%, not the 2.1% a misread p-value suggests. Kass and Raftery (JASA 90, 1995) call factors from 3 to 20 "positive" evidence.

Two cautions. Bayes factors depend strongly on the prior under \(H_1\) (a very wide prior spreads its bets and predicts the data poorly), and with huge \(n\) a tiny p-value can come with a Bayes factor favouring \(H_0\) (Lindley's paradox, 1957). Credible intervals from weak priors usually match confidence intervals closely once \(n\) is large; they differ, usefully, when \(n\) is small or the parameter is near a boundary (accuracy near 0% or 100%).

What it is used for in ML
Small evals (tens of items) where normal intervals fail; A/B tests reported as "probability B beats A"; Thompson sampling in bandits; Bayesian optimisation of hyperparameters.

12. Concentration bounds: guarantees at every \(n\)

The CLT is a statement about the limit. A concentration inequality bounds the chance that an average lands far from its mean at every \(n\), with no "approximately". Three, each built on the previous: Markov, for \(X \ge 0\), \(P(X \ge a) \le \mathbb{E}[X]/a\); Chebyshev, \(P(|X - \mu| \ge t) \le \sigma^2/t^2\) (and \(\sigma^2/n\) for a mean of \(n\) draws); Hoeffding (JASA 58, 1963, Theorem 2), for the mean of \(n\) i.i.d. variables in \([0, 1]\),

\[ P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge t\big) \le 2e^{-2nt^2} \qquad (\text{divide the exponent by } (b - a)^2 \text{ for variables in } [a, b]). \]

An accuracy on 500 examples: the chance of being off by 5 points or more is at most \(2e^{-2 \times 500 \times 0.05^2} = 2e^{-2.5} = 0.16\) by Hoeffding and \(0.25/(500 \times 0.0025) = 0.2\) by Chebyshev; the CLT approximation says 0.025 and the exact binomial answer at accuracy 0.5 is 0.028. Pushing Hoeffding below 0.05 needs \(n \ge \ln(2/0.05)/(2 \times 0.05^2) = 737.8\), so 738 examples. exact

A guarantee costs you: the bounds sit far above the exact tail

The probability that an accuracy of 0.5 measured on \(n\) examples is off by at least 5 points: exact binomial, the CLT approximation, and the two bounds. Hoeffding assumes the worst variance a bounded variable can have, which is why it is loose; it is still a guarantee.

In ML these give worst-case eval sizes, generalisation bounds, and the confidence bonuses of bandits (UCB adds a Hoeffding-shaped bonus; {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}), which must decide from finitely many samples which option is best, as RLHF data selection must.

The bounds on the tiny model, proofs, generalisation bounds, and concentration in high dimensions (depth)

Tiny model: Markov says \(P(\text{loss} \ge 2.408) \le 0.832/2.408 = 0.346\); the truth is 0.090. Chebyshev bounds the chance of landing two standard deviations (1.302) from the mean by 0.25; the truth is again 0.090 (only "sat" is that far). How loose a bound is depends on how much it assumes.

  1. Markov: \(\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[X \cdot [X \ge a]] \ge a\,P(X \ge a)\), since \(X\) is at least \(a\) where the indicator is 1 and at least 0 elsewhere.
  2. Chebyshev: apply Markov to \((X - \mu)^2 \ge 0\) with \(a = t^2\).
  3. Hoeffding, sketch (the Chernoff method): apply Markov to \(e^{\lambda(\bar X_n - \mu)}\), use independence to split the expectation into a product, bound each factor with Hoeffding's lemma \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(X - \mathbb{E}X)}] \le e^{\lambda^2(b-a)^2/8}\), and choose the best \(\lambda\).

Generalisation bounds: the same inequality, plus a union bound. For one model fixed in advance and a test set of \(n\) examples with 0/1 loss, with probability at least \(1 - \delta\), \(|\text{true error} - \text{test error}| \le \sqrt{\ln(2/\delta)/(2n)}\). If you pick the best of \(|\mathcal H|\) models on the same data, each must be covered at once: \(\delta\) becomes \(\delta/|\mathcal H|\) and the gap \(\sqrt{(\ln|\mathcal H| + \ln(2/\delta))/(2n)}\) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, Understanding Machine Learning, 2014, chapter 4). With \(n = 10{,}000\) and \(\delta = 0.05\): 0.0136 for one model, 0.0296 for the best of a million. Selecting on the test set costs you, but only logarithmically. For a training set and an infinite family, \(\ln|\mathcal H|\) is replaced by a capacity measure (VC dimension, Rademacher complexity). For deep networks these bounds are vacuous (larger than 1): Zhang et al. (ICLR 2017, arXiv 1611.03530) showed that standard image networks fit random labels perfectly, so any bound that depends only on the family's capacity cannot explain why they generalise on real labels. PAC-Bayes bounds, which depend on how far the trained weights moved from a prior and how flat the minimum is, gave the first non-vacuous numbers for such networks (Dziugaite and Roy, UAI 2017, arXiv 1703.11008).

High dimensions concentrate hard. The squared norm of a random Gaussian vector in \(d\) dimensions is close to \(d\) (its length close to \(\sqrt{d}\)), and the cosine similarity of two independent random vectors has standard deviation about \(1/\sqrt{d}\) (0.031 at \(d = 1024\)). Random vectors are nearly orthogonal and nearly equal-length, which is why random projections, hashing and random-feature models work, and part of the geometry of embeddings.

14. Cross-validation and what it really estimates

\(K\)-fold cross-validation: split the data into \(K\) folds; for each fold, train on the other \(K - 1\) and record the loss on the held-out fold; average all \(n\) held-out losses. Every example is tested once by a model that did not see it (leave-one-out: \(K = n\)). It is how models and hyperparameters are chosen on small data, and how out-of-fold predictions for stacking are made.

Any two fold models share most of their training data, so their errors are correlated

10-fold CV: each row is one model, the orange cell its held-out fold. Rows 1 and 2 share 8 of their 9 training folds, so their held-out errors move together, and the naive standard error \(s/\sqrt{n}\) of the \(n\) held-out losses is too small. Bengio and Grandvalet (JMLR 5, 2004) prove "there exists no universal (valid under all distributions) unbiased estimator of the variance of K-fold cross-validation".

It does not estimate what you think. Bates, Hastie and Tibshirani (JASA 2023, arXiv 2104.00673) show that cross-validation "estimates the average prediction error of models fit on other unseen training sets drawn from the same population", not the error of the model fitted on your data, and that naive intervals "may have coverage far below the desired level"; they propose nested cross-validation to estimate the variance. Our run simulated: linear regression with 20 Gaussian features, \(n = 100\), noise variance 1, 10-fold CV on 4,000 independent datasets. The naive 95% interval covered the error of the model actually fitted on that dataset in only 85.8% of runs, and the average error over training sets (1.253, exact for this design) in 89.5%. The CV estimate's real standard deviation across datasets was 0.214, against an average naive standard error of 0.179, and it was essentially uncorrelated with the error of the model it was meant to assess (correlation \(-0.047\)): exactly Bates et al.'s point. CV tells you how well this recipe (family, hyperparameters, training procedure) does on data like yours, not how good this fitted model is.

Selection inside CV. Choosing hyperparameters by CV and reporting the same CV score is optimistic (the best of many noisy numbers, section 9): use nested CV (an inner loop to choose, an outer loop to score) or a final untouched test set. Preprocessing fitted on all the data (scaling, feature selection) must also go inside the loop, or the held-out folds leak ({{Classical ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ffb886ff30176bc6ce}}).

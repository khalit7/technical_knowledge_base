9. Many tests at once: Bonferroni, Holm, and the false discovery rate

The question. You try 20 prompt variants and one beats the baseline with \(p = 0.03\). Is it real?

Each test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) has a 5% false-positive rate when nothing is going on. Across many tests the chances add up: 20 independent tests on true nulls produce at least one "significant" result with probability \(1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64\). That probability is the family-wise error rate (FWER).

  • Bonferroni: test each of \(m\) hypotheses at \(\alpha/m\) (0.05 / 20 = 0.0025 each). Proof by the union bound: \[ P(\text{any false positive}) \le \sum_i P(\text{false positive } i) \le m \cdot \frac{\alpha}{m} = \alpha, \] whatever the dependence between tests. The cost is power.
  • Holm (Holm 1979, Scandinavian Journal of Statistics 6): sort the p-values \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\); reject while \(p_{(k)} \le \alpha/(m - k + 1)\) and stop at the first failure. Same FWER guarantee, never less power than Bonferroni, so there is no reason to use plain Bonferroni.
  • Benjamini-Hochberg (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995, JRSS B 57): control a different quantity, the false discovery rate, the expected fraction of rejections that are false, \(\mathrm{FDR} = \mathbb{E}[V/\max(R, 1)]\) with \(V\) false rejections out of \(R\). Find the largest \(k\) with \(p_{(k)} \le k\alpha/m\) and reject the \(k\) smallest. Proved for independent tests (and for positively dependent ones, Benjamini and Yekutieli 2001). Use it when you screen many candidates and can tolerate a known share of false leads.
Worked example exact
Ten p-values illustrative: 0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.019, 0.030, 0.041, 0.045, 0.21, 0.50, 0.80. Uncorrected at 0.05: 7 rejections. Bonferroni (each below 0.005): 1. Holm: 0.001 \(\le\) 0.05/10, then 0.008 \(>\) 0.05/9 = 0.0056, stop: 1. Benjamini-Hochberg at 5%: the thresholds \(k \times 0.005\) are 0.005, 0.010, 0.015, 0.020, 0.025, ...; the first four p-values are under theirs (0.019 \(\le\) 0.020) and none after, so 4 rejections, of which on average at most 5% are false.

In plain words: if you look in many places, something will look surprising. Correct for how many places you looked, or confirm the winner on fresh data.

The hidden version. The number of comparisons includes the ones you did not write down: every prompt, seed, checkpoint, metric, subset and preprocessing choice you tried and dropped. Gelman and Loken call this the "garden of forking paths" (2013). A held-out confirmation run on new data, decided in advance, is the cleanest correction.

What it is used for in ML
Hyperparameter sweeps (the best of 50 runs is optimistically biased); evaluating on many benchmarks or slices; per-feature or per-neuron tests in interpretability; release gates that check many metrics at once.

10. Concentration inequalities and generalisation bounds

The question. The CLT is a statement about the limit. Can we have a guarantee that holds at \(n = 500\), with no "approximately"?

A concentration inequality bounds the chance that an average lands far from its mean, at every \(n\). Three of them, each built on the previous one:

Markov, Chebyshev, Hoeffding, with the proofs
  1. Markov: for \(X \ge 0\) and \(a > 0\), \(P(X \ge a) \le \mathbb{E}[X]/a\). Proof: \(\mathbb{E}[X] \ge \mathbb{E}[X \cdot [X \ge a]] \ge a\,P(X \ge a)\), since \(X\) is at least \(a\) wherever the indicator is 1 and at least 0 elsewhere.
  2. Chebyshev: \(P(|X - \mu| \ge t) \le \sigma^2/t^2\). Proof: apply Markov to \((X - \mu)^2 \ge 0\) with \(a = t^2\). For a mean of \(n\) draws, \(\sigma^2\) becomes \(\sigma^2/n\).
  3. Hoeffding (Hoeffding 1963, JASA 58, Theorem 2): for the mean of \(n\) i.i.d. variables bounded in \([0, 1]\), \[ P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge t\big) \le 2e^{-2nt^2}. \] For variables in \([a, b]\) the exponent is divided by \((b - a)^2\). Proof sketch (the Chernoff method): apply Markov to \(e^{\lambda(\bar X_n - \mu)}\), use independence to split the expectation into a product, bound each factor with Hoeffding's lemma \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda(X - \mathbb{E}X)}] \le e^{\lambda^2(b-a)^2/8}\), and choose the best \(\lambda\).
The numbers exact
Tiny model: Markov says \(P(\text{loss} \ge 2.408) \le 0.832/2.408 = 0.346\); the truth is 0.090. Chebyshev says the chance of landing two standard deviations (1.302) from the mean is at most 0.25; the truth is again 0.090 (only "sat" is that far). An accuracy on 500 examples: the chance of being off by 5 points or more is at most \(2e^{-2 \times 500 \times 0.05^2} = 2e^{-2.5} = 0.16\) by Hoeffding and \(0.25/(500 \times 0.0025) = 0.2\) by Chebyshev; the CLT approximation says 0.025 and the exact binomial answer at accuracy 0.5 is 0.028. To push Hoeffding's bound below 0.05 you need \(n \ge \ln(2/0.05)/(2 \times 0.05^2) = 737.8\), so 738 examples.

Hoeffding is looser than the CLT (it assumes the worst-case variance a bounded variable can have) but it is a guarantee rather than an approximation. Move \(n\) to compare the bounds with the exact tail:

Generalisation bounds: the same inequality, plus a union bound

A model's test error is an average of per-example losses, so Hoeffding applies directly. For one model fixed in advance and a test set of \(n\) examples with 0/1 loss, with probability at least \(1 - \delta\):

\[ \big|\text{true error} - \text{test error}\big| \le \sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2n}}. \]

If you instead pick the best of \(|\mathcal H|\) models on the same data, each must be covered at once: the union bound replaces \(\delta\) by \(\delta/|\mathcal H|\), and the gap becomes \(\sqrt{(\ln|\mathcal H| + \ln(2/\delta))/(2n)}\) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David, Understanding Machine Learning, 2014, chapter 4). With \(n = 10{,}000\) and \(\delta = 0.05\): 0.0136 for one model, 0.0296 for the best of a million. Selecting on the test set costs you, but only logarithmically.

For a training set and an infinite family of models, \(\ln|\mathcal H|\) is replaced by a capacity measure (VC dimension, Rademacher complexity). For deep networks these bounds are vacuous (larger than 1): Zhang et al. (ICLR 2017, arXiv 1611.03530) showed that standard image networks fit random labels perfectly, so any bound that depends only on the model family's capacity cannot explain why they generalise on real labels. PAC-Bayes bounds, which depend on how far the trained weights moved from a prior and how flat the minimum is, gave the first non-vacuous numbers for such networks (Dziugaite and Roy, UAI 2017, arXiv 1703.11008).

High dimensions concentrate hard. Sums of many independent terms are sharply peaked, so the squared norm of a random Gaussian vector in \(d\) dimensions is close to \(d\) (its length close to \(\sqrt{d}\)), and the cosine similarity of two independent random vectors has standard deviation about \(1/\sqrt{d}\) (0.031 at \(d = 1024\)). Random vectors are nearly orthogonal and nearly equal-length, which is why random projections, hashing and random-feature models work.

What it is used for in ML
Worst-case sample sizes for evals; generalisation bounds in learning theory; best-arm identification and confidence bounds in bandits (UCB adds a Hoeffding-shaped bonus, on {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}) and in RLHF data selection, where you must decide from finitely many samples which option is best; the geometry of embeddings.

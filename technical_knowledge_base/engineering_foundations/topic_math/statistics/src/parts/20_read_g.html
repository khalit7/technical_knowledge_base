8. Which test: t, McNemar, permutation

The t-test divides a distance by its standard error, \(t = (\bar x - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n})\), compared with Student's \(t_{n-1}\). The paired t-test is the same test on per-example differences \(d_i = a_i - b_i\) with \(\mu_0 = 0\); for two independent groups, Welch's t-test uses \((\bar x_1 - \bar x_2)/\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_2^2/n_2}\), which does not assume equal variances. Why pairing matters: when two models are scored on the same examples, most of the variation in a score is which examples are easy, which both models share; per-example differences cancel it, while comparing two separate averages carries it twice.

McNemar's test (McNemar 1947) is the paired test for right/wrong scores. Only the discordant examples count:

Only the examples where the models disagree carry evidence

B rightB wrong
A rightboth right: drops out\(b\) = 30
A wrong\(c\) = 25both wrong: drops out

500 examples; A leads by \((30 - 25)/500 = 1\) point. Under \(H_0\) each discordant example is equally likely to fall either way, and \(\chi^2 = (b - c)^2/(b + c)\) is compared with a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom (5% critical value 3.84).

Here \(\chi^2 = 25/55 = 0.45\), \(p = 0.50\): no evidence. The paired standard error of the difference is \(\sqrt{(b + c) - (b - c)^2/n}\,/\,n = \sqrt{54.95}/500 = 0.0148\), a 95% interval of \(\pm 0.029\) (about \(\pm 3\) points); treated as independent, with accuracies near 0.5, it would be \(\pm 1.96\sqrt{2 \times 0.25/500} = \pm 0.062\), about \(\pm 6.2\) points. Pairing halves the error bar but still cannot rescue a 1-point claim. With \(b = 30\), \(c = 15\) (a 3-point lead), \(\chi^2 = 225/45 = 5.0 > 3.84\), \(p = 0.025\). exact

Continuity correction and the exact version (depth)

Some texts subtract 1 from \(|b - c|\) before squaring, slightly more conservative: 4.36 and \(p = 0.037\) for the 3-point lead. With few discordant examples use the exact version: under \(H_0\), \(b \sim \text{Binomial}(b + c, \tfrac12)\), which gives \(p = 0.036\).

Permutation tests need no distribution, only a symmetry of the null (R. A. Fisher, The Design of Experiments, 1935): if A and B are equally good, each per-example difference was as likely to be \(+d_i\) as \(-d_i\). Flip the signs every possible way, recompute the mean, and count how often it is as extreme as observed.

An exact p-value by flipping signs: 16 of 256 patterns are as extreme

Eight per-example loss improvements \((0.31, -0.12, 0.25, 0.40, 0.05, 0.18, -0.07, 0.22)\) illustrative, mean 0.1525. The dots are the means of all \(2^8 = 256\) sign patterns; the orange ones are at least as far from zero as observed, so \(p = 16/256 = 0.0625\), exact at any \(n\). The paired t-test on the same numbers gives \(t = 2.353\), \(p = 0.051\): close, but it leaned on normality that eight numbers cannot confirm. With large \(n\), sample a few thousand random sign patterns.

Rank tests (Wilcoxon's signed-rank for pairs, Mann-Whitney for two groups) are the classic robust choice for ordinal or heavy-tailed scores; they test a shift in location rather than the mean. For training runs across seeds, Welch's test with few seeds works, more seeds work better; Dietterich (1998, Neural Computation 10) compares the tests for comparing learning algorithms. Which test fits which eval, with one real comparison run through all of them, is section 5 of {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}; its section 4 measures how much pairing shrinks the interval on real models.

9. Many tests at once: Bonferroni, Holm, and the false discovery rate

You try 20 prompt variants and one beats the baseline with \(p = 0.03\). Each test at \(\alpha = 0.05\) has a 5% false-positive rate when nothing is going on, so 20 independent tests on true nulls produce at least one "significant" result with probability \(1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64\), the family-wise error rate (FWER).

  • Bonferroni: test each of \(m\) hypotheses at \(\alpha/m\) (0.05 / 20 = 0.0025). By the union bound \[ P(\text{any false positive}) \le \sum_i P(\text{false positive } i) \le m \cdot \frac{\alpha}{m} = \alpha, \] whatever the dependence between tests. The cost is power.
  • Holm (Holm 1979, Scandinavian Journal of Statistics 6): sort the p-values \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\); reject while \(p_{(k)} \le \alpha/(m - k + 1)\), stop at the first failure. Same FWER guarantee, never less power, so there is no reason to use plain Bonferroni.
  • Benjamini-Hochberg (JRSS B 57, 1995) controls the false discovery rate, the expected fraction of rejections that are false, \(\mathrm{FDR} = \mathbb{E}[V/\max(R, 1)]\) with \(V\) false rejections out of \(R\): find the largest \(k\) with \(p_{(k)} \le k\alpha/m\) and reject the \(k\) smallest. Proved for independent tests and for positively dependent ones (Benjamini and Yekutieli 2001). Use it when screening many candidates with a tolerable share of false leads.

Same ten p-values, three rules: 7, 1 and 4 discoveries

Ten sorted p-values illustrative: 0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.019, 0.030, 0.041, 0.045, 0.21, 0.50, 0.80. Uncorrected at 0.05: 7 rejections. Bonferroni (each below 0.005): 1. Holm: 0.001 \(\le\) 0.05/10, then 0.008 \(>\) 0.05/9 = 0.0056, stop: 1. Benjamini-Hochberg at 5%: thresholds \(k \times 0.005\) are 0.005, 0.010, 0.015, 0.020, ...; the first four are under theirs (0.019 \(\le\) 0.020) and none after, so 4 rejections, of which on average at most 5% are false. exact

The hidden comparisons count too: every prompt, seed, checkpoint, metric, subset and preprocessing choice tried and dropped (Gelman and Loken's "garden of forking paths", 2013). Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (Psychological Science 22, 2011) showed by simulation that four common flexible choices together push a nominal 5% false-positive rate to about 61%. The best of a sweep (the best of 50 runs) is also optimistically biased as an estimate of its own performance. The cleanest fix is a held-out confirmation run on new data, decided in advance, or a pre-registered analysis. The same problem appears in evaluating many benchmarks or slices, per-feature or per-neuron tests in interpretability, and release gates that check many metrics (section 6 of {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}).

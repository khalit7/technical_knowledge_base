Common mistakes

Peeking: testing as the data arrive and stopping when significant

Each look is another test, so looking repeatedly inflates the false-positive rate even though every single test is valid. Our simulation simulated: 200,000 runs with no real effect, up to 100 observations, a z-test at 5% after every \(k\)-th observation, stopping at the first rejection.

Ten looks almost quadruple the nominal 5% (19.4%); checking after every observation gives 37.6%. Armitage, McPherson and Rowe (JRSS A 132, 1969) worked this out for repeated significance tests (their tables are not re-fetched here). Fixes: decide \(n\) in advance; or use a sequential design whose boundaries spend \(\alpha\) across looks (Pocock, O'Brien-Fleming); or always-valid sequential tests. A/B dashboards that show a live p-value invite exactly this mistake.

p-hacking and the garden of forking paths

Trying several metrics, subsets, exclusions or analysis choices and reporting the one that reaches \(p < 0.05\) is multiple testing in disguise (section 9). Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn (Psychological Science 22, 2011) showed by simulation that four common flexible choices together push a nominal 5% false-positive rate to about 61%. Pre-register the analysis, or confirm on fresh data.

Testing many variants and reporting the best

Without a multiple-comparison correction or a held-out confirmation, the best of 20 prompt variants will often look significant on noise alone (\(1 - 0.95^{20} = 0.64\)). The best of a sweep is also optimistically biased as an estimate of its own performance.

Comparing confidence intervals by whether they overlap

Two 95% intervals of equal width fail to overlap only when the estimates differ by \(2 \times 1.96 = 3.92\) standard errors, but the difference is significant at 5% already at \(1.96\sqrt{2} = 2.77\) standard errors (the difference has standard error \(\sqrt 2\) times one, for independent estimates). So "the intervals overlap" does not mean "not significant", and non-overlap corresponds to \(p \approx 0.0056\), a much stricter test (Schenker and Gentleman, The American Statistician 55, 2001). For paired data (the same examples), overlap is even less informative: test the per-example differences.

Reading a p-value as the probability the null is true

Or 1 minus it as the probability the result is real. It is the probability of data this extreme if the null were true (section 7; for the coin, \(p = 0.021\) against a posterior probability of 0.097 for "fair" under even prior odds, section 13).

Comparing models with unpaired error bars, or with no error bars

On the same eval set, use a paired test; an unpaired interval is too wide (\(\pm 6.2\) against \(\pm 2.9\) points in section 8), and none at all lets noise masquerade as progress. Always report uncertainty on benchmark deltas, with the number of examples and the test used.

Assuming i.i.d. when it fails

Near-duplicate eval items, several items from one document, or test data leaked into training all shrink the real sample size, so the error bar is wider than \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\) suggests (clustered standard errors: section 3 of {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}).

Treating "not significant" as "no effect"

A test with low power usually fails to reject even when the effect is real (section 7: 30 observations detect a half-standard-deviation shift only 78% of the time). Report the interval: it shows which effect sizes the data rule out and which they do not.

Mixing divide-by-\(n\) and divide-by-\((n-1)\) across libraries

np.var and torch.var disagree by default (section 3). Pass ddof or correction explicitly.

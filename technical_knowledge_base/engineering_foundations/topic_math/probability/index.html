Probability for ML, in one screen

A model that predicts is a model that bets. Probability is the language of those bets: it says how to write a bet down (a distribution), how to update it when evidence arrives (conditioning and Bayes' rule), how to score it once the truth is known (the log-likelihood, which becomes the loss), and how to draw from it (sampling). 1. What a probability is: counting, and belief

The question. When the tiny model says "sat: 9%", what kind of statement is that? Nothing about the next word is random in the way a dice roll is.

The picture: outcomes, and the events made of them

Write down every outcome that could happen: that list is the sample space, written Ω\Omega (capital omega). For a die, Ω={1,2,3,4,5,6}\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}; for the tiny model's next word, Ω={cat,dog,sat}\Omega = \{\text{cat}, \text{dog}, \text{sat}\}. An event is any set of outcomes you can ask about: "the die shows an even number" is the event {2,4,6}\{2, 4, 6\}; "the next word is an animal" is {cat,dog}\{\text{cat}, \text{dog}\}. A probability PP gives every event a number between 0 and 1.

The rules (Kolmogorov's three axioms)

Everything on this page follows from three rules, stated by Kolmogorov in 1933 (Foundations of the Theory of Probability, English translation, chapter 1):

P(A)≥0,P(Ω)=1,P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)  when A∩B=⌀.P(A) \ge 0, \qquad P(\Omega) = 1, \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \ \text{ when } A \cap B = \varnothing.
A,BA, B
events (sets of outcomes).
A∪BA \cup B
"A or B": the outcomes in either. A∩BA \cap B is "A and B": the outcomes in both; ⌀\varnothing is the empty set, so A∩B=⌀A \cap B = \varnothing means they cannot both happen.

In plain words: probabilities are never negative, something certainly happens, and for things that cannot happen together, the chances add.

Two consequences used constantly: P(not A)=1−P(A)P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A), and for any two events P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) (subtract the overlap, which was counted twice).

The numbers
Fair die: P(even)=3/6=0.5P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 0.5; P(at least 5)=2/6=0.333P(\text{at least } 5) = 2/6 = 0.333. Tiny model: P(animal)=P(cat)+P(dog)P(\text{animal}) = P(\text{cat}) + P(\text{dog}) =0.665+0.245=0.910= 0.665 + 0.245 = 0.910, by the third rule, since the next word cannot be both.

Two readings of the same rules

Counting (frequentist). A probability is the long-run fraction: roll the die many times and the share of even numbers settles at 0.5. This reading needs a repeatable experiment. Belief (Bayesian). A probability is a degree of belief, given what you know: "this checkpoint will beat the baseline: 70%" is about one event that happens once. Both readings obey the same three rules (Cox, 1946: his theorem shows that any consistent way of grading belief by numbers must follow them, up to relabelling), so the mathematics below holds whichever you prefer.

The tiny model's 9% is a belief: given the words "the cat", it would bet at those odds. It becomes checkable by counting when we collect many cases where it said 9% and see how often it was right; that is calibration, measured on three real models in the Calibration, real models tab.

What it is used for in ML
The sample space is the vocabulary (language models), the label set (classifiers), or the real line (regression). Belief probabilities are what a classifier's outputs mean; long-run frequencies are how we check them.

2. Conditional probability, independence, and Bayes' rule

The question. A toxicity filter is right 95% of the time on toxic messages and wrongly flags only 5% of clean ones. It flags a message. How likely is the message to be toxic? (Most people say about 95%. The answer below is 16%.)

Conditioning: restrict, then renormalise

The probability of AA given BB is the share of BB's probability that also lies in AA:

P(A|B)=P(A∩B)P(B)(P(B)>0).P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \qquad (P(B) \gt 0).
|\mid
read "given": everything to its right is known to have happened.

In plain words: throw away every outcome where BB did not happen, then rescale what is left so it adds to 1 again.

Multiplying both sides by P(B)P(B) gives the product rule, P(A∩B)=P(A|B)P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B), which is the form used to build joint probabilities step by step (section 5).

The numbers (the umbrella example, carried from the old page and rechecked)
Let X=1X = 1 mean "it rained", P(X=1)=0.3P(X = 1) = 0.3, and Y=1Y = 1 mean "the person carried an umbrella", with P(Y=1|X=1)=0.8P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) = 0.8 and P(Y=1|X=0)=0.2P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0) = 0.2. The joint cell P(X=1,Y=1)=0.8×0.3=0.24P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.8 \times 0.3 = 0.24 (product rule). Seeing an umbrella: P(Y=1)=0.8×0.3+0.2×0.7=0.38P(Y = 1) = 0.8 \times 0.3 + 0.2 \times 0.7 = 0.38 (the law of total probability, below), so P(X=1|Y=1)=0.24/0.38=0.632P(X = 1 \mid Y = 1) = 0.24 / 0.38 = 0.632. The umbrella raised rain from 0.3 to 0.632.

The law of total probability

If the events B1,…,BkB_1, \dots, B_k split the sample space (exactly one of them happens), then any event's probability is the weighted sum over the cases:

P(A)=∑i=1kP(A|Bi)P(Bi).P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A \mid B_i)\, P(B_i).

In plain words: work out AA's chance in each case, then average the cases by how likely each is. The umbrella's 0.38 above is this rule with the two cases "rain" and "no rain".

Independence, and the two meanings of "independent" (a question you flagged)

The probability meaning. AA and BB are independent when knowing one does not change the other: P(A|B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A), equivalently

P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B).P(A \cap B) = P(A)\, P(B).

For random variables (section 3), XX and YY are independent when this holds for every pair of values. It is a property of the joint distribution, nothing else. In the umbrella example the joint cell is 0.24 while P(X=1)P(Y=1)=0.3×0.38=0.114P(X = 1)P(Y = 1) = 0.3 \times 0.38 = 0.114: they differ, so rain and umbrellas are dependent. Had both conditionals been equal (say both 0.38), every cell would equal the product of its marginals and the two would be independent.

The experimental meaning. In an experiment or a regression, the independent variable is what you set or feed in (the input xx, also called a feature, covariate or regressor) and the dependent variable is what you measure (the target yy, the response): "yy depends on xx". This is about roles, not about a joint distribution. A controlled input is not thereby statistically independent of anything.

The two meanings meet in one sentence: learning is possible only because input and target are dependent in the probability sense. If P(y|x)=P(y)P(y \mid x) = P(y), the input carries no information about the target, and the best any model can do is predict the base rate. For the tiny model, the next word after "the cat" is not distributed like a random word; that dependence is what it learns.

Conditional independence, written X⟂Y|ZX \perp Y \mid Z ("X is independent of Y given Z"), means P(X,Y|Z)=P(X|Z)P(Y|Z)P(X, Y \mid Z) = P(X \mid Z)\,P(Y \mid Z): once ZZ is known, the other adds nothing. It powers Naive Bayes (features assumed independent given the class, so P(x1,…,xd|c)=∏jP(xj|c)P(x_1, \dots, x_d \mid c) = \prod_j P(x_j \mid c)), graphical models (the graph is a map of which conditional independences hold), and the i.i.d. assumption: training examples independent and identically distributed, which is what lets a dataset's probability factor into a product over examples (section 10). Two variables can be dependent yet conditionally independent: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, and are (roughly) independent given the temperature.

Bayes' rule

Write the product rule both ways, P(A∩B)=P(A|B)P(B)=P(B|A)P(A)P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A), and divide by P(B)P(B):

P(A|B)=P(B|A)P(A)P(B)=P(B|A)P(A)P(B|A)P(A)+P(B|not A)P(not A).P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \text{not } A)P(\text{not } A)}.
P(A)P(A)
the prior: belief in AA before the evidence. For a class, this is its base rate.
P(B|A)P(B \mid A)
the likelihood: how probable the evidence is if AA is true.
P(A|B)P(A \mid B)
the posterior: belief in AA after the evidence.
P(B)P(B)
the evidence (or marginal likelihood): the total chance of seeing BB, from the law of total probability. It makes the posterior add to 1.

In plain words: posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Evidence counts for as much as it is more probable under AA than under the alternatives, and it is always weighed against how common AA was to begin with.

Base rates: the filter that is "95% accurate"

Count instead of using formulas (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage's natural frequencies, 1995, which they showed people reason with far better). Take 10,000 messages with 1% toxic: 100 toxic, of which 95% (95) are flagged; 9,900 clean, of which 5% (495) are flagged. Of the 590 flagged, only 95 are toxic:

P(toxic|flag)=0.95×0.010.95×0.01+0.05×0.99=9595+495=0.161.P(\text{toxic} \mid \text{flag}) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{0.95 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{95}{95 + 495} = 0.161.
toxic, flaggedtoxic, missedclean, flagged (false alarm)clean, not flagged

Each dot is 10 of 10,000 messages (counts rounded to whole dots for the picture; the numbers are exact). Defaults reproduce the 0.161 above by construction. Inputs are illustrative.

Try: push the base rate up to 20%: the same filter's flags become mostly right. Then set the false positive rate to 0.5%: at a 1% base rate, precision jumps to 66%. The false positive rate matters more than the sensitivity when the class is rare.

What it is used for in ML
Reading any detector (toxicity, fraud, a judge model flagging failures) against the base rate of what it detects; precision against recall on imbalanced data (Evaluation metrics); a classifier trained on balanced data and deployed where the class is rare needs its prior corrected (multiply its output odds by the deployment prior odds over the training prior odds, which is Bayes' rule applied twice); Naive Bayes classifiers; and the posterior over parameters (section 12), which is the same rule with AA = "the parameters are θ\theta" and BB = "the data".

3. Random variables: PMF, PDF and CDF

The question. Events are sets of outcomes, but models output numbers: a word index, a pixel value, a count of clicks. How do we attach probabilities to numbers, including numbers that can take any real value?

The words

A random variable is a rule that turns each outcome into a number. Capitals name the variable (XX, YY); lower case names a value it might take (xx, yy). For the tiny model, let YY be the index of the next word: 1 for cat, 2 for dog, 3 for sat. Then "Y=3Y = 3" is an event, and P(Y=3)=0.090P(Y = 3) = 0.090. We write Y∼pY \sim p ("YY is distributed as pp", or "YY is drawn from pp").

A random variable is discrete when its values can be listed (word indices, counts, labels) and continuous when it takes values in an interval (a temperature, a weight, a pixel intensity in [0,1][0, 1]).

Discrete: the probability mass function

The probability mass function (PMF) lists the probability of each value: p(x)=P(X=x)p(x) = P(X = x), with p(x)≥0p(x) \ge 0 and ∑xp(x)=1\sum_x p(x) = 1. The tiny model's PMF is p(1)=0.665p(1) = 0.665, p(2)=0.245p(2) = 0.245, p(3)=0.090p(3) = 0.090.

Continuous: the probability density function

For a continuous variable, any single exact value has probability 0 (there are infinitely many values to share it with), so probabilities come from areas. A probability density function (PDF) p(x)p(x) gives probabilities by integration:

P(a≤X≤b)=∫abp(x)dx,p(x)≥0,∫−∞∞p(x)dx=1.P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b p(x)\, dx, \qquad p(x) \ge 0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\, dx = 1.
∫abp(x)dx\int_a^b p(x)\,dx
the area under the curve pp between aa and bb: add up p(x)p(x) times a tiny width dxdx across the interval.

In plain words: a density is probability per unit of xx. Only areas under it are probabilities, so a density can be larger than 1.

The numbers
A uniform variable on [0,0.5][0, 0.5] has density 2 everywhere on that interval (width 0.5 times height 2 is area 1). A Gaussian with standard deviation 0.1 has peak density 1/2π×0.01=3.9891/\sqrt{2\pi \times 0.01} = 3.989. Neither is a probability above 1; both are densities. This matters for losses: the Gaussian NLL −ln⁡p(y)-\ln p(y) can be negative, because p(y)p(y) can exceed 1.

Both kinds: the cumulative distribution function

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) works for both: F(x)=P(X≤x)F(x) = P(X \le x). It rises from 0 to 1 and never decreases. For a density, F(x)=∫−∞xp(t)dtF(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} p(t)\,dt and, the other way round, p(x)=F′(x)p(x) = F'(x) (the density is the slope of the CDF). For the tiny model, with words in the order cat, dog, sat, the CDF steps through 0.665, 0.910, 1.

The numbers (the standard Gaussian, mean 0 and standard deviation 1)
F(1)=Φ(1)=0.8413F(1) = \Phi(1) = 0.8413, where Φ\Phi (capital phi) is the standard Gaussian CDF. So P(−1≤X≤1)=Φ(1)−Φ(−1)=0.6827P(-1 \le X \le 1) = \Phi(1) - \Phi(-1) = 0.6827; within 2 standard deviations 0.9545; within 3, 0.9973 (the "68, 95, 99.7" rule); within 1.96, exactly 0.95, which is where the 1.96 in 95% error bars comes from.

The CDF is also how we sample: feed a uniform random number into the inverse of FF (section 13). And a value's quantile is the inverse CDF: the median is F−1(0.5)F^{-1}(0.5).

What it is used for in ML
PMFs are what classifiers and language models output; densities are what regression, VAEs and diffusion models output or assume; CDFs give quantiles (quantile regression, the pinball loss on Loss functions), p-values and confidence intervals (on Statistics for ML), and inverse-CDF sampling. To see each PMF, PDF and CDF move with its parameters, open the Distribution explorer.

4. Expectation, variance, covariance, correlation

The question. A loss is a number per example. Training minimises "the loss", one number. Which single number summarises a whole distribution of losses, and how do we say how spread out it is?

Expectation: the probability-weighted average

𝔼[X]=∑xxp(x)(discrete),𝔼[X]=∫xp(x)dx(continuous).\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\, p(x) \quad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\, p(x)\, dx \quad \text{(continuous)}.
𝔼\mathbb{E}
"expected value" or "mean". 𝔼x∼p[f(x)]\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] means the average of f(x)f(x) when xx is drawn from pp; papers write it everywhere.

In plain words: each value counts in proportion to how likely it is; it is the long-run average over many draws.

The average of a function of XX needs no new distribution: 𝔼[f(X)]=∑xf(x)p(x)\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \sum_x f(x)\,p(x) (sometimes called the law of the unconscious statistician, because one uses it without thinking).

The numbers
Die: 𝔼[X]=(1+2+⋯+6)/6=3.5\mathbb{E}[X] = (1 + 2 + \dots + 6)/6 = 3.5, a value the die never shows. Tiny model: if the next word really were drawn from the model's own bet pp, its expected loss is 𝔼[−ln⁡p(Y)]=−(0.665ln⁡0.665+0.245ln⁡0.245+0.090ln⁡0.090)=0.832 nats.\mathbb{E}[-\ln p(Y)] = -(0.665 \ln 0.665 + 0.245 \ln 0.245 + 0.090 \ln 0.090) = 0.832 \text{ nats}. That number has a name, the entropy of pp (owned by Information theory for ML): the loss a model expects if it is right about its own uncertainty.

Linearity of expectation

𝔼[aX+bY]=a𝔼[X]+b𝔼[Y]\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]

for any constants a,ba, b and any random variables, whether or not they are independent. The proof is one line: ∑x,y(ax+by)p(x,y)=a∑xxp(x)+b∑yyp(y)\sum_{x,y}(ax + by)\,p(x,y) = a\sum_x x\,p(x) + b\sum_y y\,p(y), summing the joint over the other variable each time. It is why the expected loss of a batch is the sum of expected per-example losses, why a minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient, and why counting by indicators works.

The numbers
Shuffle 10 name tags at random onto 10 people. Expected number who get their own tag? Let Ik=1I_k = 1 if person kk does; 𝔼[Ik]=P(own tag)=1/10\mathbb{E}[I_k] = P(\text{own tag}) = 1/10. By linearity the expected count is 10×1/10=110 \times 1/10 = 1, although the IkI_k are dependent (if nine match, the tenth must). The same argument says a model that gives the right token probability qtq_t at each of nn positions gets ∑tqt\sum_t q_t right in expectation, however the positions depend on each other.

Variance and standard deviation

Var⁡(X)=𝔼[(X−𝔼[X])2]=𝔼[X2]−𝔼[X]2.\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2.

In plain words: the average squared distance from the mean. Its square root, the standard deviation σ\sigma, is in the same units as XX.

The second form follows by expanding the square and using linearity: 𝔼[X2−2Xμ+μ2]=𝔼[X2]−2μ2+μ2\mathbb{E}[X^2 - 2X\mu + \mu^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2 with μ=𝔼[X]\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]. Scaling: Var⁡(aX+b)=a2Var⁡(X)\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X). Die: Var⁡(X)=35/12=2.917\operatorname{Var}(X) = 35/12 = 2.917.

Covariance and correlation

Cov⁡(X,Y)=𝔼[(X−𝔼X)(Y−𝔼Y)]=𝔼[XY]−𝔼[X]𝔼[Y],ρ=Cov⁡(X,Y)σXσY.\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)(Y - \mathbb{E}Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \rho = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X\, \sigma_Y}.

In plain words: covariance is positive when the two tend to be above their means together. The correlation ρ\rho (rho) rescales it to lie between −1-1 and 1, and measures only linear association.

For sums: Var⁡(X+Y)=Var⁡(X)+Var⁡(Y)+2Cov⁡(X,Y)\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y). For independent variables the covariance is 0, so variances add; that is why the variance of an average of nn independent draws is σ2/n\sigma^2 / n (the root of every error bar, on Statistics for ML).

The numbers
Umbrella: 𝔼[XY]=P(X=1,Y=1)=0.24\mathbb{E}[XY] = P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.24, so Cov⁡=0.24−0.3×0.38=0.126\operatorname{Cov} = 0.24 - 0.3 \times 0.38 = 0.126; Var⁡(X)=0.3×0.7=0.21\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21, Var⁡(Y)=0.38×0.62=0.2356\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 0.38 \times 0.62 = 0.2356; ρ=0.126/0.21×0.2356=0.566\rho = 0.126 / \sqrt{0.21 \times 0.2356} = 0.566.
Zero correlation does not mean independent. Let XX be −1-1, 0 or 1 with equal chance, and Y=X2Y = X^2. Then Cov⁡=𝔼[X3]−𝔼[X]𝔼[X2]=0−0=0\operatorname{Cov} = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0 - 0 = 0, yet YY is a function of XX. Independence implies zero covariance, not the reverse (except for jointly Gaussian variables). Mutual information (on Information theory for ML) catches any dependence.

For a vector x∈ℝdx \in \mathbb{R}^d, the covariance matrix Σ=𝔼[(x−μ)(x−μ)⊤]\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^\top] has the variances on its diagonal and the covariances off it. A categorical variable written as a one-hot vector has mean pp and covariance diag⁡(p)−pp⊤\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top; for the tiny model, the diagonal is (0.223,0.185,0.082)(0.223, 0.185, 0.082) (each pi(1−pi)p_i(1 - p_i)) and, for example, Cov⁡(cat,dog)=−0.665×0.245=−0.163\operatorname{Cov}(\text{cat}, \text{dog}) = -0.665 \times 0.245 = -0.163. The same matrix is the softmax's Jacobian and the cross-entropy's Hessian in the scores (section 7).

The law of total expectation, and of total variance

𝔼[X]=𝔼[𝔼[X|Y]],Var⁡(X)=𝔼[Var⁡(X|Y)]+Var⁡(𝔼[X|Y]).\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big], \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(X \mid Y)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big).

In plain words: average within each group, then average the group averages by group size. The spread splits into the average spread within groups plus the spread between group averages.

The numbers (illustrative)
Suppose 70% of tokens are easy, with mean loss 1.0 and variance 0.5, and 30% are hard, with mean loss 4.0 and variance 2.0. Mean loss: 0.7×1.0+0.3×4.0=1.90.7 \times 1.0 + 0.3 \times 4.0 = 1.9. Variance: within 0.7×0.5+0.3×2.0=0.950.7 \times 0.5 + 0.3 \times 2.0 = 0.95, between 0.7×(1−1.9)2+0.3×(4−1.9)2=1.890.7 \times (1 - 1.9)^2 + 0.3 \times (4 - 1.9)^2 = 1.89, total 2.84. Most of the spread is between the groups, which is why loss curves are often reported by data source.
What it is used for in ML

"Marginalising out" a variable is the single most common move in probabilistic ML: the evidence in Bayes' rule, the likelihood of a latent-variable model and the ELBO (on Variational inference and generative-model maths) are all marginals.

The chain rule of probability

Apply the product rule repeatedly:

p(x1,x2,…,xn)=p(x1)p(x2|x1)p(x3|x1,x2)⋯=∏t=1np(xt|x<t).p(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) = p(x_1)\, p(x_2 \mid x_1)\, p(x_3 \mid x_1, x_2) \cdots = \prod_{t=1}^{n} p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}).
x<tx_{\lt t}
all the earlier items x1,…,xt−1x_1, \dots, x_{t-1} (for t=1t = 1, nothing).
∏\prod
product, the multiplication counterpart of ∑\sum.

In plain words: the probability of a whole sequence is the probability of the first item, times the probability of the second given the first, and so on. It holds for any joint distribution, with no assumption at all.

This identity is the whole design of an autoregressive language model: it learns only the conditional p(xt|x<t)p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}), one softmax over the vocabulary per position, and the chain rule turns those into a probability for any text. Its training loss is the sum of the per-token losses, because the log of the product is the sum of the logs. The tiny model's bet on "sat" is one factor: if the earlier words had probabilities q1q_1 and q2q_2, the sentence "the cat sat" would get q1×q2×0.090q_1 \times q_2 \times 0.090, and its loss would be −ln⁡q1−ln⁡q2+2.408-\ln q_1 - \ln q_2 + 2.408.

What it is used for in ML
Next-token prediction (the chain rule); perplexity and bits per byte (sums of per-token losses, on Information theory for ML); latent-variable models and mixture models (marginals); graphical models and Naive Bayes (factorised joints); masked and diffusion language models, which factor the joint differently.

How they relate. A Bernoulli is a categorical with K=2K = 2 and a binomial with n=1n = 1. The question. 8. From distribution to loss: the five standard losses, derived

The question. Why is BCE the loss for yes-or-no labels, squared error for real numbers, and ez−yze^{z} - yz for counts? Is the loss a free design choice?

It is not. The recipe is always the same three moves: (1) say which distribution yy follows given the input, with the network outputting its parameter; (2) the loss for one example is −ln⁡p(y|output)-\ln p(y \mid \text{output}); (3) drop every term that does not depend on the network's output, since it changes neither the gradient nor the optimum. Over a dataset of independent examples the losses add (section 10).

Bernoulli to binary cross-entropy (BCE)

9. Proper scoring rules and calibration

The question. The log loss trains a model's probabilities. Does it train them to be honest, or could a model lower its loss by exaggerating its confidence? And how would we check?

Proper scoring rules

A scoring rule S(r,y)S(r, y) charges a forecaster who announced probability rr when outcome yy happened. Suppose the true probability of y=1y = 1 is qq. The rule is proper if the expected charge 𝔼y∼q[S(r,y)]\mathbb{E}_{y \sim q}[S(r, y)] is lowest at r=qr = q, and strictly proper if r=qr = q is the only minimum. Under a strictly proper rule, the best strategy is to report what you believe. (Formal treatment: Gneiting and Raftery, 2007, Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation.)

The log loss is strictly proper. Its expected value is f(r)=−[qln⁡r+(1−q)ln⁡(1−r)]f(r) = -[q\ln r + (1 - q)\ln(1 - r)]. Differentiate and set to zero:

f′(r)=−qr+1−q1−r=0⟹q(1−r)=r(1−q)⟹r=q,f'(r) = -\frac{q}{r} + \frac{1 - q}{1 - r} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; q(1 - r) = r(1 - q) \;\Longrightarrow\; r = q,

and f′′(r)=q/r2+(1−q)/(1−r)2>0f''(r) = q/r^2 + (1 - q)/(1 - r)^2 \gt 0, so it is the unique minimum. For KK classes the same holds: the expected loss minus its minimum is ∑kqkln⁡(qk/rk)\sum_k q_k \ln(q_k / r_k), the KL divergence, which is zero only at r=qr = q (Gibbs' inequality, on Information theory for ML).

The numbers (true q=0.7q = 0.7)
Log loss: reporting 0.7 costs 0.611 on average; exaggerating to 0.9 costs 0.765; hedging to 0.5 costs 0.693. Honesty wins.
Brier score (r−y)2(r - y)^2, also strictly proper: q(1−r)2+(1−q)r2q(1 - r)^2 + (1 - q)r^2 is 0.21 at 0.7 and 0.25 at 0.9.
Absolute error |r−y||r - y| is improper: its expectation q(1−r)+(1−q)rq(1 - r) + (1 - q)r is linear in rr, 0.42 at 0.7 but 0.30 at r=1r = 1. It rewards claiming certainty, for the same reason L1 picks the median, and the median of a Bernoulli with q>0.5q \gt 0.5 is 1. Accuracy only looks at which side of 0.5 you are on, so it cannot reward honest probabilities either.

Calibration: what it means and how it is measured

A model is calibrated when its stated confidence matches its frequency of being right: among all predictions made with confidence about 70%, about 70% are correct. Formally, P(Y=yˆ|cˆ=c)=cP(Y = \hat y \mid \hat c = c) = c for every cc, where yˆ\hat y is the predicted class and cˆ\hat c its probability.

A reliability diagram sorts predictions into bins by confidence (15 bins of width 1/15 here) and plots each bin's accuracy against its average confidence: a calibrated model sits on the diagonal. The expected calibration error summarises the gaps, weighted by how many predictions fall in each bin (as defined by Guo et al., 2017, eq. 3):

ECE=∑b=1Bnbn|acc⁡(b)−conf⁡(b)|.\text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{n}\,\big|\operatorname{acc}(b) - \operatorname{conf}(b)\big|.

acc⁡(b),conf⁡(b)\operatorname{acc}(b), \operatorname{conf}(b)

Proper scoring explains why calibration is not luck: minimising the expected log loss over a rich enough model drives rr to the true conditional probability, so a model trained by maximum likelihood and evaluated on data like its training data should come out roughly calibrated. That is what happens with pretrained language models. Calibration can be lost. Guo et al., 2017 found deep image classifiers (a 110-layer ResNet on CIFAR-100, in their Figure 1, against a well-calibrated 5-layer LeNet from 1998) overconfident; their Table 1 uses the same 15 bins as here, and that temperature scaling, dividing every logit by one number TT fitted on held-out data, "is surprisingly effective at calibrating predictions". ECE has known weaknesses: it depends on the number of bins, it looks only at the top class, and a model can score 0 while saying nothing useful (always predicting the base rate is calibrated). What it is used for in ML
Trusting a classifier's probability as a probability (risk thresholds, abstaining when unsure, cascades that escalate low-confidence cases); temperature scaling after training; checking a post-trained model's confidence before using it as a verifier or judge; choosing proper metrics (log loss, Brier) over accuracy when probabilities matter.

10. Likelihood and maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE)

The question. "Training" means choosing the weights. What principle picks them, and why does it give the sums of losses we minimise?

Likelihood is not a probability of the parameters

Fix the data DD and vary the parameters θ\theta (theta: all the model's numbers). The likelihood is L(θ)=p(D|θ)L(\theta) = p(D \mid \theta): how probable the observed data is under each setting. It is the same formula as a probability, read the other way round: as a function of yy with θ\theta fixed it sums to 1; as a function of θ\theta with yy fixed it need not.

The numbers
A coin with heads-probability pp lands heads 3 times out of 3. The likelihood is L(p)=p3L(p) = p^3. Its area over p∈[0,1]p \in [0, 1] is ∫01p3dp=1/4\int_0^1 p^3\,dp = 1/4, not 1: the likelihood is not a distribution over pp. Turning it into one needs a prior and Bayes' rule (section 12).

For a dataset of i.i.d. examples (section 2), the probability of all of them is the product, so the log-likelihood is a sum:

ln⁡p(D|θ)=∑i=1nln⁡p(yi|xi,θ).\ln p(D \mid \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta).

In plain words: the log turns the product into a sum of per-example terms, which is what makes training by minibatches possible, and it stops thousands of small probabilities underflowing to zero.

MLE

θˆMLE=arg⁡maxθ⁡ln⁡p(D|θ)=arg⁡minθ⁡∑i=1n(−ln⁡p(yi|xi,θ)).\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \ln p(D \mid \theta) = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(-\ln p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta)\big).

Maximising the likelihood and minimising the summed negative log-likelihood (NLL) are the same problem. With section 8, this says: training a network with BCE, cross-entropy, MSE, L1 or the Poisson loss is maximum-likelihood estimation under the matching distribution.

Two MLEs derived without skipping a step

The coin. hh heads and tt tails: ℓ(p)=hln⁡p+tln⁡(1−p)\ell(p) = h\ln p + t\ln(1 - p). Then ℓ′(p)=h/p−t/(1−p)\ell'(p) = h/p - t/(1 - p); setting it to 0 gives h(1−p)=tph(1 - p) = tp, so h=p(h+t)h = p(h + t) and

pˆMLE=hh+t,\hat p_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{h}{h + t},

the observed frequency. After 3 heads in 3 flips, pˆ=1\hat p = 1: a confident claim that tails is impossible. That is MLE's weakness on small data, and the reason for sections 11 and 12.

The Gaussian. Data x1,…,xnx_1, \dots, x_n, model 𝒩(μ,σ2)\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2): ℓ=−n2ln⁡(2πσ2)−12σ2∑i(xi−μ)2\ell = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2. The derivative in σ2\sigma^2 is −n2σ2+12σ4∑i(xi−μ)2-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2; zero gives σˆ2=1n∑i(xi−μˆ)2\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n \sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2.

What MLE guarantees, and what it is in information terms

  • Consistent: under regularity conditions, as nn grows θˆ\hat\theta converges to the true parameter (if the model family contains it). Asymptotically efficient: in the large-data limit no consistent estimator has lower variance (it reaches the Cramér-Rao bound, the inverse Fisher information). Invariant: the MLE of g(θ)g(\theta) is g(θˆ)g(\hat\theta). (Wasserman, All of Statistics, chapter 9; the estimator properties themselves are on Statistics for ML.)
  • MLE is minimising a KL divergence. Dividing the NLL by nn gives the average −1n∑iln⁡pθ(yi)-\frac1n\sum_i \ln p_\theta(y_i), the cross-entropy from the data's empirical distribution to the model; it equals KL(pdata‖pθ)\text{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta) plus the data's entropy, which does not depend on θ\theta. So MLE picks the model closest to the data in forward KL, which is why it is "mass-covering" (it pays heavily for giving any observed outcome tiny probability). On Information theory for ML.
What it is used for in ML
Pretraining (next-token MLE), supervised fine-tuning, every classifier and regressor; fitting the parameters of any distribution (a Gaussian for anomaly detection, a Poisson for counts); and as the baseline that MAP and Bayesian methods correct when data is scarce.

11. MAP: maximum a posteriori, or "loss plus regulariser"

The question. Weight decay, L1 penalties, add-one smoothing: are these hacks bolted onto MLE, or something principled?

Put a prior p(θ)p(\theta) on the parameters, what you believed before the data. By Bayes' rule the posterior is p(θ|D)∝p(D|θ)p(θ)p(\theta \mid D) \propto p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta), and its peak is the MAP estimate:

θˆMAP=arg⁡maxθ⁡[ln⁡p(D|θ)+ln⁡p(θ)]=arg⁡minθ⁡[NLL(θ)−ln⁡p(θ)].\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \big[\ln p(D \mid \theta) + \ln p(\theta)\big] = \arg\min_\theta \big[\text{NLL}(\theta) - \ln p(\theta)\big].

In plain words: MAP is MLE plus one extra term, minus the log-prior, which is a regulariser. Everything you train as "loss plus regulariser" is MAP under some prior, whether or not you say so.

Gaussian prior = L2, Laplace prior = L1

Give every weight an independent Gaussian prior wj∼𝒩(0,τ2)w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2). Then

the prior variance: how large you believe weights can plausibly be. A tight prior (small τ\tau) is strong regularisation.

A Laplace prior 12be−|wj|/b\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|w_j|/b} gives 1b∑j|wj|\frac1b\sum_j |w_j|, the L1 penalty, whose sharp point at zero drives many weights exactly to zero (sparsity).

A Beta(a,ba, b) prior on a coin acts like a−1a - 1 heads and b−1b - 1 tails seen in advance; a Dirichlet(α\alpha) prior on a categorical acts like αk−1\alpha_k - 1 extra counts per class. What it is used for in ML

12. Full Bayesian inference: keep every answer, weighted

The question. MLE and MAP each return one parameter value. After 3 heads in 3 flips, how sure should we be about the coin, and what should we bet on the next flip?

The posterior and the predictive distribution

the posterior: how plausible each parameter value is after the data.
p(D)=∫p(D|θ)p(θ)dθp(D) = \int p(D \mid \theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta
the evidence or marginal likelihood: the probability the model as a whole gave the data, averaged over its prior. It compares models.

a new outcome to predict.

In plain words: instead of betting with one parameter value, bet with all of them, each weighted by how plausible it is. Uncertainty about θ\theta becomes honest uncertainty about y∗y^*.

A conjugate example, derived: the Beta-Bernoulli coin

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood when the posterior has the same form as the prior. Take the prior Beta(a,b)∝pa−1(1−p)b−1\text{Beta}(a, b) \propto p^{a - 1}(1 - p)^{b - 1} and hh heads, tt tails:

p(p|D)∝ph(1−p)t⏟likelihood⋅pa−1(1−p)b−1⏟prior=pa+h−1(1−p)b+t−1⟹p|D∼Beta(a+h,b+t).p(p \mid D) \propto \underbrace{p^{h}(1 - p)^{t}}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{p^{a - 1}(1 - p)^{b - 1}}_{\text{prior}} = p^{a + h - 1}(1 - p)^{b + t - 1} \;\Longrightarrow\; p \mid D \sim \text{Beta}(a + h,\, b + t).

Updating is adding counts. The predictive probability of heads is the posterior mean, ∫01pBeta(p;a′,b′)dp=a′/(a′+b′)\int_0^1 p\,\text{Beta}(p; a', b')\,dp = a'/(a' + b'). With the mild prior Beta(2, 2) and 3 heads:

  • MLE: 3/3=13/3 = 1.
  • MAP: the mode of Beta(5, 2), (5−1)/(5+2−2)=4/5=0.8(5 - 1)/(5 + 2 - 2) = 4/5 = 0.8.
  • Bayesian predictive: the mean of Beta(5, 2), 5/7=0.7145/7 = 0.714, with posterior standard deviation 5×2/(72×8)=0.160\sqrt{5 \times 2 / (7^2 \times 8)} = 0.160.

When the integral is intractable

For a neural network the posterior over millions of weights has no closed form, so it is approximated:

  • Variational inference: fit a simple distribution (say a Gaussian per weight) to the posterior by maximising the ELBO; on Variational inference and generative-model maths.
  • Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC): a random walk whose visits are distributed like the posterior (Metropolis-Hastings, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo); averages over its samples estimate the predictive integral (section 14).
  • Laplace approximation: a Gaussian at the MAP with covariance the inverse Hessian of the loss (often approximated by the Fisher or K-FAC).
  • Deep ensembles: train several networks from different random initialisations and average their predictions, a cheap and strong practical approximation (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).
What it is used for in ML
Uncertainty for small data and for decisions (active learning, Bayesian optimisation of hyperparameters, Thompson sampling in Bandits and exploration, which draws from exactly this Beta posterior per arm); model comparison by evidence; the conceptual basis of VAEs and diffusion (a posterior over latent variables, approximated).

And since −ln⁡(−ln⁡u)=−ln⁡E-\ln(-\ln u) = -\ln E with E=−ln⁡uE = -\ln u an exponential variable, arg⁡maxi⁡(zi−ln⁡Ei)=arg⁡maxi⁡pi/Ei\arg\max_i (z_i - \ln E_i) = \arg\max_i\, p_i / E_i: the "exponential race", which is how vLLM samples (v0.11.0 random_sample: probs.div_(q).argmax(dim=-1) with q.exponential_(), avoiding a CPU-GPU synchronisation that torch.multinomial causes, per its own comment).

The numbers

Temperature, top-k and top-p: sampling from a modified distribution

Every decoding knob replaces pp by another distribution and then samples from that:

  • Temperature TT: pi(T)∝ezi/T∝pi1/Tp^{(T)}_i \propto e^{z_i / T} \propto p_i^{1/T}. At T=0.5T = 0.5, (0.867,0.117,0.016)(0.867, 0.117, 0.016), the same as squaring pp and renormalising; at T=2T = 2, (0.506,0.307,0.186)(0.506, 0.307, 0.186); as T→0T \to 0, greedy decoding.
  • Top-k: keep the kk most probable, renormalise. k=1k = 1 is greedy.
  • Top-p (nucleus): keep the smallest set of most probable items whose total reaches a threshold (0.9 here), renormalise. At top-p 0.9: cat (0.665) is not enough, cat plus dog (0.910) is, so sat is removed and the bet becomes (0.731,0.269,0)(0.731, 0.269, 0).

None of these changes the model; they change which distribution you sample. Sampling from a truncated distribution means the text's probability under the model is no longer what you sampled from, which matters when the samples train another model (RL, distillation). The full sampler stack, its order, min-p and the rest, with a live lab on a real model, is on Sampling and Decoding (its Sampler lab tab).

What it is used for in ML
Text generation; RL rollouts (GRPO and PPO sample several answers per prompt); Gumbel-top-k for sampling without replacement; Gumbel-softmax for discrete latent variables and differentiable architecture search; inverse CDF and Cholesky (section 6) for continuous draws.

14. Monte Carlo estimation

The question. Most expectations in ML (an expected reward, a predictive integral, an expected loss over all possible texts) are sums over far too many outcomes to compute. How do we estimate them?

Draw x1,…,xnx_1, \dots, x_n independently from pp and average:

𝔼x∼p[f(x)]≈μˆn=1n∑i=1nf(xi),SE⁡(μˆn)=σfn.\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] \approx \hat\mu_n = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i), \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat\mu_n) = \frac{\sigma_f}{\sqrt{n}}.

In plain words: the average of samples is unbiased (linearity of expectation), it converges to the true value as nn grows (the law of large numbers), and its error shrinks like 1/n1/\sqrt{n} (variances of independent draws add; the central limit theorem behind it is on Statistics for ML). Four times the samples halve the error, whatever the dimension.

The numbers (estimating the tiny model's entropy by sampling)
Draw words from pp and average f=−ln⁡p(word)f = -\ln p(\text{word}). The true value is 0.832; the standard deviation of ff is 0.651; so 100 draws give an estimate within about ±0.065\pm 0.065 (one standard error), 10,000 draws within ±0.0065\pm 0.0065.

Importance sampling. If you can only sample from qq, reweight: 𝔼p[f]=𝔼q[f(x)p(x)/q(x)]\mathbb{E}_p[f] = \mathbb{E}_q\big[f(x)\, p(x)/q(x)\big]. The ratio p/qp/q is the importance weight; with qq uniform over the tiny model's three words, the weights are 3p=(1.996,0.734,0.270)3p = (1.996, 0.734, 0.270). It is unbiased but its variance explodes when qq puts little mass where pp has a lot. PPO's probability ratio and every off-policy correction are importance weights (on Policy gradients and actor-critic).

What it is used for in ML
Minibatch gradients (a Monte Carlo estimate of the full-data gradient); policy gradients and RL returns; the ELBO and diffusion losses (sampled noise and time steps); MCMC predictive averages; pass@k and other sampled eval metrics.

15. The reparameterisation idea: gradients through random samples

The question. To train a VAE or a diffusion model, we need the gradient of an expectation, ∇θ𝔼x∼pθ[f(x)]\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}[f(x)], where the parameters are inside the distribution we sample from. You cannot backpropagate through "draw a random number". What can you do?

Two estimators. The score-function (REINFORCE, likelihood-ratio) estimator uses ∇θpθ=pθ∇θln⁡pθ\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta:

∇θ𝔼pθ[f(x)]=𝔼pθ[f(x)∇θln⁡pθ(x)].\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\big[f(x)\, \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x)\big].

It works for any distribution and any ff, even a non-differentiable reward (Williams, 1992), but it is noisy. The reparameterisation (pathwise) estimator writes the sample as a deterministic function of the parameters and parameter-free noise, x=gθ(ε)x = g_\theta(\varepsilon) with ε∼p(ε)\varepsilon \sim p(\varepsilon); for a Gaussian, x=μ+σεx = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon, ε∼𝒩(0,1)\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). Then the expectation is over ε\varepsilon, which does not depend on θ\theta, and the gradient moves inside:

∇θ𝔼ε[f(gθ(ε))]=𝔼ε[∇xf(x)∇θgθ(ε)].\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[f(g_\theta(\varepsilon))\big] = \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[\nabla_x f(x)\, \nabla_\theta g_\theta(\varepsilon)\big].

In plain words: move the randomness to an input that does not depend on what you train, and the sample becomes an ordinary differentiable computation.

The numbers (derived exactly; a 200,000-draw simulation in src/recompute.py agrees)
Take f(x)=x2f(x) = x^2 and x∼𝒩(μ,1)x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1), at μ=1\mu = 1. The truth: 𝔼[x2]=μ2+1\mathbb{E}[x^2] = \mu^2 + 1, gradient 2μ=22\mu = 2. Pathwise, one sample gives 2x=2(1+ε)2x = 2(1 + \varepsilon): mean 2, variance 4. Score function, one sample gives x2(x−μ)=(1+ε)2εx^2 (x - \mu) = (1 + \varepsilon)^2 \varepsilon: mean 2, variance 𝔼[(ε+2ε2+ε3)2]−4=34−4=30\mathbb{E}[(\varepsilon + 2\varepsilon^2 + \varepsilon^3)^2] - 4 = 34 - 4 = 30 (using 𝔼ε2=1\mathbb{E}\varepsilon^2 = 1, 𝔼ε4=3\mathbb{E}\varepsilon^4 = 3, 𝔼ε6=15\mathbb{E}\varepsilon^6 = 15). Subtracting a baseline b=𝔼[f]=2b = \mathbb{E}[f] = 2 from ff keeps the mean and cuts the variance to 18, still 4.5 times the pathwise estimator's. That gap is why VAEs (Kingma and Welling, 2013) use the reparameterisation, and why policy gradients, which cannot (the reward is not differentiable in the sampled text), work so hard on baselines.

Common mistakes

Reading a classifier's precision off its accuracy, ignoring the base rate

A 95%-sensitive, 5%-false-alarm filter on a 1% class is right only 16% of the time it fires (section 2). Always ask how common the positive class is.

Mixing up the two meanings of "independent"

"The dependent variable" is a role in an experiment; "these variables are dependent" is a claim about a joint distribution. Learning needs input and target to be dependent in the second sense (section 2).

Treating zero correlation as independence

Y=X2Y = X^2 with symmetric XX has zero correlation and total dependence (section 4).

Reading a density as a probability

Densities can exceed 1 and Gaussian NLLs can be negative; only areas are probabilities (section 3).

Confusing likelihood with the probability of the parameters

p(D|θ)p(D \mid \theta) as a function of θ\theta does not integrate to 1 (p3p^3 has area 1/4); the posterior needs the prior (sections 10 and 12).

Treating the loss as a free design choice

MSE on a binary label is a Gaussian model of a yes-or-no outcome, with vanishing gradients through a sigmoid (section 8).

Reading a top-1 confidence as calibrated after post-training or fine-tuning

Pretraining by maximum likelihood tends to give calibrated next-token probabilities; post-training and some fine-tuning can break it. Measure it (ECE beside NLL) and fix it with temperature scaling (section 9).

Believing MAP is "the Bayesian answer"

MAP is a point estimate that moves under reparameterisation (the coin's 0.8 becomes 0.714 in logit space); the posterior and the predictive are the Bayesian answer (sections 11 and 12).

Assuming i.i.d. when it fails

Near-duplicate items, many items from one document, or test data leaked into training make examples dependent, so the likelihood is not a clean product and every error bar is too narrow (handled on Statistics for ML).

Forgetting that decoding changes the distribution

Text sampled at top-p 0.9 or temperature 0.7 is not a sample from the model; probabilities computed under the model do not describe it (section 13).

The idea in one screen

A model's output is a probability distribution; training scores it by minus the log-probability it gave the truth, generation draws from it, and the rest of probability says how to reshape it (temperature, priors, evidence), how sure to be about averages of it, and how to compute with it without overflowing.

Fig. 1 is the root page's tiny model, the running example of every section below: one distribution, three things done with it.

  1. Scores. The model's last layer gives any real numbers: z=(2,1,0)z = (2, 1, 0) for cat, dog and sat.
  2. Softmax turns them into a bet. Exponentiate, divide by the total: p=(0.665,0.245,0.090)p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090), non-negative and adding to 1; the dots carry each word's share (sections 1 and 6).
  3. Score it. The right word was sat, so the loss is −ln⁡0.090=2.408-\ln 0.090 = 2.408: the smaller the bet on the truth, the taller this column (sections 7 and 8).
  4. Draw from it. Lay pp end to end as the CDF strip and drop a uniform number on it; u=0.7u = 0.7 lands in dog's slice, between 0.665 and 0.910 (section 10).
  5. Reshape it. Divide the scores by a temperature: at T=2T = 2 they halve and the bet flattens to (0.506,0.307,0.186)(0.506, 0.307, 0.186). Multiplying by a prior and renormalising reshapes it too (sections 5, 6 and 9).

The one idea to keep
Pick a distribution for the target, let the network output its parameters, and minus the log of the probability it gives the truth is the loss. Every standard loss falls out of that move (section 7), its gradient is always "prediction minus target" (section 7), and the probabilities it trains are honest because the log loss is a proper scoring rule (section 8).

1. Random variables and the three distributions that matter

A random variable turns each outcome into a number; Y∼pY \sim p reads "YY is drawn from pp". For the tiny model, let YY be the index of the next word (1 cat, 2 dog, 3 sat), so P(Y=3)=0.090P(Y = 3) = 0.090. A discrete variable has a probability mass function (PMF) p(x)=P(X=x)p(x) = P(X = x) that sums to 1; a continuous one has a density (PDF) whose areas are probabilities (any single value has probability 0), P(a≤X≤b)=∫abp(x)dxP(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx; both have a cumulative distribution function (CDF) F(x)=P(X≤x)F(x) = P(X \le x), and p=F′p = F' for a density. The tiny model's CDF steps through 0.665, 0.910, 1; the inverse CDF gives quantiles (the median is F−1(0.5)F^{-1}(0.5)) and is one way to sample (section 10).

For the standard Gaussian, Φ(1)=0.8413\Phi(1) = 0.8413 (Φ\Phi is its CDF), so P(−1≤X≤1)=0.6827P(-1 \le X \le 1) = 0.6827; within 2 standard deviations 0.9545, within 3, 0.9973; within 1.96, exactly 0.95, which is where the 1.96 in 95% error bars comes from.

A density is not a probability: it can be far above 1

𝒩(0,0.12)\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1^2): peak density 1/2π×0.01=3.9891/\sqrt{2\pi \times 0.01} = 3.989, yet the shaded area within one standard deviation is 0.6827. Its NLL at the peak is −ln⁡3.989=−1.384-\ln 3.989 = -1.384: Gaussian NLL losses can be negative, because densities above 1 are normal. A uniform on [0,0.5][0, 0.5] has density 2 everywhere.

How they relate. A Bernoulli is a categorical with K=2K = 2 and a binomial with n=1n = 1. A binomial is a sum of nn independent Bernoullis (so its mean and variance are nn times theirs, by linearity and by variances adding). A Poisson is the limit of a binomial with many tries and a small chance each (n→∞n \to \infty, np=λnp = \lambda). A Beta is a Dirichlet with two components. The Gaussian is the limit of sums of many independent pieces (the central limit theorem, section 4), which is why it models noise. When counts vary more than their mean (overdispersion, common when some users click far more than others), the negative binomial, with variance μ+μ2/r\mu + \mu^2/r for a dispersion parameter rr, replaces the Poisson.

Foundations: sample spaces, Kolmogorov's axioms, and counting against belief (depth)

The sample space Ω\Omega lists every outcome (for the tiny model, {cat,dog,sat}\{\text{cat}, \text{dog}, \text{sat}\}); an event is a set of outcomes ("the next word is an animal" is {cat,dog}\{\text{cat}, \text{dog}\}). Everything on this page follows from three rules stated by Kolmogorov in 1933 (Foundations of the Theory of Probability, English translation, chapter 1):

P(A)≥0,P(Ω)=1,P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)  when A∩B=⌀.P(A) \ge 0, \qquad P(\Omega) = 1, \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \ \text{ when } A \cap B = \varnothing.

Consequences: P(not A)=1−P(A)P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A), and P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B). Fair die: P(even)=3/6=0.5P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 0.5; P(at least 5)=2/6=0.333P(\text{at least } 5) = 2/6 = 0.333. Tiny model: P(animal)=0.665+0.245=0.910P(\text{animal}) = 0.665 + 0.245 = 0.910.

Counting (frequentist): a probability is a long-run fraction, which needs a repeatable experiment. Belief (Bayesian): a degree of belief given what you know ("this checkpoint will beat the baseline: 70%"). Both obey the same rules (Cox, 1946: any consistent way of grading belief by numbers must follow them, up to relabelling). The tiny model's 9% is a belief; it becomes checkable by counting when many 9% predictions are collected and scored, which is calibration (section 8). The sample space is the vocabulary (language models), the label set (classifiers) or the real line (regression).

2. The multivariate Gaussian: covariance is a shape

For x∈ℝdx \in \mathbb{R}^d with mean μ\mu and covariance matrix Σ\Sigma:

𝒩(x;μ,Σ)=1(2π)ddet⁡Σexp⁡(−12(x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ)).\mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^d \det \Sigma}} \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12 (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)\Big).

The exponent is the squared Mahalanobis distance, distance from the mean in units of the spread in each direction, so equal-density points lie on ellipses. The eigenvectors of Σ\Sigma are the ellipse's axes and the square roots of its eigenvalues their half-lengths; det⁡Σ\det \Sigma is the squared volume scale. Sampling: factor Σ=LL⊤\Sigma = LL^\top (Cholesky, LL lower triangular), draw ε\varepsilon with independent standard Gaussian entries, return x=μ+Lεx = \mu + L\varepsilon; its covariance is 𝔼[Lεε⊤L⊤]=LIL⊤=Σ\mathbb{E}[L\varepsilon\varepsilon^\top L^\top] = L\,I\,L^\top = \Sigma. This is the reparameterisation of section 11 in matrix form.

  1. Noise first. ε\varepsilon has two independent standard Gaussian entries: a round bell, 𝒩(0,I)\mathcal{N}(0, I). The dots are the first 200 of the slider's 400 seeded draws; the rings are Mahalanobis distance 1 and 2.
  2. Multiply by LL. x=Lεx = L\varepsilon with L=(100.80.6)L = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0.8 & 0.6\end{pmatrix}: every dot slides, the bell stretches into a ridge along the diagonal, and Σ=LL⊤\Sigma = LL^\top has correlation 0.8.
  3. The axes are the eigenvectors. Seen from above: the long axis points along (1,1)(1, 1) with half-length 1.8=1.342\sqrt{1.8} = 1.342, the short one along (1,−1)(1, -1) with 0.2=0.447\sqrt{0.2} = 0.447; det⁡Σ=1.8×0.2=0.36\det\Sigma = 1.8 \times 0.2 = 0.36.
  4. The exponent is a distance. Two points 1.342 from the mean: along the long axis it is Mahalanobis distance 1 and density 0.161; along the short axis it is 1.342/0.447=3.01.342 / 0.447 = 3.0 and density 0.003. Distance is measured in units of the spread in each direction.
  5. Near singular. At ρ=0.95\rho = 0.95 the eigenvalues are 1.95 and 0.05, det⁡Σ=0.0975\det\Sigma = 0.0975, and the peak density rises from 0.265 to 0.510: the ridge collapses towards a line and Σ−1\Sigma^{-1} blows up.

3. Expectation and variance, and the algebra ML leans on

𝔼x∼p[f(x)]=∑xf(x)p(x)\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \sum_x f(x)\,p(x) (an integral for densities) is the probability-weighted average; a training objective is 𝔼(x,y)∼data[ℓ]\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \text{data}}[\ell]. On the tiny model, if the next word really were drawn from its own bet, the expected loss is

𝔼[−ln⁡p(Y)]=−(0.665ln⁡0.665+0.245ln⁡0.245+0.090ln⁡0.090)=0.832 nats,\mathbb{E}[-\ln p(Y)] = -(0.665 \ln 0.665 + 0.245 \ln 0.245 + 0.090 \ln 0.090) = 0.832 \text{ nats},

the entropy of pp (owned by Information theory for ML). Four rules carry almost every argument in ML papers:

  • Linearity holds without independence: 𝔼[aX+bY]=a𝔼[X]+b𝔼[Y]\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] for any random variables. So a minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient, and counting by indicators works: shuffle 10 name tags onto 10 people and the expected number of people with their own tag is 10×1/10=110 \times 1/10 = 1, though the matches are dependent (if nine match, the tenth must). A model giving the right token probability qtq_t at each of nn positions gets ∑tqt\sum_t q_t right in expectation, however the positions depend on each other.
  • Variances add only for uncorrelated variables: Var⁡(X+Y)=Var⁡(X)+Var⁡(Y)+2Cov⁡(X,Y)\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) and Var⁡(aX+b)=a2Var⁡(X)\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X). For nn independent draws, the variance of their average is σ2/n\sigma^2 / n: the root of every error bar (section 4).
  • Zero correlation does not mean independent. XX uniform on {−1,0,1}\{-1, 0, 1\} and Y=X2Y = X^2: Cov⁡=𝔼[X3]−𝔼[X]𝔼[X2]=0\operatorname{Cov} = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0, yet YY is a function of XX. Correlation ρ=Cov⁡(X,Y)/(σXσY)\rho = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)/(\sigma_X \sigma_Y) measures only linear association (jointly Gaussian variables are the exception); mutual information (on Information theory for ML) catches any dependence.
  • A one-hot categorical has covariance diag⁡(p)−pp⊤\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top: for the tiny model the diagonal is (0.223,0.185,0.082)(0.223, 0.185, 0.082) (each pi(1−pi)p_i(1 - p_i)) and Cov⁡(cat,dog)=−0.665×0.245=−0.163\operatorname{Cov}(\text{cat}, \text{dog}) = -0.665 \times 0.245 = -0.163. The same matrix is the softmax's Jacobian and the cross-entropy's Hessian in the scores (section 7).

Average within each group, then average the group averages; the spread splits into within-group plus between-group. Illustrative: 70% of tokens easy (mean loss 1.0, variance 0.5), 30% hard (mean 4.0, variance 2.0). Mean 0.7×1.0+0.3×4.0=1.90.7 \times 1.0 + 0.3 \times 4.0 = 1.9; within 0.7×0.5+0.3×2.0=0.950.7 \times 0.5 + 0.3 \times 2.0 = 0.95, between 0.7×(1−1.9)2+0.3×(4−1.9)2=1.890.7 \times (1 - 1.9)^2 + 0.3 \times (4 - 1.9)^2 = 1.89, total 2.84. Most of the spread is between groups, which is why loss curves are often reported by data source. The law of total variance is the decomposition behind ensembles and the bias-variance split (on Statistics for ML). Variance and covariance also describe gradient noise, feature scaling and normalisation layers, and covariance matrices drive PCA and whitening.

Two classic counting results: birthday collisions and the coupon collector (depth; moved from the root's retired Practice drills)

Birthday collisions. With 23 people and 365 equally likely birthdays, the chance that all differ is ∏i=022365−i365=0.4927\prod_{i=0}^{22} \frac{365 - i}{365} = 0.4927, so at least one pair shares a birthday with probability 0.5073: just over even odds. There are (232)=253\binom{23}{2} = 253 pairs, each with a 1/365 chance, and a collision becomes likely after about 2ln⁡2⋅N\sqrt{2 \ln 2 \cdot N} draws from NN values (Birthday problem). Collisions arrive after about N\sqrt{N} draws, not NN: hashing into N=232N = 2^{32} ids (feature hashing, dedup fingerprints) gives even odds of a collision after only about 77,163 items, and sampling with replacement repeats examples sooner than intuition says.

The coupon collector (linearity of expectation at work). A sampler returns one of 10 classes uniformly each draw. Having seen kk classes, a new one appears with probability (10−k)/10(10 - k)/10, a geometric wait of 10/(10−k)10/(10 - k) draws on average; expectations add, so seeing every class takes 10(1+12+⋯+110)=10H10=29.2910\left(1 + \frac12 + \dots + \frac1{10}\right) = 10 H_{10} = 29.29 draws on average, about three times the number of classes, and the last class alone takes 10. In general nHn≈nln⁡nn H_n \approx n \ln n (Coupon collector's problem). It sizes how many samples until every class, expert or shard is seen, and how many generations until every distinct answer appears in sampling-based evaluation and pass@k.

4. The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, by simulation

Most expectations in ML (an expected reward, an expected loss over all texts, a predictive integral) are sums over too many outcomes to compute, so they are estimated by an average of draws, the Monte Carlo estimate:

𝔼x∼p[f(x)]≈μˆn=1n∑i=1nf(xi),SE⁡(μˆn)=σfn.\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] \approx \hat\mu_n = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i), \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat\mu_n) = \frac{\sigma_f}{\sqrt{n}}.

Two theorems say why this works. The law of large numbers: μˆn\hat\mu_n converges to 𝔼[f]\mathbb{E}[f] as nn grows (it is unbiased by linearity). The central limit theorem: for finite variance, the error μˆn−𝔼[f]\hat\mu_n - \mathbb{E}[f] is close to Gaussian with standard deviation σf/n\sigma_f/\sqrt{n}, whatever the shape of ff's distribution. On the tiny model, f=−ln⁡p(word)f = -\ln p(\text{word}) takes only three values (0.408, 1.408, 2.408), with mean 0.832 and standard deviation 0.651: nothing Gaussian about it.

Predict, then step: at n=100n = 100 token losses per average, how wide is the spread of the averages? (Answer: 0.651/100=0.0650.651/\sqrt{100} = 0.065; 10,000 draws give 0.0065.) And at n=1n = 1, does "within 1.96 standard errors" catch 95% of draws?

  1. n=1n = 1: each "average" is one token loss. Three spikes at 0.408, 1.408 and 2.408, nothing like a bell; the ±1.96\pm 1.96 band catches only 90.6% of them, not 95%: the Gaussian rule needs averaging first.
  2. n=2n = 2: five possible averages. The spikes start to fill in towards the middle; the spread, 0.462, is already 1/21/\sqrt{2} of one draw's.
  3. n=4n = 4: the shape is rounding off. Still skewed to the right (the bet piles mass on the cheap word cat), but the curve is a fair summary.
  4. n=10n = 10: close to Gaussian. Spread 0.206 against the CLT's 0.651/10=0.2060.651/\sqrt{10} = 0.206; 95.7% of the averages fall inside ±1.96\pm 1.96 standard errors.
  5. n=30n = 30. Spread 0.120 (theory 0.119); the running mean of the single stream (top) has settled inside its band.
  6. n=100n = 100: about ±0.065\pm 0.065. Spread 0.063 against 0.651/100=0.0650.651/\sqrt{100} = 0.065: a 100-token average pins the mean loss to about ±0.065\pm 0.065 per standard error.

Four times the samples halve the error, whatever the dimension: that is why Monte Carlo is the default in high dimensions, and why an eval of 100 items cannot separate two models 2 points apart (a 70% accuracy on 100 items has standard error 0.7×0.3/100=0.046\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3/100} = 0.046, 4.6 points). Where the Gaussian approximation fails (heavy tails, infinite variance, small nn on a skewed metric), and how to build intervals and tests from it, is Statistics for ML's.

Used for: minibatch gradients (a Monte Carlo estimate of the full-data gradient); policy gradients and RL returns; the ELBO and diffusion losses (sampled noise and time steps); MCMC predictive averages; pass@k and other sampled eval metrics.

Independence, and the two meanings of "independent"

AA and BB are independent when P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B), a property of the joint and nothing else. Here the cell is 0.24 but P(X=1)P(Y=1)=0.3×0.38=0.114P(X = 1)P(Y = 1) = 0.3 \times 0.38 = 0.114: rain and umbrellas are dependent. The experimental meaning is different: the "independent variable" is what you set or feed in (the input xx, a feature or covariate) and the "dependent variable" is what you measure (the target yy). The two meet in one sentence: learning is possible only because input and target are dependent in the probability sense. If P(y|x)=P(y)P(y \mid x) = P(y), the best any model can do is predict the base rate.

The chain rule. Applying the product rule repeatedly, with no assumption at all, p(x1,…,xn)=∏t=1np(xt|x<t)p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{t=1}^{n} p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}). This identity is the whole design of an autoregressive language model: it learns only the conditional p(xt|x<t)p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t}), one softmax per position, and the log of the product is the sum of per-token losses. If the earlier words had probabilities q1q_1 and q2q_2, "the cat sat" gets q1×q2×0.090q_1 \times q_2 \times 0.090 and loss −ln⁡q1−ln⁡q2+2.408-\ln q_1 - \ln q_2 + 2.408. Masked and diffusion language models factor the joint differently; perplexity and bits per byte, sums of per-token losses, are on Information theory for ML.

Conditional independence, X⟂Y|ZX \perp Y \mid Z, means P(X,Y|Z)=P(X|Z)P(Y|Z)P(X, Y \mid Z) = P(X \mid Z)\,P(Y \mid Z): once ZZ is known, the other adds nothing. It powers Naive Bayes (P(x1,…,xd|c)=∏jP(xj|c)P(x_1, \dots, x_d \mid c) = \prod_j P(x_j \mid c)), graphical models (the graph is a map of which conditional independences hold) and the i.i.d. assumption (training examples independent and identically distributed, so a dataset's probability factors into a product over examples, section 7). Dependent variables can be conditionally independent: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, and are roughly independent given the temperature.

Independence for random variables, and an independent version of the umbrella (depth)

For random variables, XX and YY are independent when P(x,y)=P(x)P(y)P(x, y) = P(x)P(y) holds for every pair of values; equivalently P(A|B)=P(A)P(A \mid B) = P(A). Had both umbrella conditionals been equal (say both 0.38), every cell would equal the product of its marginals and the two would be independent. A controlled input is not thereby statistically independent of anything. For the tiny model, the next word after "the cat" is not distributed like a random word; that dependence is what it learns.

Bayes' rule, with base rates

P(A|B)=P(B|A)P(A)P(B)=P(B|A)P(A)P(B|A)P(A)+P(B|not A)P(not A).P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \text{not } A)P(\text{not } A)}.

P(A)P(A) is the prior (for a class, its base rate), P(B|A)P(B \mid A) the likelihood, P(A|B)P(A \mid B) the posterior, and P(B)P(B) the evidence (marginal likelihood) that makes it add to 1: posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. 6. Softmax as a distribution, and temperature

Softmax turns any real scores into a categorical distribution, pi=ezi/∑kezkp_i = e^{z_i} / \sum_k e^{z_k}: exponentiate (everything positive), divide by the total (everything sums to 1). Two properties do most of the work:

  • Only differences of scores matter. Adding the same constant to every ziz_i cancels in the ratio: z=(2,1,0)z = (2, 1, 0) and z=(1000,999,998)z = (1000, 999, 998) are the same distribution. Ratios are exponentials of gaps: pcat/pdog=e2−1=2.718p_{\text{cat}}/p_{\text{dog}} = e^{2 - 1} = 2.718. This invariance is what makes the stable log-sum-exp possible (section 12).
  • Temperature rescales the gaps. pi(T)∝ezi/T∝pi1/Tp^{(T)}_i \propto e^{z_i / T} \propto p_i^{1/T}. At T=0.5T = 0.5 the tiny model's bet becomes (0.867,0.117,0.016)(0.867, 0.117, 0.016), the same as squaring pp and renormalising; at T=2T = 2, (0.506,0.307,0.186)(0.506, 0.307, 0.186). As T→0T \to 0 all mass goes to the top score (greedy decoding); as T→∞T \to \infty the bet becomes uniform. Dividing by T>0T \gt 0 never changes which word is top.

7. From distribution to loss: maximum likelihood

A loss is not a free design choice. 8. Proper scoring rules and calibration, measured on real models

Could a model lower its log loss by exaggerating its confidence? No. A scoring rule S(r,y)S(r, y) charges a forecaster who announced rr when yy happened; it is proper if, when the true probability is qq, the expected charge 𝔼y∼q[S(r,y)]\mathbb{E}_{y \sim q}[S(r, y)] is lowest at r=qr = q, and strictly proper if that is the only minimum (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). Under a strictly proper rule the best strategy is to report what you believe.

Why the log loss is strictly proper (proof)

9. Priors: MAP is "loss plus regulariser", the posterior keeps every answer

Put a prior p(θ)p(\theta) on the parameters. By Bayes' rule the posterior is p(θ|D)∝p(D|θ)p(θ)p(\theta \mid D) \propto p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta); its peak is the MAP estimate, and its logarithm adds one term to the loss:

θˆMAP=arg⁡minθ⁡[NLL(θ)−ln⁡p(θ)].\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_\theta \big[\text{NLL}(\theta) - \ln p(\theta)\big].

So everything trained as "loss plus regulariser" is MAP under some prior. A Gaussian prior wj∼𝒩(0,τ2)w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2) gives −ln⁡p(w)=∑jwj2/(2τ2)-\ln p(w) = \sum_j w_j^2/(2\tau^2) + const: L2, with λ=1/(2τ2)\lambda = 1/(2\tau^2), so a tight prior is strong regularisation. A Laplace prior gives 1b∑j|wj|\frac1b\sum_j |w_j|: L1, whose sharp point at zero drives weights exactly to zero. A Beta(a,ba, b) prior on a coin acts like a−1a - 1 heads and b−1b - 1 tails seen in advance, and a Dirichlet(2, ..., 2) prior on a categorical makes MAP add-one (Laplace) smoothing: word counts after "the" of cat 3, dog 1, sat 0 give MLE (0.75,0.25,0)(0.75, 0.25, 0), so "the sat" has probability 0 and infinite loss; add one to each count: (4,2,1)/7=(0.571,0.286,0.143)(4, 2, 1)/7 = (0.571, 0.286, 0.143).

When the posterior integral is intractable (depth)

For a neural network the posterior over millions of weights has no closed form, so it is approximated:

  • Variational inference: fit a simple distribution (say a Gaussian per weight) by maximising the ELBO; on Variational inference and generative-model maths.
  • Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC): a random walk whose visits are distributed like the posterior (Metropolis-Hastings, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo); averages over its samples estimate the predictive integral (section 4).
  • Laplace approximation: a Gaussian at the MAP with covariance the inverse Hessian of the loss (often approximated by the Fisher or K-FAC).
  • Deep ensembles: train several networks from different random initialisations and average their predictions, a cheap and strong practical approximation (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017).

Gumbel-softmax, at the level of why. An argmax has zero gradient almost everywhere, so a discrete sample blocks backpropagation. Replacing the argmax with a softmax at temperature τ\tau, softmax⁡((z+g)/τ)\operatorname{softmax}((z + g)/\tau), gives a differentiable approximate one-hot sample (the Gumbel-softmax or Concrete relaxation, Jang et al., 2017; Maddison et al., 2017): as τ→0\tau \to 0 it becomes the exact Gumbel-max sample, and at larger τ\tau it is smoother with lower-variance but biased gradients. The noise gg does not depend on zz, so it is a reparameterisation (section 11) for discrete choices. PyTorch's F.gumbel_softmax draws its Gumbels as -empty_like(logits).exponential_().log() (torch 2.14.1).

Used for: text generation; RL rollouts (GRPO and PPO sample several answers per prompt); Gumbel-top-k for sampling without replacement; Gumbel-softmax for discrete latent variables and differentiable architecture search; inverse CDF and Cholesky (section 2) for continuous draws.

11. Reparameterisation: why gradients can pass through a random sample

Training a VAE or a diffusion model needs ∇θ𝔼x∼pθ[f(x)]\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\theta}[f(x)], with the parameters inside the distribution being sampled. "Draw a random number" has no derivative. There are two ways round it:

  • Score function (REINFORCE, likelihood ratio): ∇θ𝔼pθ[f(x)]=𝔼pθ[f(x)∇θln⁡pθ(x)]\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f(x)] = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}\big[f(x)\, \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta(x)\big], from ∇θpθ=pθ∇θln⁡pθ\nabla_\theta p_\theta = p_\theta \nabla_\theta \ln p_\theta. It works for any distribution and any ff, even a non-differentiable reward (Williams, 1992), but it only sees ff's value, not its slope, so it is noisy: one sample x2(x−μ)=(1+ε)2εx^2 (x - \mu) = (1 + \varepsilon)^2 \varepsilon has mean 2 and variance 30.
  • Reparameterisation (pathwise): write the sample as x=gθ(ε)x = g_\theta(\varepsilon) with parameter-free noise, for a Gaussian x=μ+σεx = \mu + \sigma\varepsilon, ε∼𝒩(0,1)\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). The expectation is now over ε\varepsilon, which does not depend on θ\theta, so the gradient moves inside: ∇θ𝔼ε[f(gθ(ε))]=𝔼ε[∇xf(x)∇θgθ(ε)]\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[f(g_\theta(\varepsilon))] = \mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\nabla_x f(x)\, \nabla_\theta g_\theta(\varepsilon)]. One sample 2x=2(1+ε)2x = 2(1 + \varepsilon) has mean 2 and variance 4.

Subtracting a baseline b=𝔼[f]=2b = \mathbb{E}[f] = 2 from ff keeps the score-function mean and cuts its variance to 18, still 4.5 times the pathwise estimator's. That gap is why VAEs (Kingma and Welling, 2013) use the reparameterisation, and why policy gradients, which cannot (the reward is not differentiable in the sampled text), work so hard on baselines. The ELBO, the VAE and the diffusion loss that use it, and the variance gap measured up to 100 dimensions, are on Variational inference and generative-model maths; baselines and advantages on Policy gradients and actor-critic.

12. Log-probabilities and log-sum-exp

Probabilities of whole sequences are products of many small numbers, so every library works in log space. A 100-token sentence at the tiny model's 0.090 per token has probability 0.090100=10−104.60.090^{100} = 10^{-104.6}: below float32's smallest positive number (about 1.4×10−451.4 \times 10^{-45}), so it rounds to 0 and its log to −∞-\infty. Its log-probability, 100×(−2.408)=−240.8100 \times (-2.408) = -240.8, is an ordinary number, and products become sums (the chain rule of section 5, the i.i.d. sum of section 7).

The one place a log-probability needs care is the softmax normaliser. ln⁡py=zy−ln⁡∑kezk\ln p_y = z_y - \ln\sum_k e^{z_k}, and ln⁡∑kezk\ln\sum_k e^{z_k}, the log-sum-exp, overflows for large scores. Because softmax depends only on differences of scores (section 6), subtract the largest first:

LSE⁡(z)=ln⁡∑kezk=m+ln⁡∑kezk−m,m=maxk⁡zk.\operatorname{LSE}(z) = \ln \sum_k e^{z_k} = m + \ln \sum_k e^{z_k - m}, \qquad m = \max_k z_k.

The rule: keep logits and log-probabilities, call the fused functions (cross_entropy, log_softmax, logsumexp), never log(softmax(z)).

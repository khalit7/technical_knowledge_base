10. Likelihood and maximum-likelihood estimation (MLE)

The question. "Training" means choosing the weights. What principle picks them, and why does it give the sums of losses we minimise?

Likelihood is not a probability of the parameters

Fix the data \(D\) and vary the parameters \(\theta\) (theta: all the model's numbers). The likelihood is \(L(\theta) = p(D \mid \theta)\): how probable the observed data is under each setting. It is the same formula as a probability, read the other way round: as a function of \(y\) with \(\theta\) fixed it sums to 1; as a function of \(\theta\) with \(y\) fixed it need not.

The numbers
A coin with heads-probability \(p\) lands heads 3 times out of 3. The likelihood is \(L(p) = p^3\). Its area over \(p \in [0, 1]\) is \(\int_0^1 p^3\,dp = 1/4\), not 1: the likelihood is not a distribution over \(p\). Turning it into one needs a prior and Bayes' rule (section 12).

For a dataset of i.i.d. examples (section 2), the probability of all of them is the product, so the log-likelihood is a sum:

\[ \ln p(D \mid \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \ln p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta). \]

In plain words: the log turns the product into a sum of per-example terms, which is what makes training by minibatches possible, and it stops thousands of small probabilities underflowing to zero.

MLE

\[ \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \ln p(D \mid \theta) = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(-\ln p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta)\big). \]

Maximising the likelihood and minimising the summed negative log-likelihood (NLL) are the same problem. With section 8, this says: training a network with BCE, cross-entropy, MSE, L1 or the Poisson loss is maximum-likelihood estimation under the matching distribution.

Two MLEs derived without skipping a step

The coin. \(h\) heads and \(t\) tails: \(\ell(p) = h\ln p + t\ln(1 - p)\). Then \(\ell'(p) = h/p - t/(1 - p)\); setting it to 0 gives \(h(1 - p) = tp\), so \(h = p(h + t)\) and

\[ \hat p_{\text{MLE}} = \frac{h}{h + t}, \]

the observed frequency. After 3 heads in 3 flips, \(\hat p = 1\): a confident claim that tails is impossible. That is MLE's weakness on small data, and the reason for sections 11 and 12.

The Gaussian. Data \(x_1, \dots, x_n\), model \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\): \(\ell = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2\). The derivative in \(\mu\) is \(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)\); zero gives \(\hat\mu = \frac1n \sum_i x_i\), the sample mean. The derivative in \(\sigma^2\) is \(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2\); zero gives \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n \sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2\).

The numbers
Data \((2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9)\): \(\hat\mu = 40/8 = 5\); squared deviations sum to 32, so \(\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} = 32/8 = 4\). The familiar "divide by \(n - 1\)" estimate is \(32/7 = 4.571\): the MLE of the variance is biased low, because \(\hat\mu\) was fitted to the same data. Why, and when it matters, is on {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}.

What MLE guarantees, and what it is in information terms

  • Consistent: under regularity conditions, as \(n\) grows \(\hat\theta\) converges to the true parameter (if the model family contains it). Asymptotically efficient: in the large-data limit no consistent estimator has lower variance (it reaches the Cramér-Rao bound, the inverse Fisher information). Invariant: the MLE of \(g(\theta)\) is \(g(\hat\theta)\). (Wasserman, All of Statistics, chapter 9; the estimator properties themselves are on {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}.)
  • MLE is minimising a KL divergence. Dividing the NLL by \(n\) gives the average \(-\frac1n\sum_i \ln p_\theta(y_i)\), the cross-entropy from the data's empirical distribution to the model; it equals \(\text{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta)\) plus the data's entropy, which does not depend on \(\theta\). So MLE picks the model closest to the data in forward KL, which is why it is "mass-covering" (it pays heavily for giving any observed outcome tiny probability). On {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}.
What it is used for in ML
Pretraining (next-token MLE), supervised fine-tuning, every classifier and regressor; fitting the parameters of any distribution (a Gaussian for anomaly detection, a Poisson for counts); and as the baseline that MAP and Bayesian methods correct when data is scarce.

11. MAP: maximum a posteriori, or "loss plus regulariser"

The question. Weight decay, L1 penalties, add-one smoothing: are these hacks bolted onto MLE, or something principled?

Put a prior \(p(\theta)\) on the parameters, what you believed before the data. By Bayes' rule the posterior is \(p(\theta \mid D) \propto p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\), and its peak is the MAP estimate:

\[ \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \big[\ln p(D \mid \theta) + \ln p(\theta)\big] = \arg\min_\theta \big[\text{NLL}(\theta) - \ln p(\theta)\big]. \]

In plain words: MAP is MLE plus one extra term, minus the log-prior, which is a regulariser. Everything you train as "loss plus regulariser" is MAP under some prior, whether or not you say so.

Gaussian prior = L2, Laplace prior = L1

Give every weight an independent Gaussian prior \(w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\). Then

\[ -\ln p(w) = \sum_j \frac{w_j^2}{2\tau^2} + \text{const} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat w_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_w \Big[\text{NLL}(w) + \lambda \|w\|_2^2\Big], \quad \lambda = \frac{1}{2\tau^2}. \]
\(\tau^2\)
the prior variance: how large you believe weights can plausibly be. A tight prior (small \(\tau\)) is strong regularisation.
\(\|w\|_2^2\)
\(\sum_j w_j^2\), the squared length of the weight vector.

A Laplace prior \(\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|w_j|/b}\) gives \(\frac1b\sum_j |w_j|\), the L1 penalty, whose sharp point at zero drives many weights exactly to zero (sparsity).

The numbers (one-weight ridge regression)
Fit \(y = wx\) to \(x = (1, 2, 3)\), \(y = (2, 3, 7)\) with Gaussian noise of variance \(\sigma^2\). The MLE minimises \(\sum_i (y_i - wx_i)^2\): \(\hat w = \sum x_i y_i / \sum x_i^2 = 29/14 = 2.071\). With the prior, the objective becomes \(\sum_i (y_i - wx_i)^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2} w^2\) (multiply the NLL by \(2\sigma^2\)); with \(\sigma^2/\tau^2 = 1\), \(\hat w = 29/(14 + 1) = 1.933\): pulled towards the prior mean 0.

Two cautions. In adaptive optimisers such as Adam, an L2 term in the loss is not the same as decoupled weight decay (AdamW, {{Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05101}}); the two coincide only for plain SGD. Both are on {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}. And MAP is not invariant to reparameterisation: the peak of a density moves when you change variables, because densities pick up a Jacobian factor. For the coin below, the posterior Beta(5, 2) peaks at \(p = 0.8\); written as a density over the logit \(\eta = \ln\frac{p}{1 - p}\), it gains the factor \(dp/d\eta = p(1 - p)\), becomes \(\propto p^5 (1 - p)^2\), and peaks at \(p = 5/7 = 0.714\). The MLE has no such problem (invariance above); the full posterior has none either. It is one reason to prefer the posterior to its peak.

Priors as imaginary data: smoothing

A Beta(\(a, b\)) prior on a coin acts like \(a - 1\) heads and \(b - 1\) tails seen in advance; a Dirichlet(\(\alpha\)) prior on a categorical acts like \(\alpha_k - 1\) extra counts per class. With Dirichlet(2, ..., 2), MAP is add-one (Laplace) smoothing, the classic fix for n-gram language models that give unseen words probability 0.

The numbers
Counts of the word after "the" in a tiny corpus: cat 3, dog 1, sat 0. MLE: \((0.75, 0.25, 0)\), so "the sat" gets probability 0 and infinite loss if it ever occurs. Add one to each count: \((4, 2, 1)/7 = (0.571, 0.286, 0.143)\).
What it is used for in ML
Weight decay and L1 (Gaussian and Laplace priors); smoothing counts (Dirichlet priors); label smoothing (read as a prior towards the uniform distribution); interpreting the regularisation strength as a belief about weight size; seeing why the "same" regulariser behaves differently under a reparameterisation.

9. Priors: MAP is "loss plus regulariser", the posterior keeps every answer

Put a prior \(p(\theta)\) on the parameters. By Bayes' rule the posterior is \(p(\theta \mid D) \propto p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta)\); its peak is the MAP estimate, and its logarithm adds one term to the loss:

\[ \hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\min_\theta \big[\text{NLL}(\theta) - \ln p(\theta)\big]. \]

So everything trained as "loss plus regulariser" is MAP under some prior. A Gaussian prior \(w_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\) gives \(-\ln p(w) = \sum_j w_j^2/(2\tau^2)\) + const: L2, with \(\lambda = 1/(2\tau^2)\), so a tight prior is strong regularisation. A Laplace prior gives \(\frac1b\sum_j |w_j|\): L1, whose sharp point at zero drives weights exactly to zero. A Beta(\(a, b\)) prior on a coin acts like \(a - 1\) heads and \(b - 1\) tails seen in advance, and a Dirichlet(2, ..., 2) prior on a categorical makes MAP add-one (Laplace) smoothing: word counts after "the" of cat 3, dog 1, sat 0 give MLE \((0.75, 0.25, 0)\), so "the sat" has probability 0 and infinite loss; add one to each count: \((4, 2, 1)/7 = (0.571, 0.286, 0.143)\).

The full posterior goes further: bet with every parameter value, weighted by its plausibility, through the predictive distribution

\[ p(y^* \mid D) = \int p(y^* \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid D)\, d\theta, \qquad p(D) = \int p(D \mid \theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta. \]

The evidence \(p(D)\) is the probability the model as a whole gave the data; it compares models. For a coin, a Beta prior is conjugate: \(p^{h}(1 - p)^{t} \cdot p^{a - 1}(1 - p)^{b - 1} = p^{a + h - 1}(1 - p)^{b + t - 1}\), so the posterior is \(\text{Beta}(a + h, b + t)\): updating is adding counts, and the predictive probability of heads is the posterior mean \(a'/(a' + b')\). With the mild prior Beta(2, 2) and 3 heads in 3: MLE 1; MAP, the mode of Beta(5, 2), \(4/5 = 0.8\); Bayesian predictive, its mean, \(5/7 = 0.714\), with posterior standard deviation \(\sqrt{5 \times 2 / (7^2 \times 8)} = 0.160\).

After flip 3 the Bayesian predictive was \(5/7\) for heads, so the tails cost \(-\ln(2/7) = 1.253\) nats, large but finite; MLE had bet 0 on tails. The Bayesian sum over all 12 predictions is 8.518 nats, and it equals exactly \(-\ln p(D)\), minus the log evidence of the 12 flips under Beta(2, 2): by the chain rule (section 5), the evidence is the product of the sequential predictive probabilities. After 12 flips the MLE (0.667), MAP (0.643) and mean (0.625) are close: the prior's 2 imaginary flips are being swamped.

Where priors are used, ridge by hand, L2 against weight decay, and why MAP is not invariant (depth)

Used for: weight decay and L1 (Gaussian and Laplace priors); smoothing counts (Dirichlet priors); label smoothing (read as a prior towards the uniform distribution); regularisation strength read as a belief about weight size; uncertainty for small data and decisions (active learning, Bayesian optimisation, Thompson sampling in {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}, which draws from exactly this Beta posterior per arm); model comparison by evidence; the conceptual basis of VAEs and diffusion (a posterior over latent variables, approximated). Credible intervals against confidence intervals, and Bayes factors, are on {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}.

One-weight ridge regression. Fit \(y = wx\) to \(x = (1, 2, 3)\), \(y = (2, 3, 7)\) with Gaussian noise of variance \(\sigma^2\). The MLE minimises \(\sum_i (y_i - wx_i)^2\): \(\hat w = \sum x_i y_i / \sum x_i^2 = 29/14 = 2.071\). With the prior, the objective is \(\sum_i (y_i - wx_i)^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2} w^2\) (the NLL times \(2\sigma^2\)); with \(\sigma^2/\tau^2 = 1\), \(\hat w = 29/(14 + 1) = 1.933\), pulled towards the prior mean 0. \(\tau^2\) is the prior variance: how large you believe weights can plausibly be; \(\|w\|_2^2 = \sum_j w_j^2\).

L2 is not weight decay in Adam. In adaptive optimisers an L2 term in the loss is not the same as decoupled weight decay (AdamW, {{Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05101}}); the two coincide only for plain SGD. Both are on {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}.

MAP is not invariant to reparameterisation: the peak of a density moves when you change variables, because densities pick up a Jacobian factor. The coin's posterior Beta(5, 2) peaks at \(p = 0.8\); as a density over the logit \(\eta = \ln\frac{p}{1 - p}\) it gains the factor \(dp/d\eta = p(1 - p)\), becomes \(\propto p^5 (1 - p)^2\), and peaks at \(p = 5/7 = 0.714\). The MLE has no such problem (it is invariant), nor does the full posterior: one reason to prefer the posterior to its peak, and why MAP is not "the Bayesian answer". With the flat prior Beta(1, 1), the predictive is \((h + 1)/(n + 2)\), Laplace's rule of succession (0.8 after 3 heads in 3).

When the posterior integral is intractable (depth)

For a neural network the posterior over millions of weights has no closed form, so it is approximated:

  • Variational inference: fit a simple distribution (say a Gaussian per weight) by maximising the ELBO; on {{Variational inference and generative-model maths|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}.
  • Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC): a random walk whose visits are distributed like the posterior (Metropolis-Hastings, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo); averages over its samples estimate the predictive integral (section 4).
  • Laplace approximation: a Gaussian at the MAP with covariance the inverse Hessian of the loss (often approximated by the Fisher or K-FAC).
  • Deep ensembles: train several networks from different random initialisations and average their predictions, a cheap and strong practical approximation ({{Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474}}).

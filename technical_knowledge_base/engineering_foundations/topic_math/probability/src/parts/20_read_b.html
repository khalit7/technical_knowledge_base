2. Conditional probability, independence, and Bayes' rule

The question. A toxicity filter is right 95% of the time on toxic messages and wrongly flags only 5% of clean ones. It flags a message. How likely is the message to be toxic? (Most people say about 95%. The answer below is 16%.)

Conditioning: restrict, then renormalise

The probability of \(A\) given \(B\) is the share of \(B\)'s probability that also lies in \(A\):

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \qquad (P(B) \gt 0). \]
\(\mid\)
read "given": everything to its right is known to have happened.

In plain words: throw away every outcome where \(B\) did not happen, then rescale what is left so it adds to 1 again.

Multiplying both sides by \(P(B)\) gives the product rule, \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)\), which is the form used to build joint probabilities step by step (section 5).

The numbers (the umbrella example, carried from the old page and rechecked)
Let \(X = 1\) mean "it rained", \(P(X = 1) = 0.3\), and \(Y = 1\) mean "the person carried an umbrella", with \(P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) = 0.8\) and \(P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0) = 0.2\). The joint cell \(P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.8 \times 0.3 = 0.24\) (product rule). Seeing an umbrella: \(P(Y = 1) = 0.8 \times 0.3 + 0.2 \times 0.7 = 0.38\) (the law of total probability, below), so \(P(X = 1 \mid Y = 1) = 0.24 / 0.38 = 0.632\). The umbrella raised rain from 0.3 to 0.632.

The law of total probability

If the events \(B_1, \dots, B_k\) split the sample space (exactly one of them happens), then any event's probability is the weighted sum over the cases:

\[ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A \mid B_i)\, P(B_i). \]

In plain words: work out \(A\)'s chance in each case, then average the cases by how likely each is. The umbrella's 0.38 above is this rule with the two cases "rain" and "no rain".

Independence, and the two meanings of "independent" (a question you flagged)

The probability meaning. \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when knowing one does not change the other: \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\), equivalently

\[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)\, P(B). \]

For random variables (section 3), \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent when this holds for every pair of values. It is a property of the joint distribution, nothing else. In the umbrella example the joint cell is 0.24 while \(P(X = 1)P(Y = 1) = 0.3 \times 0.38 = 0.114\): they differ, so rain and umbrellas are dependent. Had both conditionals been equal (say both 0.38), every cell would equal the product of its marginals and the two would be independent.

The experimental meaning. In an experiment or a regression, the independent variable is what you set or feed in (the input \(x\), also called a feature, covariate or regressor) and the dependent variable is what you measure (the target \(y\), the response): "\(y\) depends on \(x\)". This is about roles, not about a joint distribution. A controlled input is not thereby statistically independent of anything.

The two meanings meet in one sentence: learning is possible only because input and target are dependent in the probability sense. If \(P(y \mid x) = P(y)\), the input carries no information about the target, and the best any model can do is predict the base rate. For the tiny model, the next word after "the cat" is not distributed like a random word; that dependence is what it learns.

Conditional independence, written \(X \perp Y \mid Z\) ("X is independent of Y given Z"), means \(P(X, Y \mid Z) = P(X \mid Z)\,P(Y \mid Z)\): once \(Z\) is known, the other adds nothing. It powers Naive Bayes (features assumed independent given the class, so \(P(x_1, \dots, x_d \mid c) = \prod_j P(x_j \mid c)\)), graphical models (the graph is a map of which conditional independences hold), and the i.i.d. assumption: training examples independent and identically distributed, which is what lets a dataset's probability factor into a product over examples (section 10). Two variables can be dependent yet conditionally independent: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, and are (roughly) independent given the temperature.

Bayes' rule

Write the product rule both ways, \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A)\), and divide by \(P(B)\):

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \text{not } A)P(\text{not } A)}. \]
\(P(A)\)
the prior: belief in \(A\) before the evidence. For a class, this is its base rate.
\(P(B \mid A)\)
the likelihood: how probable the evidence is if \(A\) is true.
\(P(A \mid B)\)
the posterior: belief in \(A\) after the evidence.
\(P(B)\)
the evidence (or marginal likelihood): the total chance of seeing \(B\), from the law of total probability. It makes the posterior add to 1.

In plain words: posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Evidence counts for as much as it is more probable under \(A\) than under the alternatives, and it is always weighed against how common \(A\) was to begin with.

Base rates: the filter that is "95% accurate"

Count instead of using formulas (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage's natural frequencies, 1995, which they showed people reason with far better). Take 10,000 messages with 1% toxic: 100 toxic, of which 95% (95) are flagged; 9,900 clean, of which 5% (495) are flagged. Of the 590 flagged, only 95 are toxic:

\[ P(\text{toxic} \mid \text{flag}) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{0.95 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{95}{95 + 495} = 0.161. \]

Try: push the base rate up to 20%: the same filter's flags become mostly right. Then set the false positive rate to 0.5%: at a 1% base rate, precision jumps to 66%. The false positive rate matters more than the sensitivity when the class is rare.

What it is used for in ML
Reading any detector (toxicity, fraud, a judge model flagging failures) against the base rate of what it detects; precision against recall on imbalanced data ({{Evaluation metrics|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a29acef722e842a29b}}); a classifier trained on balanced data and deployed where the class is rare needs its prior corrected (multiply its output odds by the deployment prior odds over the training prior odds, which is Bayes' rule applied twice); Naive Bayes classifiers; and the posterior over parameters (section 12), which is the same rule with \(A\) = "the parameters are \(\theta\)" and \(B\) = "the data".

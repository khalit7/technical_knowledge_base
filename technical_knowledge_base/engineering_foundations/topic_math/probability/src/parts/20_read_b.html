1. Random variables and the three distributions that matter

A random variable turns each outcome into a number; \(Y \sim p\) reads "\(Y\) is drawn from \(p\)". For the tiny model, let \(Y\) be the index of the next word (1 cat, 2 dog, 3 sat), so \(P(Y = 3) = 0.090\). A discrete variable has a probability mass function (PMF) \(p(x) = P(X = x)\) that sums to 1; a continuous one has a density (PDF) whose areas are probabilities (any single value has probability 0), \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx\); both have a cumulative distribution function (CDF) \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\), and \(p = F'\) for a density. The tiny model's CDF steps through 0.665, 0.910, 1; the inverse CDF gives quantiles (the median is \(F^{-1}(0.5)\)) and is one way to sample (section 10).

A density is not a probability: it can be far above 1

\(\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1^2)\): peak density \(1/\sqrt{2\pi \times 0.01} = 3.989\), yet the shaded area within one standard deviation is 0.6827. Its NLL at the peak is \(-\ln 3.989 = -1.384\): Gaussian NLL losses can be negative, because densities above 1 are normal. A uniform on \([0, 0.5]\) has density 2 everywhere.

For the standard Gaussian, \(\Phi(1) = 0.8413\) (\(\Phi\) is its CDF), so \(P(-1 \le X \le 1) = 0.6827\); within 2 standard deviations 0.9545, within 3, 0.9973; within 1.96, exactly 0.95, which is where the 1.96 in 95% error bars comes from.

Numbers. Binomial: a model with true accuracy 0.7 on a 500-item eval scores \(350\) on average, with standard deviation \(\sqrt{500 \times 0.7 \times 0.3} = 10.25\) items (2.0 points): the noise in any single eval run. Poisson: at rate \(\lambda = 2\), \(P(Y = 3) = 2^3 e^{-2} / 3! = 0.180\). Laplace with \(b = 1\) has variance 2. Beta(2, 2) has mean 0.5 and standard deviation 0.224. Dirichlet(2, 1, 1) has mean \((0.5, 0.25, 0.25)\).

How they relate. A Bernoulli is a categorical with \(K = 2\) and a binomial with \(n = 1\). A binomial is a sum of \(n\) independent Bernoullis (so its mean and variance are \(n\) times theirs, by linearity and by variances adding). A Poisson is the limit of a binomial with many tries and a small chance each (\(n \to \infty\), \(np = \lambda\)). A Beta is a Dirichlet with two components. The Gaussian is the limit of sums of many independent pieces (the central limit theorem, section 4), which is why it models noise. When counts vary more than their mean (overdispersion, common when some users click far more than others), the negative binomial, with variance \(\mu + \mu^2/r\) for a dispersion parameter \(r\), replaces the Poisson.

Foundations: sample spaces, Kolmogorov's axioms, and counting against belief (depth)

The sample space \(\Omega\) lists every outcome (for the tiny model, \(\{\text{cat}, \text{dog}, \text{sat}\}\)); an event is a set of outcomes ("the next word is an animal" is \(\{\text{cat}, \text{dog}\}\)). Everything on this page follows from three rules stated by Kolmogorov in 1933 ({{Foundations of the Theory of Probability, English translation, chapter 1|https://archive.org/details/foundationsofthe00kolm}}):

\[ P(A) \ge 0, \qquad P(\Omega) = 1, \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \ \text{ when } A \cap B = \varnothing. \]

Consequences: \(P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)\), and \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\). Fair die: \(P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 0.5\); \(P(\text{at least } 5) = 2/6 = 0.333\). Tiny model: \(P(\text{animal}) = 0.665 + 0.245 = 0.910\).

Counting (frequentist): a probability is a long-run fraction, which needs a repeatable experiment. Belief (Bayesian): a degree of belief given what you know ("this checkpoint will beat the baseline: 70%"). Both obey the same rules ({{Cox, 1946|https://doi.org/10.1119/1.1990764}}: any consistent way of grading belief by numbers must follow them, up to relabelling). The tiny model's 9% is a belief; it becomes checkable by counting when many 9% predictions are collected and scored, which is calibration (section 8). The sample space is the vocabulary (language models), the label set (classifiers) or the real line (regression).

2. The multivariate Gaussian: covariance is a shape

For \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\) with mean \(\mu\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma\):

\[ \mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^d \det \Sigma}} \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12 (x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)\Big). \]

The exponent is the squared Mahalanobis distance, distance from the mean in units of the spread in each direction, so equal-density points lie on ellipses. The eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\) are the ellipse's axes and the square roots of its eigenvalues their half-lengths; \(\det \Sigma\) is the squared volume scale. Sampling: factor \(\Sigma = LL^\top\) (Cholesky, \(L\) lower triangular), draw \(\varepsilon\) with independent standard Gaussian entries, return \(x = \mu + L\varepsilon\); its covariance is \(\mathbb{E}[L\varepsilon\varepsilon^\top L^\top] = L\,I\,L^\top = \Sigma\). This is the reparameterisation of section 11 in matrix form.

Predict, then drag: what happens to the ellipse and to \(\det\Sigma\) as \(\rho\) goes to 0.95? Default: \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.8 \\ 0.8 & 1\end{pmatrix}\), \(L = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0 \\ 0.8 & 0.6\end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues 1.8 and 0.2.

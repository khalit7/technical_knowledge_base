9. Proper scoring rules and calibration

The question. The log loss trains a model's probabilities. Does it train them to be honest, or could a model lower its loss by exaggerating its confidence? And how would we check?

Proper scoring rules

A scoring rule \(S(r, y)\) charges a forecaster who announced probability \(r\) when outcome \(y\) happened. Suppose the true probability of \(y = 1\) is \(q\). The rule is proper if the expected charge \(\mathbb{E}_{y \sim q}[S(r, y)]\) is lowest at \(r = q\), and strictly proper if \(r = q\) is the only minimum. Under a strictly proper rule, the best strategy is to report what you believe. (Formal treatment: {{Gneiting and Raftery, 2007|https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437}}, Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation.)

The log loss is strictly proper. Its expected value is \(f(r) = -[q\ln r + (1 - q)\ln(1 - r)]\). Differentiate and set to zero:

\[ f'(r) = -\frac{q}{r} + \frac{1 - q}{1 - r} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; q(1 - r) = r(1 - q) \;\Longrightarrow\; r = q, \]

and \(f''(r) = q/r^2 + (1 - q)/(1 - r)^2 \gt 0\), so it is the unique minimum. For \(K\) classes the same holds: the expected loss minus its minimum is \(\sum_k q_k \ln(q_k / r_k)\), the KL divergence, which is zero only at \(r = q\) (Gibbs' inequality, on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}).

The numbers (true \(q = 0.7\))
Log loss: reporting 0.7 costs 0.611 on average; exaggerating to 0.9 costs 0.765; hedging to 0.5 costs 0.693. Honesty wins.
Brier score \((r - y)^2\), also strictly proper: \(q(1 - r)^2 + (1 - q)r^2\) is 0.21 at 0.7 and 0.25 at 0.9.
Absolute error \(|r - y|\) is improper: its expectation \(q(1 - r) + (1 - q)r\) is linear in \(r\), 0.42 at 0.7 but 0.30 at \(r = 1\). It rewards claiming certainty, for the same reason L1 picks the median, and the median of a Bernoulli with \(q \gt 0.5\) is 1. Accuracy only looks at which side of 0.5 you are on, so it cannot reward honest probabilities either.

Calibration: what it means and how it is measured

A model is calibrated when its stated confidence matches its frequency of being right: among all predictions made with confidence about 70%, about 70% are correct. Formally, \(P(Y = \hat y \mid \hat c = c) = c\) for every \(c\), where \(\hat y\) is the predicted class and \(\hat c\) its probability.

A reliability diagram sorts predictions into bins by confidence (15 bins of width 1/15 here) and plots each bin's accuracy against its average confidence: a calibrated model sits on the diagonal. The expected calibration error summarises the gaps, weighted by how many predictions fall in each bin (as defined by {{Guo et al., 2017, eq. 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599}}):

\[ \text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{n}\,\big|\operatorname{acc}(b) - \operatorname{conf}(b)\big|. \]
\(n_b\)
number of predictions in bin \(b\); \(n\) in total.
\(\operatorname{acc}(b), \operatorname{conf}(b)\)
the share correct, and the average confidence, in bin \(b\).

Proper scoring explains why calibration is not luck: minimising the expected log loss over a rich enough model drives \(r\) to the true conditional probability, so a model trained by maximum likelihood and evaluated on data like its training data should come out roughly calibrated. That is what happens with pretrained language models. Measured here on two small open base models and one instruct-tuned version, predicting the next token of WikiText-2 (20,480 tokens each, top-1 confidence, computed offline; full diagrams in the {{Calibration, real models|#t-cal}} tab):

Both base models are close to the diagonal: when they put 70% on their top token, they are right about 70% of the time (GPT-2's 0.700 bin: 71.7% correct; Qwen's: 64.4%, its largest gap). The instruct-tuned Qwen, run on the same raw text without a chat template, is slightly overconfident (average top-1 confidence 46.2% against 44.1% correct), and dividing its logits by \(T = 1.05\) cuts its ECE from 0.022 to 0.012. These are small models on 20,480 tokens of one Wikipedia sample (Qwen may have seen Wikipedia in training; GPT-2's WebText removed it, {{Radford et al., 2019, section 2.1|https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf}}), so read them as a demonstration of the measurement, not as a ranking.

Calibration can be lost. {{Guo et al., 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599}} found deep image classifiers (a 110-layer ResNet on CIFAR-100, in their Figure 1, against a well-calibrated 5-layer LeNet from 1998) overconfident; their Table 1 uses the same 15 bins as here, and that temperature scaling, dividing every logit by one number \(T\) fitted on held-out data, "is surprisingly effective at calibrating predictions". It cannot change which class is top (dividing by \(T \gt 0\) keeps the order), so accuracy is untouched. Post-training can also cost calibration: the {{GPT-4 technical report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774}} (Figure 8) says "the pre-trained model is highly calibrated" on an MMLU subset, and that "post-training hurts calibration significantly". Label smoothing changes the target, so it trades calibration of the top class for other behaviour (measured on {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}).

ECE has known weaknesses: it depends on the number of bins, it looks only at the top class, and a model can score 0 while saying nothing useful (always predicting the base rate is calibrated). Report it beside the NLL or Brier score, which are proper. For judge models, calibration of a judge's scores is on {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}.

What it is used for in ML
Trusting a classifier's probability as a probability (risk thresholds, abstaining when unsure, cascades that escalate low-confidence cases); temperature scaling after training; checking a post-trained model's confidence before using it as a verifier or judge; choosing proper metrics (log loss, Brier) over accuracy when probabilities matter.

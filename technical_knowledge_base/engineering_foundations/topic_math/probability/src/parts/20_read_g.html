8. Proper scoring rules and calibration, measured on real models

Could a model lower its log loss by exaggerating its confidence? No. A scoring rule \(S(r, y)\) charges a forecaster who announced \(r\) when \(y\) happened; it is proper if, when the true probability is \(q\), the expected charge \(\mathbb{E}_{y \sim q}[S(r, y)]\) is lowest at \(r = q\), and strictly proper if that is the only minimum ({{Gneiting and Raftery, 2007|https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437}}). Under a strictly proper rule the best strategy is to report what you believe.

The numbers (true \(q = 0.7\))
Log loss: reporting 0.7 costs 0.611 on average; exaggerating to 0.9 costs 0.765; hedging to 0.5 costs 0.693. Honesty wins.
Brier score \((r - y)^2\), also strictly proper: \(q(1 - r)^2 + (1 - q)r^2\) is 0.21 at 0.7 and 0.25 at 0.9.
Absolute error \(|r - y|\) is improper: its expectation \(q(1 - r) + (1 - q)r\) is linear in \(r\), 0.42 at 0.7 but 0.30 at \(r = 1\). It rewards claiming certainty, for the same reason L1 picks the median, and the median of a Bernoulli with \(q \gt 0.5\) is 1. Accuracy only looks at which side of 0.5 you are on, so it cannot reward honest probabilities either.
Why the log loss is strictly proper (proof)

The expected log loss is \(f(r) = -[q\ln r + (1 - q)\ln(1 - r)]\). Set its derivative to zero:

\[ f'(r) = -\frac{q}{r} + \frac{1 - q}{1 - r} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; q(1 - r) = r(1 - q) \;\Longrightarrow\; r = q, \]

and \(f''(r) = q/r^2 + (1 - q)/(1 - r)^2 \gt 0\), so it is the unique minimum. For \(K\) classes the expected loss minus its minimum is \(\sum_k q_k \ln(q_k / r_k)\), the KL divergence, zero only at \(r = q\) (Gibbs' inequality, on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}).

So a model trained by maximum likelihood, on data like its test data, should come out calibrated: among predictions made with confidence about 70%, about 70% are right (\(P(Y = \hat y \mid \hat c = c) = c\)). A reliability diagram bins predictions by confidence (15 bins here) and plots each bin's accuracy against its confidence; the expected calibration error is the size-weighted gap ({{Guo et al., 2017, eq. 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599}}):

\[ \text{ECE} = \sum_{b=1}^{B} \frac{n_b}{n}\,\big|\operatorname{acc}(b) - \operatorname{conf}(b)\big|. \]

Dividing every logit by \(T\) before the softmax is temperature scaling. At \(T = 0.6\) every GPT-2 bar falls below the diagonal (overconfident: ECE 0.250 against 0.015); at 1.6 they rise above it. The instruct model's bars at \(T = 1\) sit a little below, and its best \(T\) by NLL is 1.05. Accuracy never moves: dividing by \(T\) keeps the top token. The NLL is proper, so the \(T\) minimising it is the temperature-scaling fit (Guo et al. fit it on a validation set; here it is fitted and shown on the same tokens, an in-sample illustration); ECE's minimum need not coincide with it.

Both base models are close to the diagonal: when they put 70% on their top token, they are right about 70% of the time (GPT-2's 0.700 bin: 71.7% correct; Qwen's: 64.4%, its largest gap). The instruct-tuned Qwen, run on raw text without a chat template, is slightly overconfident (average top-1 confidence 46.2% against 44.1% correct), and \(T = 1.05\) cuts its ECE from 0.022 to 0.012. Small models on 20,480 tokens of one Wikipedia sample (Qwen may have seen Wikipedia in training; GPT-2's WebText removed it, {{Radford et al., 2019, section 2.1|https://cdn.openai.com/better-language-models/language_models_are_unsupervised_multitask_learners.pdf}}): a demonstration of the measurement, not a ranking.

Calibration can be lost, by depth and by post-training, and one fitted temperature recovers much of it (evidence below). Report ECE beside the NLL or Brier score, which are proper: ECE depends on the number of bins, looks only at the top class, and scores 0 for a model that always predicts the base rate.

The evidence that calibration is lost and fixed, and what calibration is used for (depth)

{{Guo et al., 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599}} found deep image classifiers overconfident (a 110-layer ResNet on CIFAR-100, their Figure 1, against a well-calibrated 5-layer LeNet from 1998; their Table 1 uses the same 15 bins as here) and that temperature scaling "is surprisingly effective at calibrating predictions". The {{GPT-4 technical report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774}} (Figure 8) says "the pre-trained model is highly calibrated" on an MMLU subset, and that "post-training hurts calibration significantly". Label smoothing changes the target, so it trades calibration of the top class for other behaviour (measured on {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}). Metric definitions are on {{Evaluation metrics|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a29acef722e842a29b}}; a judge's calibration on {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}.

Used for: trusting a classifier's probability as a probability (risk thresholds, abstaining when unsure, cascades that escalate low-confidence cases); temperature scaling after training; checking a post-trained model's confidence before using it as a verifier or judge; choosing proper metrics (log loss, Brier) over accuracy when probabilities matter.

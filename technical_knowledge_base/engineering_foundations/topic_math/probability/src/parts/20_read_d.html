5. Conditioning, independence and Bayes' rule

The question. A toxicity filter catches 95% of toxic messages and wrongly flags only 5% of clean ones. It flags a message. How likely is the message to be toxic? (Most people say about 95%. The answer below is 16%.)

The umbrella example (carried from the old page and rechecked): \(X = 1\) "it rained", \(P(X = 1) = 0.3\); \(Y = 1\) "carried an umbrella", with \(P(Y = 1 \mid X = 1) = 0.8\) and \(P(Y = 1 \mid X = 0) = 0.2\). The joint distribution, one cell per pair:

  • Joint: the four cells. Marginal: sum out the other variable, \(P(x) = \sum_y P(x, y)\), the sums in the margins (for densities, \(p(x) = \int p(x, y)\,dy\)). "Marginalising out" is the most common move in probabilistic ML: the evidence in Bayes' rule, the likelihood of a latent-variable model and the ELBO are marginals.
  • Conditional: one row or column, renormalised, \(P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)\): given rain, \((0.24, 0.06)/0.30 = (0.8, 0.2)\). The product rule \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)\) builds the cells: \(0.8 \times 0.3 = 0.24\). The law of total probability \(P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)\) builds the margins: \(P(Y = 1) = 0.8 \times 0.3 + 0.2 \times 0.7 = 0.38\). So \(P(X = 1 \mid Y = 1) = 0.24 / 0.38 = 0.632\): the umbrella raised rain from 0.3 to 0.632.

The chain rule. Applying the product rule repeatedly, with no assumption at all, \(p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \prod_{t=1}^{n} p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\). This identity is the whole design of an autoregressive language model: it learns only the conditional \(p(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\), one softmax per position, and the log of the product is the sum of per-token losses. If the earlier words had probabilities \(q_1\) and \(q_2\), "the cat sat" gets \(q_1 \times q_2 \times 0.090\) and loss \(-\ln q_1 - \ln q_2 + 2.408\). Masked and diffusion language models factor the joint differently; perplexity and bits per byte, sums of per-token losses, are on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}.

Independence, conditional independence, and the two meanings of "independent"

\(A\) and \(B\) are independent when \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\), a property of the joint and nothing else. Here the cell is 0.24 but \(P(X = 1)P(Y = 1) = 0.3 \times 0.38 = 0.114\): rain and umbrellas are dependent. The experimental meaning is different: the "independent variable" is what you set or feed in (the input \(x\), a feature or covariate) and the "dependent variable" is what you measure (the target \(y\)). The two meet in one sentence: learning is possible only because input and target are dependent in the probability sense. If \(P(y \mid x) = P(y)\), the best any model can do is predict the base rate.

Conditional independence, \(X \perp Y \mid Z\), means \(P(X, Y \mid Z) = P(X \mid Z)\,P(Y \mid Z)\): once \(Z\) is known, the other adds nothing. It powers Naive Bayes (\(P(x_1, \dots, x_d \mid c) = \prod_j P(x_j \mid c)\)), graphical models (the graph is a map of which conditional independences hold) and the i.i.d. assumption (training examples independent and identically distributed, so a dataset's probability factors into a product over examples, section 7). Dependent variables can be conditionally independent: ice-cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, and are roughly independent given the temperature.

Independence for random variables, and an independent version of the umbrella (depth)

For random variables, \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent when \(P(x, y) = P(x)P(y)\) holds for every pair of values; equivalently \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\). Had both umbrella conditionals been equal (say both 0.38), every cell would equal the product of its marginals and the two would be independent. A controlled input is not thereby statistically independent of anything. For the tiny model, the next word after "the cat" is not distributed like a random word; that dependence is what it learns.

Bayes' rule, with base rates

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \text{not } A)P(\text{not } A)}. \]

\(P(A)\) is the prior (for a class, its base rate), \(P(B \mid A)\) the likelihood, \(P(A \mid B)\) the posterior, and \(P(B)\) the evidence (marginal likelihood) that makes it add to 1: posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Counting with natural frequencies (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage, 1995, who showed people reason with them far better): of 10,000 messages with 1% toxic, 100 are toxic and 95 of those are flagged; 9,900 are clean and 495 of those are flagged. Of the 590 flagged, only 95 are toxic:

\[ P(\text{toxic} \mid \text{flag}) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.01}{0.95 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99} = \frac{95}{95 + 495} = 0.161. \]

Predict, then drag: which lifts precision more at a 1% base rate, sensitivity from 95% to 100%, or the false positive rate from 5% to 0.5%?

Used for: reading any detector (toxicity, fraud, a judge flagging failures) against its base rate; precision against recall on imbalanced data ({{Evaluation metrics|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a29acef722e842a29b}}); correcting a classifier trained on balanced data and deployed where the class is rare (multiply its output odds by the deployment prior odds over the training prior odds, Bayes' rule applied twice); Naive Bayes; and the posterior over parameters (section 9), the same rule with \(A\) = "the parameters are \(\theta\)" and \(B\) = "the data".

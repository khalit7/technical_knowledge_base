3. Random variables: PMF, PDF and CDF

The question. Events are sets of outcomes, but models output numbers: a word index, a pixel value, a count of clicks. How do we attach probabilities to numbers, including numbers that can take any real value?

The words

A random variable is a rule that turns each outcome into a number. Capitals name the variable (\(X\), \(Y\)); lower case names a value it might take (\(x\), \(y\)). For the tiny model, let \(Y\) be the index of the next word: 1 for cat, 2 for dog, 3 for sat. Then "\(Y = 3\)" is an event, and \(P(Y = 3) = 0.090\). We write \(Y \sim p\) ("\(Y\) is distributed as \(p\)", or "\(Y\) is drawn from \(p\)").

A random variable is discrete when its values can be listed (word indices, counts, labels) and continuous when it takes values in an interval (a temperature, a weight, a pixel intensity in \([0, 1]\)).

Discrete: the probability mass function

The probability mass function (PMF) lists the probability of each value: \(p(x) = P(X = x)\), with \(p(x) \ge 0\) and \(\sum_x p(x) = 1\). The tiny model's PMF is \(p(1) = 0.665\), \(p(2) = 0.245\), \(p(3) = 0.090\).

Continuous: the probability density function

For a continuous variable, any single exact value has probability 0 (there are infinitely many values to share it with), so probabilities come from areas. A probability density function (PDF) \(p(x)\) gives probabilities by integration:

\[ P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b p(x)\, dx, \qquad p(x) \ge 0, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x)\, dx = 1. \]
\(\int_a^b p(x)\,dx\)
the area under the curve \(p\) between \(a\) and \(b\): add up \(p(x)\) times a tiny width \(dx\) across the interval.

In plain words: a density is probability per unit of \(x\). Only areas under it are probabilities, so a density can be larger than 1.

The numbers
A uniform variable on \([0, 0.5]\) has density 2 everywhere on that interval (width 0.5 times height 2 is area 1). A Gaussian with standard deviation 0.1 has peak density \(1/\sqrt{2\pi \times 0.01} = 3.989\). Neither is a probability above 1; both are densities. This matters for losses: the Gaussian NLL \(-\ln p(y)\) can be negative, because \(p(y)\) can exceed 1.

Both kinds: the cumulative distribution function

The cumulative distribution function (CDF) works for both: \(F(x) = P(X \le x)\). It rises from 0 to 1 and never decreases. For a density, \(F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} p(t)\,dt\) and, the other way round, \(p(x) = F'(x)\) (the density is the slope of the CDF). For the tiny model, with words in the order cat, dog, sat, the CDF steps through 0.665, 0.910, 1.

The numbers (the standard Gaussian, mean 0 and standard deviation 1)
\(F(1) = \Phi(1) = 0.8413\), where \(\Phi\) (capital phi) is the standard Gaussian CDF. So \(P(-1 \le X \le 1) = \Phi(1) - \Phi(-1) = 0.6827\); within 2 standard deviations 0.9545; within 3, 0.9973 (the "68, 95, 99.7" rule); within 1.96, exactly 0.95, which is where the 1.96 in 95% error bars comes from.

The CDF is also how we sample: feed a uniform random number into the inverse of \(F\) (section 13). And a value's quantile is the inverse CDF: the median is \(F^{-1}(0.5)\).

What it is used for in ML
PMFs are what classifiers and language models output; densities are what regression, VAEs and diffusion models output or assume; CDFs give quantiles (quantile regression, the pinball loss on {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}), p-values and confidence intervals (on {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}), and inverse-CDF sampling. To see each PMF, PDF and CDF move with its parameters, open the {{Distribution explorer|#t-dist}}.

4. Expectation, variance, covariance, correlation

The question. A loss is a number per example. Training minimises "the loss", one number. Which single number summarises a whole distribution of losses, and how do we say how spread out it is?

Expectation: the probability-weighted average

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\, p(x) \quad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\, p(x)\, dx \quad \text{(continuous)}. \]
\(\mathbb{E}\)
"expected value" or "mean". \(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)]\) means the average of \(f(x)\) when \(x\) is drawn from \(p\); papers write it everywhere.

In plain words: each value counts in proportion to how likely it is; it is the long-run average over many draws.

The average of a function of \(X\) needs no new distribution: \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \sum_x f(x)\,p(x)\) (sometimes called the law of the unconscious statistician, because one uses it without thinking).

The numbers
Die: \(\mathbb{E}[X] = (1 + 2 + \dots + 6)/6 = 3.5\), a value the die never shows. Tiny model: if the next word really were drawn from the model's own bet \(p\), its expected loss is \[ \mathbb{E}[-\ln p(Y)] = -(0.665 \ln 0.665 + 0.245 \ln 0.245 + 0.090 \ln 0.090) = 0.832 \text{ nats}. \] That number has a name, the entropy of \(p\) (owned by {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}): the loss a model expects if it is right about its own uncertainty.

Linearity of expectation

\[ \mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y] \]

for any constants \(a, b\) and any random variables, whether or not they are independent. The proof is one line: \(\sum_{x,y}(ax + by)\,p(x,y) = a\sum_x x\,p(x) + b\sum_y y\,p(y)\), summing the joint over the other variable each time. It is why the expected loss of a batch is the sum of expected per-example losses, why a minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient, and why counting by indicators works.

The numbers
Shuffle 10 name tags at random onto 10 people. Expected number who get their own tag? Let \(I_k = 1\) if person \(k\) does; \(\mathbb{E}[I_k] = P(\text{own tag}) = 1/10\). By linearity the expected count is \(10 \times 1/10 = 1\), although the \(I_k\) are dependent (if nine match, the tenth must). The same argument says a model that gives the right token probability \(q_t\) at each of \(n\) positions gets \(\sum_t q_t\) right in expectation, however the positions depend on each other.

Variance and standard deviation

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}[X])^2\big] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2. \]

In plain words: the average squared distance from the mean. Its square root, the standard deviation \(\sigma\), is in the same units as \(X\).

The second form follows by expanding the square and using linearity: \(\mathbb{E}[X^2 - 2X\mu + \mu^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - 2\mu^2 + \mu^2\) with \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]\). Scaling: \(\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)\). Die: \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 35/12 = 2.917\).

Covariance and correlation

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \mathbb{E}X)(Y - \mathbb{E}Y)\big] = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \rho = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X\, \sigma_Y}. \]

In plain words: covariance is positive when the two tend to be above their means together. The correlation \(\rho\) (rho) rescales it to lie between \(-1\) and 1, and measures only linear association.

For sums: \(\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)\). For independent variables the covariance is 0, so variances add; that is why the variance of an average of \(n\) independent draws is \(\sigma^2 / n\) (the root of every error bar, on {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}).

The numbers
Umbrella: \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.24\), so \(\operatorname{Cov} = 0.24 - 0.3 \times 0.38 = 0.126\); \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 0.38 \times 0.62 = 0.2356\); \(\rho = 0.126 / \sqrt{0.21 \times 0.2356} = 0.566\).
Zero correlation does not mean independent. Let \(X\) be \(-1\), 0 or 1 with equal chance, and \(Y = X^2\). Then \(\operatorname{Cov} = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0 - 0 = 0\), yet \(Y\) is a function of \(X\). Independence implies zero covariance, not the reverse (except for jointly Gaussian variables). Mutual information (on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}) catches any dependence.

For a vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\), the covariance matrix \(\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^\top]\) has the variances on its diagonal and the covariances off it. A categorical variable written as a one-hot vector has mean \(p\) and covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\); for the tiny model, the diagonal is \((0.223, 0.185, 0.082)\) (each \(p_i(1 - p_i)\)) and, for example, \(\operatorname{Cov}(\text{cat}, \text{dog}) = -0.665 \times 0.245 = -0.163\). The same matrix is the softmax's Jacobian and the cross-entropy's Hessian in the scores (section 7).

The law of total expectation, and of total variance

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big], \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(X \mid Y)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big). \]

In plain words: average within each group, then average the group averages by group size. The spread splits into the average spread within groups plus the spread between group averages.

The numbers (illustrative)
Suppose 70% of tokens are easy, with mean loss 1.0 and variance 0.5, and 30% are hard, with mean loss 4.0 and variance 2.0. Mean loss: \(0.7 \times 1.0 + 0.3 \times 4.0 = 1.9\). Variance: within \(0.7 \times 0.5 + 0.3 \times 2.0 = 0.95\), between \(0.7 \times (1 - 1.9)^2 + 0.3 \times (4 - 1.9)^2 = 1.89\), total 2.84. Most of the spread is between the groups, which is why loss curves are often reported by data source.
What it is used for in ML
Every training objective is an expectation over data, \(\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \text{data}}[\ell]\), estimated by a minibatch average (unbiased by linearity); variance and covariance describe gradient noise, feature scaling and normalisation layers; covariance matrices drive PCA, whitening and the multivariate Gaussian (section 6); the law of total variance is the decomposition behind ensembles and the bias-variance split (on {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}).

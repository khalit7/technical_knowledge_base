3. Expectation and variance, and the algebra ML leans on

\(\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \sum_x f(x)\,p(x)\) (an integral for densities) is the probability-weighted average; a training objective is \(\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \text{data}}[\ell]\). On the tiny model, if the next word really were drawn from its own bet, the expected loss is

\[ \mathbb{E}[-\ln p(Y)] = -(0.665 \ln 0.665 + 0.245 \ln 0.245 + 0.090 \ln 0.090) = 0.832 \text{ nats}, \]

the entropy of \(p\) (owned by {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}). Four rules carry almost every argument in ML papers:

  • Linearity holds without independence: \(\mathbb{E}[aX + bY] = a\,\mathbb{E}[X] + b\,\mathbb{E}[Y]\) for any random variables. So a minibatch gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient, and counting by indicators works: shuffle 10 name tags onto 10 people and the expected number of people with their own tag is \(10 \times 1/10 = 1\), though the matches are dependent (if nine match, the tenth must). A model giving the right token probability \(q_t\) at each of \(n\) positions gets \(\sum_t q_t\) right in expectation, however the positions depend on each other.
  • Variances add only for uncorrelated variables: \(\operatorname{Var}(X + Y) = \operatorname{Var}(X) + \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(aX + b) = a^2 \operatorname{Var}(X)\). For \(n\) independent draws, the variance of their average is \(\sigma^2 / n\): the root of every error bar (section 4).
  • Zero correlation does not mean independent. \(X\) uniform on \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\) and \(Y = X^2\): \(\operatorname{Cov} = \mathbb{E}[X^3] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 0\), yet \(Y\) is a function of \(X\). Correlation \(\rho = \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)/(\sigma_X \sigma_Y)\) measures only linear association (jointly Gaussian variables are the exception); mutual information (on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}) catches any dependence.
  • A one-hot categorical has covariance \(\operatorname{diag}(p) - pp^\top\): for the tiny model the diagonal is \((0.223, 0.185, 0.082)\) (each \(p_i(1 - p_i)\)) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\text{cat}, \text{dog}) = -0.665 \times 0.245 = -0.163\). The same matrix is the softmax's Jacobian and the cross-entropy's Hessian in the scores (section 7).
Definitions, the die and umbrella numbers, and the laws of total expectation and variance (depth)

\(\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x)\); \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \sum_x f(x)\,p(x)\) needs no new distribution (the law of the unconscious statistician). Linearity in one line: \(\sum_{x,y}(ax + by)\,p(x,y) = a\sum_x x\,p(x) + b\sum_y y\,p(y)\). \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mathbb{E}X)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2] - \mathbb{E}[X]^2\) (expand the square, use linearity); the standard deviation \(\sigma\) is its square root, in the units of \(X\). \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]\). For a vector, the covariance matrix is \(\Sigma = \mathbb{E}[(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^\top]\).

Die: \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 3.5\), a value it never shows; \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 35/12 = 2.917\). Umbrella (section 5): \(\mathbb{E}[XY] = P(X = 1, Y = 1) = 0.24\), so \(\operatorname{Cov} = 0.24 - 0.3 \times 0.38 = 0.126\); \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = 0.3 \times 0.7 = 0.21\), \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) = 0.38 \times 0.62 = 0.2356\); \(\rho = 0.126 / \sqrt{0.21 \times 0.2356} = 0.566\).

\[ \mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big], \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(X \mid Y)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\big). \]

Average within each group, then average the group averages; the spread splits into within-group plus between-group. Illustrative: 70% of tokens easy (mean loss 1.0, variance 0.5), 30% hard (mean 4.0, variance 2.0). Mean \(0.7 \times 1.0 + 0.3 \times 4.0 = 1.9\); within \(0.7 \times 0.5 + 0.3 \times 2.0 = 0.95\), between \(0.7 \times (1 - 1.9)^2 + 0.3 \times (4 - 1.9)^2 = 1.89\), total 2.84. Most of the spread is between groups, which is why loss curves are often reported by data source. The law of total variance is the decomposition behind ensembles and the bias-variance split (on {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}). Variance and covariance also describe gradient noise, feature scaling and normalisation layers, and covariance matrices drive PCA and whitening.

Two classic counting results: birthday collisions and the coupon collector (depth; moved from the root's retired Practice drills)

Birthday collisions. With 23 people and 365 equally likely birthdays, the chance that all differ is \(\prod_{i=0}^{22} \frac{365 - i}{365} = 0.4927\), so at least one pair shares a birthday with probability 0.5073: just over even odds. There are \(\binom{23}{2} = 253\) pairs, each with a 1/365 chance, and a collision becomes likely after about \(\sqrt{2 \ln 2 \cdot N}\) draws from \(N\) values ({{Birthday problem|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birthday_problem}}). Collisions arrive after about \(\sqrt{N}\) draws, not \(N\): hashing into \(N = 2^{32}\) ids (feature hashing, dedup fingerprints) gives even odds of a collision after only about 77,163 items, and sampling with replacement repeats examples sooner than intuition says.

The coupon collector (linearity of expectation at work). A sampler returns one of 10 classes uniformly each draw. Having seen \(k\) classes, a new one appears with probability \((10 - k)/10\), a geometric wait of \(10/(10 - k)\) draws on average; expectations add, so seeing every class takes \(10\left(1 + \frac12 + \dots + \frac1{10}\right) = 10 H_{10} = 29.29\) draws on average, about three times the number of classes, and the last class alone takes 10. In general \(n H_n \approx n \ln n\) ({{Coupon collector's problem|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupon_collector%27s_problem}}). It sizes how many samples until every class, expert or shard is seen, and how many generations until every distinct answer appears in sampling-based evaluation and pass@k.

4. The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, by simulation

Most expectations in ML (an expected reward, an expected loss over all texts, a predictive integral) are sums over too many outcomes to compute, so they are estimated by an average of draws, the Monte Carlo estimate:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] \approx \hat\mu_n = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i), \qquad \operatorname{SE}(\hat\mu_n) = \frac{\sigma_f}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

Two theorems say why this works. The law of large numbers: \(\hat\mu_n\) converges to \(\mathbb{E}[f]\) as \(n\) grows (it is unbiased by linearity). The central limit theorem: for finite variance, the error \(\hat\mu_n - \mathbb{E}[f]\) is close to Gaussian with standard deviation \(\sigma_f/\sqrt{n}\), whatever the shape of \(f\)'s distribution. On the tiny model, \(f = -\ln p(\text{word})\) takes only three values (0.408, 1.408, 2.408), with mean 0.832 and standard deviation 0.651: nothing Gaussian about it.

Predict, then step: at \(n = 100\) token losses per average, how wide is the spread of the averages? (Answer: \(0.651/\sqrt{100} = 0.065\); 10,000 draws give 0.0065.) And at \(n = 1\), does "within 1.96 standard errors" catch 95% of draws?

Four times the samples halve the error, whatever the dimension: that is why Monte Carlo is the default in high dimensions, and why an eval of 100 items cannot separate two models 2 points apart (a 70% accuracy on 100 items has standard error \(\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3/100} = 0.046\), 4.6 points). Where the Gaussian approximation fails (heavy tails, infinite variance, small \(n\) on a skewed metric), and how to build intervals and tests from it, is {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}'s.

Used for: minibatch gradients (a Monte Carlo estimate of the full-data gradient); policy gradients and RL returns; the ELBO and diffusion losses (sampled noise and time steps); MCMC predictive averages; pass@k and other sampled eval metrics.

Importance sampling: estimating under \(p\) with draws from \(q\) (depth)

If you can only sample from \(q\), reweight: \(\mathbb{E}_p[f] = \mathbb{E}_q\big[f(x)\, p(x)/q(x)\big]\). The ratio \(p/q\) is the importance weight; with \(q\) uniform over the tiny model's three words, the weights are \(3p = (1.996, 0.734, 0.270)\). It is unbiased but its variance explodes when \(q\) puts little mass where \(p\) has a lot. PPO's probability ratio and every off-policy correction are importance weights (on {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}); the IWAE bound is importance sampling inside the ELBO (on {{Variational inference and generative-model maths|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}).

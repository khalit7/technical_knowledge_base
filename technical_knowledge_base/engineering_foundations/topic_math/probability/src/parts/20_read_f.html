7. From distribution to loss: maximum likelihood

A loss is not a free design choice. The recipe is three moves: (1) say which distribution \(y\) follows given the input, with the network outputting its parameter; (2) the loss for one example is \(-\ln p(y \mid \text{output})\); (3) drop every term that does not depend on the output. For i.i.d. examples the probability of the dataset is a product, so the log-likelihood is a sum, \(\ln p(D \mid \theta) = \sum_i \ln p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta)\), and

\[ \hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \ln p(D \mid \theta) = \arg\min_\theta \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(-\ln p(y_i \mid x_i, \theta)\big). \]

Training a network with BCE, cross-entropy, MSE, L1 or the Poisson loss is maximum-likelihood estimation under the matching distribution:

Why "prediction minus target" every time. Bernoulli, categorical, Gaussian (fixed \(\sigma\)) and Poisson are all in the exponential family, \(p(y \mid \eta) = h(y)\exp\big(\eta^\top T(y) - A(\eta)\big)\), where \(\eta\), the natural parameter, is exactly what the last layer outputs (the logit, the logits, the log-rate, the mean), \(T(y)\) is the sufficient statistic (\(y\) or the one-hot vector) and \(A\) is the log-partition function, the log of the normaliser. Differentiating \(A\) gives the mean, \(A'(\eta) = \mathbb{E}[T(Y)]\), so the NLL \(\ell = A(\eta) - \eta T(y) - \ln h(y)\) has

\[ \frac{d\ell}{d\eta} = \mathbb{E}[T(Y)] - T(y), \qquad \frac{d^2\ell}{d\eta^2} = A''(\eta) = \operatorname{Var}(T) \ge 0: \]

what the model expected minus what was observed, with a curvature that is the model's own variance, independent of the label and never negative, so the loss is convex in the logits. On the tiny model with target sat, the gradient is \(p - e_{\text{sat}} = (0.665, 0.245, -0.910)\), the root page's famous result; had the target been cat, \(\ell = -2 + 2.408 = 0.408\) and the gradient \((-0.335, 0.245, 0.090)\). Bernoulli curvature \(p(1 - p)\) is 0.25 at \(z = 0\) and 0.09 at \(z = \ln 9 = 2.197\) (where \(p = 0.9\)): a confident output sits on a flatter part of the loss. That same number is the Hessian in the logit, the variance of the Bernoulli and its Fisher information; the three readings are on the root's "Curvature is read three ways".

The numbers (spam filter, carried and rechecked)
Output \(\hat p = 0.8\). Spam (\(y = 1\)): loss \(-\ln 0.8 = 0.223\), gradient \(0.8 - 1 = -0.2\). Not spam (\(y = 0\)): loss \(-\ln 0.2 = 1.609\), gradient \(0.8\). The confident wrong answer costs 7.2 times the right one, without bound as it nears certainty. Squared error on a yes-or-no label is a Gaussian model of a binary outcome, the wrong distribution, and through a sigmoid it barely moves a confidently wrong model (what the logit gradient means, measured: {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}). The loss and the distribution are one decision.

What MLE is, in one more sentence each. The likelihood \(L(\theta) = p(D \mid \theta)\) is not a distribution over \(\theta\): a coin with 3 heads in 3 has \(L(p) = p^3\), whose area over \([0, 1]\) is \(1/4\), not 1; turning it into one needs a prior (section 9). Dividing the NLL by \(n\) gives the cross-entropy from the data's empirical distribution to the model, which is \(\text{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_\theta)\) plus a constant, so MLE minimises forward KL and is "mass-covering" (it pays heavily for giving any observed outcome tiny probability; on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}). And on small data it overfits: after 3 heads in 3 flips, \(\hat p_{\text{MLE}} = 1\), a confident claim that tails is impossible, the reason for section 9. Its estimator properties (bias, variance, consistency) are on {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}.

Used for: pretraining (next-token MLE), supervised fine-tuning, every classifier and regressor; fitting any distribution's parameters (a Gaussian for anomaly detection, a Poisson for counts); why outputs are left as logits and the loss is computed on them (fused, stable, convex in the logit); which output activation goes with which target. The catalogue of losses beyond these five (focal, Huber, hinge, contrastive, z-loss) is on {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}; the gradient checks are in the root's {{Gradient lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}}.

Each of the five losses derived, with numbers (depth)

Bernoulli to BCE. \(y \mid x \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\hat p)\), \(\hat p = \sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\): \(\ell = -\big[y \ln \hat p + (1 - y)\ln(1 - \hat p)\big]\). Using \(\ln \hat p = -\ln(1 + e^{-z})\) and \(\ln(1 - \hat p) = -z - \ln(1 + e^{-z})\): \(\ell = \ln(1 + e^{z}) - yz\), the form \(A(z) - zT(y)\), so \(\partial \ell / \partial z = \sigma(z) - y = \hat p - y\). Through the chain rule instead, \(\partial \ell/\partial \hat p = (\hat p - y)/(\hat p(1 - \hat p))\) and \(\sigma'(z) = \hat p(1 - \hat p)\) cancel ({{Calculus and optimisation for ML, section 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eb8b49ed6a0f897f3b#rd-loss}} has every step). PyTorch's torch.distributions.Bernoulli.log_prob is literally -binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logits, value) (torch 2.14.1 source, saved in src/inputs/).

Categorical to softmax cross-entropy. \(\ell = -\ln p_y = -z_y + \ln \sum_{k} e^{z_k}\), \(\partial \ell/\partial z_k = p_k - [k = y]\): the derivative of \(\ln\sum_k e^{z_k}\) in \(z_k\) is \(p_k\), that of \(-z_y\) is \(-1\) for \(k = y\). With \(K = 2\) and \(z = (z_1, 0)\) this is exactly sigmoid plus BCE. Tiny model, target sat: \(\ell = -z_{\text{sat}} + \ln 11.107 = 0 + 2.408 = 2.408\); raise the right score, lower the others in proportion to the probability they took.

Gaussian to squared error. \(y \mid x \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat y, \sigma^2)\), \(\sigma\) fixed: \(\ell = \frac{(y - \hat y)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \tfrac12 \ln(2\pi\sigma^2)\), \(\partial \ell/\partial \hat y = (\hat y - y)/\sigma^2\); MSE is Gaussian maximum likelihood, and the constant minimising it over a sample is the mean. \(\sigma = 1\), prediction 2.5, target 3: \(\ell = 0.125 + 0.919 = 1.044\), of which only the 0.125 depends on the model; gradient \(-0.5\). Learning the variance too (heteroscedastic regression): \(\ell = \tfrac12\big(\ln \sigma^2 + (y - \hat y)^2/\sigma^2\big)\) plus a constant, exactly PyTorch's gaussian_nll_loss (with full=False it drops \(\tfrac12\ln 2\pi\), and it clamps \(\sigma^2\) at eps; torch 2.14.1 source in src/inputs/). The optimum is \(\sigma^2 = (y - \hat y)^2\): with residual 0.5, the loss is 0.125 at \(\sigma^2 = 1\) and \(-0.193\) at \(\sigma^2 = 0.25\). The \(1/\sigma^2\) weight lets the model give up on hard points by inflating \(\sigma^2\); {{Seitzer et al., 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.09168}} propose the \(\beta\)-NLL fix.

Laplace to absolute error. \(\ell = |y - \hat y|/b + \ln 2b\), gradient \(\operatorname{sign}(\hat y - y)/b\). Heavier tails than the Gaussian, so a large residual is charged linearly and one outlier cannot dominate; the minimising constant is the median. \(b = 1\), prediction 2.5, target 3: \(\ell = 0.5 + \ln 2 = 1.193\); gradient \(-1\).

Poisson to the Poisson loss. \(\lambda = e^{z}\) (the log link keeps the rate positive): \(\ell = \lambda - y\ln\lambda + \ln y! = e^{z} - yz + \ln y!\), gradient \(\lambda - y\); \(\ln y!\) is dropped (PyTorch's PoissonNLLLoss with log_input=True). Rate 2, observed 3: \(P(Y = 3) = 8e^{-2}/6 = 0.180\), NLL \(2 - 3\ln 2 + \ln 6 = 1.712\), model-dependent part \(2 - 3 \times 0.693 = -0.079\) (a loss that drops constants can be negative); gradient \(-1\).

\(h(y)\) is a base measure that does not depend on \(\eta\) (it drops out of every gradient); \(A(\eta) = \ln \sum_y h(y) e^{\eta^\top T(y)}\). Check one row by hand: \(p^y(1 - p)^{1 - y} = \exp\big(y \ln\frac{p}{1 - p} + \ln(1 - p)\big)\), and \(\ln(1 - p) = -\ln(1 + e^z)\), so \(\eta = z\), \(T = y\), \(A = \ln(1 + e^z)\), \(h = 1\). The Laplace is not in the family (\(|y - \mu|\) cannot be written as \(\eta\, T(y)\)). Reference: Bishop, PRML, section 2.4; Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction, section 3.4.

Fact 1: \(A'\) is the mean. With the derivative of \(\ln u\) being \(u'/u\):

\[ A'(\eta) = \frac{\sum_y h(y)\, T(y)\, e^{\eta T(y)}}{\sum_y h(y)\, e^{\eta T(y)}} = \sum_y T(y)\, \underbrace{h(y)\, e^{\eta T(y) - A(\eta)}}_{p(y \mid \eta)} = \mathbb{E}[T(Y)]. \]

Once more: \(A''(\eta) = \mathbb{E}[T^2] - \mathbb{E}[T]^2 = \operatorname{Var}(T)\), never negative, so \(A\) is convex. Fact 2 follows: \(\ell = A(\eta) - \eta T(y) - \ln h(y)\) has \(d\ell/d\eta = A'(\eta) - T(y)\).

When the network's output is linear in the features, this is a generalised linear model (GLM); the inverse of \(A'\) is the canonical link: logit for Bernoulli (logistic regression), log for Poisson (Poisson regression), identity for Gaussian (linear regression). At the maximum-likelihood optimum, the average predicted \(T\) equals the average observed \(T\) (set the summed gradient to zero), so a fitted logistic regression with an intercept predicts the training base rate exactly on average. The Fisher information equals \(A''\) with respect to \(\eta\); with respect to the weights it equals only the generalised Gauss-Newton part of the Hessian ({{Martens, 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.1193}}), which is what natural gradient uses. The exponential family is the maximum-entropy distribution matching given expectations (on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}); in energy-based models, \(A\) is the log of the partition function they cannot compute.

Two MLEs derived without skipping a step, and what MLE guarantees (depth)

The coin. \(h\) heads and \(t\) tails: \(\ell(p) = h\ln p + t\ln(1 - p)\), \(\ell'(p) = h/p - t/(1 - p) = 0\) gives \(h(1 - p) = tp\), so \(\hat p_{\text{MLE}} = h/(h + t)\), the observed frequency. As a function of \(y\) with \(\theta\) fixed the likelihood formula sums to 1; as a function of \(\theta\) with \(y\) fixed it need not. The log also stops thousands of small probabilities underflowing to zero (section 12) and makes minibatch training possible.

The Gaussian. \(\ell = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2\). In \(\mu\): \(\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu) = 0\) gives \(\hat\mu = \frac1n \sum_i x_i\). In \(\sigma^2\): \(-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 = 0\) gives \(\hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n \sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2\). Data \((2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9)\): \(\hat\mu = 40/8 = 5\); squared deviations sum to 32, so \(\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} = 32/8 = 4\), against the "divide by \(n - 1\)" estimate \(32/7 = 4.571\): the MLE of the variance is biased low because \(\hat\mu\) was fitted to the same data (why, and when it matters: {{Statistics for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d810f896cf688ce9aed1b}}).

Guarantees (under regularity conditions; Wasserman, All of Statistics, chapter 9). Consistent: \(\hat\theta\) converges to the true parameter as \(n\) grows, if the model family contains it. Asymptotically efficient: in the large-data limit no consistent estimator has lower variance (it reaches the Cramér-Rao bound, the inverse Fisher information). Invariant: the MLE of \(g(\theta)\) is \(g(\hat\theta)\). MLE is the baseline that MAP and Bayesian methods correct when data is scarce.

Probability for ML, in one screen

A model that predicts is a model that bets. Probability is the language of those bets: it says how to write a bet down (a distribution), how to update it when evidence arrives (conditioning and Bayes' rule), how to score it once the truth is known (the log-likelihood, which becomes the loss), and how to draw from it (sampling). Every idea below is used on the same running example as the {{Topic: math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cdb851c0835b25a538}} page: a tiny language model whose scores for the next word after "the cat" are \(z = (2, 1, 0)\) for cat, dog, sat, which softmax turns into the bet \(p = (0.665, 0.245, 0.090)\); the right word was sat, so the loss was \(-\ln 0.090 = 2.408\).

The one idea to keep
Pick a distribution for the target, let the network output its parameters, and minus the log of the probability it gives the truth is the loss. Every standard loss falls out of that move (section 8), its gradient is always "prediction minus target" (section 7), and the probabilities it trains are honest because the log loss is a proper scoring rule (section 9).

1. What a probability is: counting, and belief

The question. When the tiny model says "sat: 9%", what kind of statement is that? Nothing about the next word is random in the way a dice roll is.

The picture: outcomes, and the events made of them

Write down every outcome that could happen: that list is the sample space, written \(\Omega\) (capital omega). For a die, \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}\); for the tiny model's next word, \(\Omega = \{\text{cat}, \text{dog}, \text{sat}\}\). An event is any set of outcomes you can ask about: "the die shows an even number" is the event \(\{2, 4, 6\}\); "the next word is an animal" is \(\{\text{cat}, \text{dog}\}\). A probability \(P\) gives every event a number between 0 and 1.

The rules (Kolmogorov's three axioms)

Everything on this page follows from three rules, stated by Kolmogorov in 1933 ({{Foundations of the Theory of Probability, English translation, chapter 1|https://archive.org/details/foundationsofthe00kolm}}):

\[ P(A) \ge 0, \qquad P(\Omega) = 1, \qquad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \ \text{ when } A \cap B = \varnothing. \]
\(A, B\)
events (sets of outcomes).
\(A \cup B\)
"A or B": the outcomes in either. \(A \cap B\) is "A and B": the outcomes in both; \(\varnothing\) is the empty set, so \(A \cap B = \varnothing\) means they cannot both happen.

In plain words: probabilities are never negative, something certainly happens, and for things that cannot happen together, the chances add.

Two consequences used constantly: \(P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)\), and for any two events \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\) (subtract the overlap, which was counted twice).

The numbers
Fair die: \(P(\text{even}) = 3/6 = 0.5\); \(P(\text{at least } 5) = 2/6 = 0.333\). Tiny model: \(P(\text{animal}) = P(\text{cat}) + P(\text{dog})\) \(= 0.665 + 0.245 = 0.910\), by the third rule, since the next word cannot be both.

Two readings of the same rules

Counting (frequentist). A probability is the long-run fraction: roll the die many times and the share of even numbers settles at 0.5. This reading needs a repeatable experiment. Belief (Bayesian). A probability is a degree of belief, given what you know: "this checkpoint will beat the baseline: 70%" is about one event that happens once. Both readings obey the same three rules ({{Cox, 1946|https://doi.org/10.1119/1.1990764}}: his theorem shows that any consistent way of grading belief by numbers must follow them, up to relabelling), so the mathematics below holds whichever you prefer.

The tiny model's 9% is a belief: given the words "the cat", it would bet at those odds. It becomes checkable by counting when we collect many cases where it said 9% and see how often it was right; that is calibration, measured on three real models in the {{Calibration, real models|#t-cal}} tab.

What it is used for in ML
The sample space is the vocabulary (language models), the label set (classifiers), or the real line (regression). Belief probabilities are what a classifier's outputs mean; long-run frequencies are how we check them.

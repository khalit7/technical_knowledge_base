13. Hybrid search: words and meaning together

Lexical and vector search fail differently. BM25 misses paraphrases ("stock" for "share"); vectors miss exact identifiers and rare names, and sometimes rank a vaguely related text above an exact match. Running both and merging the two ranked lists often beats either, but not always: when one retriever is much stronger on your data, an equal-weight fusion can pull it down. Measure it. The two scores cannot simply be added: a BM25 score of 12.3 and a cosine of 0.81 are on unrelated scales. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) ({{Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher, SIGIR 2009|http://cormack.uwaterloo.ca/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf}}) ignores the scores and uses only the ranks:

RRF(d) = Σlists L 1 / (k + rankL(d))
rankL(d)
d's position in list L, starting at 1 (a list that does not contain d adds nothing)
k
60 in the paper ("fixed during a pilot investigation"); Elasticsearch's and OpenSearch's default rank_constant is also 60

RRF on the six questions: the BM25 ranking from section 4 and the cosine ranking from all-MiniLM-L6-v2 for the same query measured.

Try: with a small k the top of each list dominates; with a large k, appearing in both lists matters more than where. "How do I invest in stock market?" lacks the word "share" but ranks well by meaning, so fusion keeps it near the top; the Kohinoor question matches no query word, so only the vector list contributes to it.

Measured on real labelled data (1,000 Quora test questions over the 522,931, and all 300 SciFact claims over 5,183 scientific abstracts, the set the evaluation page also uses; top 100 from each retriever, scored with nDCG@10 and recall@10) measured:

The SQL you would deploy, one statement in Postgres (the pattern of {{pgvector-python's RRF example|https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector-python/blob/master/examples/hybrid_search/rrf.py}}; the lexical half here ranks by ts_rank, so it inherits section 4's weakness; it scored @@q_rrf_sql@@ on Quora at @@rrf_sql_p50@@ ms median measured):

WITH lex AS (
  SELECT id, row_number() OVER (ORDER BY ts_rank(tsv, query) DESC) AS r
  FROM q, websearch_to_tsquery('english', $1) query
  WHERE tsv @@ query ORDER BY ts_rank(tsv, query) DESC LIMIT 100),
sem AS (
  SELECT id, row_number() OVER (ORDER BY embedding <=> $2) AS r
  FROM (SELECT id, embedding FROM q ORDER BY embedding <=> $2 LIMIT 100) s)
SELECT coalesce(lex.id, sem.id) AS id,
       coalesce(1.0 / (60 + lex.r), 0) + coalesce(1.0 / (60 + sem.r), 0) AS score
FROM lex FULL JOIN sem USING (id)
ORDER BY score DESC LIMIT 10;

The measured version OR-ed the query's lexemes instead of websearch_to_tsquery's AND, because requiring every word left @@q_and_empty@@% of Quora questions with no lexical match at all.

Weighting. Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and Weaviate let you weight the lists or fuse normalised scores instead of ranks (OpenSearch's normalization processor, {{docs|https://docs.opensearch.org/latest/search-plugins/search-pipelines/normalization-processor/}}); tune the weights on a labelled set, never by eye. On Quora, where duplicates are paraphrases, the right weight for BM25 would be low; on SciFact, where claims share rare scientific terms with their abstracts, BM25 earns its place.

After fusion, many systems re-order the top 20 to 100 with a cross-encoder reranker, a model that reads query and document together; chunking, reranking and the rest of retrieval-augmented generation belong to {{Topic: rag-and-retrieval|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b89145c37dfe8a3b0b}}. Learned sparse vectors (SPLADE-style, stored in pgvector as sparsevec) are a third retriever some teams add.

14. pgvector or a dedicated vector database?

A "vector database" is an ANN index plus storage, filtering and an API, sold as its own system. pgvector adds the same index types to the Postgres you may already run. The honest comparison is about operations and size, not about the algorithm: most of them run HNSW.

Stay in Postgres (pgvector) when

  • the vectors describe rows you already keep there, and queries join or filter on them (tenant, permissions, dates);
  • you need the vector, its text and its metadata to change in one transaction, deletes to take effect at once (a user deletes a document; its embedding must stop being found), and one backup and failover story;
  • the index fits comfortably in the server's memory (see the arithmetic below).

Consider a dedicated store when

  • the index no longer fits one server's memory, or vector load would crowd out your transactional workload;
  • most queries are heavily filtered and you need in-graph filtering tuned for it, or thousands of isolated tenants;
  • you want disk-based or quantised indexes Postgres lacks (DiskANN, PQ, int8), GPU builds, or object-storage economics;
  • a separate team owns search and wants to scale and rebuild it independently.

"Under 10M vectors pgvector wins": unconfirmed
The often-quoted thresholds (pgvector below about 10 million vectors, a dedicated store above about 100 million) have no primary measurement behind them; the parent page's atlas marks them unconfirmed. They are where to start measuring, not a crossover. The evidence that exists is vendor-run: Tiger Data reported Postgres with pgvector plus their pgvectorscale extension (not plain pgvector) on 50M 768-dimension vectors at 99% recall with 28 times lower p95 latency than Pinecone's s1 pods ({{vendor blog, 2024-06-11|https://www.tigerdata.com/blog/pgvector-vs-pinecone}}); Zilliz, the company behind Milvus, publishes {{VectorDBBench|https://github.com/zilliztech/VectorDBBench}}.

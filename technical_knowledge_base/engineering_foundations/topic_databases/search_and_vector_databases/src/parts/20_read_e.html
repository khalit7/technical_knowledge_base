7. Vectors in Postgres: distance and storage

A vector is an ordered list of numbers; an embedding model is trained so that texts with similar meaning get vectors pointing in similar directions. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 turns the running question into 384 numbers, the first eight , with length (norm) @@qnorm@@ because the model normalises its output. No single number means anything on its own; only distances between vectors do. Training, choosing and judging models is {{Embedding models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81508f99e1fa78c2bb41}}'s. For the test question "@@ex_q@@" the three nearest stored questions by cosine were "@@ex1@@" (@@ex1c@@), "@@ex2@@" (@@ex2c@@) and "@@ex3@@" (@@ex3c@@) measured.

If every vector has length 1, cosine = dot product and |a−b|2 = 2 − 2·cos(a,b), so the three orders are identical. Most text models normalise (OpenAI says its embeddings are normalised to length 1, {{OpenAI embeddings guide|https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/embeddings}}), so pick the cheapest operator: pgvector's {{README|https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector#exact-search}} advises inner product, and the exact scan with <#> took @@exact_ip_p50@@ ms against @@exact_p50@@ ms with cosine measured. Without normalisation dot product favours long vectors, and length can carry meaning (some recommendation models let it encode popularity): use the measure the model was trained with, and index with the matching operator class, or the index is not used.

What a vector costs. pgvector stores a vector(n) as 4 bytes per number plus 8 of header: 384 × 4 + 8 = 1,544 bytes (confirmed by pg_column_size measured), and 6,152 for the common 1,536-dimension models ({{README|https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector#vector-type}}). The 522,931 vectors take @@vec_mb@@ MB, @@vec_share@@% of the whole @@heap_mb@@ MB table: the texts are tiny next to them. At 100 million 1,536-dimension vectors that is 615 GB before any index.

8. pgvector's indexes, measured: recall against latency

Exact k-nearest-neighbour (kNN) search computes the distance to every stored vector: 522,931 × 384 = about 201 million multiply-adds per query. Postgres ran ORDER BY embedding <=> $1 LIMIT 10 as a parallel sequential scan with a top-N heap sort, reading @@exact_buf@@ pages of 8 KB from memory, in @@exact_p50@@ ms median (@@exact_p95@@ ms p95) measured: fine for a nightly job, too slow for a search box, and linear in the table. An approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) index looks at a small part of the data and sometimes misses a true neighbour; its quality is recall@k, the share of the true k nearest it returns. Quote recall and latency together: any index is fast at low enough recall.

pgvector offers two ANN indexes and you choose one (there is no default). HNSW is a layered graph of near neighbours walked greedily; hnsw.ef_search (default 40) is the number of candidates a query keeps, its recall dial. IVFFlat files each vector under its nearest of lists k-means centroids and scans the closest ivfflat.probes lists (default 1). How both work inside is taught on {{Vector indexes from the inside|n:3f85c17b0d0d81f29707c130b2ebcfb1}} (IVF in section 3, HNSW in section 6, on the same 522,931 Quora vectors). Here is what they buy in Postgres. Predict: at the defaults, how many times faster than exact is HNSW, and what share of the true top 10 does it return?

The README's starting points for IVFFlat: lists = rows / 1000 up to 1M rows, √rows above; probes = √lists ({{README, IVFFlat|https://github.com/pgvector/pgvector#ivfflat}}). Build it after the data is loaded, or the centroids describe the wrong data, and rebuild it as new data drifts away from them. HNSW ({{Malkov and Yashunin, arXiv 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09320}}) has no training step, so it can be created on an empty table; builds are slower, the graph is fastest in RAM, every insert searches the graph, and deletes leave holes repaired only by VACUUM (section 13).

Exact search is still right when the set is small (thousands to roughly 100,000 vectors; the old page's "below about 100k" is a rule of thumb, not a measured line: at 100k vectors of 384 numbers it would be about a fifth of the time measured here), when a filter has already cut the candidates to a few thousand (section 10), and for the ground truth that measures an ANN index's recall.

Not in pgvector: product quantisation, int8, a disk-resident graph. Tiger Data's open pgvectorscale extension adds a StreamingDiskANN index ({{GitHub|https://github.com/timescale/pgvectorscale}}); Azure Database for PostgreSQL has pg_diskann (GA May 2025); Google AlloyDB has the alloydb_scann index ({{AlloyDB docs|https://docs.cloud.google.com/alloydb/docs/ai/store-index-query-vectors}}).

9. Smaller vectors in pgvector

Most of the memory is the vectors themselves, so quantisation, fewer bits per number, is the lever (how scalar, binary and product quantisation work is {{Vector indexes from the inside|n:3f85c17b0d0d81f29707c130b2ebcfb1}}'s sections 4 and 5). pgvector offers two forms beside float32 vector (6,144 bytes of floats at 1,536 dimensions): halfvec (float16, 0.7.0+), 3,072 bytes, with a very small recall loss, and it lifts HNSW's limit from 2,000 to 4,000 dimensions; and bit via binary_quantize (the sign of each number), 192 bytes, compared by Hamming distance. Both are on the chart above.

Binary needs re-ranking, and a model that tolerates it. Alone, the 1-bit index found @@bin_rec@@ of the true top 10: 384 signs keep too little of a small model's meaning. Fetching more candidates by Hamming distance and re-ordering them with the full vectors recovers it (@@bin100_rec@@ with 100 candidates, @@bin400_rec@@ with 400) measured; larger models lose less.

CREATE INDEX ON q USING hnsw ((binary_quantize(embedding)::bit(384)) bit_hamming_ops);
SELECT id FROM (
  SELECT id, embedding FROM q
  ORDER BY binary_quantize(embedding)::bit(384) <~> binary_quantize($1) LIMIT 100  -- needs hnsw.ef_search >= 100
) s ORDER BY embedding <=> $1 LIMIT 10;

12. Semantic caching: the only cache that can be wrong

An exact cache matches keys byte for byte, which almost never fires on natural language: "What is your refund policy?" and "How do refunds work?" are the same question in different bytes. A semantic cache turns each incoming prompt into an embedding (a vector of a few hundred numbers from a small model, such that texts with similar meaning point in similar directions), finds the most similar stored prompt, and returns its stored answer if the cosine similarity (the cosine of the angle between the two vectors; 1 means the same direction) is above a threshold; otherwise it calls the model and stores the new pair. GPTCache made the design popular ({{repository|https://github.com/zilliztech/GPTCache}}; its README warns "you may encounter false positives during cache hits"); LiteLLM ships it as a proxy option (type: "redis-semantic" with similarity_threshold: 0.8, {{LiteLLM docs|https://docs.litellm.ai/docs/proxy/caching_semantic}}); Redis sells it as a service (LangCache, {{Redis, spring 2025 release|https://redis.io/blog/spring-release-2025/}}). Every other cache on this page is exact, so a hit is provably the right answer; a semantic hit is a guess that two different questions deserve the same answer. That makes it a model-quality change disguised as an infrastructure change. Where it sits in an LLM gateway, beside exact response caching, is on {{Gateways and routing|n:3f15c17b0d0d81e4a964c129a3e520ae}}; this section owns how often it is wrong.

Measured: hits against wrong answers as the threshold moves

Two real labelled datasets and two small open embedding models, on the laptop's CPU measured: Quora Question Pairs (the GLUE validation split: pairs of real user questions, labelled duplicates by people; {{dataset|https://huggingface.co/datasets/nyu-mll/glue}}), whose non-duplicates are hard on purpose (same topic, different answer: exactly the near misses a cache must refuse); and PAWS ( pairs built by swapping or copying words, so almost every pair shares nearly all its words; are paraphrases; {{dataset|https://huggingface.co/datasets/google-research-datasets/paws}}). Models: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (22.7 million parameters, ms to embed one query on two CPU threads) and BAAI bge-small-en-v1.5 (33.4 million, ms). For each pair the cache holds the first question and the second arrives as a query: served if their similarity reaches the threshold, and right only for a duplicate. True-hit rate: the share of duplicates served; false-hit rate: the share of non-duplicates served.

Hand-written pairs illustrative inputs, similarity measured with all-MiniLM-L6-v2: one or two changed tokens flip the answer while the cosine barely moves

The pattern is structural, not a tuning problem: negation ("safe" against "unsafe"), direction ("from New York to Paris" against "from Paris to New York"), quantifiers ("under" against "over 100 dollars") and qualifiers ("after it has shipped") change the answer while changing one or two tokens, and contribute little to an averaged sentence vector, while two genuinely equivalent phrasings with different words can score lower. A threshold cannot separate them. The same failure inside a retrieval system, where claims with opposite verdicts are more similar to each other than claims that agree, is measured in section 8 of {{RAG in production|n:3f85c17b0d0d81aa9ba9c0ceac9e28c1}}.

What published measurements say: AWS, a 97% study, vCache, and a correction (depth)

On other traffic the curve can look far kinder, which is the lesson: it is a property of your traffic and your model. AWS's test on chatbot queries (Claude 3 Haiku answers, Titan embeddings), as reported by Portkey ({{Portkey, semantic caching thresholds|https://portkey.ai/blog/semantic-caching-thresholds/}}): threshold 0.99, 23.5% hits at 92.1% accuracy; 0.95, 56.0% at 92.6%; 0.90, 74.5% at 92.3%; 0.80, 87.6% at 91.8%; 0.75, 90.3% at 91.2%: accuracy barely moved. One study of a semantic cache for LLM queries reports hit rates of 61.6 to 68.8% with "positive hit rates exceeding 97%", roughly 3% of hits wrong ({{GPT Semantic Cache, arXiv 2411.05276|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05276}}). Portkey's advice is to start between 0.90 and 0.95, build a validation set with "queries that look similar but should produce different answers", and treat a false-positive rate above roughly 3 to 5% as the limit of the embedding model, not of the threshold. Research systems learn the threshold instead: vCache finds a single static threshold gives "unexpected error rates", learns one per cached prompt, and reports "up to 12.5× higher cache hit and 26× lower error rates" ({{arXiv 2502.03771|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03771}}).

Correction to the old page
The old page attributed "start near 0.95" to Portkey; its current post says "Start with a threshold between 0.90 and 0.95". The AWS figures it quoted are confirmed in Portkey's post (a secondary report of AWS's test).

How to run one safely, if you must

It works best on high-volume, low-variance, factual traffic (FAQ-style support questions, documentation lookups, classification prompts) and badly on open-ended generation, agent steps, or anything where users expect a fresh answer.

  1. Try the exact caches first: prompt caching (section 11), an exact-match cache on the normalised request (tenant, model, full prompt, temperature, tools), caching retrieval results and embeddings of repeated documents.
  2. Scope the key: tenant, user where answers are personal, model and prompt version, and the retrieved context. "What is my balance?" from two users is the same text with different answers, and a cache shared across tenants can hand one customer another's answer. Never cache answers that depend on live state (stock, balances, order status) or on time ("current pricing").
  3. Verify before serving: require extracted entities, numbers and dates to match exactly; or re-rank the top few candidates with a cross-encoder or a small judge model instead of trusting one cosine score.
  4. Measure your own curve: a few thousand real queries with a no-cache baseline, replayed through the cache, judged; plus a deliberate slice of near-miss pairs from your domain, which the natural distribution under-represents.
  5. In production: log the similarity of every hit, audit a sample of hits, alert on the false-hit rate as a service level objective, and keep a kill switch. When the knowledge behind the answers changes, version the cache namespace (a reindex invalidates everything at once) or tag entries with the documents they came from.

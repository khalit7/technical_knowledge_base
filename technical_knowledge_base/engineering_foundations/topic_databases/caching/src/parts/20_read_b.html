The idea in one screen

A cache is a second, faster copy of data, and it pays because traffic is skewed: a copy of the popular few absorbs most reads. Everything hard follows from it being a copy: it can go stale (invalidation), it cannot hold everything (eviction), its misses still cost (weigh each hit by the miss it avoids), and one kind, the semantic cache, can hand back a wrong answer.

One chat request passes up to nine copies; the further out, the bigger the saving and the harder to invalidate

The chat product of {{Topic: databases|n:3cd5c17b0d0d815d8841ec845f5f3324}}: a request asks each copy in turn, outermost first, and the source of truth (Postgres, then the SSD) answers only when every copy misses. Hit times are from the ladder in section 2 (measured on the laptop or sourced there). The web copies are exact; the LLM lane's prompt and KV caches are exact too, and only the semantic cache (section 12) answers a question with another question's answer. Sections 3 and 4 are about the copies you must invalidate yourself, 5 about what a full one throws away, 6 about what each hit is worth.

You need: SQL and a primary key ({{SQL and data modelling|n:3ef5c17b0d0d813980dcd474e19f6c3f}}), what a commit is ({{Transactions and concurrency|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8155aa64ddeb09da144e}}). Running example: the root's chat product, its 10-million-message Postgres database, a Redis in front of it and an LLM behind it. measured: recorded for this page on a laptop (Apple M1 Pro, 16 GB) on 5 October 2026; a laptop has no network between client and server, so its timings are best cases, while hit ratios and counts carry over to production. illustrative: an input made up to show a shape.

Hit, miss
a request the cache answers; one it cannot, which goes to the source and usually leaves a copy behind.
Hit ratio
hits divided by all requests.
TTL (time to live)
an expiry time after which the cache drops an entry by itself.
Cache-aside
the app reads the cache, fills it from the database on a miss, and deletes the key after a write (section 3).
Invalidation
retiring a cached copy that is no longer true.
Eviction policy
the rule for choosing what to drop when the cache is full (LRU, LFU, ...).
Stampede
many requests missing the same hot key at once and all rebuilding it (section 7).
Lease
a token the cache hands out with a miss, permitting one fill; a delete revokes it (section 3).

1. Why caches work: a few things get most of the requests

A cache only helps if requests repeat. Locality is the name for that: temporal (used now, likely used again soon: a user's open conversation is re-read on every message) and spatial (stored together, used together: why hardware fetches 64-byte lines and Postgres 8 KB pages). Application caches live on the first kind plus skew: a small set of items far more popular than the rest. The chat product has no public access log, so this page uses the largest public one: one hour of English Wikipedia (15 September 2026, 12:00 to 13:00 UTC, desktop and mobile summed by title), views of distinct pages ({{Wikimedia pageviews|https://dumps.wikimedia.org/other/pageviews/2026/2026-09/}}) measured.

One per cent of the pages got a third of the views

Pages sorted from most to least viewed. A cache that could hold exactly the most popular pages, under requests drawn independently from these counts, would hit on the share of views those pages get: that is the first view. Computed in measure/m_skew.py.

The top 0.1% of pages (about ) got of views, the top 1% , the top 10% . And pages () were viewed exactly once: no cache helps with those, because the first request for anything is a miss (a compulsory miss).

On log scales, views against rank fall near a straight line: views ≈ C / rankα, a Zipf distribution. Fitted on ranks 10 to 100,000, this hour gives α = ; the larger α, the more the top dominates (at α = 1 the first item is twice as popular as the second and ten times the tenth). Web caches have been measured near this shape for decades: Breslau et al. found α "ranging from 0.64 to 0.83" across the web proxy traces they studied ({{INFOCOM 1999|https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~cao/papers/zipf-implications.html}}), which is why the {{Cache lab|#t-lab}} offers Zipf workloads at α 0.7, 0.9 and 1.1 beside the real trace.

Popularity drifts, slowly. Over six consecutive hours (06:00 to 12:00 UTC), of each hour's 10,000 most viewed pages stayed in the next hour's top 10,000 measured. That drift separates eviction policies: pure frequency counting keeps yesterday's news; pure recency forgets steady favourites (measured in section 5). For the chat product: each user's own conversation list is hot only while that user is active, so a TTL of minutes fits it; shared things (the model list, feature flags, a popular shared prompt) are hot forever and need invalidation instead.

How the request trace was built from hourly counts (provenance)

The hourly file has no timestamps inside the hour, so the 2,000,000-request replay used in section 5 draws requests without replacement from the hour's views in random order. That keeps the skew exactly and removes any ordering inside the hour; the six-hour trace keeps the hour-to-hour drift. Both are real popularity, not a synthetic distribution.

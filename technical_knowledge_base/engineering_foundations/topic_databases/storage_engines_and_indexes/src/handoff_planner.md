## Verbatim (the old section)

## Query planning, and why the planner picks a bad plan (3 min)
A planner turns SQL into a physical plan by estimating the cost of alternatives from table statistics: three access paths (sequential scan, index scan, index-only scan or bitmap scan), three join algorithms (nested loop, hash join, merge join), and a costing model over estimated row counts. It picks the cheapest estimated plan, which is not always the cheapest plan.
Why it goes wrong, in rough order of how often you will hit it:
1. **Stale or insufficient statistics.** The planner thinks a table has 1000 rows because ANALYZE has not run since the bulk load. Fix: ANALYZE, and raise `default_statistics_target` for skewed columns.
2. **Correlated predicates.** The planner assumes independence, so `WHERE country = 'UK' AND city = 'London'` gets an estimate that multiplies two selectivities and lands orders of magnitude too low, which turns a hash join into a nested loop over millions of rows. Fix in Postgres: extended statistics (`CREATE STATISTICS`).
3. **Non-sargable predicates.** Wrapping the indexed column in a function or a type cast (`WHERE lower(email) = ...`, `WHERE created_at::date = ...`) makes the index unusable. Fix: an expression index, or rewrite the predicate to leave the column bare.
4. **Parameter sniffing and generic plans.** A prepared statement plans once for a typical parameter, then reuses that plan for an atypical one. A plan that suits `status = 'pending'` (rare) is a disaster for `status = 'done'` (most rows).
5. **Cardinality estimation through joins and aggregates.** Errors compound multiplicatively up the plan tree; a three-join query with a 10x error at the bottom is a 1000x error at the top. This is the known fundamental weakness of cost-based optimisation, not a bug you can configure away.
6. **Cost constants that do not match the hardware.** Postgres defaults assume spinning disks. On NVMe, `random_page_cost` of 4 is wrong and pushes the planner towards sequential scans; 1.1 is the common setting.
How to work: read `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` and compare estimated rows against actual rows at every node. The lowest node where they diverge badly is the problem. Buffers tells you whether you were reading from cache or disk. Everything else is downstream of that one comparison.
Most of those six are estimation failures rather than costing failures, which is what makes a learned planner a plausible attack: train a model on measured outcomes and it corrects the estimator from data instead of from a statistical model of the data. A 4B distillation fine-tuned to write Postgres plans reached a 1.81x geometric mean speedup and a 44.7% latency reduction on the join-heavy queries of the Join Order Benchmark in September 2026, with zero regressions when the best of three candidate plans was taken. The catch is economic rather than technical: generating and measuring three candidates costs far more than the planner does, so it pays only where the same analytic query runs repeatedly, and it makes no sense at all on transactional traffic. The result and its training recipe are on <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3cd5c17b0d0d815d8841ec845f5f3324">Topic: databases</mention-page>.

## Corrections and status (checked 2026-10-04; see the root's `src/read/children_notes.md`)
- **Learned planner (corrected).** Source: rohanbansal.com/qorl (2026-09-16). The model writes pg_hint_plan hints, not whole plans. Its own pick gave 1.40x with 7 regressions. The 1.81x geometric mean and 44.7% latency reduction hold only when taking the best of three rollouts per query (up to 15 candidates, each executed); zero regressions only in that setting. JOB (113 queries) was watched throughout development, so it is not a held-out test. Trained on 13,646 CEB queries. The untrained model gave no plan for 99 of 113 (88%). Cost about $1,200. The root's Reading section 8 already carries this; link it.
- **random_page_cost**: default 4.0 is in the docs (runtime-config-query); "1.1 is the common setting" on NVMe is folklore: unconfirmed, cite a vendor guide (for example a managed-Postgres provider's default) or measure.
- **Correlated predicates**: the root's Query plans tab measured an estimate of 129 rows against 7,411 actual, fixed by CREATE STATISTICS (case 5, 10M messages). Reuse it.
- **"Three access paths (sequential scan, index scan, index-only scan or bitmap scan)"**: that list has four; say four.
- **Failure modes 1 to 6** (stale statistics, correlated predicates, non-sargable predicates, generic plans, compounding errors, cost constants): standard, unconfirmed as a ranking ("in rough order of how often you will hit it" has no source).
- **Compounding errors** "10x at the bottom is 1000x at the top for three joins": arithmetic illustration, label it illustrative; Leis et al., "How Good Are Query Optimizers, Really?" (VLDB 2015) is the primary source for the size of real estimation errors on JOB.
- **EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)**: verified (using-explain docs). Note: since PostgreSQL 18 EXPLAIN ANALYZE includes BUFFERS by default (release notes, 18.0).

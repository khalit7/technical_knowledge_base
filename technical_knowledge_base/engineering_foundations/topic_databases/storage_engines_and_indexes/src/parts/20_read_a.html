Storage engines and indexes in one screen

About RT_MIN minutes to read (RT_WORDS words), plus the optional animations and the two tabs. It assumes only the parent page's first four sections; every new term is explained where it first appears and collected in the Glossary. Numbers marked measured were measured for this page on a laptop (Apple M1 Pro, 16 GB, internal SSD, PostgreSQL 16.2 and 18.6, RocksDB through the rocksdict binding, 2026-10-04; other jobs shared the machine, so treat times as plus or minus 30%); numbers marked illustrative are drawn to explain, not measured.

A storage engine is the part of a database that decides how rows are laid out on disk, how a change is made durable, and how one row is found again without reading everything. Two designs dominate: the B-tree (Postgres, MySQL, SQLite, SQL Server) updates sorted pages in place; the LSM-tree (RocksDB, Cassandra, ScyllaDB) appends changes and merges them later. Both sit on the same two tools: fixed-size pages cached in memory, and a write-ahead log that makes a commit survive a power cut.

The running example is the parent page's chat product: one table, messages, with a million rows (id, chat_id, role, model, tokens, content, created_at), generated by the same script with the same seed as the root's measurements, so the numbers agree: 19,235 pages of 8 KB, 157 MB, and a 9.3 MB index on chat_id measured.

The one idea to keep
No layout is best at reading, writing and saving space at once (the RUM conjecture). A B-tree favours reads; an LSM-tree favours writes; an index buys read speed with write cost and space. A professional picks the corner to give up on purpose, then measures it: this page measures all three on real engines.

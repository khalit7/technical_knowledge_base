4. Replication: a live copy on another machine

A backup gets the data back after hours. A replica (also standby) is a second Postgres server that is kept a copy of the first, the primary, within milliseconds, so it can take over in seconds and can serve read-only queries meanwhile. The theory (synchronous against asynchronous replication, what consistency a reader sees, why failover can lose data) is on {{Distributed systems fundamentals|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bc45c31d915b4d33}}; this section is how Postgres does it and what to watch.

Streaming replication, set up for real

In streaming replication a process on the primary, the WAL sender, streams WAL records over a normal database connection to a WAL receiver on the replica, which writes them to its own pg_wal/, flushes them, and replays them into its table files exactly as crash recovery would ({{high availability docs|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/warm-standby.html}}). The replica starts as a base backup of the primary; pg_basebackup -R also writes the connection settings and a standby.signal file that tells the new server to stay a replica:

Replication lag, measured three ways

The primary reports each replica's progress in pg_stat_replication as three delays ({{monitoring docs|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/monitoring-stats.html}}): write_lag (the replica has received and written the WAL, not yet flushed it to disk), flush_lag (written and flushed: it would survive a crash of the replica) and replay_lag (applied, so queries on the replica can see it). They separate the three ways a replica falls behind: the network (write), the replica's disk (flush), or its replay (one process applying everything, often blocked by queries running on the replica).

Pausing replay with pg_wal_replay_pause() stands in for the most common real cause: a long query on the replica that needs rows the replay is about to remove. Postgres then has to choose between cancelling the query and delaying replay; max_standby_streaming_delay (default 30 s) sets how long replay waits, and hot_standby_feedback = on instead tells the primary not to remove those rows (which brings bloat back to the primary: section 6). On a real server, lag also comes from a replica on smaller hardware than the primary, a saturated network, or a burst of writes such as a big UPDATE or an index build.

What lag means for the application (which reads may go to a replica, read-your-writes after a user's own change, and Jepsen's finding that reads on replicas can even disagree about the order of two commits, a Long Fork) is section 3 of {{Scaling a database|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81f0aaceee1e21b972be}}, "Read replicas in practice".

Replication slots, and why they fill disks

If a replica disconnects for a while, the primary may already have recycled the WAL it needs to catch up. A replication slot fixes that: it is a bookmark on the primary that says "keep all WAL from this position until this replica confirms it". The danger is the other side of the same promise: if the replica never comes back, the primary keeps WAL forever, until its disk is full and it stops. Measured, with the replica stopped and the chat database taking ordinary updates:

Size of the primary's pg_wal/ after each step (after a checkpoint), colour by the slot's wal_status. measured

Since PostgreSQL 13, max_slot_wal_keep_size caps what a slot may hold (default -1, unlimited: {{replication settings|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/runtime-config-replication.html}}); past the cap the slot is marked lost and the replica must be rebuilt from a fresh base backup (here 2.3 s for 774 MB on one laptop; hours for terabytes over a network). Since PostgreSQL 18, idle_replication_slot_timeout can also invalidate slots that have been inactive too long. The wal_status column tells you how close you are: reserved (within max_wal_size), extended (beyond it, still kept), unreserved (will be removed at the next checkpoint), lost ({{pg_replication_slots|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/view-pg-replication-slots.html}}).

Rule
Set max_slot_wal_keep_size to what your disk can afford, alert on pg_replication_slots where active is false or retained WAL exceeds a few GB, and drop slots for replicas that are gone. Abandoned slots also hold back vacuum when they carry an xmin (logical slots always do), which leads to section 7.

Synchronous replication: no lost commits, at a price

By default replication is asynchronous: the primary confirms a commit to the client as soon as its own WAL is flushed, and the replica gets it a moment later. If the primary dies in that moment, a failover loses those commits. With synchronous_standby_names set, a commit waits until a named standby confirms it, and synchronous_commit chooses how far: remote_write (the standby has written it), on (flushed), remote_apply (replayed, so a read on the standby will see it) ({{WAL settings|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/runtime-config-wal.html}}). local and off skip the standby; off does not even wait for the primary's own disk, so a crash can lose the last fraction of a second of commits (but never corrupts the database). Two things follow. On one machine, waiting for the standby cut throughput by more than half (27,568 to 11,562 inserts per second with on), mainly from the extra round trip on every commit; across availability zones (about 1 ms apart) or regions (tens of ms) the cost grows with distance. And the synchronous setting has a sharp edge: if the only synchronous standby goes away, every commit on the primary waits forever:

Read the last lines carefully: cancelling the wait does not undo the commit, it was already durable on the primary; it only might not be on the standby. The usual production setting is therefore synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (replica_a, replica_b)': wait for any one of two standbys (quorum commit), so losing one does not stop writes ({{syntax|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/runtime-config-replication.html}}). Patroni can also switch synchronous mode off automatically when no standby is healthy, trading a small data-loss window for availability.

Logical replication: copying rows, not files

Streaming replication copies the whole cluster byte for byte, so the replica must run the same major version and cannot be written to. Logical replication instead decodes the WAL into row changes ("insert this row into messages") and applies them on a subscriber, a normal writable database that can run a different major version and hold only some tables. You create a PUBLICATION on the source and a SUBSCRIPTION on the target (it requires wal_level = logical). Its uses: major-version upgrades with minutes of downtime (section 9), moving a database to another host or cloud, feeding a few tables to an analytics database, and change data capture into Kafka with Debezium (which reads a logical slot: see {{Queues, streams and async work|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81aebf91df927f215b6e}}). Its limits ({{restrictions|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/logical-replication-restrictions.html}}): schema changes (DDL) are not replicated, sequence values are not replicated, large objects are not replicated, and every replicated table needs a primary key or a replica identity for updates and deletes.

5. Failover: when the primary dies

Failover is making a replica the new primary and pointing the application at it. Postgres itself does exactly one step of it, promotion: the replica stops replaying, starts a new timeline and accepts writes. Measured:

The hard parts are everything around that command: deciding that the primary is really dead (and not just slow or cut off from the monitor), making sure it can no longer accept writes, moving the application's connections, and bringing the old primary back as a replica. Get the first two wrong and you have split brain: two servers that both think they are the primary, each accepting different writes. Staged here by promoting the replica while the old primary keeps running:

The same row id now holds two different messages on the two servers. Neither is "right"; merging them afterwards is manual, row by row. GitHub's 21 October 2018 outage began this way: a 43-second network partition triggered an automated failover across data centres, writes landed on both sides, and reconciling them kept the site degraded for 24 hours and 11 minutes ({{GitHub post-incident analysis|https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/oct21-post-incident-analysis/}}; MySQL, but the lesson is the same).

Fencing is how real systems prevent it: before promoting, make sure the old primary cannot accept writes, by stopping it, cutting its network, or powering it off through the cloud API (the old name is STONITH, "shoot the other node in the head"). The old primary is then repaired with pg_rewind, which copies back only the blocks that changed since the two histories diverged instead of a full base backup:

The write that went to the old primary is gone: pg_rewind discarded it. That is correct for the database and a data loss for whoever wrote it, which is why fencing first is the rule. (pg_rewind needs wal_log_hints = on or data checksums, and the old primary's WAL back to the last common checkpoint; the first attempt for this page failed until wal_keep_size kept it.)

Patroni and managed failover

Doing this by hand at 3 a.m. is how split brains happen, so production setups automate it. Patroni (from Zalando, now {{patroni/patroni|https://github.com/patroni/patroni}}, latest v4.1.5, 2026-08-12) runs beside each Postgres server and keeps a leader lock in a consensus store (etcd, Consul, ZooKeeper or Kubernetes). Only the holder of the lock may be primary; the lock has a time to live, so a primary that cannot renew it (because it is cut off) demotes itself, and a replica promotes only after acquiring the lock. That turns "who is primary" into a question the consensus store answers once, which is exactly the problem Raft solves (theory on {{Distributed systems fundamentals|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bc45c31d915b4d33}}). Patroni can also use a hardware or software watchdog to reset a primary that hangs. On Kubernetes, {{CloudNativePG|https://github.com/cloudnative-pg/cloudnative-pg/releases}} (v1.30.1, 2026-09-23) plays the same role. Applications find the current primary through a DNS name, a load balancer, a pooler, or a multi-host connection string with target_session_attrs=read-write.

Managed services run the equivalent for you. Amazon Aurora says a failover to a replica typically restores service in under 60 seconds and often under 30 ({{Aurora high availability|https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/AuroraUserGuide/Concepts.AuroraHighAvailability.html}}). Two things remain yours: the application must reconnect and retry (connections to the dead primary fail mid-transaction; see {{Reliability engineering|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8152b8dececf34196174}} for retries), and with asynchronous replication the last commits before the failure may be lost. Jepsen also found that RDS Multi-AZ clusters for PostgreSQL 13.15 to 17.4 could show reads on replicas that violate snapshot isolation (Long Fork), because of how stock Postgres replicas decide which transactions are visible ({{Jepsen, 2025-04-29|https://jepsen.io/analyses/amazon-rds-for-postgresql-17.4}}): read from the primary when correctness matters.

3. Replication: a live copy on another machine

A backup gets the data back after hours. A replica (or standby) is a second server kept within milliseconds of the primary, so it can take over in seconds and serve read-only queries meanwhile. In streaming replication a WAL sender process on the primary streams WAL records over an ordinary connection to a WAL receiver on the replica, which writes them, flushes them and replays them into its table files exactly as crash recovery would ({{high availability docs|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/warm-standby.html}}). The replica starts as a base backup; pg_basebackup -R also writes the connection settings and a standby.signal file that keeps it a replica.

One WAL record, three places it can wait: the three lags name them

Drawn, not to scale. The primary reports each replica's progress in pg_stat_replication ({{monitoring docs|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/monitoring-stats.html}}): write_lag (received and written, not yet flushed), flush_lag (on the replica's disk, survives a replica crash), replay_lag (applied, visible to queries there). They separate the network, the replica's disk, and its single replay process.

Normally replay lag is about 1 ms; 9.5 s into the pause the primary reported write_lag 00:00:00.000247, replay_lag 00:00:09.355926 and 79 MB not yet replayed. Pausing replay (pg_wal_replay_pause()) stands in for the most common real cause: a long query on the replica that needs rows the replay is about to remove. Lag also comes from a replica on smaller hardware, a saturated network, or a write burst such as a big UPDATE or an index build. Replication slots, and why they fill disks

If a replica disconnects, the primary may recycle WAL the replica still needs. A replication slot prevents that: a bookmark on the primary that keeps all WAL from its position until the consumer confirms it. The other side of the promise: if the replica never comes back, the primary keeps WAL until its disk is full and it stops. Measured with the replica stopped and ordinary updates running, the slot held 1091 MB (wal_status extended) and pg_wal/ reached 1120 MB:

A stopped replica's slot grows pg_wal/ without limit; a cap releases it

Size of the primary's pg_wal/ after each step (after a checkpoint), colour by the slot's wal_status. measured

Since PostgreSQL 13 max_slot_wal_keep_size caps what a slot may hold (default -1, unlimited: {{replication settings|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/runtime-config-replication.html}}); past it the slot is marked lost and the replica must be rebuilt from a fresh base backup (here 2.3 s for 774 MB on one laptop; hours for terabytes over a network). Since PostgreSQL 18 idle_replication_slot_timeout can also invalidate slots inactive too long. wal_status says how close you are: reserved (within max_wal_size), extended (beyond it, still kept), unreserved (removed at the next checkpoint), lost ({{pg_replication_slots|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/view-pg-replication-slots.html}}).

Rule
Set max_slot_wal_keep_size to what your disk can afford, alert on slots where active is false or retained WAL exceeds a few GB, and drop slots for replicas that are gone. A slot that carries an xmin (logical slots always do) also holds back vacuum and freezing (sections 5 and 6).

Synchronous replication: no lost commits, at a price

By default replication is asynchronous: the primary confirms a commit once its own WAL is flushed, and the replica gets it a moment later; if the primary dies in that moment, failover loses those commits. With synchronous_standby_names set, a commit waits for a named standby, and synchronous_commit picks how far: remote_write (written there), on (flushed there), remote_apply (replayed, so a read there sees it) ({{WAL settings|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/runtime-config-wal.html}}). local and off skip the standby; off does not even wait for the primary's own disk, so a crash can lose the last fraction of a second of commits (it never corrupts the database).

Waiting for a standby more than halves commit throughput, even on one machine

Single-row inserts per second, 8 clients, 10 s each, both servers on one laptop (loopback "network"). measured macOS fsync does not force the drive's cache, so every rate is higher than on a server with honest flushes; read the ratios.

With on throughput fell from 27,568 to 11,562 inserts per second, mostly the extra round trip per commit; across availability zones (about 1 ms apart) or regions (tens of ms) the cost grows with distance. The sharp edge: if the only synchronous standby goes away, every commit on the primary waits forever. The usual production setting is therefore synchronous_standby_names = 'ANY 1 (replica_a, replica_b)': wait for any one of two (quorum commit), so losing one does not stop writes. Patroni can also drop synchronous mode when no standby is healthy, trading a small data-loss window for availability.

Logical replication: copying rows, not files

Streaming replication copies the cluster byte for byte, so the replica runs the same major version and cannot be written to. Logical replication decodes the WAL into row changes and applies them on a subscriber, a normal writable database that may run a different major version and hold only some tables: a PUBLICATION on the source, a SUBSCRIPTION on the target, wal_level = logical. It is used for major upgrades with seconds of downtime (section 10), moving to another host or cloud, feeding tables to analytics, and change data capture into Kafka with Debezium, which reads a logical slot ({{Queues, streams and async work|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81aebf91df927f215b6e}}). Its limits ({{restrictions|https://www.postgresql.org/docs/18/logical-replication-restrictions.html}}): schema changes (DDL), sequence values and large objects are not replicated, and every replicated table needs a primary key or replica identity for updates and deletes.

4. Failover: when the primary dies

Failover is making a replica the new primary and pointing the application at it. Postgres does one step, promotion: the replica stops replaying, starts a new timeline and accepts writes (pg_ctl promote, 0.12 s here). The hard parts are around it: deciding the primary is really dead and not just cut off from the monitor, making sure it can no longer accept writes, moving the connections, and bringing the old primary back. (GitHub's 2018 outage began this way; below.) Fencing prevents it: before promoting, make sure the old primary cannot accept writes, by stopping it, cutting its network or powering it off through the cloud API (the old name is STONITH, "shoot the other node in the head"). The old primary is then repaired with pg_rewind, which copies back only the blocks changed since the histories diverged (211 MB of 908 MB here) and discards the old side's writes: correct for the database, a data loss for whoever wrote them, which is why fencing comes first.

GitHub's 21 October 2018 outage began this way: a 43-second network partition triggered an automated cross-site failover, writes landed on both sides, and reconciling them kept the site degraded for 24 hours and 11 minutes ({{GitHub post-incident analysis|https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/oct21-post-incident-analysis/}}; MySQL, same lesson).

pg_rewind needs wal_log_hints = on or data checksums, and the old primary's WAL back to the last common checkpoint; the first attempt for this page failed until wal_keep_size kept it.

Patroni and managed failover

Patroni ({{patroni/patroni|https://github.com/patroni/patroni}}) runs beside each Postgres server and keeps a leader lock with a time to live in a consensus store (etcd, Consul, ZooKeeper or Kubernetes). Only the holder may be primary: a primary that cannot renew the lock demotes itself, and a replica promotes only after acquiring it, so "who is primary" is answered once by the store, the problem Raft solves ({{Distributed systems fundamentals|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bc45c31d915b4d33}}). A hardware or software watchdog can reset a primary that hangs. On Kubernetes, {{CloudNativePG|https://github.com/cloudnative-pg/cloudnative-pg/releases}} plays the same role. Applications find the current primary through a DNS name, a load balancer, a pooler, or a multi-host connection string with target_session_attrs=read-write. Managed services run the equivalent: Amazon Aurora says a failover typically restores service in under 60 seconds and often under 30 ({{Aurora high availability|https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonRDS/latest/AuroraUserGuide/Concepts.AuroraHighAvailability.html}}). Two things stay yours: the application must reconnect and retry, because connections to the dead primary fail mid-transaction ({{Reliability engineering|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8152b8dececf34196174}}), and with asynchronous replication the last commits before the failure may be lost. Jepsen also found that RDS Multi-AZ clusters for PostgreSQL 13.15 to 17.4 could show replica reads that violate snapshot isolation (Long Fork), because of how stock replicas decide which transactions are visible ({{Jepsen, 2025-04-29|https://jepsen.io/analyses/amazon-rds-for-postgresql-17.4}}): read from the primary when correctness matters.

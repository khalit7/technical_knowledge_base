6. Congestion control: sharing a link nobody owns

The receive window protects the receiver; nothing in it protects the routers between. If every sender sent as fast as its window allowed, router queues would overflow and throughput would collapse, as the internet did in October 1986 ({{Jacobson and Karels, 1988|https://ee.lbl.gov/papers/congavoid.pdf}}). So each sender also keeps a congestion window (cwnd), its own estimate of what the path can carry, and probes for more while watching for overload.

Slow start. A new connection starts at 10 segments ({{RFC 6928|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6928}}; TCP_INIT_CWND 10 in Linux) and adds one per ACK, doubling every round trip, until a loss or the slow-start threshold. Reaching a 125 MB window from 14.6 KB takes about log2(125 MB / 14.6 KB) ≈ 13 round trips, 1.3 s on a 100 ms path (derived); this page's 100 KB home-line BDP fills in about 3. A 2 KB API response finishes inside slow start, so for it latency, not bandwidth, is everything. Linux also returns to slow start after a connection has been idle for one RTO (tcp_slow_start_after_idle, default on), so a burst after a pause starts small again.

Reno: after slow start, add one segment per round trip and halve on loss, additive increase, multiplicative decrease ({{RFC 5681|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681}}). Fair and stable, and hopeless on long fat paths: after one loss at a 10,000-segment window, one segment per 100 ms round trip needs 5,000 round trips, over 8 minutes, to get back (derived). CUBIC, the default in the Linux, Windows and Apple stacks ({{RFC 9438 s1|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9438#section-1}}; this Mac reports net.inet.tcp.use_newreno: 0 and 209 sockets on CUBIC), grows as a cubic in time since the last loss, W(t) = C (t − K)3 + Wmax with C = 0.4, cuts to 0.7 instead of 0.5, and K is the time to climb back to the window where the loss happened: back fast, careful past the old level, then accelerating. Because it runs on time, not round trips, long-RTT flows recover as fast as short ones.

After each loss CUBIC is back near the old window in about 9 s (K, derived); Reno needs tens of seconds

Both start in slow start; queueing delay and timeouts are left out.

The cost of filling the pipe: queues. Reno and CUBIC learn they went too fast only when a queue overflows, so they keep the bottleneck's buffer full. measured on this page's home line, ping every 100 ms with one 25 MB download in the middle: the download ran at 44.1 Mbit/s and the round trip went from a 14.4 ms median at idle to 37.0 ms while it ran: about 23 ms of queue, roughly 125 KB waiting at the bottleneck (44.1 Mbit/s × 22.6 ms, derived). Section 5's runs show it too: the window-limited downloads ended with a current RTT of 17 to 22 ms, the ones that filled the link 35 to 49 ms. Interactive traffic on that link (a video call, the next streamed token) waits in the same queue: bufferbloat.

BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time, from Google) models the path instead of reacting to loss: it estimates bottleneck bandwidth and minimum RTT and paces at that rate, keeping the queue near empty. It shines on long paths with random, non-congestive loss, where CUBIC would back off for nothing, and is in Linux since 4.9 ({{commit 0f8782ea1497|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/0f8782ea14974ce992618b55f0c041ef43ed0b78}}). Its current version is still an IETF draft, {{draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr-06|https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr/}} (6 July 2026), not an RFC; check what a kernel ships with sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_available_congestion_control. Only the sender chooses: on a download, the server's algorithm decides, not yours.

Signals other than loss, and when not to touch it (depth)

ECN ({{RFC 3168|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3168}}) lets a router mark a packet "congestion experienced" instead of dropping it, and the receiver echoes the mark; this Mac negotiated it on every connection recorded here. L4S ({{RFC 9330|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9330}}, 2023) builds on it for very low queueing delay; ISPs deploy it, you do not tune it on a cluster.

Inside one data centre on a clean network the default is almost always right; the wins from BBR are on long, lossy paths (cross-region replication, uploads from a home line). One BBR flow can take more than its share from CUBIC flows on a shallow-buffer link, so test before switching a fleet.

4. Loss: acknowledgements, timers and head-of-line blocking

The receiver keeps saying "I have every byte up to n" (a cumulative ACK); a segment sent but not yet acknowledged is in flight. If segment 5 of 10 is lost, 6 to 10 still arrive, but the receiver can only repeat "up to the end of 4": these repeats are duplicate ACKs, and the bytes of 6 to 10 wait in the receive buffer, invisible to the application, until 5 is repaired. That wait is head-of-line blocking, and it is why HTTP/2's many streams on one TCP connection all stall on one loss while HTTP/3's do not (the root's {{On the wire|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ec9355f4eecd6eed02}} tab measured one lost packet delaying all three HTTP/2 streams and only one HTTP/3 stream).

A sender decides a segment is lost in one of two ways:

  • Retransmission timeout (RTO). It keeps a smoothed RTT and a variance and sets RTO = smoothed RTT + 4 × variance ({{RFC 6298|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6298}}); nothing acknowledged before the timer fires means resend, and double the timer for the next try. RFC 6298 says the RTO should never go below 1 second; Linux uses a 200 ms floor (TCP_RTO_MIN (HZ / 5)), a 1 s initial value and a 120 s ceiling ({{tcp.h|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/include/net/tcp.h}}). A timeout is expensive: at least 200 ms idle, and the congestion window collapses to one segment (section 6).
  • Fast retransmit. Three duplicate ACKs mean later segments are arriving, so the missing one is probably lost: resend at once, one round trip after the loss instead of a timer later ({{RFC 5681 s3.2|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681#section-3.2}}). SACK (selective acknowledgement, {{RFC 2018|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2018}}) also reports which later blocks arrived, so several holes are repaired in one round trip.

A token stream breaks fast retransmit. It needs later segments to generate duplicate ACKs, and a stream sends one small segment every 50 ms or so; if the last segment is lost there is nothing behind it. Linux's answer is RACK-TLP ({{RFC 8985|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8985}}): call a segment lost when one sent after it has been acknowledged and a reordering window has passed, and when the tail goes quiet, send a tail loss probe after about two round trips to provoke an ACK that reveals the hole.

The model's rules (what is simplified)

One-way delay is half the round trip; segments up to 1,448 bytes; the receiver ACKs every second segment or after 40 ms (Linux's minimum delayed ACK) and at once for out-of-order data ({{RFC 5681 s4.2|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681#section-4.2}}); the sender retransmits on three duplicate ACKs ({{s3.2|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681#section-3.2}}), after a tail-loss probe at two round trips ({{RFC 8985 s7|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8985#section-7}}), or when its timer fires at RTT + 200 ms, doubling after each expiry; Nagle holds a small write while data is unacknowledged ({{RFC 9293 s3.7.4|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9293#section-3.7.4}}). No TLS (as on the root's plain port), no congestion window limit (seven small segments never reach the initial window of 10), no queueing. Real stacks differ in detail (RACK time-based detection, SACK); the order of events and the size of each wait are the point.

Loss on a real path, and the counters that show it (depth)

In two of this page's twelve downloads the receiver held out-of-order bytes for repair (36,608 and 254,880 bytes, tcpi_rxoutoforderbytes, measured); the other ten saw none. The application never noticed: TCP repaired the holes and delivered in order, and the cost (about a round trip, a smaller window) is invisible unless you read the per-connection counters.

Retransmissions inside a data centre should be near zero; growth there is a real fabric or NIC problem. Linux: nstat -az TcpRetransSegs before and after a test, and ss -ti per connection (retrans: current/total, lost:, rto:). macOS: netstat -s -p tcp | grep -i retrans. A connection whose RTO has backed off to tens of seconds looks hung; it is waiting for its timer.

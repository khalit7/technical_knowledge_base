5. Caching: TTLs, negative answers and stale answers

The rule. Every record carries a TTL (time to live), chosen by the zone owner: the number of seconds a cache may keep it. A resolver that fetches a record with TTL 300 serves it for up to 300 seconds, counting down, then asks again. Nothing can push a change into caches or invalidate them from outside. So "DNS propagation" is a myth: the authoritative server has the new data the moment you save it, and you are waiting for every cache that holds the old answer to expire.

Fig. 3 · A cache keeps what it has until the TTL runs out

  1. The resolver fetches. At 0 s the client asks for weights.llm.test; the resolver fetches A 10.53.0.81 from the authoritative server (4 ms) and caches it for its TTL, 30 s.
  2. The address changes at the source. At 7.0 s the record is changed on the authoritative server, which answers 10.53.0.82 from 9.5 s (it reloads its zone file every second). The resolver's copy has 23 s left.
  3. The resolver keeps the old answer. Asked every two seconds, it answers 10.53.0.81 with the TTL counting down, 21, 18, 16 ... 2, whatever the authoritative server now says: nothing pushes the change into the cache.
  4. Expired, fetched again. At 30.4 s, 30.4 s after it first fetched it, the copy has expired; the resolver asks again and answers 10.53.0.82, TTL 30.
  1. "No" is cached too. At 0 s the name does not exist: NXDOMAIN, which the resolver caches for min(300, 30) = 30 s, the SOA record's TTL and its MINIMUM field.
  2. The name is created. At 4.6 s the record is added; the authoritative server answers 10.53.0.83 from 7.1 s.
  3. The resolver still says it does not exist. Its cached "no" counts down, 23 ... 2 s, and every answer is NXDOMAIN.
  4. The "no" expires. At 30.2 s the resolver asks again and answers 10.53.0.83, now cached for that record's own TTL, 300 s.
  1. Both resolvers cache the record. Two Unbound resolvers, one default and one with serve-expired: yes, fetch A 10.53.0.81, TTL 30.
  2. The source stops. At 0.6 s the authoritative server for llm.test. is stopped.
  3. The TTLs run out. Both copies count down to zero (derived from the TTL of 30 s); from then on a resolver must ask the server, which does not answer.
  4. Default: no answer at all. Asked at 34.6 s, the default resolver has nothing to give, and dig gives up waiting: no reply, not even SERVFAIL.
  5. Serve-expired: the old answer, late. Asked at 54.9 s, the other resolver tries the server for about 1.8 s, then answers with the expired record, 10.53.0.81 with TTL 30, after 1,809 ms (and 1,806 ms again at 59.9 s).

With a TTL of a day the same picture lasts a day. A TTL is also a per-cache countdown, not a global clock: api.anthropic.com came back with 292 seconds left and, five seconds later from the same public resolver address, 212 seconds left, 80 lower instead of 5 (measured over DoT), because one address hides many caches filled at different times.

Planning a change

Lower the TTL (to 60 to 300 s) at least one old TTL before the change, because caches that fetched just before you lowered it keep the old TTL. Make the change. Keep the old address serving until the old TTL has passed since the change, then raise the TTL again. Clients add their own layer: a JVM caches for 30 s by default, and a long-lived connection never re-resolves at all (section 6). Predict: with a one-hour TTL lowered to 60 s only 30 minutes ahead, how long until every cache has the new address? Then set the lead slider to 30 min.

The model's assumptions

Every cache is assumed to be kept warm by steady traffic, with fetch times spread evenly over one TTL; a real cache that sees little traffic refetches later and flips sooner. Clients with their own cache add up to 30 s spread evenly; pinned clients never flip. Checked against an independent Python version by check_embed.py.

Negative caching: "no" is cached too

A resolver also caches NXDOMAIN and NODATA, so that a typo hammered in a loop does not reach the authoritative servers every time. For how long is set by the zone's SOA record, which the "no" carries in its authority section: the minimum of the SOA record's own TTL and its last field, MINIMUM ({{RFC 2308 s5|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2308#section-5}}; MINIMUM once meant something else and was repurposed for this). In the lab, llm.test's SOA has TTL 300 and MINIMUM 30, so a "no" lives min(300, 30) = 30 seconds:

For anthropic.com both numbers are 1,800 (SOA ... 604800 1800, measured), so a name looked up a moment before it is created stays missing for up to half an hour for everyone behind that cache; a zone with MINIMUM 86400 would make it a day. Keep MINIMUM between about 300 and 900 seconds on zones that change, and do not let deploy scripts or health checks look up names before they exist (the root's Failure lab "NXDOMAIN" case shows what each client library prints). Failures are cached as well: a resolver that gets no usable answer must remember that for at least 1 second and at most 5 minutes, and back off rather than retry hard ({{RFC 9520 s3|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9520#section-3}}, December 2023), which is why a broken zone can stay broken for a few minutes after it is fixed.

Serving stale answers when the source is down

When an authoritative server is unreachable and a cached record has expired, a resolver normally has nothing to give: SERVFAIL, or, as recorded below, no answer at all before the client gives up. Serve-stale ({{RFC 8767|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767}}) lets it return the expired record instead, marked with a short TTL (30 s recommended), after trying the servers for a moment (1.8 s recommended). Recorded: two Unbound resolvers, one with serve-expired: yes, both cached weights.llm.test; then the zone's server was stopped and the TTL allowed to run out:

It trades correctness for availability: a client may get an address that has changed. That is usually the right trade for internal names during an outage of the DNS provider, and it softened the .de outage (section 9). Unbound and BIND leave it off by default; public resolvers and Kubernetes CoreDNS set their own policies.

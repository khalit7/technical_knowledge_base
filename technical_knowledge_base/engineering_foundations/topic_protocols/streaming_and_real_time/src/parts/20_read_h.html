9. Webhooks: the provider calls you

A fine-tuning job runs for four hours: a stream held that long is fragile (Wikimedia cuts its streams at 15 minutes) and polling is wasteful. With a webhook you register a URL, and when something happens the provider POSTs a JSON description of the event to it. It is a convention on HTTP, not a protocol, and correctness lives in three rules both sides must follow; a community specification, Standard Webhooks, writes them down ({{spec|https://github.com/standard-webhooks/standard-webhooks/blob/main/spec/standard-webhooks.md}}, v1.0.0 December 2025; the parent topic's Protocol atlas checked its versions).

Rule 1: verify the signature, over the raw bytes. Your endpoint is public; anyone can POST to it. The provider signs each delivery with an HMAC-SHA256 under a secret it shared with you. Standard Webhooks signs id.timestamp.body and sends webhook-signature: v1,<base64>; Stripe signs timestamp.body; GitHub signs the body only, so it has no built-in replay protection (formats below). measured The same JSON re-serialised by json.loads and json.dumps failed verification, and a correctly signed message 10 minutes old was rejected (evidence below). So read the raw body before any framework parses it, compare with a constant-time function (hmac.compare_digest), and reject stale timestamps so a captured request cannot be replayed.

Rules 2 and 3: duplicates and disorder are normal. A provider retries any delivery that did not get a 2xx in time, with backoff over hours or days: Standard Webhooks' example schedule is immediately, then 5 s, 5 min, 30 min, 2 h, 5 h, 10 h, 14 h, 20 h, 24 h (about 75 hours in all), and Stripe retries "for up to three days" in live mode. Delivery is therefore at least once (duplicates allowed, loss not): the same event arrives several times (a slow handler that finished after the sender gave up), and in any order (a retried early event lands after a later one). Stripe says both plainly: no order guarantee, and endpoints "might occasionally receive the same event more than once". Predict: two events, a receiver that does 1.5 s of work before answering a sender with a 1 s timeout; how many times does the work run?

measured src/lab/webhooks.py: a sender following Standard Webhooks' rules (its first four delays, scaled down 60 times, and a 1 s timeout) delivers job.running and, 20 ms later, job.succeeded for one fine-tuning job. The receiver answers the first attempt of job.running with 503 (a deploy in progress). The work takes 1.5 s. Fig. 9 runs the two receivers side by side: blue envelopes are the sender's attempts, the answers ride back, and each bar is one finished piece of work.

  1. Every delivery is signed. The receiver recomputes the HMAC over the bytes exactly as they arrived. Over the raw body it verified; over the same JSON after json.loads and json.dumps it failed ("No matching signature found"); a correctly signed message 10 minutes old failed too ("Message timestamp too old").
  2. The first attempts (0 to 0.12 s). Both receivers answer the first job.running with 503. The careful receiver answered 200 within milliseconds to job.succeeded, and then to the retried job.running, queueing both; the naive receiver did the work before answering, so the sender, with its 1 s timeout, saw a failure.
  3. Retries pile up (0.2 to 2.7 s). Every naive attempt outlives the sender's timeout, so each event is retried and each copy is worked: three runs, the stored status flipping back from "succeeded" to "running" whenever a late copy of job.running finished. The careful worker finishes both events and writes the status as re-read from the source of truth: "succeeded", although job.running finished last.
  4. Attempt 3, 5 s later (5 minutes, scaled down 60 times). Only the naive receiver is still being retried: two more runs of the work, the status going back to "running" before "succeeded" again.
  5. Attempt 4, 30 s later (30 minutes, scaled). Two more: the work ran 7 times at the naive receiver, against 2 at the careful one, which ignored duplicates by webhook-id and kept the right final status.
The receiver checklist
  1. Verify the signature over the raw body; reject stale timestamps; compare in constant time.
  2. Store the event id before working; if it is already there, answer 200 and stop.
  3. Answer 2xx quickly (Stripe: "before any complex logic that could cause a timeout"; Standard Webhooks suggests senders allow 15 to 30 s), then work from a queue.
  4. Do not trust order: treat an event as "something changed about job X" and fetch X's current state (a thin payload design, which also keeps less data in transit).
  5. Answer 410 Gone to tell a sender to stop sending to this URL; 429, 502 and 504 ask it to slow down.

      The sender's side. Sending webhooks (an agent platform notifying customers, A2A push notifications) means making HTTP requests to URLs your users chose, from inside your network. Standard Webhooks tells senders to route them through a proxy that refuses internal addresses, or an attacker registers http://169.254.169.254/ and reads your cloud metadata (server-side request forgery, SSRF; the {{DNS|n:3c65c17b0d0d812387d8f92b546f07af}} page covers the rebinding variant). Sign every delivery, keep payloads small (the spec suggests under 20 KB), retry with jitter, and disable endpoints that fail for days, telling their owner another way. The outbox pattern that makes "update the database and send the webhook" reliable is on {{Queues, streams and async work|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81aebf91df927f215b6e}}; webhook API design on {{Service APIs|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cb88a3f1f63e85f27b}} (section 6).

      Designing webhooks as part of your own API (signing, the retry schedule, event shapes, thin against snapshot events) is on {{Service APIs|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cb88a3f1f63e85f27b}}, section 6; this section is the receiver's and the sender's wire-level discipline. Inside your own infrastructure the same need is met by a queue or event bus (SNS, EventBridge, Pub/Sub, Kafka) with the same at-least-once discipline: a function triggered by a queue also sees duplicates. An ML engineer meets webhooks at the end of long jobs: fine-tuning, batch inference, evaluations, training runs on a managed platform.

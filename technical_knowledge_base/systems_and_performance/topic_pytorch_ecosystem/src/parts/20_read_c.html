2. The dispatcher: one table of kernels per operator

For every operator PyTorch keeps a table of kernels indexed by dispatch key. Each tensor carries a set of keys; the dispatcher takes the union over a call's inputs, removes the keys switched off for the current thread, and runs the kernel of the highest-priority key left. That kernel does its job (autocast casts, autograd records a node) and calls the operator again with its own key masked out, until a backend kernel does the arithmetic. Keys with nothing to do are fallthrough and skipped. On MPS the keys are MPS, AutogradMPS and AutocastMPS; on a CUDA tensor CUDA, AutogradCUDA and AutocastCUDA.

Two switches show the mechanism. torch.no_grad() leaves the autograd key in place and tells its kernel to record nothing; torch.inference_mode() removes the key, so that layer is skipped outright (the timings are in the comparison above). Autocast works the other way round: every tensor always carries an Autocast key, every thread starts with it excluded, and entering torch.autocast lifts the exclusion, turning casting on for every operator without touching a tensor ({{c10/core/DispatchKeySet.h L670 to L686|https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/blob/v2.14.1/c10/core/DispatchKeySet.h#L670-L686}}, v2.14.1).

Why the dispatcher spans the whole topic: it is the extension point
Everything that changes PyTorch's behaviour without changing your model plugs in as a kernel for some key: autograd itself, autocast, a new device (MPS, XPU and out-of-tree backends register their own keys), functionalisation for the compiler, TorchDispatchMode recorders like the one that counted 923 calls in {{Inside one step|#t-step}}, and tensor subclasses such as DTensor (section 7) and torchao's quantized tensors (section 8). Every call pays a few microseconds of Python and dispatch before any arithmetic, nothing for a large matrix multiply and a lot for the small element-wise operations of a transformer: one of the two things torch.compile removes (section 5).

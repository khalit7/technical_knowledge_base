7. Distributed: the same program in several processes

The model. Distributed PyTorch runs one copy of your script per device. torchrun --nproc_per_node 8 train.py starts 8 processes and gives each a rank (0 to 7) and the world size (8); torch.distributed.init_process_group(backend) connects them through c10d, PyTorch's communication layer, using NCCL between NVIDIA GPUs or gloo on CPUs. The processes then cooperate only through collectives, operations every rank calls together: all-reduce (everyone ends with the sum), all-gather (everyone ends with everyone's pieces), reduce-scatter (sum, then each keeps one piece), broadcast (one rank's tensor to all). The rule that explains every hang: all ranks must call the same collectives, with the same shapes, in the same order.

Everything here was run for real: the running example on 2 and 4 CPU processes with gloo, every collective recorded by PyTorch's flight recorder (a per-rank log of recent collectives) measured on Apple M1 Pro, CPU, gloo, 1 thread per rank ({{Distributed lab|#t-dist}}). The theory of why these schemes save memory, and their communication volumes at scale, is on {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}}; this section is what PyTorch's APIs actually do.

DDP and FSDP2 on the same step

DDP (DistributedDataParallel) keeps a full copy of the model on every rank, gives each rank different data, and averages the gradients with all-reduces during backward, grouped into buckets so communication overlaps with the rest of backward. FSDP2 (fully_shard, since 2.4) splits every parameter across ranks; each rank keeps only its piece and the matching optimizer state, gathers a block's full weights just before using it, frees them after, and reduce-scatters the gradients so each rank receives only the part it owns.

One training step on 2 ranks: what each rank holds, and every collective it calls

Collectives, their order and sizes are rank 0's flight-recorder entries for the second step (step 1) of the running example at world size 2, measured on Apple M1 Pro, CPU, gloo; bars show each parameter group's share held by the rank (full or half), with gathered weights drawn lighter. Both runs give the same losses, 3.80137, 3.09551, 3.16247.

The {{Distributed lab|#t-dist}}, section 5, draws both strategies' collectives on one timeline to scale with their bytes, and checks Distributed Training's communication ratios against the recorded payloads. Peak memory per rank = weights + AdamW state + DDP's gradient buckets + the step's peak of activations and temporaries, from the profiler's allocation events measured on Apple M1 Pro, CPU ({{Distributed lab|#t-dist}}, section 6). The 8-block, width-512 variant has 25.3M parameters.

Three things the measurements add to the textbook picture. DDP's first step puts every gradient in a single bucket and only then rebuilds buckets in the order gradients actually arrived (from step 1: 1,051,648 B, then 11,696,128 B), because the default 25 MiB cap applies only after that first pass (torch/nn/parallel/distributed.py at v2.14.1; {{Distributed lab|#t-dist}}, section 3). Tuning bucket_cap_mb after watching one step tells you nothing about the first. FSDP2 makes 14 collectives per step against DDP's 2 all-reduces, trading many smaller messages for memory. And on the tiny model, activations dominate, so sharding the weights saves less than the ratio of ranks suggests: the step's own peak is 43.9 MB on 2 and on 4 FSDP2 ranks, and only the persistent part shrinks; on the bigger variant, where weights and AdamW state are 303 MB per DDP rank, FSDP2 on 4 ranks cuts the peak from 548 MB to 249 MB.

DTensor and DeviceMesh: one vocabulary for every split

A DeviceMesh arranges the ranks as an n-dimensional grid (say 2 × 4: two groups of four). A DTensor is a tensor plus a mesh plus one placement per mesh dimension: Shard(d) (split along dimension d), Replicate() (a full copy) or Partial() (each rank holds a part of a sum not yet added up). Each row was run with redistribute on 2 and 4 CPU ranks measured on Apple M1 Pro ({{Distributed lab|#t-dist}}, section 7, which lets you pick any mesh and placement). Replicate to Partial is refused at 2.14.1 ("only Shard to Partial(sum) redistribution is supported"); an uneven Shard(0) of 6 rows over 4 ranks gives 2, 2, 2 and 0 rows. Gloo has no native reduce-scatter: it all-reduces the whole tensor and keeps a chunk (ProcessGroupGloo.cpp), so byte counts from CPU runs overstate that one collective; NCCL has a real one.

Tensor and context parallelism are placements too; pipelining instead gives each rank different layers. Tensor parallelism (TP) splits the weight matrices inside a layer: ColwiseParallel on the MLP's first matrix and RowwiseParallel on the second leave one all-reduce per MLP in forward and one in backward (8 all-reduces of 524,288 B per step for the running example on 2 ranks, loss equal to one process). Applied blindly to the fused qkv matrix, ColwiseParallel gave rank 0 all of q and half of k, and the forward failed: fused layers need a custom split. Pipeline parallelism (torch.distributed.pipelining) puts different layers on different ranks and streams micro-batches through them; with 2 stages and 4 micro-batches the 1F1B schedule peaked at 16.8 and 12.4 MB per rank against GPipe's 21.1 and 21.1 MB, and the tied embedding, now on two stages, needed a manual all-reduce of its gradient. Context parallelism (2.7) splits the sequence inside attention for long contexts; it was not run here. torchtitan composes all of them on one mesh.

Checkpoints. Distributed checkpointing (DCP, torch.distributed.checkpoint) has every rank write its own shards plus one metadata file, and can load into a different number of ranks: saved from 4 FSDP2 ranks (a 144,161-byte .metadata and four files of about 9.8 MB), it loaded into 2 ranks and into a single process with a largest parameter difference of 6.9e-6 against a one-process run of the same steps.

What a hang looks like

A rank that skips one all-reduce leaves the others waiting. With a 10-second timeout, both ranks failed with "Timed out waiting 10000ms for recv operation to complete", and the flight recorder showed why: at sequence number 4, rank 0 was in all_reduce while rank 1 was in barrier. The default timeouts are 10 minutes for NCCL and 30 minutes for other backends (init_process_group docstring at 2.14.1), so in production a hang looks like a silent job for ten minutes. A shape mismatch was caught at once with TORCH_DISTRIBUTED_DEBUG=DETAIL ("Detected mismatch between collectives on ranks ... Tensor shapes: 1000vs 999"), at the price of 2 extra all-gathers per collective, and monitored_barrier names the missing rank ("Rank 1 failed to pass monitoredBarrier in 5000 ms") measured, CPU, gloo ({{Distributed lab|#t-dist}}, section 11, has each case with its error text and flight-recorder entries).

What carries over to NVIDIA GPUs, and what does not
Carries over: which collectives run, in what order, with what sizes (except gloo's reduce-scatter), bucket layouts, sharding and memory per rank, DCP files, the hang diagnostics (the flight recorder works on any backend at 2.14). Does not: speed. Gloo over local sockets is nothing like NCCL over NVLink, so no step time from these runs predicts a GPU cluster; overlap of communication with compute, the reason buckets and prefetching exist, only pays where communication is fast and asynchronous. Two macOS quirks met on the way: torchrun hangs at start-up unless MASTER_ADDR=127.0.0.1, and fully_shard without an explicit CPU mesh fails because it picks the MPS accelerator.
Predict: 2 ranks, and your code does if rank == 0: dist.all_reduce(loss_for_logging). What happens?
A hang. Rank 0's all-reduce pairs with whatever collective rank 1 calls next (a DDP bucket all-reduce, a barrier), so sizes and order no longer match; with NCCL nothing errors until the watchdog's 10-minute timeout. Log on all ranks or reduce on all ranks, and read the flight recorder to find which rank is where.

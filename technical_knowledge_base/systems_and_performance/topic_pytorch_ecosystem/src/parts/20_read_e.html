4. nn.Module, the optimizer and the loop

An nn.Module registers its parameters (tensors autograd treats as leaves), its buffers (saved, not trained) and its sub-modules, so model.parameters() finds every weight; the state_dict is the name-to-tensor dictionary you save and load. The running example ties its output layer to its input embedding, so model.parameters() yields 52 tensors while the state_dict has 53 entries, two names for one storage measured here; tools that walk names see the weight twice (torch.export listed 53 parameter inputs on the {{Compile lab|#t-comp}}). An optimizer holds the parameters and its own state (AdamW: two tensors per parameter); it comes as a for-loop, a foreach (each operation over all tensors at once) or a fused (one kernel) implementation, and at 2.14.1 the CPU and MPS default to the for-loop ({{_foreach_utils.py L8 to L23|https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/blob/v2.14.1/torch/utils/_foreach_utils.py#L8-L23}}).

The step every later section edits, written once:

# one training step (device = "cuda", "mps" or "cpu") model.train() opt = torch.optim.AdamW(groups, lr=6e-4, betas=(0.9, 0.95), weight_decay=0.1, fused=True) # section 6 for step in range(steps): for micro in range(accum): # gradient accumulation x, y = get_batch(cfg, step * accum + micro, device) # seeded: batch k is the same on every run with torch.autocast(device, dtype=torch.bfloat16): # the Autocast key of section 2 logits, loss = model(x, y) (loss / accum).backward() # gradients add up across micro-batches torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0) opt.step(); sched.step() opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)

Three lines carry most of the surprises, and each has an owner. Autocast runs matrix multiplies in bfloat16 and keeps softmax, losses and norms in float32, with per-device lists; float16 also needs torch.amp.GradScaler, because its narrow range lets small gradients underflow to zero, while bfloat16 has float32's range; and fewer bits only pay on hardware built for them (Making training fast, section 6; formats on {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}). Accumulation divides by the number of micro-batches, which is exact only when every micro-batch counts the same number of tokens ({{Libraries on top of PyTorch|n:3c65c17b0d0d81019d98f99978dc26f6}}, section 3), and under DDP all but the last micro-batch belong in model.no_sync() (section 7). Seeding by step number is what lets every page of this topic, and four libraries, reproduce the same losses.

What this page measured and read for this section, in full (optional)
  • Mixed precision (AMP). torch.autocast is the Autocast key of section 2: it casts the inputs of chosen operators (matrix multiplies, attention) to bfloat16 and leaves precision-sensitive ones (softmax, losses, norms) in float32. The weights and the optimizer stay in float32. With float16 instead of bfloat16 you also need torch.amp.GradScaler, because float16's narrow range lets small gradients underflow to zero; bfloat16 has float32's range and needs no scaler. The lists are per device: on the CPU the running example kept layer norms, adds, log_softmax and the loss in float32, while on MPS log_softmax ran in bfloat16 ({{Tensors, the dispatcher and autograd|n:3f85c17b0d0d81138595c1c6cd52f795}}, section 2.4). And fewer bits only pay on hardware built for them: on this laptop a bfloat16 autocast step of a larger variant (8 blocks, dmodel 512, 25.4 million parameters) was slower than float32 on MPS (208.0 against 143.0 ms) and over 100 times slower on the CPU, which has no bfloat16 arithmetic (43.7 s against 0.37 s) measured on Apple M1 Pro ({{Making training fast|n:3c65c17b0d0d810d9e1fdf9ec942c20d}}, section 6).
  • Determinism. Seed everything (torch.manual_seed), make the data order a function of the step, and turn on torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True) when you need bit-identical reruns (some GPU kernels are non-deterministic by default). The running example's batches are seeded by step number, which is what let the {{Ecosystem map|#t-map}} tab show the same three losses, 3.80137, 3.18793, 3.16783, from a plain loop, Lightning 2.6.6, the Hugging Face Trainer 5.19.0 and Accelerate 1.15.0 measured on Apple M1 Pro, CPU.
A framework is a set of defaults
That identical-loss result needed the Trainer's defaults switched off. Out of the box, the Hugging Face Trainer at 5.19.0 clips gradients at max_grad_norm=1.0, uses learning rate 5e-5 with a linear decay schedule, weight decay 0, adamw_torch_fused and seed 42, and shuffles the data; Lightning 2.6.6 does not clip and trains in 32-bit by default read from the installed libraries, Apple M1 Pro. Porting a loop to a framework changes the run unless you set these.

Accumulating 4 micro-batches without dividing the loss by 4 gives a gradient 4 times larger than one batch 4 times as large, because the mean loss of a big batch is the average of the four micro-batch means, and backward() sums. (That holds when every micro-batch has the same number of counted tokens. With padding or packed sequences of different lengths it does not: averaging the micro-batch means gave a 59.1% relative gradient error in a measured case, and of the Trainer, Accelerate and Lightning only the Trainer corrected it, and only for a model whose forward accepts **kwargs; {{Libraries on top of PyTorch|n:3c65c17b0d0d81019d98f99978dc26f6}}, section 3.) AdamW nearly hides the error (it divides by a running scale of the gradient), but gradient clipping, SGD and anything that logs gradient norms do not. Divide the loss, or compare gradient norms against a one-batch run.

7. Memory: what fills it during one step

The problem. A job that runs out of memory stops; a job that barely fits runs with a small batch and wastes the hardware. Before cutting memory you need to know what holds it and when, because the peak is a moment, not a total.

Five things live in memory during training (sizes for float32 and AdamW, with P parameters):

Parameters4 bytes × P. Alive the whole time.
Gradients4 bytes × P. Created during backward, layer by layer; freed by zero_grad(set_to_none=True).
Optimizer stateAdamW keeps two running averages per parameter: 8 bytes × P. Created at the first step(), alive from then on.
ActivationsTensors the forward pass saves for backward (section 3 of the root). They grow through forward and shrink through backward. They scale with batch × sequence length.
TemporariesShort-lived: a matmul's output before the next op consumes it, gradients of activations flowing back through backward.

So the fixed cost is 16 bytes per parameter (4 + 4 + 8). Mixed-precision training with float32 master weights also comes to 16 bytes, split differently (2 + 2 + 12 in the ZeRO paper's accounting; {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}} owns that arithmetic and how sharding divides it). The variable cost is the activations, and the peak usually lands at the start of backward, when every activation is still saved and the first gradients start to appear.

What carries over to NVIDIA GPUs. The categories and their sizes are the same bytes on any device for the same dtype and the same kernels. Two things differ: the attention kernel decides whether a sequence-by-sequence matrix is saved (section 10), and the CUDA caching allocator adds its own overhead on top of the live bytes shown here (cached free blocks, rounding of allocation sizes; {{Memory allocators|n:3f05c17b0d0d81209ae9d856695dc958}} has the details), which is why memory_reserved exceeds the profiler's peak.

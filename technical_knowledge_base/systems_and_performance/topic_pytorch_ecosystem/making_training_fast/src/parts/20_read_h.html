Common mistakes, each with the section that shows it (14)
  • Timing GPU code without a synchronize: you time the queuing, not the work; use torch.utils.benchmark.Timer, which synchronises for you at 2.14.1 (1).
  • Reading the big .item() bar as the problem: it is where queued work surfaces; removing the .item() moves the wait elsewhere unless the GPU had idle time to overlap. Look at the device lane, or time phases with a synchronize after each (3).
  • Applying the standard checklist without measuring: bfloat16 autocast and torch.compile made this machine slower; each fix is a hypothesis (3).
  • Accumulating gradients without dividing the loss: the gradient comes out exactly the number of micro-batches times too large (7).
  • Clipping before unscaling with a GradScaler: you clip the scaled gradient (65,536 times too large at the default scale) and the threshold means nothing (7).
  • Assuming torch.manual_seed seeds everything: it does not seed Python's random or NumPy in the main process; DataLoader workers seed all three for you (4, 7).
  • Ignoring load_state_dict(strict=False)'s return value: missing keys keep their random initialisation silently; and tied weights break safetensors, loading and to_empty (7).
  • Adding up 16 bytes per parameter and all activations as "the peak": gradients grow while activations shrink, so the peak is the larger of two moments and the sum overestimates it (8).
  • A selective-checkpointing policy that names the wrong ops: it saves nothing and silently behaves like full checkpointing (9).
  • Calling F.linear_cross_entropy without options: the reference path saves no memory at 2.14.1 (10).
  • Expecting sdpa_kernel([FLASH_ATTENTION]) to force a kernel everywhere: on MPS it is ignored; on CPU and CUDA it restricts the choice and raises if nothing qualifies (11).
  • Calling flex_attention without torch.compile, or building the BlockMask every step: eager materialises the whole score matrix; build the mask once per shape (11).
  • Assuming "int4" means 4 bits in memory: IntxWeightOnlyConfig(int4) stored a byte per value and came out larger than int8 (12).
  • Leaving the profiler on for the whole run: use a schedule (2).
Interview rehearsal: thirteen questions with short answers (optional)
Your training step got slower after you added torch.cuda.synchronize() for timing. Why, and is the number wrong?
It removed CPU and GPU overlap, so the step is a little slower; the number is now honest about one step's latency. For throughput, time many steps between two synchronizes.
How do you tell a data-loading bottleneck from a compute one?
In the trace: gaps on the GPU before forward and long time inside the DataLoader's __next__. Or time next(iter(loader)) alone against the step. Fix with workers, prefetching, pre-processing offline.
Your MFU is 30%. Where do you look?
The trace's device lane for gaps (data, launches, synchronisations), then the share of non-matmul kernels. Gaps are fixed by workers, fusion, CUDA graphs and removing per-step syncs; a matmul share that is already high means the remaining lever is precision (bfloat16 or float8 tensor cores) or larger matmuls.
Where is peak memory usually reached in a training step, and what lowers it?
At the start of backward (all activations saved, first gradients appearing). Checkpointing, smaller micro-batch, shorter sequence, fused losses, flash attention. If the peak is at the end of backward, it is gradients and optimizer state: shard them (FSDP2) or use a lighter optimizer.
Full checkpointing against selective checkpointing?
Full keeps only block inputs and recomputes the block's forward (about one extra forward). SAC saves chosen ops (typically matmuls) and recomputes the rest: less recompute, less saving. Which wins depends on where the bytes are: here the matmul outputs were most of the bytes.
Why does a large vocabulary blow up memory, and what fixes it?
Logits are batch × sequence × vocabulary floats, plus their softmax and gradient. A fused linear cross-entropy computes them in chunks and never holds them all (nn.LinearCrossEntropyLoss, PyTorch 2.13+).
Why does only float16 need a GradScaler?
float16 has 5 exponent bits: gradients below about 6e-8 vanish (1,636 of 3.19 million here) and the maximum is 65,504. bfloat16 has float32's 8 exponent bits, so its range is the same as float32's.
Your parameter count from the state_dict is higher than from parameters(). Why?
Tied (shared) parameters appear once per name in the state_dict: 3,197,952 against 3,186,944 elements here.
What does SDPA do when you pass an arbitrary attn_mask on CUDA?
The flash backend only supports is_causal, so it falls to memory-efficient attention (supports additive masks) or math. FlexAttention covers structured masks without a dense mask tensor.
What is a BlockMask and why does it matter?
A tile-level summary of a mask_mod: per row of tiles, which tiles are fully allowed, partly allowed, or empty. The kernel skips empty tiles entirely, so sparse patterns (sliding window, documents) cost less than causal.
Weight-only int8 against dynamic int8 activations: when each?
Weight-only when memory-bound (small batch decoding: each weight is read once per token). Quantized activations when compute-bound (large batches, prefill, training), where int8 or float8 matmuls are faster than float ones.
Why does float8 training need special hardware, and what does torchao do?
Speed needs float8 tensor cores (compute capability 8.9 or newer). torchao's Float8Linear casts inputs, weights and gradients to float8 with per-tensor or per-row scales and keeps master weights in high precision; on other hardware it can only emulate.
for-loop, foreach, fused optimizers?
Several ops per tensor; a few multi-tensor ops per step; one kernel for all. Fused is fastest where supported; foreach is the CUDA default at 2.14.1.

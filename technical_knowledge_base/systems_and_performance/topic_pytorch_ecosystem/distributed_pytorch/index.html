The idea in one screen

Distributed PyTorch runs the same script in several processes, one per device, which cooperate only through collectives (calls every process of a group makes together, such as summing a tensor across all of them). Every API on this page is a rule for what each process holds and which collectives it calls: copy the model and split the data (DDP), shard the model's state too (FSDP2), split the matrices (tensor parallel), split the sequence (sequence and context parallel), split the layers (pipelining), and save it all without gathering it in one place (DCP).

1. Launch, process groups and the store (c10d)

Eight Python processes start on eight GPUs. Before any tensor moves, each must learn who it is, find the others and agree on a channel. c10d (PyTorch's distributed core) does this in three layers: a store (a small key-value server everyone can reach), a process group (a set of ranks plus a backend) and the backend itself.

torchrun starts the workers and names them; the store introduces them; a mesh cuts them into groups

torchrun --nproc_per_node 4 train.py starts 4 copies with RANK, LOCAL_RANK, WORLD_SIZE, MASTER_ADDR and MASTER_PORT set. init_process_group("gloo") connects each to a TCPStore at that address; every rank writes its address under a key and reads the others' (the rendezvous). init_device_mesh("cpu", (2, 2), mesh_dim_names=("dp", "tp")) lays ranks 0 to 3 out as [[0, 1], [2, 3]] and creates one group per row ("tp": {0, 1}, {2, 3}) and per column ("dp": {0, 2}, {1, 3}); recorded on rank 1: coordinate (0, 1), tp group [0, 1], dp group [1, 3]. Measured, 4 CPU processes.

Neighbouring ranks share a row, which is why torchtitan puts tensor parallelism on the innermost (last) mesh dimension: neighbouring ranks share a server and its fast links (section 10). A collective can also run on any subset you name, dist.new_group([0, 1]). The Mesh explorer shows every group of every layout run here.

Elastic restarts

When one process dies, the others block in their next collective. torchrun --max-restarts N turns that into a restart: its agent kills the survivors, runs the rendezvous again and starts every worker from the top of the script, with TORCHELASTIC_RESTART_COUNT incremented. Nothing is kept in memory, so the script must resume from its own checkpoint. Run here with rank 1 crashing at step 3:

2. Collectives: what each one leaves on every rank, and what it costs

Every collective c10d offers, called once with 1 MiB (262,144 float32 values) per rank on 4 ranks, as PyTorch's flight recorder (a per-process log of every collective: name, sizes, dtypes, sequence number) saw it. The five verbs on toy values are in the parent lab's collectives primer (Distributed lab tab, section 2).

Measured, 4 CPU processes, gloo. "Bytes a ring sends per rank" is the textbook cost for S bytes on n ranks; backends pick their own algorithms (Interconnects and scaling owns them).

Two things the recording adds. reduce_scatter_tensor appears as an all_reduce of the whole tensor, because gloo has no reduce-scatter (it all-reduces and keeps a chunk; ProcessGroupGloo.cpp L1557): every gradient reduction on this page reads as an all-reduce. And all_to_all, the one the lab does not draw, sends rank r's j-th piece to rank j (recorded on 4 ranks, rank 1 sent ; every rank's piece number 1 landed on rank 1): it is what expert parallelism and Ulysses-style sequence parallelism are built on.

Cost per call: a fixed latency, then bytes over bandwidth

A collective's time is roughly α + S/B: a fixed latency α per call plus the bytes over an effective bandwidth. Small messages are all latency and big ones all bandwidth, which is why DDP groups gradients into buckets of megabytes and FSDP gathers a whole block at once. Predict first, then read the chart.

Predict: at 64 MiB on 2 processes, which is fastest: all-reduce, all-gather, or broadcast?
Broadcast, 10.7 ms against 25.7 ms for all-reduce and 26.9 ms for all-gather. A broadcast moves the data one way; an all-reduce must both combine and redistribute (in ring terms 2(n − 1)/n of S per rank, against (n − 1)/n for each half), and gloo's CPU reduction adds arithmetic. That factor of about two between an all-reduce and either half is the same one that makes FSDP's traffic about 1.5 times DDP's.

Up to about 256 KiB every collective costs only its latency; past a few hundred KiB, time grows with the bytes

Measured on Apple M1 Pro, CPU, gloo over loopback, one thread per process, under the shared bench lock: median of 15 timed calls after 3 warm-ups, time of the slowest rank, barrier before each call. Load average at the end . The fit is least squares on the medians; where its two terms are equal (α × B) lies between about 160 KiB and 1.3 MiB for these collectives (derived from the fit). The shape transfers: a latency floor for small messages, then time growing with bytes, and all-reduce costing about two of its halves. The numbers do not. 3. DDP: buckets that all-reduce while backward runs

DDP (DistributedDataParallel) keeps a full model on every rank and averages gradients during backward. The C++ reducer (torch/csrc/distributed/c10d/reducer.cpp) puts one autograd hook on each parameter. Gradients are grouped into buckets; when every gradient of a bucket is ready, the bucket goes to a communication hook (by default an all-reduce) and backward carries on. In the first step all gradients share one bucket; after it, DDP rebuilds the buckets in the order the gradients actually arrived, the first one closing at about 1 MiB and the rest at 25 MiB (bucket_cap_mb), so the first bucket fills early in backward.

After the rebuild, the first all-reduce leaves 1 ms into backward instead of after it

Step 0: one bucket of 12,747,776 bytes, all-reduced 30.7 ms into backward, when the last gradient arrived. Step 1: buckets of 1,051,648 bytes (ln_f and block 3's mlp.proj: 4 tensors) and 11,696,128 bytes (the other 48), all-reduced at 1.0 and 17.25 ms. Measured: the parent lab's DDP run (2 CPU processes, gloo), reshaped by src/ddp_timeline.py. On NCCL the all-reduce runs on its own CUDA stream while backward computes; on gloo with one thread per process the overlap is limited, so read the order, not the gaps.

Three switches change that machinery, each measured here on 2 processes.

Unused parameters. The reducer waits for every parameter of a bucket; one that forward did not use never gets a gradient, so its bucket never completes and DDP raises at the next step. find_unused_parameters=True walks the autograd graph from the outputs every step and all-reduces a used-parameter bitmap (216 bytes here: 54 parameters × 4 bytes); static_graph=True promises the same set every step, learns it once and rebuilds buckets after step 1.

Gradient accumulation. With 4 micro-batches per optimizer step, only the sum matters, so all-reducing after each wastes three. with ddp.no_sync(): lets backward accumulate locally:

for i, (x, y) in enumerate(micro_batches):         # 4 per optimizer step
    ctx = ddp.no_sync() if i < 3 else contextlib.nullcontext()
    with ctx:
        _, loss = ddp(x, y)
        (loss / 4).backward()                        # the last backward all-reduces the sum
opt.step(); opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
Predict: after buckets are rebuilt, how many collectives do 4 micro-batches issue with and without no_sync?

Communication hooks. ddp.register_comm_hook(state, hook) replaces each bucket's all-reduce with any function returning a future of the reduced bucket. PyTorch ships plain all-reduce, compression to 16 bits (fp16_compress_hook: 6,373,888 bytes per step instead of 12,747,776, gradient error 2.8e-4) and PowerSGD (Vogels et al. 2019, arXiv 1905.13727), which replaces each gradient matrix by a rank-r product P QT and all-reduces only P and Q (327,344 bytes at rank 4, 39 times fewer), keeping what it dropped as error feedback for the next step. Fewer bytes is not a faster step on NVLink, where 12.75 MB is cheap and PowerSGD's extra matrix work may cost more than it saves; the paper reports its gains on slower networks.

4. FSDP2: fully_shard, one unit gathered at a time

FSDP2 (torch.distributed.fsdp.fully_shard) is PyTorch's ZeRO stage 3 (the form is on Distributed Training, section 4): each rank keeps 1/n of every parameter, gradient and optimizer state, gathers a unit's full weights just before using them and reduce-scatters its gradients. You call fully_shard on each block, then on the whole model; each call makes one unit. What it does to a module is concrete:

  • every parameter becomes a DTensor (section 5) with placement Shard(0): on 4 ranks block 0's mlp.fc.weight [1024, 256] is [256, 256] per rank;
  • the module's class is swapped for a subclass that also inherits FSDPModule (checked: GPT becomes FSDPGPT, bases FSDPModule then GPT; each Block becomes FSDPBlock), so model.unshard(), reshard() and set_requires_gradient_sync() exist;
  • forward pre/post hooks and autograd hooks schedule the gathers and reductions (how they sit on the tensor-subclass machinery: Extending PyTorch).

One step on 4 ranks: 9 all-gathers bring each unit in, 5 reductions send its gradients out

Rank 0's step in issue order. The root unit (embeddings, final LayerNorm, the tied head) is gathered once and kept through backward, because backward needs it first; each block is gathered for its forward, freed, gathered again for its backward, and its gradients reduced: 1 + 4 + 4 = 9 all-gathers (28,160 bytes in for the root, 789,760 per block) and 4 + 1 = 5 reductions (3,159,040 bytes per block, 112,640 for the root; on gloo each is an all-reduce of the full gradient, section 2). The parent's Distributed lab tab animates this step against DDP's. Measured, 4 CPU processes, step 1.

Prefetching: issue the next gather early

By default FSDP2 prefetches implicitly: while block i runs forward it has already issued block i + 1's all-gather; in backward, the next block's. block.set_modules_to_forward_prefetch([...]) and set_modules_to_backward_prefetch([...]) name the modules explicitly, here two ahead.

5. DTensor and DeviceMesh: placements, and how an operation picks its output's

A DTensor (torch.distributed.tensor) is one logical tensor spread over a DeviceMesh, with one placement per mesh dimension: Shard(d) (split along tensor dimension d), Replicate() (every rank has all of it) or Partial() (every rank has a full-shape tensor and the true value is their sum). FSDP2, tensor parallel and DCP are all built on it.

A matrix product of two shards is a pending sum, free; a nonlinearity on that sum forces an all-reduce first

2 ranks, A 4 × 6 and B 6 × 8 as run here. Left: the three placements of A. Right: A split by columns times B split by rows leaves each rank a full-shape partial product (S(1) x S(0) → P, no communication); relu of a sum is not the sum of relus, so DTensor all-reduces to Replicate before applying it. Measured, 2 CPU processes; collectives as CommDebugMode counted them; every result checked against plain tensors.

How it decides. DTensor is a tensor subclass (built as the Extending PyTorch child describes, with a C++ fast path). For every ATen operation it asks the sharding propagator: given the inputs' placements, which output placement can be computed locally, and if none, which inputs must be redistributed first? Each operation registers a strategy, a list of (output placements, required input placements) pairs; DTensor picks the cheapest, runs any redistribution (a collective), then runs the plain operation on the local pieces. Four rules fall out of 28 recorded cases, and they are most of tensor parallelism:

  • Linear operations commute with the sum, so a Partial passes through them free: S(1) x S(0) → P; Partial + Partial and 2 × Partial stay Partial.
  • Nonlinear ones do not. relu(Partial) forced an all-reduce first. This is why Megatron's MLP puts the GeLU between a column split and a row split.
  • Operations that need a whole row need it gathered. Softmax and LayerNorm along a sharded dimension all-gathered it (output Replicate); along an unsharded dimension they ran locally. Sequence parallelism (section 6) uses exactly this: LayerNorm is per token, so splitting the sequence is free.
  • A row-sharded embedding table gives a new kind of placement, MaskPartial: each rank looks up the ids it owns, writes zeros elsewhere, and the sum is pending. That is vocabulary-parallel embedding.

Moving between placements is redistribute, one collective per change (Shard to Replicate is an all-gather, Partial to Replicate an all-reduce, Partial to Shard a reduce-scatter); the parent lab's redistribution explorer (Distributed lab tab, section 7) has every pair. Wrapping existing tensors is DTensor.from_local(t, mesh, [Shard(0)]), which declares that the ranks' local tensors together form one DTensor and moves no data; to_local() returns the local piece without copying.

A custom operator needs a strategy. An operator DTensor does not know raises at once. Recorded for a small torch.library op dpt::rowscale(x, s) = x * s[:, None]: Registering a strategy fixes it:

from torch.distributed.tensor.experimental import register_sharding

@register_sharding(torch.ops.dpt.rowscale.default)
def rowscale_strategy(x, s):
    # (output placements, input placements) pairs DTensor may choose from
    return [([Shard(0)], [Shard(0), Shard(0)]),       # rows split: each rank scales its own rows
            ([Replicate()], [Replicate(), Replicate()]),
            ([Shard(1)], [Shard(1), Replicate()])]     # columns split: needs all of s

The fourth line asked for x by columns and s by rows; no strategy matches, so DTensor redistributed s to Replicate with one all-gather and picked the third strategy. Measured, 2 CPU processes.

6. Tensor and sequence parallelism: parallelize_module and its plan

Tensor parallelism (TP) splits the weight matrices inside a layer across ranks that all see the same batch (the Megatron split and its maths: Distributed Training, section 5, and the Megatron-LM paper page). PyTorch's API is parallelize_module(module, mesh, plan), where the plan maps submodule names to styles: ColwiseParallel (shard the weight's output dimension: each rank computes some output features), RowwiseParallel (shard the input dimension: each rank produces a Partial sum, then an all-reduce), SequenceParallel and PrepareModuleInput (convert a module's inputs between placements). Each style turns the module's parameters into DTensors and installs hooks that convert inputs and outputs.

Making attention splittable. The running example fuses q, k and v into one qkv projection, which cannot be column-split blindly (the parent lab found rank 0 got all of q and half of k); torchtitan's models use separate wq, wk, wv. Here each block's attention was swapped at run time for an equivalent module with separate q, k, v built from the same weights, whose forward uses as many heads as its local shard holds (src/exp/dpt_parts.py; the model file is unchanged). With 2 ranks each holds 2 of the 4 heads; losses equal one process at every step (3.801371, 3.187931, 3.167831) and gradients agree to 2.1e-6.

The plan decides the communication: 24 all-reduces or 16. Colwise on q, k and v separately means each wraps the same input in its own DTensor, and in backward each sends its own input-gradient all-reduce: 3 per attention instead of 1. Wrapping the attention input once ("attn": PrepareModuleInput(input_layouts=Replicate(), desired_input_layouts=Replicate(), use_local_output=False)) makes q, k and v share one DTensor input, whose gradients add up locally before a single all-reduce. Recorded per step: 24 all-reduces of 524,288 bytes with the first plan, 16 with the second (8 in forward: one after each attention and MLP of 4 blocks; 8 in backward). Same losses, same gradients, one third less communication.

Sequence parallelism: stop duplicating the cheap parts

Under TP, everything between the matrix multiplies (LayerNorms, the residual stream, dropout) runs on the full activation on every rank. Sequence parallelism (Korthikanti et al. 2022, arXiv 2205.05198) splits those along the sequence instead, which is free because they work token by token: each all-reduce becomes a reduce-scatter (each rank keeps its half of the tokens, summed) followed later by an all-gather (rebuild the full sequence before the next matrix multiply). In the plan, the LayerNorms get SequenceParallel(), the row-wise projections output Shard(1) (the sequence dimension), and PrepareModuleInput gathers the attention's and the MLP's inputs back to Replicate() (src/exp/tp_sp.py). The same block, before and after, recorded on 2 processes:

Sequence parallelism turns each all-reduce into a reduce-scatter and an all-gather, and halves the tokens between them

Each lane is one rank's activation for one block's forward, the 64 tokens drawn as 8 tiles of 8; dark = rows it holds, gold = a Partial sum not yet reduced, light = just gathered. Placements and local shapes at each module boundary are the recorded ones (ln1 input local shape (8, 64, 256) under TP, (8, 32, 256) under TP + SP); counters are per training step of the whole model (4 blocks, forward and backward), from the flight recorder. Measured, 2 CPU processes, gloo.

What the recording says: the all-reduces become pairs of all-gathers (262,144 bytes in per rank: half the activation) and reduce-scatters (gloo runs them as all-reduces of the full 524,288 bytes; NCCL would move half). In ring terms the bytes per step are about the same as TP's (34 halves of 262,144 bytes, 8.9 MB, against 16 all-reduces costing 524,288 bytes each on 2 ranks, 8.4 MB), because an all-reduce is a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather; the gain is memory and work. Saved activations per rank fell from 22.17 MB to 20.06 MB here (one process: 47.41 MB): small, because on this model the LayerNorm and residual activations are a small part of what is saved. On long sequences and many layers it is the saving that makes TP + SP + selective recomputation fit (Korthikanti et al., Table 2; on Distributed Training, section 5).

Predict: in TP + SP, what does a LayerNorm's weight gradient need at the end of backward?
An all-reduce. Each rank computed the LayerNorm on its own half of the tokens, so each holds a Partial gradient of the (replicated) weight. SequenceParallel makes the weight a Replicate DTensor, and DTensor reduces its Partial gradient for you: the gradients matched one process to 1.7e-6. Under plain TP the LayerNorm saw all tokens on every rank and no reduction was needed.

7. Context parallelism: the API, run on a CPU

At long context the activations of attention grow with the sequence and can outgrow one GPU on their own, so context parallelism (CP) splits the sequence inside attention too: each rank keeps the queries of its own tokens and the keys and values travel, by ring passes or one all-gather, with partial outputs merged by their log-sum-exp (the form, its three traffic patterns and the causal load balancing: Distributed Training, section 8; the online softmax it reuses: FlashAttention paper page). PyTorch's API is a context manager that shards the named buffers along their sequence dimension and swaps the attention kernel for a ring version (with a causal mask it also reorders the tokens so that rank 0 gets the first and last chunk, balancing the work): with context_parallel(mesh, buffers=[q, k, v], buffer_seq_dims=[2, 2, 2]): F.scaled_dot_product_attention(...). Run here on the real q, k and v of the running example's block 0 (8 × 4 heads × 64 tokens × 64), split over 2 and 4 processes:

  • The public API fails on a CPU: Its handlers cover the CUDA flash, memory-efficient and cuDNN attention kernels only (_context_parallel/_attention.py L907-955); on a CPU, SDPA dispatches to a different kernel.
  • The ring itself works. PyTorch's ring template (_templated_ring_attention) called with the CPU flash kernel, load balancing off, matched full attention on every rank's tokens to . With the default rotation the "ring" is one all-gather of each rank's K and V (); with set_rotate_method("alltoall") it is n − 1 exchanges ().
  • A model must be written for CP. The running example numbers its positions arange(T) of the local length, so each rank would count its tokens from 0; torchtitan passes positions in. torchtitan applies CP to every attention module before TP and FSDP (models/llama3/parallelize.py L46-51, 0.3.0) and folds the cp axis into the FSDP mesh, because ranks that split one sequence still need their weight gradients summed (distributed/parallel_dims.py, docstring of build_mesh). Not run on the whole model here.

8. Pipelining: stages and schedules

torch.distributed.pipelining gives each rank a contiguous run of layers (a stage, PipelineStage(module, stage_index, num_stages, device)), cuts the batch into micro-batches and streams them through: forward activations go to the next stage by send/recv, their gradients come back. A schedule object (Schedule1F1B(stage, n_microbatches, loss_fn), then schedule.step(x, target=y)) decides each rank's order of forward (F) and backward (B) work. The idle fill and drain, the bubble, is (p − 1)/(m + p − 1) of the step for p equal stages and m micro-batches (derived on Distributed Training, section 6). At 2.14.1 the library has seven schedules, and all seven ran here:

Every schedule's recorded order on two ranks: zero-bubble schedules fill the idle gaps with weight gradients

The 8-block, dmodel 512 variant of the running example (25.3M parameters, so that compute outweighs Python overhead), cut into 2 stages (GPipe, 1F1B) or 4 (two per rank), batch 8 × 64 in 4 or 8 micro-batches, 2 CPU processes (4 for GPipe and 1F1B at 8 micro-batches). Every F, B, I and W action was timed by wrapping the stage's methods; the median of 5 timed steps is drawn. The ideal clock replays each rank's recorded order with F = 1, B = 2, I = 1, W = 1 units and no communication, respecting what each action waits for: the schedule's own bubble, apart from this machine's noise. Measured on Apple M1 Pro, CPU, gloo, under the bench lock.

Bubble = 1 − (time in compute actions) / (step time), per rank; formula column with p = 2 ranks. Every schedule's gradients matched one process (largest difference 1.3e-7 on the untied parameters).

What the schedules change. 1F1B runs a backward as soon as one is possible, so a stage holds at most p micro-batches of activations instead of m (memory, not bubble). Interleaved 1F1B gives each rank v stages spread along the model (rank 0 holds stages 0 and 2), which divides the fill and drain by v at the price of v times more send/recv. Zero-bubble schedules (Qi et al. 2023, arXiv 2401.10241) split each backward into the input gradient (I, needed by the previous stage now) and the weight gradient (W, needed only by the optimizer), and slot the Ws into what would have been idle time. The V layouts (ZBVZeroBubble, DualPipeV from DeepSeek-V3) give rank 0 the first and the last stage, so the pipeline turns round on the same rank. Measured: ZBVZeroBubble idled 0.08 and 0.07 of the step at m = 4 against 1F1B's 0.19 and 0.10.

A V schedule silently scales a tied weight twice
In the V layouts the token embedding (stage 0) and the output layer (stage 3) share one rank, and here one Parameter. But every stage divides its parameters' gradients by the number of micro-batches at the end of its backward (scale_grads, stage.py L857-869), and a parameter in two stages is divided twice, once after part of its gradient has arrived. Recorded: the tied gradient came out 0.37 of the right one, with no error. Loop-style schedules put the two copies on different ranks, where a manual all-reduce of the two copies fixes them (largest difference 1.6e-7 over these runs; 1.5e-7 in the parent lab). Untie the weights, or pass scale_grads=False and divide once yourself.

10. Composing PP, FSDP and TP the torchtitan way

Real jobs combine several of these on one mesh. torchtitan (PyTorch's reference trainer; release 0.3.0 read here) does it in a fixed order, and the order is the lesson:

  1. Mesh, with tensor parallel innermost: dimensions ["pp", "dp_replicate", "fsdp", "tp"] unflattened from one world mesh (distributed/parallel_dims.py, build_mesh). Ranks next to each other share a server, so the chattiest axis (TP: collectives in every layer) gets the fastest links and the quietest (PP: point-to-point once per micro-batch) spans servers.
  2. Pipeline split first: the model (built on the meta device, no memory) is cut into stage modules, and everything below is applied to each stage (distributed/pipeline_parallel.py pipeline_llm, L65-125).
  3. Per stage: context parallel, then TP, then activation checkpointing, then torch.compile per block, then FSDP2 last (models/llama3/parallelize.py L42-94). FSDP2 goes last because it shards whatever DTensors TP produced along one more mesh dimension; compile goes before it so each block compiles once.
  4. Then weights are materialised and initialised on each rank (to_empty, init_weights), and the schedule (1F1B by default, config/configs.py L210) drives training.

Each layer of the recipe cuts the same weight again: TP's half, then FSDP's half of that

11. Debugging: hangs, mismatches and the flight recorder

The rule behind every hang: all ranks of a group must call the same collectives, in the same order, with matching sizes. A collective completes only when every member calls it, so one rank that skips one, calls a different one, or dies leaves the rest waiting until the timeout (10 minutes for NCCL, 30 for other backends by default at 2.14.1). The parent lab caused three hangs on purpose; this page met the rule three more times without trying: PowerSGD's callbacks (section 3), async_save on the default group (section 9) and a from_local with wrongly sized pieces (section 5).

The first sequence number at which ranks disagree is the bug

The parent lab's "skipped all-reduce", dumped from both ranks and lined up by torchfrtrace: collectives 1 to 3 (all-reduces) agree; at sequence number 4 rank 0 called an all_reduce that only rank 0 runs (a logging all-reduce under if rank == 0) while rank 1 went on to barrier: COLLECTIVE_TYPE_MISMATCH, culprit rank 0, with the stack naming the line. Measured, 2 CPU processes, gloo, torch 2.14.1.

Every process group keeps a ring buffer of its recent collectives (TORCH_FR_BUFFER_SIZE entries: name, sizes, dtypes, sequence number, stack). On NCCL, TORCH_NCCL_DUMP_ON_TIMEOUT=1 makes the watchdog write one file per rank when a collective times out, and torchfrtrace <dir> (shipped with PyTorch as torch.distributed.flight_recorder.fr_trace) lines the ranks up and names the first mismatch. So for a 512-GPU job that hangs: find the first sequence number where ranks disagree on the operation or its sizes, or where a rank is missing, then read that rank's stack. torchrun sets TORCH_NCCL_ASYNC_ERROR_HANDLING=1 for its workers, which makes the NCCL watchdog abort the process on a timeout instead of leaving it hung.

Mistakes seen on this page, one line each (1 min)
  • init_process_group() with no backend on a machine with an accelerator: c10d picked "mps:gloo" here and every CPU collective failed (section 1, depth).
  • Expecting an elastic restart to keep the job's state: workers restart from the top of the script (section 1).
  • Leaving find_unused_parameters=True on "to be safe": a graph walk and a bitmap all-reduce every step (section 3).
  • Three ColwiseParallel styles on q, k, v and nothing else: 24 all-reduces instead of 16 (section 6).
  • dcp.async_save on the default process group, or any collectives issued from callbacks and threads: ranks issue them in different orders and the job aborts (sections 3 and 9).

  • Expecting gradient accumulation to remove FSDP2's all-gathers: it removes reductions only (section 4).

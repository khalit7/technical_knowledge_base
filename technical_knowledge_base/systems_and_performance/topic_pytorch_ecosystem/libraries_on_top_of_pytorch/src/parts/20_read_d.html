3. Loss averaging under gradient accumulation

A language-model batch rarely has the same number of real tokens in every row: padding positions get the label -100 and the loss is a mean over the real tokens. Split the batch into micro-batches, take each one's mean, divide by the number of micro-batches and add, and you get a mean of means, not the mean over the whole batch. Tokens in a short micro-batch count for more.

The animation, step by step

  1. 8 rows, 237 real tokens. The full batch weights every real token by 1/237, so a row's share of the gradient is its token count over 237 (grey bars: rows of 64, 60, 4, 6, 50, 40, 8 and 5 tokens). Now split it into 4 micro-batches of 2 rows and accumulate.
  2. Micro-batch 1: 124 real tokens. Its mean loss divides by 124, then by 4: each token weighs 1/496, about half its full-batch weight.
  3. Micro-batch 2: 10 real tokens. Divided by 10, then by 4: each token weighs 1/40, about 5.9 times its full-batch weight. These two short rows now pull as hard as micro-batch 1's long ones.
  4. Micro-batch 3: 90 real tokens. Each token weighs 1/360, about two thirds of its full-batch weight.
  5. Micro-batch 4: 13 real tokens. Each token weighs 1/52, about 4.6 times its full-batch weight.
  6. A different gradient. Measured on the real model, the accumulated gradient is 59.1% away from the full batch's (relative error). The 10 tokens of micro-batch 2 count as much as the 124 of micro-batch 1.
  1. 8 rows, 237 real tokens. The full batch weights every real token by 1/237 (grey bars). This time the step first counts the real tokens of all 4 micro-batches: 237.
  2. Micro-batch 1: 124 real tokens. Its summed loss is divided by all 237 tokens: each token weighs 1/237, exactly as in the full batch.
  3. Micro-batch 2: 10 real tokens. Summed and divided by 237: its two short rows keep their small share.
  4. Micro-batch 3: 90 real tokens. Summed and divided by 237.
  5. Micro-batch 4: 13 real tokens. Summed and divided by 237; the shares now add up to 1.000, as in one batch.
  6. The full-batch gradient. Measured on the real model: relative error 0. Same as one batch of 8, at a quarter of the activation memory.

The formula, same names. The full-batch loss is sum of token losses / N, with N the real tokens in the whole batch, so each token's gradient weight is 1/N. The mean of means gives a token in micro-batch j the weight 1/(K · nj), with K micro-batches and nj tokens in micro-batch j. The fix is to sum each micro-batch's token losses and divide by N, which must be known before the first backward: count the labels of all micro-batches first, and across ranks with one all-reduce of a single integer.

Why the Trainer has two behaviours. It passes the step's real-token count (num_items_in_batch) to the model only if the model's forward accepts **kwargs. A transformers model does and divides its summed loss by that count; your own nn.Module usually does not, and the Trainer silently falls back to the mean of means. torchtitan all-reduces the count over the data-parallel ranks and sets FSDP2's own gradient division to 1.0, because averaging again over ranks would divide twice.

Within a few hundredths: against at step 100 measured on Apple M1 Pro, CPU, and the gap changes sign along the way. On a toy run AdamW absorbs much of a per-step reweighting, so the curves look alike; the gradient is still a different estimator, biased towards short sequences. It matters most when sequence lengths vary a lot and correlate with content (short chat turns against long documents), with SGD-like optimisers, and whenever you compare a run with accumulation to one without.

How to check your own setup. Run one step as one batch and as K micro-batches on the same rows with clipping off, and compare the gradients with torch.allclose or the relative error above: the only way to know what your library and model combination does.

Source lines and history: when the libraries fixed it (provenance)

The Trainer counts the real tokens of all the micro-batches of one step ({{trainer.py L2281 to L2342|https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/v5.19.0/src/transformers/trainer.py#L2281-L2342}}) and passes the count only to a forward with **kwargs ({{L519 to L529|https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/v5.19.0/src/transformers/trainer.py#L519-L529}}), else divides by the number of micro-batches ({{L2089 to L2094|https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/v5.19.0/src/transformers/trainer.py#L2089-L2094}}). Checked on a transformers model: . torchtitan sums labels != -100 over every micro-batch, all-reduces the count and divides each micro-batch's summed loss by it ({{trainer.py L884 to L904|https://github.com/pytorch/torchtitan/blob/v0.3.0/torchtitan/trainer.py#L884-L904}}), then sets FSDP2's divide factor to 1.0 ({{fsdp.py L27 to L41|https://github.com/pytorch/torchtitan/blob/v0.3.0/torchtitan/distributed/fsdp.py#L27-L41}}).

The Trainer's token-count path arrived in October 2024 ({{PR 34191|https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/pull/34191}}; the {{Hugging Face blog post|https://huggingface.co/blog/gradient_accumulation}} tells the story). Its extension across data-parallel ranks, average_tokens_across_devices (default True at 5.19.0), answers {{issue 34242|https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/issues/34242}} (2024-10-18), which pointed out that averaging per rank has the same flaw.

1. What a training cluster is

A node is one server. A GPU training node typically holds 8 GPUs, two CPUs, local NVMe disks and one network card per GPU; Meta trained Llama 3 405B on servers of eight GPUs and two CPUs, up to 16K H100s in all published (Llama 3 §3.3.1). A cluster is many such nodes plus four things that turn them into one machine:

The parts of a cluster, and which network each byte travels on

Schematic, not to scale. The gradient all-reduce of a data-parallel step runs on the purple links inside a node and the orange fabric between nodes; data loading, checkpoints, logins and the scheduler's own messages use the green network.

  • Two networks for GPUs. Inside a node, GPUs talk over NVLink (the "scale-up" domain); between nodes, over a scale-out fabric, InfiniBand or Ethernet with RDMA (RoCE), usually one 400 Gb/s card per GPU. Per direction, an H100's NVLink is about 9 times one such card (450 against 50 GB/s), which is why tensor parallelism stays inside a node and data parallelism crosses nodes. The interconnects page owns these numbers and the network topologies (fat trees, rails).
  • A front-end network (ordinary Ethernet) for logins, the scheduler, container images, data and checkpoints, unless storage has its own fabric.
  • Shared storage that every node sees: a parallel file system or object storage (section 6).
  • Control machines: a login node where users edit and submit, and the controller (Slurm's slurmctld, Kubernetes' API server and scheduler, Ray's head node) that tracks every node and job.

Two units recur everywhere. A GPU-hour is one GPU for one hour, the unit of both cost and quota. A rank is one training process, normally one per GPU; a job on 128 nodes of 8 GPUs has 1,024 ranks.

2. How a job gets machines: schedulers

The problem. There are more jobs than GPUs, and a distributed job is useless until it holds all its GPUs at once. A scheduler (also called a workload manager or batch system) keeps a queue of pending jobs, each with a request (8 nodes of 8 GPUs for 12 hours), and a policy that decides which job starts next and on which nodes. Every scheduler in this topic combines the same handful of ideas:

  • FIFO with priorities. First in, first out, ordered by a priority number. Simple, but one big job at the head blocks everything behind it (head-of-line blocking) while nodes sit idle waiting for enough of them to free up.
  • Backfill. Let a later, smaller job run in those idle nodes if it will finish before the big job could have started anyway. The scheduler can only know that from each job's time limit, which is why every Slurm job asks for one (--time). EASY backfill protects only the first waiting job's reserved start; conservative backfill protects every waiting job.
  • Fairshare. Each team has a share of the cluster; a team that used more than its share recently gets lower priority, with past usage decaying (a half-life of days to weeks), so heavy users sink and light users rise.
  • Preemption. A higher-priority job can evict a running one, which is killed or requeued; it only costs little if the evicted job checkpoints (section 5).
  • Gang scheduling (all-or-nothing). Start every process of a job together or none of them. Without it, two jobs can each grab half the cluster and wait forever for the rest. OpenAI hit exactly that on Kubernetes: "Kubernetes might schedule only half of each experiment's pods, leading to a deadlock where neither experiment can make progress", fixed with a gang-scheduling plugin published (OpenAI, Jan 2021).
  • Topology-aware placement. Put a job's nodes under as few network switches as possible, because collectives across distant switches are slower.
  • Quotas and borrowing. Teams own a guaranteed amount; idle quota can be lent to others and reclaimed (by preemption) when its owner needs it.

Under FIFO every start matched this simulator to within seconds; under backfill six of eight did, and F and H started differently: Slurm reserves whole nodes for the job it protects, so F (7 minutes) could not start at minute 2 without overlapping B's reservation on both nodes and started with B at minute 6, while H (3 GPUs, 2 minutes) took the hole at minute 3. A scheduler packs only as finely as its plan resolves resources, and Slurm's plan resolves nodes (backfill.c at the version that ran; the lab quotes the lines). The jobs that jumped the queue were started by Slurm's backfill pass, not its main loop: the main loop stops at the first job that cannot start (B), and even on an idle cluster the Slurm from zero child page's log shows sched/backfill: _start_job: Started JobId=125, 444 ms after sbatch.

Backfill's gain here: everything finishes at 15 instead of 17, utilisation rises from 71% to 81%, the mean wait falls from 8.3 to 3.5, and B still starts at the same moment. The cost is a dependency on honest time limits: a job that asks for 3 times what it needs looks too long to fit in any gap and is not backfilled.

Predict: your 2-node job asks for 24 hours but needs 1. The cluster has 2 idle nodes for the next 3 hours, held for a big job after that. Under backfill, does yours start now?
No. The scheduler plans with your time limit, not your real runtime, so a 24-hour request cannot fit a 3-hour gap. Asking for 2 hours would have started it now. This is the most common reason a small job "waits forever" on a busy Slurm cluster.

3. Slurm, Kubernetes and Ray

Three systems hand out machines for training today, and they come from three different worlds.

  • Slurm comes from supercomputing (HPC). You ask for an allocation (whole nodes, for a time limit) with a batch script (sbatch); when it starts, srun launches your processes on every node of it. A central daemon (slurmctld) runs the queue with priorities, backfill and fairshare built in; a daemon on each node (slurmd) starts and watches the processes. An allocation is all-or-nothing, so gang scheduling comes free. Containers are an add-on (Pyxis with Enroot, or Apptainer).
  • Kubernetes comes from running web services. Everything is a container in a pod; you declare the state you want in YAML and controllers keep making it true; the kube-scheduler places pods one at a time on nodes that advertise GPUs through a device plugin (nvidia.com/gpu: 8). It filters nodes on GPUs, but its default scoring weighs only CPU and memory, so it does not pack GPUs unless you configure it to. It has no job queue, no fairshare and, by default, no gang scheduling (Kubernetes 1.37 has a native one behind the GenericWorkload feature gate, beta and off by default); for training you add Kueue (queues, team quotas, borrowing, and admitting a job only when all of it fits; its pod webhook is fail-closed, so while Kueue is down no pod can be created in the namespaces it manages), JobSet (a group of pods treated as one job, with stable DNS names for rendezvous) or Volcano, and often the Kubeflow Trainer on top.
  • Ray comes from distributed Python. It is a runtime rather than a cluster scheduler: a head node keeps cluster state (the GCS, Global Control Service), a raylet on each node schedules tasks (remote functions) and actors (remote objects with state), and a shared-memory object store moves data between them. Ray Train starts a group of worker actors and sets up PyTorch's process group for you; placement groups reserve bundles of GPUs together (PACK, SPREAD, STRICT_PACK). Ray usually runs on Kubernetes (through KubeRay) or on plain VMs, and leaves multi-team queues and quotas to the layer below (Kueue can admit Ray jobs: Kueue docs).

4. Launching a distributed job and finding peers

The problem. The scheduler hands you nodes; your training script needs one process per GPU, each knowing who it is and how to reach the others. Four numbers say who a process is: its rank (0 to W−1 across the job), the world size W (how many processes), its local rank (its index on its own node, which picks its GPU) and its node's index. A launcher starts the processes and gives them those numbers, usually as environment variables (RANK, WORLD_SIZE, LOCAL_RANK):

  • torchrun (PyTorch's launcher, also called the elastic agent) runs once per node and starts --nproc-per-node workers, restarting them if they fail.
  • Slurm: srun starts tasks on every node of the allocation and sets SLURM_PROCID, SLURM_NTASKS, SLURM_LOCALID and the node list; scripts either map those to PyTorch's names or run one torchrun per node.
  • Kubernetes: each pod of an indexed Job or JobSet gets an index and, through a headless service, a stable DNS name, so pod 0's name can serve as the meeting address.
  • Ray Train starts the worker actors and sets all of it itself.

Rendezvous is how the processes meet. Everyone is told one address, MASTER_ADDR:MASTER_PORT (usually rank 0's node); a small key-value server there, PyTorch's TCPStore (the "c10d store"), collects each rank's details, and nobody proceeds until all W have arrived. Then init_process_group builds the process group, the communicator that collectives such as all-reduce run on: gloo on CPUs (what this page's running example uses), NCCL on NVIDIA GPUs, which shares a unique id through the same store and then wires up its own rings and trees between GPUs.

What it looked like when one node of the running example never arrived, in the Launch lab: on Slurm (c10d rendezvous) nothing was logged for ten minutes, then at 602 s a bare torch.distributed.elastic.rendezvous.api.RendezvousTimeoutError with no message; on Kubernetes (static rendezvous) at 903 s, torch.distributed.DistStoreError: Timed out after 901 seconds waiting for clients. 1/2 clients joined.; on Ray, a fifth worker that could never be placed, the job never failed at all: The worker group startup timed out after 60.0 seconds waiting for 5 workers, retried every 60 s indefinitely (13 attempts in about 12 minutes, until it was stopped by hand) measured. Those times are set by timeouts, so they repeat; every waiting process held its CPUs (and fake GPUs) the whole time.

What it costs, and why it matters. The start of a job is a chain: wait in the queue, pull the container image to every node, start the containers, rendezvous, build the communicators, load the first batch, run the first step. Repeated five times per system (2 nodes x 2 ranks, the image already on every node), the running example's median from submit to its first finished step was 7.5 s on Slurm, 5.8 s on Kubernetes and 10.0 s on Ray measured on Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM, shared machine. By stage (medians, which need not add up to the median total): Slurm 0.7 s from sbatch to the batch script, 1.3 s for torchrun to import, 1.1 s of c10d rendezvous (the dynamic backend polls once a second), 2.5 s to spawn the workers and import torch, 0.01 s for init_process_group, 2.0 s for data, model and the DDP wrapper; Kubernetes 0.9 s for Kueue admission, scheduling and container start, 1.9 s of torchrun imports, 0.03 s of static rendezvous, 1.1 s of worker start, 1.3 s of data, model and DDP; Ray 1.6 s for the driver to import Ray, then 4.4 s for Ray Train's controller, placement group and worker processes, 0.9 s until the worker actors were ready, 0.3 s for the process group and 2.0 s for data, model and DDP. Pulling the image is not in these numbers; on a real cluster a multi-gigabyte image pulled to every node is often the longest stage, which is why images are cached on nodes. At thousands of GPUs every link of that chain is paid on every restart, which is why section 5's failure arithmetic counts restart time.

How a launch goes wrong. Almost every multi-node launch failure is a hang, not a crash, because the ranks that did arrive wait politely for the ones that did not. The usual causes: a wrong MASTER_ADDR (a name that resolves differently on each node), a port blocked by a firewall, a world size that does not match the processes actually started, a rank that crashed before it reached the store, or, after start-up, one rank stuck while the others wait inside a collective. The tools: TORCH_DISTRIBUTED_DEBUG=DETAIL and NCCL_DEBUG=INFO for logs, py-spy dump on a hung process to see where it waits, and PyTorch's flight recorder, which records each rank's last collectives so you can find the rank that is out of step. One trap, measured here on CPU builds only (torch 2.14.1 in a Linux container and on macOS; CUDA builds not checked): TORCH_DISTRIBUTED_DEBUG=DETAIL with DDP crashed the process with a segmentation fault (exit code -11) inside DDP's constructor, before any training; INFO worked, and DETAIL on raw collectives did report the mismatch. If DETAIL crashes, fall back to INFO.

Predict: a 512-GPU job has printed nothing for 20 minutes after start. 511 ranks' logs end at "init_process_group". What is the most likely cause?
The 511 identical logs are the clue: they are all waiting for the 512th. Look for the missing rank (the scheduler's per-node logs, that node's container status, a crash before init), not at the 511 that are fine. Running out of memory would crash with an error, not hang at init.

5. Failures and how runs survive them

The problem. A synchronous training job is a chain as strong as its weakest GPU: when one GPU, cable, switch, host or process fails, every rank stops at the next collective. Parts fail rarely, but a big job holds many parts. Meta's Llama 3 405B pre-training had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days on up to 16,384 H100s, about 78% of them confirmed or suspected hardware, yet kept "higher than 90% effective training time" published (Llama 3 §3.3.4, Table 5). That is one stop every 3.1 hours derived (54 × 24 / 419).

Why scale makes it worse. If each GPU (with its share of host and network) fails independently, a job on N GPUs is interrupted N times as often, so the mean time between failures (MTBF) of a job falls as 1/N. At Llama 3's rate, a 1,000-GPU job would stop about every 51 hours and a 100,000-GPU job every 30 minutes derived (assuming interruptions scale with GPU count, which is roughly but not exactly true: some causes are per job or per switch).

A newer table, a different top cause. Meta's 2026 report on a recurring pre-training job of about 32K GPUs counts 678 unexpected interruptions: GPU HBM3 memory is now the largest cause (155, 22.9%), then PCIe devices (18.0%) and NCCL watchdog timeouts (9.0%), with faulty GPU compute, Llama 3's top row, down to fourth (7.4%); the job averaged 2.3 interruptions per 1,000 servers per day and 95 to 97% effective training time published (arXiv 2602.00277, §2.2 and Table 1, Jan 2026). Llama 3's rate was about 3.8 per 1,000 servers per day derived (419 / 54 days / 2,048 servers). Read the same paper with a calculator: it also says that "with 100K GPUs, there is a failure every 18 minutes", which its own rate does not give. 100,000 GPUs at 8 per server are 12,500 servers; 12.5 × 2.3 = 28.75 interruptions a day, one about every 50 minutes derived. (18 minutes would need about 6.4 per 1,000 servers per day, close to the 6.5 per 1,000 node-days Meta reported for an older research cluster: Kokolis et al. 2024.)

How runs survive: checkpoint and restart. Every so often all ranks save the model, optimiser and data-loader state (a checkpoint); after a failure the job restarts from the last one. You lose the work since that checkpoint plus the restart time, and you pay for writing checkpoints even when nothing fails. Too frequent wastes time writing, too rare wastes time redoing; Young's classic answer is an interval of about √(2 · C · M), with C the time to write one checkpoint and M the job's MTBF (Young 1974; refined by Daly 2006). The hardware reliability page derives it and shows where it breaks.

The same run through the same two failures, with and without checkpoints

illustrative: a job needing 20 hours of work, failures at hours 9 and 17 of wall-clock time, restart 0.5 hours, checkpoint write 0.1 hours. The measured version is in the Failure lab: the running example killed mid-run on Slurm, Kubernetes and Ray (a crashed rank was seen in under a second everywhere; a lost node took 30 to 50 s of heartbeat timeouts to notice; resumed runs restarted from step 100 and redid 20 steps; Slurm requeued onto a spare node by itself in 166 s, Ray waited for capacity, and a silent node blocked a JobSet restart until an operator tainted it out of service); measured on Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM, shared machine.

How often will my job stop, and how often should it checkpoint?

derived: MTBF = 3.09 h × 16,384 / N (Llama 3's rate); interval τ = √(2CM); expected loss ≈ C/τ + (τ/2 + R)/M, the first-order approximation, which overstates the loss once τ nears M. The Failure lab has the full goodput simulator.

The other defences, each answering one part of the arithmetic above:

  • Make checkpoints cheap. Asynchronous checkpointing copies state to host memory in seconds and uploads it while training continues; DeepSeek checkpoints "typically every 5 minutes" at over 10 GiB/s per node, so a failure loses at most 5 minutes published (Fire-Flyer §VII-A). Google went further for Gemini Ultra: no periodic checkpoints to storage, but "redundant in-memory copies of the model state", which raised the largest job's goodput "from 85% to 97%" published (Gemini §3).
  • Make restarts fast. Keep hot spares (Gemini held back a few TPU cubes per SuperPod "for hot standbys and rolling maintenance"), cache container images on nodes, and restart only the failed part when the framework allows it.
  • Keep bad nodes out. A health check runs before or between jobs; a node that fails is drained (Slurm) or cordoned and tainted (Kubernetes) so nothing new lands on it. Ai2's Beaker runs a tensor multiplication before each workload and on failure "cordons the associated host and reschedules the workload" published (OLMo 2 §6.3); OpenAI's nodes join Kubernetes with a "preflight" taint that only passing GPU tests remove.
  • Shrink instead of stopping. Elastic training (torchrun with a minimum and maximum node count) continues with fewer workers; it changes the global batch or the data order unless the code compensates, so most large pre-training runs restart at full size instead.
  • Watch for slow, not just dead. A straggler (a GPU running hot and throttled, a flapping link) slows every synchronous step to its pace without failing anything.

6. Storage: where data and checkpoints live

The tiers, fastest and smallest first: GPU memory; the node's RAM; the node's local NVMe disks (fast, but gone with the node); a parallel file system shared by all nodes, such as Lustre, IBM Storage Scale (GPFS), WEKA or VAST, which stripes each file over many servers so thousands of clients can read and write at once; and object storage (S3, Google Cloud Storage, or an S3-compatible store such as Ceph RGW or SeaweedFS on premises; MinIO's open-source server was archived in 2026, see the Tool map), cheapest and nearly limitless but with higher latency and no in-place edits. Network file systems (NFS) are fine for home directories and code, and a common bottleneck when used for anything else.

Training does two very different kinds of I/O. Reading data is a steady stream of large sequential reads, easy to hide behind compute with prefetching (the storage and loading page owns formats, shards and streaming). Writing checkpoints is a burst: every rank writes at the same moment while the GPUs wait, so the file system's aggregate write bandwidth, not its capacity, sets the cost.

Checkpoint arithmetic. With mixed-precision Adam a checkpoint holds about 14 bytes per parameter (2 for bf16 weights, 4 for fp32 master weights, 4 and 4 for Adam's two moments; an assumption, as layouts vary). A 70B model's checkpoint is then about 0.98 TB, and writing it in one minute needs about 16 GB/s of aggregate write bandwidth; a 405B model's is about 5.7 TB, about 94 GB/s for one minute derived. Real operators size storage for exactly this burst:

  • Meta's Tectonic for Llama 3: 240 PB on 7,500 SSD servers, 2 TB/s sustained and 7 TB/s peak, built for checkpoint bursts of 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU published (Llama 3 §3.3.1).
  • DeepSeek's 3FS: 8 TB/s of aggregate reads across its cluster published (Fire-Flyer §VI-B).
  • Ai2: a WEKA cluster measured at 761 Gbps read/write with 64 clients on one cluster, and Google Cloud Storage "up to 1 GB/s per VM" on the other published (OLMo 2 §6.1).

Three habits follow. Shard: every rank writes only its own piece, in parallel (PyTorch's Distributed Checkpoint), instead of gathering one file on rank 0. Stage: copy to host RAM or local NVMe in seconds, then upload in the background (asynchronous checkpointing). Keep files big: millions of small files overload a parallel file system's metadata servers long before its bandwidth runs out.

The running example, measured. Its checkpoint is 1.39 MB (1,386,049 bytes: fp32 weights plus AdamW's two moments, 12.3 bytes per parameter). In the recorded cluster runs it took a median 4.3 ms to write to a Docker volume (Slurm) and 155 ms to the host folder that kind shares into the VM, 36 times slower for the same file. A 304 MB checkpoint, written with fsync, took 0.20 s on the Mac's own SSD, 0.39 s on a Docker volume, 2.61 s to an S3-compatible store (SeaweedFS) from a container and 7.31 s to the shared host folder; on that slow folder, asynchronous checkpointing cut the time training stood still per checkpoint from 1.81 s to 78 ms measured on Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM / native, shared machine (medians of 4 to 5; the Storage lab has every tier).

7. Cost and observability: where the GPU-hours go

A run is paid in GPU-hours, whatever happens inside them. Follow one paid GPU-hour down to useful arithmetic and every section of this page appears as a loss:

  1. Allocated or idle: GPUs paid for but not given to any job (fragmentation, a big job waiting for enough nodes, gaps backfill could not fill). The ratio is scheduling efficiency, measured from the scheduler's own accounting.
  2. Allocated but not training: image pulls, rendezvous, loading checkpoints, restarts after failures, steps redone since the last checkpoint, time blocked writing checkpoints. What is left is goodput, the share of time making real training progress (Llama 3's "effective training time", Gemini's 85% to 97%).
  3. Training but not using the chip: the step itself runs below the hardware's peak because of communication, memory traffic and small kernels. MFU (model FLOPs utilisation) is the model's useful FLOPs per second over the chip's peak; Llama 3 405B ran at 38 to 43% BF16 MFU published (Llama 3 Table 4). The performance math page owns how to compute it.
Predict: nvidia-smi shows every GPU of your job at 100% utilisation. Is the job using the GPUs well?
That number is the "percent of time over the past sample period during which one or more kernels was executing on the GPU" (nvidia-smi docs): one small kernel occupying a single streaming multiprocessor (out of more than a hundred) counts as busy, and so does a GPU spinning inside a collective, waiting for a slow peer. Better meters come from NVIDIA's DCGM: SM activity, "the fraction of time at least one warp was active on a multiprocessor, averaged over all multiprocessors", for which "a value of 0.8 or greater is necessary, but not sufficient", and tensor-pipe activity (DCGM profiling metrics). The real test is MFU computed from tokens per second.

What to watch on a training cluster: per job, tokens per second and step time (a drop is the first sign of a straggler), loss, and the time since the last good checkpoint; per GPU, SM and tensor activity, memory, temperature, power and error counters (exported by DCGM to Prometheus and drawn in Grafana); per cluster, queue length and wait times, allocated against idle GPUs, nodes drained and why. On the local clusters, scheduling efficiency failed three different ways measured: Slurm's eight-job queue kept 71% of its 8 GPUs' time allocated under FIFO and 80% with backfill; Kubernetes without gang admission held all 12 GPUs for 246 s with no training step run (half of each of three jobs waiting at a rendezvous), while Kueue's all-or-nothing admission fixed that but could fill at most 67% of a 12-GPU quota with 8-GPU jobs; Ray's placement groups waited holding nothing (Cost lab).

8. ML platforms: the loop around the jobs

A scheduler runs one job; a team runs thousands, and needs to know which code, data and settings produced which checkpoint, which checkpoint became which model, and how to rerun any of it. Three kinds of tool do this, usually on top of Slurm or Kubernetes rather than instead of them:

  • Experiment tracking: every run's configuration, metrics, logs and artefacts in one searchable place (MLflow, Weights & Biases, which CoreWeave bought in May 2025, CoreWeave; Neptune's hosted service was turned off on March 5, 2026 after it agreed to be acquired by OpenAI, Neptune).
  • Workflow or pipeline engines: a graph of steps (prepare data, train, evaluate, convert, register) with retries and caching (Airflow, Kubeflow Pipelines, Flyte, Metaflow, Prefect, Dagster).
  • Model registries: named, versioned models with their lineage and stage (candidate, approved, serving), often part of the tracker.

A training team's minimum is a tracker and a registry; a pipeline engine pays off once the same multi-step process (for example a nightly evaluation of every new checkpoint) runs often enough that doing it by hand causes mistakes.

9. Choosing

The scheduler matters less than people argue: the evidence in section 3 shows every large operator extending whatever it chose with health checks, fast restarts and storage sized for checkpoints. Choose by what else the cluster must do and who will use it.

Common mistakes

Asking for far more time than the job needs
The scheduler plans with your limit, so the job is never backfilled and waits behind everything (section 2). Ask for the expected time plus a margin; checkpoint so that hitting the limit costs little.
Submitting multi-pod training to plain Kubernetes
The default scheduler places pods one at a time; two jobs can each get part of what they need and deadlock, holding GPUs that do nothing (OpenAI's 2021 experience). Admit jobs as a whole: Kueue, JobSet with Kueue, Volcano, or the coscheduling plugin. A rendezvous address that only works on one node
MASTER_ADDR=localhost or a short host name that other nodes resolve differently makes every other rank wait until the timeout. Use the first node of the allocation (Slurm) or pod 0's DNS name through the headless service (JobSet). The log of the node that cannot resolve the name is the one that tells you.
Trusting the default timeouts, or setting them too short
At 30 minutes (gloo) or 10 minutes (NCCL) a hang burns that much of every GPU before anything fails; but a timeout shorter than your slowest legitimate wait (a first-step compile, a large synchronous checkpoint) kills healthy jobs. Set it on purpose, and add a watchdog on step progress. Know the two NCCL messages apart (torch 2.14.1, ProcessGroupNCCL.cpp): Watchdog caught collective operation timeout: ... ran for ... milliseconds before timing out. means a collective exceeded its timeout (a peer is missing or stuck); ProcessGroupNCCL's watchdog got stuck for ... seconds without making progress in monitoring enqueued collectives. means the watchdog thread itself hung (often a stuck CUDA or NCCL call), after 480 s by default (TORCH_NCCL_HEARTBEAT_TIMEOUT_SEC).
A restart that lands on the same broken node
If the failed node is not drained or cordoned, the requeued job is placed on it again and fails again: a restart loop that looks like a software bug. Health checks must take the node out automatically (Ai2's Beaker cordons and reschedules; Slurm runs a HealthCheckProgram periodically on every node, and that script drains the node itself, for example with scontrol update NodeName=... State=drain: slurm.conf).
A checkpoint that restores the model but not the run
Without the optimiser state, the learning-rate scheduler step, the data loader's position and the random-number generators, a resumed run sees different data and its loss jumps. Save all of them, and test a restore before you need one. Measured in the Failure lab: seven resumed 4-rank runs on three schedulers all continued the same trajectory, matching the uninterrupted loss at 8 of 10 logged steps and differing at two by one float32 rounding step, because DDP rebuilds its gradient buckets after a process's first step; with 2 ranks, or with the rebuild switched off, resume was bit-exact.
Checkpoints only on node-local disk, or gathered on rank 0
Local disk dies with the node it was meant to protect against; gathering everything on rank 0 is slow and can run that rank out of memory. Stage locally if you like, but upload, and shard the write across ranks.
Reading 100% GPU utilisation as efficiency
It means a kernel was running (section 7). Track tokens per second and MFU.
Elastic restarts that silently change the experiment
Continuing on fewer workers changes the global batch size (or the steps per epoch) unless the code compensates; torchrun also reassigns ranks between restarts, so code that assumed a fixed rank-to-data mapping reads the wrong shards.

Interview questions

Design the training platform for a 1,000-GPU lab: scheduler, storage, failure handling, cost controls.

Shape: 125 nodes of 8 GPUs, NVLink inside, one 400 Gb/s NIC per GPU on a rail-optimised fabric, a separate front-end network. Scheduler: Slurm with fairshare and QOS if most work is large training by researchers; Kubernetes with Kueue (team ClusterQueues in a cohort, borrowing, preemption) and JobSet if the lab also serves models and runs pipelines; either way gang admission and topology-aware placement, and a low-priority preemptible queue that soaks up idle GPUs. Failures: at Llama 3's rate a 1,000-GPU job stops about every 51 hours; with a 1-minute blocking checkpoint, Young gives about 78 minutes between checkpoints and about 3% loss with 10-minute restarts (the calculator in section 5). Add asynchronous checkpoints, preflight and periodic health checks with automatic drain, a few spare nodes, and automatic requeue. Storage: a parallel file system sized for the checkpoint burst (a 70B model is about 1 TB, so about 16 GB/s to write it in a minute), object storage for datasets and old checkpoints, local NVMe for staging. Cost: per-team quotas, dashboards of allocated against idle GPUs, goodput and MFU per job, alerts on step time and on GPUs allocated but idle.

A 512-GPU job is stuck at init. What do you check, in order?

(1) Did every rank start? Count processes or pods against the world size; find the missing one in the scheduler's per-node logs or the pod status (image pull, out of memory, a bad node). (2) Can every node reach MASTER_ADDR:MASTER_PORT, and does the name resolve the same everywhere? (3) Do all ranks agree on the world size and the rendezvous id? (4) If all arrived and it hangs inside init_process_group or the first collective: NCCL_DEBUG=INFO for transport errors, py-spy dump on a few ranks, the flight recorder to find the rank out of step, then that node's health (NICs down, GPU Xid errors). Drain the bad node and resubmit.

Your job has been pending for hours. Why?

Ask the scheduler: Slurm's squeue prints a reason such as "Resources" (waiting for nodes), "Priority" (higher-priority jobs exist for the partition) or a QOS limit (squeue reason codes); on Kubernetes, kubectl describe the pod's events and Kueue's Workload status (quota exhausted, waiting to be admitted). Then fix the cause: a smaller or shorter request, another partition, or quota.

Slurm or Kubernetes for training?

Slurm gives batch semantics (queue, backfill, fairshare, all-or-nothing allocations) for free and is simplest for big synchronous jobs on a fixed cluster. Kubernetes gives one platform for everything and an ecosystem, but needs Kueue or Volcano and JobSet to behave like a batch system. The deciding question is what else must run on the cluster and who operates it. Both are used at frontier scale (section 3).

Why does distributed training need gang scheduling?

Synchronous training cannot start until all ranks exist; a partly started job holds GPUs and does nothing. Two partly started jobs can each wait forever for GPUs the other holds: a deadlock. All-or-nothing admission prevents both.

How often should a 16,000-GPU run checkpoint?

Estimate the job's MTBF (Llama 3: about 3.1 hours on 16,384 GPUs) and the blocking write time C; Young's √(2CM) gives about 19 minutes for C = 1 minute. Then make C smaller (asynchronous, in-memory copies) because the loss falls with √C, and make restarts faster because each one costs R on top.

Goodput against MFU?

Goodput is the share of paid time spent making progress (lost to queues, launches, failures, checkpoints); MFU is how much of the chip's peak the progress uses while it runs. Cost per useful FLOP is the product of both: 90% goodput at 40% MFU uses 36% of what you paid for.

One GPU in a data-parallel job is 20% slower. What happens?

Every step waits for it at the gradient all-reduce, so the whole job runs about 20% slower, and the other GPUs show high "utilisation" while waiting. Find it from per-rank step or collective times, then drain the node.

What does torchrun do when one worker dies?

The agent stops and restarts all workers, up to --max-restarts; "if k<=n workers fail all workers are stopped and restarted" (torchrun's docstring, torch 2.14.1). A lost node (agent failure) is left to the job manager: fail the job or replace the node.

Job and request

A unit of work that asks for resources: 8 GPUs on 1 node for 2 hours. It holds them from start to end, even while it does nothing with them.

Time limit

The longest a job may run (--time in Slurm). The scheduler plans with it, because it cannot know the real runtime; the job is killed when it is reached.

Queue and priority

Pending jobs in the order the scheduler will consider them. Priority is the number that sets that order: submit time, user, fairshare, partition.

Utilisation, wait, makespan

Utilisation: GPU-time used by jobs divided by GPU-time available. Wait: start minus submit. Makespan: first submit to last end.

1. FIFO and backfill, on real Slurm

The problem. Jobs ask for different amounts. Take them strictly in order (FIFO, first in first out) and a big job at the head of the queue that does not fit yet blocks every job behind it, even small ones that would fit in the GPUs sitting idle. Backfill lets a later job jump ahead into those idle GPUs if doing so does not delay the jobs ahead of it. To know that, the scheduler builds a plan of the future from every running job's time limit: when each will end at the latest, and so when each waiting job could start.

Two flavours differ in whose start is protected. EASY backfill (from the Extensible Argonne Scheduling sYstem) reserves a start time only for the first waiting job; a later job may jump ahead if it does not delay that one. Conservative backfill reserves a start for every waiting job and lets a job jump only if it delays none of them. Slurm's own header calls its plugin conservative, and the code reserves for every job it tests (below).

What to look for. FIFO leaves visible troughs in the busy-GPU line: each is a big job at the head of the queue waiting for room while small jobs that would fit sit behind it. Both backfills fill them, which cuts the mean wait by a large factor at loads near 1. EASY usually beats conservative on mean wait because it protects only one reservation, at the price of predictability: under EASY a job's promised start can slip, while under conservative backfill every waiting job keeps the start it was given (Slurm reports it in squeue --start). Raise the limit padding and the plan gets worse in a specific way: jobs that will end early look as if they will run long, so the scheduler leaves gaps it could have filled, or fills gaps with jobs that free up sooner than planned. 3. Fairshare: who goes first when everyone is waiting

The problem. Order by submit time and a team that queues 500 jobs on Monday owns the cluster until Wednesday. Fairshare orders by how much each user or team has used recently against how much they are entitled to.

How it works in Slurm. Each account (a team) and each user in it has shares, its entitled fraction. Every running job adds usage: its billed resources times seconds (here a fake GPU bills 1 and a CPU 0.25, TRESBillingWeights). Usage decays exponentially with a half-life (default 7 days; 10 minutes on this lab cluster so that it moves while you watch). From shares and usage Slurm computes a fairshare factor between 0 and 1, and the job's priority is a weighted sum of factors (the multifactor plugin): here 10,000 times fairshare plus 1,000 times age, with the other weights zero.

level_fs = S / U     factor = rank / (number of users)
S
the association's shares divided by the total shares of its siblings (teama among teama, teamb and the root user)
U
its decayed usage divided by its parent's usage; U = 0 gives level_fs = infinity
rank
users are ranked by walking the account tree, highest level_fs first at every level; ties share a rank

That is Fair Tree, Slurm's default: the flag is set unless PriorityFlags turns it off (src/common/read_config.c:4843: conf->priority_flags = PRIORITY_FLAGS_FAIR_TREE;). Its point is that a team's total usage is compared first: a user in a heavy team ranks below every user of a light team, however little that user ran personally. The older formula, still available with PriorityFlags=NO_FAIR_TREE, is 2^(−U/S) (priority_multifactor.c:1951). Decay is applied every PriorityCalcPeriod as usage × decay_factorseconds, with decay_factor = 1 − 0.693 / half-life (priority_multifactor.c:1649).

4. Preemption: taking GPUs back

The problem. An urgent job (a deadline, a production evaluation, a quota owner returning) should not wait hours for a long training job to finish. Preemption stops a running job to free its resources. What happens to the victim is the preempt mode: CANCEL (killed), REQUEUE (killed and put back in the queue to start over, from its last checkpoint if it wrote one), SUSPEND (paused in memory; useless for GPUs whose memory stays full).

The lab config. PreemptType=preempt/partition_prio with PreemptMode=REQUEUE: jobs in partition urgent (PriorityTier=10) may requeue jobs in batch (tier 1) on the same nodes. bob runs a 12-GPU job in batch; 40 seconds later alice submits a 4-GPU job to urgent.

To run a 4-GPU job for 50 s, preemption cost bob 41 s of lost work, a 2-minute hold and 8 idle GPUs for 50 s.

The cost. Everything the victim computed since its last checkpoint is lost, plus its restart time, and it waits again in the queue. Preemption is therefore only as cheap as the victim's checkpoint interval: a training job that may be preempted should checkpoint often and resume automatically (#SBATCH --requeue, and a script that loads the latest checkpoint, as the running example does). 5. Gang scheduling: all of a job or none of it

The problem. A distributed training job is useless until every rank is running: rank 0 waits at the rendezvous for the others (the Launch lab shows it), and every GPU it holds meanwhile does nothing. A scheduler that places a job's pieces one by one can give a job part of what it needs. With two or more such jobs in a full cluster, each holds part and waits for the rest, which only the others could free: a deadlock. Gang scheduling starts all of a job's pieces together or none of them.

Who has it. In Slurm a job's allocation is one object: it gets all its nodes at once or stays pending, so gangs come free. Kubernetes' default scheduler places pods, one scheduling cycle per pod, and knows nothing of the job they belong to. Upstream is adding a native path (KEP-4671, "Gang Scheduling using Workload Object": alpha in v1.35, its GenericWorkload feature gate beta in v1.37), but the gate is off by default: the v1.37.0 kube-scheduler --help here lists GenericWorkload=true|false (BETA - default=false). Until then gangs come from an add-on: Kueue (admits a whole workload against quota, then lets the pods be created), Volcano (its own scheduler, with a PodGroup and minMember), or the coscheduling plugin of scheduler-plugins. In Ray, a placement group reserves all its bundles atomically (section 7).

6. Quotas and borrowing

The problem. Two teams each own half of a cluster. If team B is idle, should team A be allowed to use B's half? Yes, or the cluster sits half empty; but when B comes back, B must get its half quickly. Quota is a team's guaranteed amount; borrowing lets a team use another's unused quota; reclaiming takes it back by preempting the borrower.

In Kueue. A ClusterQueue holds a team's nominalQuota per resource; ClusterQueues in the same cohort lend unused quota to each other (up to an optional borrowingLimit); preemption.reclaimWithinCohort lets a queue preempt workloads of cohort members that are borrowing. Namespaces submit to a LocalQueue that points at a ClusterQueue. In Slurm the same ideas are QOS and association limits (GrpTRES) plus partition preemption; Slurm has no lending between limits, so idle capacity is used through a low-priority preemptible partition or QOS instead.

7. Placement and topology

The problem. Which machines a job gets matters as much as how many. The gradient all-reduce of every step crosses the network between nodes; a job spread across distant switches pays more for each collective than one packed under one switch, and a job split across many partly used nodes leaves fragments nobody else can use. Topology-aware placement keeps a job's nodes close; packing fills nodes before opening new ones; spreading does the opposite (for fault tolerance, or memory bandwidth).

The backfill plan uses your limit. A 2-hour job that asks for 24 hours cannot fit in a 3-hour hole before a reservation and waits behind everything. Ask for the expected time plus a margin, and checkpoint.

GPUs come with CPUs and memory. A job that takes all CPUs or memory of a node strands its remaining GPUs (section 1's first attempt). Request CPUs and memory per GPU (--cpus-per-gpu, --mem-per-gpu, or tasks with --gpus-per-task) and check AllocTRES.

Quota without capacity
Kueue admits against quota, not nodes. If the ClusterQueue's quota is larger than the healthy capacity (a drained node, fragmented GPUs), admitted workloads end up with Pending pods. Keep quota equal to what nodes can deliver, or use TAS, which checks node capacity too.
Borrowing without preemption
If reclaimWithinCohort is Never, a team that lent its quota waits until the borrower's jobs end, which for training can be days. Allow reclaim, and make borrowers' jobs checkpoint and resume.

10. Interview questions

What is backfill, and why does it need time limits?

Backfill starts a lower-priority job ahead of its turn when it fits in idle resources and will finish (by its time limit) before the reserved start of higher-priority jobs, or uses resources they will not need. The scheduler cannot know real runtimes, so it plans with limits; over-asked limits make jobs look too long to fit in any hole.

EASY or conservative backfill?

EASY reserves only for the first waiting job: more jobs can jump, so usually lower mean wait, but later jobs' predicted starts can slip. Conservative reserves for every waiting job: predictable starts, slightly less packing. Slurm is conservative within bf_max_job_test and bf_window; its reservations block whole nodes.

A 2-GPU job has been pending for hours on a cluster with free GPUs. What do you check?

The reason (squeue): Priority means it is behind a waiting job, Resources that it does not fit now. Its time limit against the hole before the next reservation (squeue --start for the jobs ahead). Whether the free GPUs come with free CPUs and memory (scontrol show node: AllocTRES). Limits on its account or QOS (reasons such as AssocGrpGRES). Its fairshare (sprio, sshare).

Why does distributed training need gang scheduling, and how do you get it on Kubernetes?

All ranks must run at once; a partial allocation holds GPUs that do nothing, and several partial jobs can deadlock the cluster (section 5 reproduced it). On Kubernetes: Kueue (admit the whole workload against quota, with waitForPodsReady), Volcano (PodGroup minMember), the coscheduling plugin, or, from v1.35 onward, the upstream Workload API behind the GenericWorkload gate.

Design the scheduling for a 1,000-GPU lab with three teams.

Gang allocation of whole nodes for training; a quota per team equal to its budget, with borrowing of idle quota and reclaim by preemption (Kueue cohorts, or Slurm accounts with a preemptible scavenger QOS); fairshare with a half-life of days so heavy weeks are paid back; time limits enforced and backfill on, with a short-job partition for debugging and evaluation; topology-aware placement for jobs above one switch's worth of nodes; every preemptible job checkpointing and requeueing automatically; dashboards of queue wait per team and of allocated-but-idle GPUs (the Cost lab).

How is fairshare computed in Slurm?

Usage (billed resources times seconds) decays with a half-life; Fair Tree compares, level by level in the account tree, shares over usage (S/U), ranks users depth first, and gives each user rank / number of users; priority adds weighted factors (fairshare, age, size, partition, QOS). A user in a team that used a lot ranks below every user of a team that used less.

4. Rendezvous: how the processes find each other


The processes start at different moments on different machines and know only one thing in common: the meeting address. Rendezvous is the protocol that turns "some processes, somewhere" into "W processes that know each other's addresses and agree on their ranks". PyTorch builds it on the TCPStore, also called the c10d store: one small key-value server (a TCP socket with set, get, add and wait on keys) that every process connects to as a client.


With torchrun there are two meetings, one per layer:


    

  1. Agents meet (one torchrun per node). With --rdzv-backend=c10d (the Slurm script), every agent connects to a store at --rdzv-endpoint; the agent whose host name matches the endpoint hosts it. Each agent adds itself to a shared participant list; when the list reaches --nnodes the round is complete and each node gets its node rank. With the static backend (torchrun's default, used on Kubernetes here) there is no negotiation: --node-rank is given, node 0 hosts the store, and the others connect and wait.
    2. 

  2. Workers meet (one process per GPU). Each worker calls init_process_group. Its store is the agent's (torchrun tells it so with TORCHELASTIC_USE_AGENT_STORE=True), so it only connects as a client; then the communication backend (gloo here, NCCL on GPUs) uses the store to exchange the addresses it needs and connects every pair of ranks.

6. Building the process group: gloo here, NCCL on GPUs


init_process_group creates the process group: the object collectives such as all_reduce run on. It needs a store (from the rendezvous) and a backend, the library that moves the bytes:


    

  • gloo (what ran here): each rank opens a listening socket, writes its address into the store, reads everyone else's, and connects to every other rank (a full mesh of TCP connections). Collectives then run over those sockets on the CPU.

  • NCCL (NVIDIA GPUs; described, not run): rank 0 creates a unique id (ncclGetUniqueId, which contains the address of a bootstrap socket), PyTorch passes it to every rank through the store, and each rank calls ncclCommInitRank. 9. Common mistakes and interview questions


Setting MASTER_ADDR=localhost (or a short host name) in a multi-node script
Every node then meets itself. Each node's torchrun hosts its own store and waits for a partner that is waiting somewhere else; nothing errors until the join timeout. Use the first host of the allocation on Slurm (scontrol show hostnames $SLURM_JOB_NODELIST | head -n1) and pod 0's DNS name through the headless Service on Kubernetes.


Opening only port 29500 in the firewall
With torchrun's c10d backend the workers use a second, random port on node 0 (section 5), and gloo and NCCL open more ports between every pair of ranks. Allow all TCP traffic between the job's nodes on the training network, or pin the interface (GLOO_SOCKET_IFNAME, NCCL_SOCKET_IFNAME).


Hard-coding WORLD_SIZE instead of reading it from the launcher
When the job is resized (fewer nodes, a different --ntasks), the processes wait for ranks that will never exist. Read it from the launcher (WORLD_SIZE under torchrun, SLURM_NTASKS under srun).


Reading the 511 healthy logs instead of looking for the missing one
When most ranks wait at the rendezvous or the first collective, the cause is the rank that is not there: its node, its pod status, its own log. Count processes or pods against the world size first.


Raising every timeout "to be safe"
A 30-minute process-group timeout means a stuck job wastes 30 minutes of the whole cluster before anything restarts it. Keep the start-up timeouts close to how long start-up really takes (this lab's numbers give the scale), and keep the collective timeout above the longest legitimate pause (a checkpoint, an evaluation) but not far above it.


Forgetting that the rendezvous id must be unique per job
Two jobs that reuse one --rdzv-id and one endpoint join the same rendezvous. Use the scheduler's job id ($SLURM_JOB_ID), as the script here does.


Interview questions


What does torchrun do that a plain srun python train.py does not?
It runs the rendezvous between nodes (so ranks are assigned and the meeting address is published, without the script computing them), sets the PyTorch environment variables for each worker, monitors the workers and, with --max-restarts, tears them all down and restarts the group after a failure, re-running the rendezvous. With plain srun you map SLURM_PROCID to RANK yourself and Slurm's own options handle failures.


Walk me through what happens between init_process_group being called and the first all-reduce.
The process connects to the TCPStore at MASTER_ADDR:MASTER_PORT (retrying until the timeout if nobody listens yet), the backend registers this rank's address in the store and reads everyone else's, then connects to the other ranks (gloo: a full mesh of TCP connections; NCCL: a bootstrap ring, then topology detection and rings and trees over NVLink and the network). DDP then broadcasts rank 0's parameters to everyone, and the first backward pass all-reduces gradients bucket by bucket.


A 512-GPU job is stuck at init. What do you check, in order?
(1) Did every rank start: count processes or pods against the world size, and look at the missing one's node and scheduler log. (2) Can every node reach MASTER_ADDR:MASTER_PORT, and does the name resolve the same everywhere. (3) Do all ranks agree on world size and rendezvous id. (4) If all arrived and it hangs in init_process_group or the first collective: NCCL_DEBUG=INFO (interface choice, transport errors), py-spy dump on a few ranks, the flight recorder to find the rank out of step, then that node's health. Drain the bad node and resubmit.


Static or dynamic (c10d) rendezvous: when would you choose each?
Static when the scheduler already fixes node ranks and the address of node 0 (a Kubernetes Indexed Job with a headless Service, an MPI-style launch): no negotiation, no extra round. Dynamic when nodes may arrive in any order, be replaced, or vary in number (elastic training with --nnodes=min:max, spot instances): the agents negotiate ranks each round and can re-run the rendezvous after a failure.


Why do launch timeouts matter for cost?
Every minute a broken launch waits, every GPU in the allocation is paid for and idle. A missing node with default settings costs 10 minutes of the whole job before torchrun gives up (the join timeout); a hang inside a collective costs the process-group timeout (10 minutes for NCCL by default, 30 for gloo). On 1,024 GPUs a 10-minute wait is about 170 GPU-hours. Detect early (health checks before launch), and set timeouts from measured start-up times.


How does a pod in a JobSet know where rank 0 is before rank 0 exists?
The name is deterministic: JobSet names pods <jobset>-<replicatedJob>-<job index>-<pod index>, sets each pod's hostname and subdomain, and creates a headless Service named after the JobSet that publishes pods even before they are ready. So mi-train-node-0-0.mi-train can be written into the spec, and resolves to pod 0's address once it is scheduled.

1. What fails at scale, from the one table everyone quotes


The problem. In synchronous data-parallel training every rank (one process, usually one GPU) computes gradients on its share of the batch, then all ranks sum them in an all-reduce before anyone takes the next step. So every step waits for every rank: if one GPU, host, cable or process fails, all the others stop at the next collective. Llama 3's paper puts it plainly: "a single GPU failure may require a restart of the entire job" published.


Meta published the best-known failure record: during a 54-day snapshot of Llama 3 405B pre-training on up to 16,384 H100 GPUs there were 466 job interruptions, 47 planned (firmware upgrades, configuration or dataset updates) and 419 unexpected, broken down by cause in Table 5 (Llama 3, section 3.3.4) published. Why scale makes it worse. Divide 419 interruptions by 16,384 GPUs and 54 days: about one unexpected interruption per GPU every 5.8 years calculation. A second public record, smaller and older: Meta's OPT-175B ran on 992 80GB A100s and "hardware failures contributed to at least 35 manual restarts and the cycling of over 100 hosts over the course of 2 months", plus an estimated "70+ automatic restarts" (OPT, section 2.5) published. Per GPU that is at least 1.8e-3 restarts per GPU-day against Llama 3's 4.7e-4 interruptions per GPU-day, roughly 3.7 times as often calculation; the two count different things (restarts against interruptions, "2 months" taken as 60 days) on different hardware two years apart, so read it as "the order of magnitude is stable", not as a trend.


Three shapes recur in both records, and they are the three this lab breaks on purpose: a process dies (a GPU error, an uncorrectable memory error or a software bug makes a rank exit), a machine is lost (power supply, chassis, host maintenance), and something hangs: Llama 3 notes that failures "in either the remote GPU or NVLink connectivity often manifest as stalled load/store operations within CUDA kernels without returning a clear error code" published, and 7 of its interruptions are NCCL watchdog timeouts.







2. Kill it: the same failures on Slurm, Kubernetes and Ray


Who can notice a failure, and how. Three layers watch a training job, each with its own clock:


    

  • The other ranks. They talk to the failed rank over TCP (gloo here, NCCL on GPUs).   • The launcher on each machine (torchrun's agent, a Ray Train worker, the container runtime) sees its own child processes exit and their exit codes immediately.

  • The scheduler. A resumed run should continue the same training trajectory, not a nearby one. The running example is built for that: the batch of step s depends only on the seed and s, there is no dropout, kernels are forced deterministic, and the checkpoint holds the optimiser state. So we compared the global loss of every resumed run with the uninterrupted run at every logged step after the resume.

Almost, and the "almost" is instructive. All seven resumed runs, on three schedulers, agree with each other exactly, and with the uninterrupted run at 8 of 10 logged steps including the last one; at steps 110 and 180 they differ in the last bit. A difference that is identical everywhere is not noise; it is a deterministic change in the arithmetic. We found it with three more runs in one container:




Why. DDP does not all-reduce gradients one tensor at a time: it packs them into flat buckets, and after the first iteration of every process it rebuilds the buckets in the order gradients actually became ready (PyTorch's DistributedDataParallel._pre_forward calls reducer._rebuild_buckets(), "called only once during whole training period"). A restarted process takes its first step with the initial layout, the uninterrupted run with the rebuilt one. The layout changes which slice of the flat buffer each piece of the all-reduce handles, and so the order in which the four ranks' values are added for a given gradient element our reading of the mechanism; the experiment shows the cause. Floating-point addition is not associative, so (a+b)+(c+d) and ((a+b)+c)+d can differ in the last bit; with two ranks there is only a+b = b+a, which is exact, and that is why the setup's 2-rank resume check passed. Switch the rebuild off in both runs and the 4-rank resume is exact.


Why it matters, and when it does not. A one-ulp change is far below anything that affects training. But "bit for bit" is a powerful debugging tool (a resumed run that matches proves the checkpoint holds everything), and on GPUs nondeterministic kernels and different collective algorithms usually take it away anyway. Then test resume the practical way: the loss at the first logged steps after resuming must match the original run to the precision you expect, with no jump. A jump means something was not saved: the optimiser state (a fresh AdamW takes large first steps), the learning-rate schedule step, the data loader's position (the run sees data again or skips it), or the random-number generators (dropout masks, sampling).

8. Elastic training and hot spares, measured with torchrun


Elastic training lets a job continue with fewer machines instead of stopping: torchrun --nnodes=1:2 accepts any number of nodes between 1 and 2.   • Restarts reuse the rendezvous store by default, and that broke multi-node restarts here. With the variable set, each attempt gets a new store on a new port, and on that page's run the restart resumed 4.3 s after the crash. Test your own restart path before relying on it.
    • 

  • Shrinking counts as a restart; growing does not. The restart counter went to 1 when B was lost and stayed at 1 when C joined: "membership changes do not count as retries" (elastic/agent/server/api.py line 958). With a batch that does not divide evenly, or a data loader that shards by rank, it would also change which data each step sees.

Most large pre-training runs do not shrink: a smaller world changes the batch per GPU, the parallelism layout and the learning-rate schedule's assumptions. 9. Goodput: how often to checkpoint, given how your scheduler recovers


Goodput is "the time spent computing useful new steps over the elapsed time of the training job" (Gemini's definition, Gemini 1.0, section 3). Throughput counts every step computed, including the ones redone after a failure; goodput counts each step once. Three things eat into it: time spent writing checkpoints (C each time), work redone since the last checkpoint after each failure, and the recovery time R (detect, reschedule, start, reload). Checkpoint often and you pay C often; rarely, and each failure costs more rework. Young's classic answer for the interval of work between checkpoints is τ ≈ √(2CM), with M the job's mean time between failures; Daly refined it to √(2C(M+R)) + C when R is not small. The reliability page derives both and simulates them; here the new input is R, the thing this lab measured, and the point is how much the recovery path matters next to the checkpoint interval.

10. Common mistakes and interview questions


Shipping a job spec with no retries
failurePolicy.maxRestarts: 0 (JobSet), FailureConfig(max_failures=0) (Ray Train) and plain torchrun (default --max-restarts=0) all turn one crashed rank into a dead run: measured on all three in section 2. Slurm never retries a job that failed by itself; resubmit it from a wrapper or let torchrun restart inside the allocation.


Trusting a restart path you have never exercised
Our 2-node torchrun --max-restarts=2 restarts failed with Gloo connectFullMesh failed with ... timed out connecting: SO_ERROR: Connection refused until TORCH_DISABLE_SHARE_RDZV_TCP_STORE=1 was set. Kill a rank in a test job before the real run needs the restart.


Reading torchrun's "Root Cause" as the culprit in a hang
It names the first process that exited with an error, which in a hang is a healthy rank that timed out (section 5). The Launch lab saw the same on Kubernetes: each pod's summary named a waiting rank (rank 0 on pod 0, rank 2 on pod 1) while the stalled rank 3 appeared only as exitcode: -15 (SIGTERM), killed by its own agent. Find the rank everyone waited for, and dump its stack.


Assuming a restart resumes from the newest checkpoint on disk
With Ray Train's own checkpoint API it may not. The Ray child page crashed rank 3 at step 120 with checkpoints every 50 steps: in 3 of 3 runs the new worker group restored step 50, although every worker had reported step 100 and its files were already in storage. Ray Train v2 registers a checkpoint only when the controller holds that report from every worker (report_handler.py in Ray 2.59.0: the method at line 40 says "Wait for all workers to report training results", and line 63, if not all(self._training_report_queues): return, skips any poll in which one worker's report is missing); the crashed rank's report never left its process. 70 steps were redone instead of 20, and the step-100 directory stayed behind as an orphan that num_to_keep did not delete. Log which checkpoint a restart loaded, and compare it with the newest on disk.


Leaving the timeouts at their defaults without knowing them
Slurm notices a dead node after SlurmdTimeout (300 s default), a hung NCCL collective errors after 10 minutes (PyTorch 2.14), Kubernetes evicts pods from an unreachable node after 300 s. Each is idle time on every GPU of the job, per failure. Choose them; keep them above your longest legitimate pause (checkpoint save, evaluation).


Assuming Kubernetes recovers from a dead node by itself
A pod on a node whose kubelet is gone stays Terminating; a Job waiting for it never fails over (measured: stuck for over 7 minutes until the node.kubernetes.io/out-of-service taint). Automate the taint with a node-health controller.


A health check that only exits non-zero
Slurm ignores the exit code of HealthCheckProgram; the script must run scontrol update ... state=drain itself (section 7).


Hunting a straggler by the longest all-reduce
The rank with the longest wait is a victim; the slow rank waits least (12 ms against 63 ms, section 6).


Hosting the elastic rendezvous on any node
With the c10d backend one agent hosts the store; lose that node and every agent dies with RendezvousConnectionError (section 8).


Interview questions (short answers; expand)


A 2,048-GPU job at Llama 3's failure rate, checkpoints take 30 s to write. How often does it fail and how often should it checkpoint?
MTBF scales as 1/N: 3.09 h x 16,384 / 2,048 = about 24.7 h. Young: τ = √(2 x 30 s x 89,100 s) = about 2,310 s, roughly every 39 minutes of work; checkpoint overhead C/τ is about 1.3% and expected rework τ/2M about 1.3% calculation. Then say what would change it: async checkpointing shrinks C, slow recovery adds R/M per failure.


A node dies mid-run. What happens on Slurm, on Kubernetes with JobSet, on Ray Train?
Peers error at once or at the collective timeout. Slurm notices after SlurmdTimeout, sets the node DOWN, requeues a --requeue job (held about 2 minutes) onto healthy nodes. Kubernetes marks the node NotReady after 50 s; with JobSet maxRestarts above 0 the JobSet recreates its pods, but a pod on a dead kubelet blocks that until the node is tainted out of service. Ray marks the node dead after its health checks (30 s here) and Ray Train restarts the worker group if max_failures allows, but only once enough resources exist: without a spare it waits.


A job is stuck at step 40,000: no error, GPUs at 0% except one. What do you do?
It is a hang: everyone waits in a collective for one rank. Dump stacks of all ranks (py-spy, or the NCCL flight recorder dump), find the rank not in the collective, check its node (Xid errors in the kernel log, ECC, NVLink, the NIC), drain it, restart from the last checkpoint. Afterwards, shorten the collective timeout or add a watchdog so this costs minutes, not hours.


Your resumed run's loss differs from the original in the 7th digit. Bug?
Probably not, if it is a last-bit difference with no jump: the reduction order changed (here DDP rebuilds its buckets after a process's first step; on GPUs, nondeterministic kernels). A jump at the resume step is a bug: optimiser state, LR schedule step, data position or RNG state was not restored.


Design failure handling for a 1,000-GPU training platform.

Detect: per-job heartbeats, collective timeouts of minutes not tens of minutes, flight recorder, per-rank step-time telemetry for stragglers. Recover: job-level retries with a fresh rendezvous, spare nodes so a restart never waits, asynchronous checkpoints sized by τ ≈ √(2CM) (at 1,000 GPUs and Llama 3's rate, M is about 51 h), checkpoint load path tested. 3. The burst: every rank writes at once


Training is synchronous: at the checkpoint step, every rank reaches the save at the same moment, and the storage system sees all of them arrive together. Whatever the aggregate write bandwidth is, they share it. Who writes what then decides both the bytes and the time:


    

  • Rank 0 writes everything (train.py's way, fine for DDP, where every rank holds a full copy): one writer, one link.
    • 

  • Every rank writes everything: the forgotten if rank == 0. Same file content, written N times.
    • 

  • Consolidated (gather, then one file): with a sharded model (FSDP, ZeRO-3), the pieces are first sent to rank 0 over the network, then rank 0 writes one file. Needs the whole state in rank 0's memory.
    • 

  • Sharded (PyTorch Distributed Checkpoint, DCP): every rank writes its own piece to its own file, in parallel, plus a small .metadata file mapping pieces to tensors. No gather, no single writer.
    •

4. Asynchronous checkpointing: stop for the copy, not for the write


A synchronous checkpoint stops training for the whole write. An asynchronous checkpoint splits it in two: first stage the state into host memory (from GPU memory, a PCIe copy of each rank's shard), then let training continue while a background thread writes the staged copy to storage. Training stands still only for the copy. The cost: a second copy of the state in host RAM, and the write must finish before the next checkpoint starts.

Published, at scale: PyTorch and IBM report that asynchronous DCP cut the time a 7B model's training stood still per checkpoint from 148.8 s to 6.3 s on average, the 6.3 s being the copy to CPU memory published (PyTorch blog, November 2024). Gemini went further and kept redundant copies of model state in other hosts' memory, raising its largest job's goodput from 85% to 97% (Gemini 1.0 report, section 3).


The async trap we hit: give the background writer its own process group


DCP's save runs collectives (it gathers every rank's plan and agrees the file is complete). async_save runs them in a background thread; if that thread uses the same process group as training, two threads issue collectives on one channel at once. Our first attempt (4 ranks, gloo, native) hung: every rank at 0% CPU after the first checkpoint, nothing printed for over four minutes. A rerun crashed 3 s after the checkpoint, two ranks aborting with:


gloo::EnforceNotMet: [enforce fail at .../third_party/gloo/gloo/transport/uv/pair.cc:253] op.nread == op.preamble.nbytes


The fix is one line, the one the PyTorch blog recommends: create a group once, ck_pg = dist.new_group(backend="gloo"), and pass process_group=ck_pg to async_save. All runs above use it.

The small-files problem. Each file costs an open, a metadata lookup and a close; each object costs an HTTP request. At 4 KiB per file those fixed costs are the whole bill. 8. Common mistakes


Writing the checkpoint in place, or renaming without fsync
A crash mid-write leaves a half file, and the next start loads it: "PytorchStreamReader failed reading zip archive: failed finding central directory" (above). train.py writes step_N.pt.tmp, renames it, then updates latest: a reader never sees a partial file. But it does not call fsync before the rename, so after a power loss (not a process crash) the renamed file can still be empty on some file systems. Write, fsync the file, rename, fsync the directory; on macOS, F_FULLFSYNC. Object stores give atomicity for free: an object appears only when its upload completes.


Every rank saves the full state
The forgotten if rank == 0 in DDP: N copies of the same bytes, at the same moment, on the same storage. Here, 4 times the bytes took 3 times as long on a shared pipe. Use rank 0 for small DDP models, DCP for anything sharded.


Consolidating a sharded model to one file every time
Gathering the full state onto one rank needs that rank to hold all of it in memory and pushes it through one node's link. Save sharded during training; consolidate once, offline, when exporting.


Running async checkpointing on the training process group
Two threads issuing collectives on one group: a hang or a crash ("op.nread == op.preamble.nbytes" with gloo here). Give async_save its own group.


Starting the next async checkpoint before the last one finished
If the upload takes longer than the interval, checkpoints pile up in host memory. Wait on the previous future before staging the next (dist_ckpt.py does), and size the interval so the upload fits.


Trusting that a path is local
On kind, /shared looks like any directory and was 36 times slower than Slurm's volume for the same 1.39 MB file. Measure a mount with the real write (size, fsync, rename) before putting checkpoints on it.


Millions of small files on a parallel file system
Metadata servers, not disks, become the limit; the whole cluster slows down, not only your job. Shard data and keep checkpoint files per rank, not per tensor.


Keeping every checkpoint, or only the last one
Every checkpoint of a 405B run is 5.7 TB; keeping all of them is expensive. Keeping only the latest means one corrupted or bad checkpoint (a loss spike saved right before a crash) leaves nothing to fall back on. Keep the last few, plus periodic milestones, and delete the rest automatically.







9. Interview questions


How big is the checkpoint of a 70B model trained with AdamW in mixed precision, and how long does it take to write?
About 14 bytes per parameter (bf16 weights 2, fp32 master 4, two fp32 moments 8): 0.98 TB. Time = size / the slowest pipe: at 100 GB/s of aggregate storage bandwidth, about 10 s if sharded across enough nodes that their links are not the limit; through one node's 50 GB/s link (consolidated), about 20 s plus the gather.


Your 1,024-GPU job spends 8% of its time checkpointing. What do you do?
First measure where the time goes: gather, serialisation, or storage bandwidth. Then: shard (DCP) so every node writes in parallel; go asynchronous so training stops only for the GPU to host copy; check the storage system's aggregate bandwidth against size / target time and buy bandwidth if it is the limit; and choose the interval from the failure rate (the Young/Daly optimum in the Failure lab), not by habit.


Why can't you just checkpoint to local NVMe?
You can, and should, as a first stage: it is fast and parallel. But a checkpoint on a node that dies is gone or unreachable, and the restarted job usually lands on different nodes. So copy it onward (to the parallel file system or object storage) in the background, or replicate it to peers' memory or disks as Gemini did.


Lustre or S3 for checkpoints?
A parallel file system gives the highest burst bandwidth and POSIX semantics (rename, partial reads), but it is priced by capacity and its bandwidth comes with capacity. Object storage is cheap and scales with parallel requests, with tens of milliseconds per request and whole-object writes. Common answer: write to the parallel file system or local NVMe on the critical path, then copy to object storage for durability and retention.


A job resumed from a checkpoint and the loss jumped. What do you check?
Whether the checkpoint holds everything that carries state: optimiser moments (a fresh AdamW restarts its estimates), the learning-rate schedule's step, data loader position (the data seen again or skipped), and RNG states. Then whether the world size or parallel layout changed and the load resharded correctly; then whether you loaded the latest complete checkpoint and not a partial one.

The words, from zero




GPU-hour
One GPU held for one hour. A cloud bill, a reservation and a scheduler's accounting (sacct's AllocTRES times Elapsed) all count this, not work done. 1,024 GPUs for a day is 24,576 GPU-hours.


Allocated vs used
The scheduler allocates GPUs to a job from its start to its end. Whether a kernel ran on them is a separate question that only the GPU's own counters answer.


Scheduling efficiency
Allocated GPU-hours divided by the GPU-hours the cluster could have given out over the same period. What is left over is idle (no job wanted it) or fragmentation (jobs wanted GPUs but the free ones were in the wrong shape: 4 free GPUs spread over 4 nodes cannot run a job that needs 4 on one node).


Goodput
The share of wall-clock time that becomes kept training progress, after failures, restarts and checkpoint pauses. The Failure lab derives it and its optimum checkpoint interval; this tab only prices it.


MFU and HFU
Model FLOPs Utilisation: the FLOPs the model needs per step (about 6 per parameter per token) divided by what the GPUs could do at peak in that time. Hardware FLOPs Utilisation also counts recomputed work, so it is never below MFU. What "GPU utilisation 100%" does and does not mean


nvidia-smi's utilisation, and DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL that dashboards plot from it, is defined as the "Percent of time over the past sample period during which one or more kernels was executing on the GPU" (nvidia-smi documentation). One kernel on one of the chip's SMs (streaming multiprocessors, its compute units: 132 on an H100 SXM, GPU architecture) counts the same as a kernel filling all of them. DCGM's profiling metrics look inside: SM activity is "the fraction of time at least one warp was active on a multiprocessor, averaged over all multiprocessors", and its documentation's own example is that a kernel using a fifth of the SMs for the whole interval and a kernel using all of them for a fifth of the interval both read 0.2 (DCGM feature overview, profiling metrics, read 2026-10-16).

Common mistakes


Reporting GPU utilisation as efficiency
nvidia-smi's number is a duty cycle of "any kernel running". A training job can read 100% while hung, or while one SM in 132 works. Report MFU (from the log) and SM and tensor activity (from DCGM) instead.


Pricing a run at ideal GPU-hours
6ND divided by peak times MFU is the floor. Goodput (failures, restarts, checkpoint stalls) and idle reservation multiply it: the calculator's Llama 3 405B defaults add about  on top of the ideal hours.


Comparing an on-demand price with a cluster price
A single 8-GPU node on demand and a reserved InfiniBand cluster are different products: Lambda's cluster price per GPU is above its single-node on-demand price. Price the thing a multi-node run needs.


Buying a reservation and not watching the queue
A reservation is paid whether jobs run or not, so idle, fragmentation and deadlocks become direct cost. Measure scheduling efficiency (allocated over capacity) every week, and look for jobs that hold GPUs with no steps logged.


Long collective timeouts on big jobs
A hang holds every GPU of the job until the timeout fires. On 1,024 GPUs a 30-minute default is 512 GPU-hours per hang. Shorter timeouts and a watchdog on the step counter cut it, at the risk of killing a slow but healthy first step.


Treating short test jobs as representative
The 200-step job here spent most of its allocation starting up. Startup is fixed, so its share shrinks with job length; measure efficiency on runs as long as the real ones, or subtract startup explicitly.

Wanting both is common enough that SchedMD (Slinky) and CoreWeave (SUNK) sell Slurm running on Kubernetes. Ray goes inside either for Python-level orchestration.


"Why is the default Kubernetes scheduler not enough for distributed training?"
It places one pod at a time and has no queue or quota between teams. A 16-pod job can get 10 pods placed and wait forever for the rest while holding the GPUs (two such jobs deadlock). You add all-or-nothing admission and queues: Kueue keeps the default scheduler and admits whole jobs against quotas; Volcano and KAI replace the scheduler with gang scheduling built in. The Scheduling lab shows the deadlock and its fix.


"A team's manifests use PyTorchJob. What do you tell them?"
Training Operator v1 is in maintenance on its release-1.9 branch, and Kueue calls its integration legacy and deprecated. New work should use Kubeflow Trainer v2's TrainJob (run as a JobSet) or a JobSet directly. Note Trainer v2 still calls itself alpha, and Kueue 0.20 also stopped serving its own v1beta1 API, so check both upgrades together.


"How do you decide whether an infrastructure dependency is safe to adopt?"
Owner's notice first (README banners, shutdown pages), then the archived flag, then the date of the last release, and only then commit activity. Then who owns it and how it makes money: the 2024 to 2026 record has a tracker shut down three months after its acquisition (Neptune), an open-source object store archived in favour of a commercial edition (MinIO), and a vendor platform with no release since March 2025 (Determined). Prefer projects under a foundation (Kubernetes, Kueue, Ray in the PyTorch Foundation, Airflow) and keep data exportable.


"Where does Ray fit if you already have Slurm or Kubernetes?"
Ray is not a cluster scheduler between teams; it schedules tasks and actors inside one Ray cluster that Slurm or Kubernetes (KubeRay) gave it. Use it when the job itself is many cooperating Python processes (RL with generation, reward and training workers; data pipelines feeding GPUs); plain DDP pretraining does not need it.

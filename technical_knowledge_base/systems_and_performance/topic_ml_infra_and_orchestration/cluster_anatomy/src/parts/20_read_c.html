4. From rack to cluster: the levels and why placement matters

Rack. A rack is a steel frame about 2 m tall with shared power feeds and, often, a switch at the top (top-of-rack, ToR). How many GPUs go in one is decided mostly by watts and cooling (section 6): Meta's Llama 3 cluster puts 16 GPUs (two servers) behind each rack switch ({{Llama 3, section 3.3.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}) published; NVIDIA's B200 SuperPOD puts "two DGX B200 systems per rack" ({{SuperPOD components|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx-superpod/reference-architecture-scalable-infrastructure-b200/latest/dgx-superpod-components.html}}) vendor; an NVL72 is one rack of 72.

Pod, or scalable unit. The block that is designed once and copied: a set of racks whose GPUs reach each other at full NIC speed. NVIDIA's scalable unit (SU) is 32 DGX nodes with 8 leaf switches, one per rail ({{SuperPOD network fabrics|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx-superpod/reference-architecture-scalable-infrastructure-b200/latest/network-fabrics.html}}); Meta's pod (which it also calls an AI zone) is 192 racks, 3,072 GPUs, with "full bisection bandwidth" inside (Llama 3 report). How the leaves and spines inside a pod are wired, and what rail-optimised means, is section 6 of the {{interconnects page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81808563fd41fb163af0}}.

Cluster. Pods joined by a top layer of switches, which is usually oversubscribed: fewer uplinks than downlinks, so pods cannot all talk to each other at full speed at once. Meta's 8 pods are joined at 1:7; the "cross-AI Zone connectivity is oversubscribed by design", and "the job scheduler tries to minimize cross-zone traffic" ({{Meta engineering, Aug 5 2024|https://engineering.fb.com/2024/08/05/data-center-engineering/roce-network-distributed-ai-training-at-scale/}}) published.

How big can each level be? It is set by switch ports. With switches of k ports, a two-layer fat tree reaches k2/2 network cards and a three-layer one k3/4 ({{Al-Fares et al. 2008|https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1402958.1402967}}): 2,048 and 65,536 with 64-port switches derived. The arithmetic reproduces published designs: DeepSeek's Fire-Flyer 2 used 40-port QM8700 switches, and 402/2 = 800 is exactly its "800-port Fat-Tree" "configured with 20 spine switches and 40 leaf switches" authors; NVIDIA's 4-SU SuperPOD has 32 leaves and 16 spines, which the {{Sizing calculator|#t-casize}} gets from the same formula.

The same 16-GPU job, placed two ways: packed against spread

A cluster shaped like Llama 3's, drawn small: 2 pods of 4 racks, 2 servers of 8 GPUs per rack (the real pods have 192 racks). One ring all-reduce of 1 GB (gradients of about 0.5B parameters in bf16). Switches crossed per ring edge: 0 inside a server (NVLink), 1 inside a rack, 3 between racks of one pod, 5 between pods. Time is the bandwidth term only, 2(N-1)/N x 1 GB / slowest edge derived: latency per hop and congestion are left out, so real gaps are larger.

What the animation shows. On an idle cluster both placements take the same 37.5 ms in this model, because each GPU's own 50 GB/s card is the limit either way. The difference appears when the cluster is busy: the spread job's two cross-pod edges share the 1:7 top layer with every other job, and the ring runs at the pace of its slowest edge, 262.5 ms derived, seven times longer. It also crosses 36 switches per trip round the ring against 2, so every step pays more latency, and it depends on 8 racks and 2 pods being healthy instead of one rack. That is why schedulers try to pack: Slurm's topology/tree plugin and --switches option, Kueue's Topology Aware Scheduling, and DeepSeek's HAI platform, which allows "cross-zone computing tasks" to be "limited to one at most" (Fire-Flyer 2, section III-B) authors.

Predict. A data-parallel job gets 16 free GPUs, scattered one per node, on a cluster where the pods are idle. Is it slower than the same job packed into 2 nodes?
Scattered placement costs little on an idle network and a lot on a busy one, which is the worst kind of problem to debug: the same job is fast one day and slow the next. Packed placement also has a cost for the scheduler: waiting for 2 whole nodes in one rack takes longer than taking any 16 free GPUs, the trade-off the parent's {{Scheduling lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b5925bfd1d8665dd4b}} measures. The {{Message path|#t-capath}} tab follows a single message along each route.

5. Storage tiers at cluster level

The problem. Training data and checkpoints are far larger than any GPU's memory, and the storage that every node can see is far slower than the storage inside one node. A cluster therefore has a ladder of tiers, each bigger, slower and longer-lived than the one above, and the job of the infrastructure is to keep the GPUs fed from the top of it while the truth lives at the bottom.

Per node against the whole cluster. NVIDIA asks that each SuperPOD node can read faster than 40 GB/s, and sizes the shared file system at 40 GB/s of read per 32-node SU in its standard tier, 125 GB/s in the enhanced one, with write "at least ½ of the read performance" ({{SuperPOD storage guidance, Tables 5 and 6|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx-superpod/reference-architecture-scalable-infrastructure-b200/latest/storage-architecture.html}}) vendor. Put together: 32 nodes each able to pull 40 GB/s could ask for 1,280 GB/s, and the standard tier gives 40, so only one node in 32 can read at its peak at a time derived. Storage is sized for the average plus bursts, and the local NVMe exists to absorb the difference: stage the dataset once, read it many times locally.

Predict. A 4-SU SuperPOD (1,016 GPUs) writes a checkpoint of 4 GB per GPU, the top of Llama 3's "1 MB to 4 GB per GPU". With the standard tier's 80 GB/s of write, how long do the GPUs wait if nothing is asynchronous?
1,016 x 4 GB = 4.06 TB; at 80 GB/s that is 50.8 s derived (16.3 s on the enhanced tier's 250 GB/s). Every 30 minutes that is almost 3% of the run spent writing. That is why checkpoints first go to host memory or local NVMe and drain to shared storage in the background: the parent's {{Storage lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b5925bfd1d8665dd4b}} measures async checkpointing on the running example, and the sibling {{Storage and data movement|n:3fc5c17b0d0d818a94ced3fc7c3331fe}} covers parallel file systems in depth.

Real examples. Llama 3's Tectonic: 240 PB on 7,500 SSD servers, 2 TB/s sustained and 7 TB/s peak; Fire-Flyer 2's 3FS: 180 storage nodes, 8 TB/s of read on the compute fabric; AI2's Jupiter: WEKA (section 7). Formats, shards and data loaders are on {{Storage and loading at scale|n:3f95c17b0d0d81398782efcb29d950d4}}; the page cache and fsync on {{Storage and file systems|n:3f05c17b0d0d8141be7acc1313570cbf}}.

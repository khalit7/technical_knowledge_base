6. Storage: where data and checkpoints live

The tiers, fastest and smallest first: GPU memory; the node's RAM; its local NVMe disks (fast, but gone with the node); a parallel file system shared by all nodes (Lustre, IBM Storage Scale, WEKA, VAST, DeepSeek's 3FS), which spreads each file over many servers so thousands of clients can read and write at once; and object storage (S3, Google Cloud Storage, or an S3-compatible store on premises), cheapest and nearly limitless but slower per request and with no in-place edits. NFS is fine for home directories and code and a common bottleneck for anything else.

Training does two very different kinds of I/O. Reading data is a steady stream of large reads, easy to hide behind compute with prefetching (formats, shards and streaming loaders are {{Storage and loading at scale|n:3f95c17b0d0d81398782efcb29d950d4}}'s). Writing a checkpoint is a burst: every rank writes at the same moment while the GPUs wait. With mixed-precision Adam a checkpoint holds about 14 bytes per parameter, so a 70B model's is about 0.98 TB and writing it in a minute needs about 16 GB/s of aggregate write bandwidth derived. Three habits follow: shard (every rank writes its own piece, as PyTorch's Distributed Checkpoint does), stage (copy to host RAM or local NVMe in seconds and upload in the background) and keep files big (millions of small files overload the metadata servers long before the bandwidth runs out).

Storage that operators built for the checkpoint burst (published)

A 405B model's checkpoint at the same 14 bytes per parameter is about 5.7 TB, about 94 GB/s to write in one minute derived. What operators built:

All published by the operators about their own systems. The 14 bytes are an assumption (bf16 weights 2, fp32 master weights 4, Adam's two moments 4 and 4); layouts vary.

7. Cost and meters: where the GPU-hours go

A run is paid in GPU-hours whatever happens inside them. Follow one paid GPU-hour down to useful arithmetic and every section of this page reappears as a loss, each measured by a different meter:

  1. Paid but not allocated: GPUs given to no job (fragmentation, a big job waiting for enough nodes, gaps backfill could not fill). The ratio allocated over paid is scheduling efficiency, read from the scheduler's own accounting.
  2. Allocated but not training: image pulls, rendezvous, checkpoint loads, restarts, steps redone since the last checkpoint, time blocked writing checkpoints. What remains is goodput, the share of time that became kept progress (Llama 3's "effective training time", Gemini's 85% to 97%).
  3. Training but not using the chip: communication, memory traffic and small kernels keep the step below peak. MFU (model FLOPs utilisation) is the model's useful FLOPs per second over the chip's peak; Llama 3 405B ran at 38 to 43% BF16 MFU ({{Llama 3 Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS2}}) published. How to count it is {{Performance math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81faad1ef390b0e54d08}}'s.

Scheduling efficiency on the three local clusters failed three different ways measured: Slurm's eight-job queue kept 71% of its 8 GPUs' time allocated under FIFO and 80% with backfill; Kubernetes without gang admission held all 12 GPUs for 246 s with no step run, while Kueue fixed that and could fill at most 67% of the quota with 8-GPU jobs (section 2); Ray's placement groups waited holding nothing. 8. ML platforms: the loop around the jobs

A scheduler runs one job; a team runs thousands and must know which code, data and settings produced which checkpoint, which checkpoint became which model, and how to rerun any of it. Three kinds of tool do this, usually on top of Slurm or Kubernetes rather than instead of them: experiment tracking (every run's configuration, metrics and artefacts in one searchable place: MLflow, Weights & Biases), workflow or pipeline engines (a graph of steps such as prepare, train, evaluate, register, with retries and caching: Airflow, Kubeflow Pipelines, Flyte, Metaflow, Prefect, Dagster) and model registries (named, versioned models with lineage and an alias such as "champion"). A training team's minimum is a tracker and a registry; a pipeline engine pays off once the same multi-step process runs often enough that doing it by hand causes mistakes. Managed clouds bundle all three with the cluster.

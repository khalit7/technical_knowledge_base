6. Storage: where data and checkpoints live

The tiers, fastest and smallest first: GPU memory; the node's RAM; the node's local NVMe disks (fast, but gone with the node); a parallel file system shared by all nodes, such as Lustre, IBM Storage Scale (GPFS), WEKA or VAST, which stripes each file over many servers so thousands of clients can read and write at once; and object storage (S3, Google Cloud Storage, or MinIO on premises), cheapest and nearly limitless but with higher latency and no in-place edits. Network file systems (NFS) are fine for home directories and code, and a common bottleneck when used for anything else.

Training does two very different kinds of I/O. Reading data is a steady stream of large sequential reads, easy to hide behind compute with prefetching (the {{storage and loading page|n:3f95c17b0d0d81398782efcb29d950d4}} owns formats, shards and streaming). Writing checkpoints is a burst: every rank writes at the same moment while the GPUs wait, so the file system's aggregate write bandwidth, not its capacity, sets the cost.

Checkpoint arithmetic. With mixed-precision Adam a checkpoint holds about 14 bytes per parameter (2 for bf16 weights, 4 for fp32 master weights, 4 and 4 for Adam's two moments; an assumption, as layouts vary). A 70B model's checkpoint is then about 0.98 TB, and writing it in one minute needs about 16 GB/s of aggregate write bandwidth; a 405B model's is about 5.7 TB, about 94 GB/s for one minute derived. Real operators size storage for exactly this burst:

  • Meta's Tectonic for Llama 3: 240 PB on 7,500 SSD servers, 2 TB/s sustained and 7 TB/s peak, built for checkpoint bursts of 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU published ({{Llama 3 §3.3.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS1}}).
  • DeepSeek's 3FS: 8 TB/s of aggregate reads across its cluster published ({{Fire-Flyer §VI-B|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.14158}}).
  • Ai2: a WEKA cluster measured at 761 Gbps read/write with 64 clients on one cluster, and Google Cloud Storage "up to 1 GB/s per VM" on the other published ({{OLMo 2 §6.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656}}).

Three habits follow. Shard: every rank writes only its own piece, in parallel (PyTorch's Distributed Checkpoint), instead of gathering one file on rank 0. Stage: copy to host RAM or local NVMe in seconds, then upload in the background (asynchronous checkpointing). Keep files big: millions of small files overload a parallel file system's metadata servers long before its bandwidth runs out.

7. Cost and observability: where the GPU-hours go

A run is paid in GPU-hours, whatever happens inside them. Follow one paid GPU-hour down to useful arithmetic and every section of this page appears as a loss:

  1. Allocated or idle: GPUs paid for but not given to any job (fragmentation, a big job waiting for enough nodes, gaps backfill could not fill). The ratio is scheduling efficiency, measured from the scheduler's own accounting.
  2. Allocated but not training: image pulls, rendezvous, loading checkpoints, restarts after failures, steps redone since the last checkpoint, time blocked writing checkpoints. What is left is goodput, the share of time making real training progress (Llama 3's "effective training time", Gemini's 85% to 97%).
  3. Training but not using the chip: the step itself runs below the hardware's peak because of communication, memory traffic and small kernels. MFU (model FLOPs utilisation) is the model's useful FLOPs per second over the chip's peak; Llama 3 405B ran at 38 to 43% BF16 MFU published ({{Llama 3 Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS2}}). The {{performance math page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81faad1ef390b0e54d08}} owns how to compute it.
Predict: nvidia-smi shows every GPU of your job at 100% utilisation. Is the job using the GPUs well?
That number is the "percent of time over the past sample period during which one or more kernels was executing on the GPU" ({{nvidia-smi docs|https://docs.nvidia.com/deploy/nvidia-smi/index.html}}): one small kernel occupying a single streaming multiprocessor (out of more than a hundred) counts as busy, and so does a GPU spinning inside a collective, waiting for a slow peer. Better meters come from NVIDIA's DCGM: SM activity, "the fraction of time at least one warp was active on a multiprocessor, averaged over all multiprocessors", for which "a value of 0.8 or greater is necessary, but not sufficient", and tensor-pipe activity ({{DCGM profiling metrics|https://docs.nvidia.com/datacenter/dcgm/latest/learn/modules/profiling.html}}). The real test is MFU computed from tokens per second.

What to watch on a training cluster: per job, tokens per second and step time (a drop is the first sign of a straggler), loss, and the time since the last good checkpoint; per GPU, SM and tensor activity, memory, temperature, power and error counters (exported by DCGM to Prometheus and drawn in Grafana); per cluster, queue length and wait times, allocated against idle GPUs, nodes drained and why. 8. ML platforms: the loop around the jobs

A scheduler runs one job; a team runs thousands, and needs to know which code, data and settings produced which checkpoint, which checkpoint became which model, and how to rerun any of it. Three kinds of tool do this, usually on top of Slurm or Kubernetes rather than instead of them:

  • Experiment tracking: every run's configuration, metrics, logs and artefacts in one searchable place (MLflow, Weights & Biases, which CoreWeave bought in May 2025, {{CoreWeave|https://coreweave.com/news/coreweave-completes-acquisition-of-weights-biases-2}}; Neptune's hosted service was turned off on March 5, 2026 after it agreed to be acquired by OpenAI, {{Neptune|https://docs.neptune.ai/transition_hub}}).
  • Workflow or pipeline engines: a graph of steps (prepare data, train, evaluate, convert, register) with retries and caching (Airflow, Kubeflow Pipelines, Flyte, Metaflow, Prefect, Dagster).
  • Model registries: named, versioned models with their lineage and stage (candidate, approved, serving), often part of the tracker.

A training team's minimum is a tracker and a registry; a pipeline engine pays off once the same multi-step process (for example a nightly evaluation of every new checkpoint) runs often enough that doing it by hand causes mistakes.

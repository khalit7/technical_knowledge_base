Failure lab: kill a rank, a node, a pod, and watch what each system does

A training job on many machines is only as alive as its least healthy part. This lab breaks the running example on purpose, on all three local clusters, and records what happens: who notices, how fast, what the scheduler decides, where the run resumes, how much work is redone, and whether the loss comes back bit for bit.

Every timing on this tab is measured on an Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM, shared machine, with the tiny 4-rank running example (a real cluster takes minutes where this takes seconds to start). Orders of events, error messages, which checkpoint a run resumed from and the losses are exact. Published figures are marked published, arithmetic on them calculation, and interpretations that are ours rather than a source's our reading.

1. What fails at scale, from the one table everyone quotes

The problem. In synchronous data-parallel training every rank (one process, usually one GPU) computes gradients on its share of the batch, then all ranks sum them in an all-reduce before anyone takes the next step. So every step waits for every rank: if one GPU, host, cable or process fails, all the others stop at the next collective. Llama 3's paper puts it plainly: "a single GPU failure may require a restart of the entire job" published.

Meta published the best-known failure record: during a 54-day snapshot of Llama 3 405B pre-training on up to 16,384 H100 GPUs there were 466 job interruptions, 47 planned (firmware upgrades, configuration or dataset updates) and 419 unexpected, broken down by cause in Table 5 ({{Llama 3, section 3.3.4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}) published. Table check calculation. The 18 counts add up to 419, as the text says. One printed percentage is wrong: "Faulty GPU 148, 30.1%", but 148 / 419 = 35.3%; every other row matches its count, and the printed percentages add up to only 94.9%. The text's "GPU issues are the largest category, accounting for 58.7%" is the sum of the printed GPU-row percentages, so it inherits the slip: by counts the six GPU rows hold 268 interruptions, 64.0%. The text's "approximately 78%" attributed to confirmed or suspected hardware cannot be rebuilt exactly from any grouping of rows: its own definition (GPU and host components plus unplanned host maintenance) gives 321, 76.6%; adding network switches and cables gives 85.0%.

Why scale makes it worse. Divide 419 interruptions by 16,384 GPUs and 54 days: about one unexpected interruption per GPU every 5.8 years calculation. Each GPU is reliable; the job is not, because it fails when any of its parts does. If failures are independent, a job on N GPUs fails N times as often, so Llama 3's job stopped about every 3.1 hours. The {{Reading tab's calculator|#t-read}} turns any cluster size into a job MTBF and a checkpoint interval.

A second public record, smaller and older: Meta's OPT-175B ran on 992 80GB A100s and "hardware failures contributed to at least 35 manual restarts and the cycling of over 100 hosts over the course of 2 months", plus an estimated "70+ automatic restarts" ({{OPT, section 2.5|https://arxiv.org/html/2205.01068v4#S2.SS5}}) published. Per GPU that is at least 1.8e-3 restarts per GPU-day against Llama 3's 4.7e-4 interruptions per GPU-day, roughly 3.7 times as often calculation; the two count different things (restarts against interruptions, "2 months" taken as 60 days) on different hardware two years apart, so read it as "the order of magnitude is stable", not as a trend.

Three shapes recur in both records, and they are the three this lab breaks on purpose: a process dies (a GPU error, an uncorrectable memory error or a software bug makes a rank exit), a machine is lost (power supply, chassis, host maintenance), and something hangs: Llama 3 notes that failures "in either the remote GPU or NVLink connectivity often manifest as stalled load/store operations within CUDA kernels without returning a clear error code" published, and 7 of its interruptions are NCCL watchdog timeouts.

Failure lab: kill a rank, a node, a pod, and watch what each system does

A training job on many machines is only as alive as its least healthy part. This lab broke the running example on purpose on all three local clusters and recorded what each scheduler did: who noticed, how fast, what it decided, and where the run resumed. That comparison is the root's subject; why things fail at scale, every recovery design and how to account for the time lost are the child page {{Fault tolerance at scale|n:3fc5c17b0d0d815786d1c1dddb311a9b}}'s.

Every timing on this tab is measured on an Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM, shared machine, with the tiny 4-rank running example (a real cluster takes minutes where this takes seconds to start). Orders of events, error messages, which checkpoint a run resumed from and the losses are exact. Published figures are marked published, arithmetic on them calculation.

1. The words, and why scale makes failure routine

In synchronous data-parallel training every rank (one process, usually one GPU) computes gradients on its share of the batch, then all ranks sum them in an all-reduce before anyone takes the next step, so one failed GPU, host, cable or process stops all the others at the next collective. A job on N GPUs fails about N times as often as one GPU: Llama 3 405B, on up to 16,384 H100s, had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days, one every 3.1 hours ({{Llama 3, section 3.3.4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}) published, about one per GPU every 5.8 years calculation. Its Table 5, row by row with its misprint, is on {{Fault tolerance at scale|n:3fc5c17b0d0d815786d1c1dddb311a9b}}'s Failure taxonomies tab.

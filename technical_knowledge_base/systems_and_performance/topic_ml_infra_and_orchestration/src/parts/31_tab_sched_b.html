2. The same policies at scale simulated

Eight jobs show the mechanism; a real cluster has hundreds of jobs of very different sizes arriving all day. The simulator that predicted the Slurm runs above (checked against them, and its Python and JavaScript versions checked against each other by src/sched/scripts/check_sim.mjs) runs here on a synthetic workload: 300 jobs from 1 GPU up to a quarter of the cluster, runtimes from 10 minutes to a day, arriving at random so that the work offered is a chosen fraction of the cluster's capacity (the offered load). Each job's time limit is its runtime times a random factor up to the "limit padding" you choose, the way users round limits up.

Policy switcher on a bigger cluster

Illustrative workload, not a trace of any real cluster. The faint orange line is FIFO for comparison. Utilisation is measured from the first to the last submission, so the empty tail after the last job does not count.

What to look for. FIFO leaves visible troughs in the busy-GPU line: each is a big job at the head of the queue waiting for room while small jobs that would fit sit behind it. Both backfills fill them, which cuts the mean wait by a large factor at loads near 1. EASY usually beats conservative on mean wait because it protects only one reservation, at the price of predictability: under EASY a job's promised start can slip, while under conservative backfill every waiting job keeps the start it was given (Slurm reports it in squeue --start). Raise the limit padding and the plan gets worse in a specific way: jobs that will end early look as if they will run long, so the scheduler leaves gaps it could have filled, or fills gaps with jobs that free up sooner than planned. The big-job column is where policies part ways: backfill helps small jobs most, and a big job's wait depends on reservations being honoured.

Predict: set the load to 1.2 (more work arrives than the cluster can do). What happens to the mean wait under every policy?
Above a load of 1 the queue grows without limit; a scheduler can only choose who waits. That is why real clusters add quotas and fairshare (next section): the question becomes who waits, not whether anyone does.

3. Fairshare: who goes first when everyone is waiting

The problem. Order by submit time and a team that queues 500 jobs on Monday owns the cluster until Wednesday. Fairshare orders by how much each user or team has used recently against how much they are entitled to.

How it works in Slurm. Each account (a team) and each user in it has shares, its entitled fraction. Every running job adds usage: its billed resources times seconds (here a fake GPU bills 1 and a CPU 0.25, TRESBillingWeights). Usage decays exponentially with a half-life (default 7 days; 10 minutes on this lab cluster so that it moves while you watch). From shares and usage Slurm computes a fairshare factor between 0 and 1, and the job's priority is a weighted sum of factors (the multifactor plugin): here 10,000 times fairshare plus 1,000 times age, with the other weights zero.

level_fs = S / U     factor = rank / (number of users)
S
the association's shares divided by the total shares of its siblings (teama among teama, teamb and the root user)
U
its decayed usage divided by its parent's usage; U = 0 gives level_fs = infinity
rank
users are ranked by walking the account tree, highest level_fs first at every level; ties share a rank

That is Fair Tree, Slurm's default: the flag is set unless PriorityFlags turns it off (src/common/read_config.c:4843: conf->priority_flags = PRIORITY_FLAGS_FAIR_TREE;). Its point is that a team's total usage is compared first: a user in a heavy team ranks below every user of a light team, however little that user ran personally. The older formula, still available with PriorityFlags=NO_FAIR_TREE, is 2^(−U/S) (priority_multifactor.c:1951). Decay is applied every PriorityCalcPeriod as usage × decay_factorseconds, with decay_factor = 1 − 0.693 / half-life (priority_multifactor.c:1649).

Read it with the formula: teama has used everything (U = 1, S = 1/3, level_fs 0.33), teamb nothing (level_fs infinity), so bob ranks with the root user at the top (rank 4 of 4, factor 1.0) and alice, the heavy user in the heavy team, at the bottom (1 of 4, 0.25). carol, who ran nothing, still sits below bob (0.5) because her team is heavy. Ninety seconds later bob had used 74 units and his factor fell to 0.75 (rank 3: below the root user's infinite level_fs), still far above alice's 0.25, which is why b4 beat a1 in the second wave. Age (up to 1,000 here) never came close to the fairshare gap.

When it matters. Fairshare only reorders the queue; it never stops a running job (preemption, next, does that), and a heavy team still runs whenever nobody else is waiting. The half-life is the policy knob: short means "fair this afternoon", long (the 7-day default) means "fair this week", and a big run burns its team's priority for weeks afterwards. Check it with sshare -a -l (shares, usage, LevelFS, FairShare per association) and sprio -l (each pending job's priority split into factors).

4. Preemption: taking GPUs back

The problem. An urgent job (a deadline, a production evaluation, a quota owner returning) should not wait hours for a long training job to finish. Preemption stops a running job to free its resources. What happens to the victim is the preempt mode: CANCEL (killed), REQUEUE (killed and put back in the queue to start over, from its last checkpoint if it wrote one), SUSPEND (paused in memory; useless for GPUs whose memory stays full).

The lab config. PreemptType=preempt/partition_prio with PreemptMode=REQUEUE: jobs in partition urgent (PriorityTier=10) may requeue jobs in batch (tier 1) on the same nodes. bob runs a 12-GPU job in batch; 40 seconds later alice submits a 4-GPU job to urgent.

slurmctld.log, Slurm 24.11.5 (lines for jobs 39 and 40). sacct -D keeps both runs of job 39: the first PREEMPTED after 41 s, the second COMPLETED.

What happened. alice's urgent job started 1 s after submission: Slurm requeued all 12 GPUs of bob's job to get the 4 it needed. Then two things a newcomer does not expect. First, the other 8 GPUs sat idle: bob's job needs all 12, so it could not restart beside the urgent job. Second, bob's job did not restart when the urgent job ended at 02:46:59; it waited until 02:48:17 with reason (BeginTime). A requeued job gets a begin time of now plus the credential lifetime plus one second (src/slurmctld/job_mgr.c:16389, begin_time = now + cred_lifetime + 1;), so that the old run's credentials and I/O are gone before the new run starts: 121 s here (requeued at 02:46:09, eligible at 02:48:10; the next backfill pass started it). To run a 4-GPU job for 50 s, preemption cost bob 41 s of lost work, a 2-minute hold and 8 idle GPUs for 50 s.

The cost. Everything the victim computed since its last checkpoint is lost, plus its restart time, and it waits again in the queue. Preemption is therefore only as cheap as the victim's checkpoint interval: a training job that may be preempted should checkpoint often and resume automatically (#SBATCH --requeue, and a script that loads the latest checkpoint, as the running example does). The {{Failure lab|#t-fail}} measures the resume.

9. Choosing

The scheduler matters less than people argue: the evidence in section 3 shows every large operator extending whatever it chose with health checks, fast restarts and storage sized for checkpoints. Choose by what else the cluster must do and who will use it.

Your situationStart withWhy
A fixed bare-metal GPU cluster, mostly big training runs, researchers used to HPCSlurm (managed by the provider, or Slurm on Kubernetes)queues, backfill, fairshare and gang allocation out of the box; one script per job; the least to debug
A platform team already running Kubernetes for serving and pipelines, or elastic cloud capacityKubernetes with Kueue and JobSet (and the Kubeflow Trainer)one control plane for every workload; quotas and borrowing across teams; containers everywhere
A job made of many cooperating programs: RL with rollout workers and inference engines, data processing, hyperparameter searchRay, on Kubernetes through KubeRaytasks, actors and an object store make the program itself distributed; Kubernetes and Kueue still handle teams and quotas
A few nodes, one teamtorchrun over SSH, or Slurm on the nodesa scheduler pays off once people queue for the same GPUs

Whatever the choice, the same five things decide whether 1,000 GPUs do useful work: gang admission (no partial jobs), health checks with automatic draining, checkpoints that are sharded and asynchronous, storage sized for the checkpoint burst, and meters for goodput and MFU, not just utilisation.

Common mistakes

Asking for far more time than the job needs
The scheduler plans with your limit, so the job is never backfilled and waits behind everything (section 2). Ask for the expected time plus a margin; checkpoint so that hitting the limit costs little.
Submitting multi-pod training to plain Kubernetes
The default scheduler places pods one at a time; two jobs can each get part of what they need and deadlock, holding GPUs that do nothing (OpenAI's 2021 experience). Admit jobs as a whole: Kueue, JobSet with Kueue, Volcano, or the coscheduling plugin. TODO (sched lab): the real kubectl get pods picture of the deadlock on kind.
A rendezvous address that only works on one node
MASTER_ADDR=localhost or a short host name that other nodes resolve differently makes every other rank wait until the timeout. Use the first node of the allocation (Slurm) or pod 0's DNS name through the headless service (JobSet). TODO (launch lab): the real error text and the time to error.
Trusting the default timeouts, or setting them too short
At 30 minutes (gloo) or 10 minutes (NCCL) a hang burns that much of every GPU before anything fails; but a timeout shorter than your slowest legitimate wait (a first-step compile, a large synchronous checkpoint) kills healthy jobs. Set it on purpose, and add a watchdog on step progress.
A restart that lands on the same broken node
If the failed node is not drained or cordoned, the requeued job is placed on it again and fails again: a restart loop that looks like a software bug. Health checks must take the node out automatically (Ai2's Beaker cordons and reschedules; Slurm runs a HealthCheckProgram periodically on every node, and that script drains the node itself, for example with scontrol update NodeName=... State=drain: {{slurm.conf|https://slurm.schedmd.com/slurm.conf.html}}).
A checkpoint that restores the model but not the run
Without the optimiser state, the learning-rate scheduler step, the data loader's position and the random-number generators, a resumed run sees different data and its loss jumps. Save all of them, and test a restore before you need one. Measured in the {{Failure lab|#t-fail}}: seven resumed 4-rank runs on three schedulers all continued the same trajectory, matching the uninterrupted loss at 8 of 10 logged steps and differing at two by one float32 rounding step, because DDP rebuilds its gradient buckets after a process's first step; with 2 ranks, or with the rebuild switched off, resume was bit-exact.
Checkpoints only on node-local disk, or gathered on rank 0
Local disk dies with the node it was meant to protect against; gathering everything on rank 0 is slow and can run that rank out of memory. Stage locally if you like, but upload, and shard the write across ranks.
Reading 100% GPU utilisation as efficiency
It means a kernel was running (section 7). Track tokens per second and MFU.
Elastic restarts that silently change the experiment
Continuing on fewer workers changes the global batch size (or the steps per epoch) unless the code compensates; torchrun also reassigns ranks between restarts, so code that assumed a fixed rank-to-data mapping reads the wrong shards.

Interview questions

Design the training platform for a 1,000-GPU lab: scheduler, storage, failure handling, cost controls.

Shape: 125 nodes of 8 GPUs, NVLink inside, one 400 Gb/s NIC per GPU on a rail-optimised fabric, a separate front-end network. Scheduler: Slurm with fairshare and QOS if most work is large training by researchers; Kubernetes with Kueue (team ClusterQueues in a cohort, borrowing, preemption) and JobSet if the lab also serves models and runs pipelines; either way gang admission and topology-aware placement, and a low-priority preemptible queue that soaks up idle GPUs. Failures: at Llama 3's rate a 1,000-GPU job stops about every 51 hours; with a 1-minute blocking checkpoint, Young gives about 78 minutes between checkpoints and about 3% loss with 10-minute restarts (the calculator in section 5). Add asynchronous checkpoints, preflight and periodic health checks with automatic drain, a few spare nodes, and automatic requeue. Storage: a parallel file system sized for the checkpoint burst (a 70B model is about 1 TB, so about 16 GB/s to write it in a minute), object storage for datasets and old checkpoints, local NVMe for staging. Cost: per-team quotas, dashboards of allocated against idle GPUs, goodput and MFU per job, alerts on step time and on GPUs allocated but idle.

A 512-GPU job is stuck at init. What do you check, in order?

(1) Did every rank start? Count processes or pods against the world size; find the missing one in the scheduler's per-node logs or the pod status (image pull, out of memory, a bad node). (2) Can every node reach MASTER_ADDR:MASTER_PORT, and does the name resolve the same everywhere? (3) Do all ranks agree on the world size and the rendezvous id? (4) If all arrived and it hangs inside init_process_group or the first collective: NCCL_DEBUG=INFO for transport errors, py-spy dump on a few ranks, the flight recorder to find the rank out of step, then that node's health (NICs down, GPU Xid errors). Drain the bad node and resubmit.

Your job has been pending for hours. Why?

Ask the scheduler: Slurm's squeue prints a reason such as "Resources" (waiting for nodes), "Priority" (higher-priority jobs exist for the partition) or a QOS limit ({{squeue reason codes|https://slurm.schedmd.com/squeue.html#SECTION_JOB-REASON-CODES}}); on Kubernetes, kubectl describe the pod's events and Kueue's Workload status (quota exhausted, waiting to be admitted). Then fix the cause: a smaller or shorter request, another partition, or quota.

Slurm or Kubernetes for training?

Slurm gives batch semantics (queue, backfill, fairshare, all-or-nothing allocations) for free and is simplest for big synchronous jobs on a fixed cluster. Kubernetes gives one platform for everything and an ecosystem, but needs Kueue or Volcano and JobSet to behave like a batch system. The deciding question is what else must run on the cluster and who operates it. Both are used at frontier scale (section 3).

Why does distributed training need gang scheduling?

Synchronous training cannot start until all ranks exist; a partly started job holds GPUs and does nothing. Two partly started jobs can each wait forever for GPUs the other holds: a deadlock. All-or-nothing admission prevents both.

How often should a 16,000-GPU run checkpoint?

Estimate the job's MTBF (Llama 3: about 3.1 hours on 16,384 GPUs) and the blocking write time C; Young's √(2CM) gives about 19 minutes for C = 1 minute. Then make C smaller (asynchronous, in-memory copies) because the loss falls with √C, and make restarts faster because each one costs R on top.

Goodput against MFU?

Goodput is the share of paid time spent making progress (lost to queues, launches, failures, checkpoints); MFU is how much of the chip's peak the progress uses while it runs. Cost per useful FLOP is the product of both: 90% goodput at 40% MFU uses 36% of what you paid for.

One GPU in a data-parallel job is 20% slower. What happens?

Every step waits for it at the gradient all-reduce, so the whole job runs about 20% slower, and the other GPUs show high "utilisation" while waiting. Find it from per-rank step or collective times, then drain the node.

What does torchrun do when one worker dies?

The agent stops and restarts all workers, up to --max-restarts; "if k<=n workers fail all workers are stopped and restarted" (torchrun's docstring, torch 2.14.1). A lost node (agent failure) is left to the job manager: fail the job or replace the node.

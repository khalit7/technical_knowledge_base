5. Failures and how runs survive them

The problem. A synchronous training job is a chain as strong as its weakest GPU: when one GPU, cable, switch, host or process fails, every rank stops at the next collective. Parts fail rarely, but a big job holds many parts. Meta's Llama 3 405B pre-training had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days on up to 16,384 H100s, about 78% of them confirmed or suspected hardware, yet kept "higher than 90% effective training time" published ({{Llama 3 §3.3.4, Table 5|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}). That is one stop every 3.1 hours derived (54 × 24 / 419).

Why scale makes it worse. If each GPU (with its share of host and network) fails independently, a job on N GPUs is interrupted N times as often, so the mean time between failures (MTBF) of a job falls as 1/N. At Llama 3's rate, a 1,000-GPU job would stop about every 51 hours and a 100,000-GPU job every 30 minutes derived (assuming interruptions scale with GPU count, which is roughly but not exactly true: some causes are per job or per switch).

A newer table, a different top cause. Meta's 2026 report on a recurring pre-training job of about 32K GPUs counts 678 unexpected interruptions: GPU HBM3 memory is now the largest cause (155, 22.9%), then PCIe devices (18.0%) and NCCL watchdog timeouts (9.0%), with faulty GPU compute, Llama 3's top row, down to fourth (7.4%); the job averaged 2.3 interruptions per 1,000 servers per day and 95 to 97% effective training time published ({{arXiv 2602.00277, §2.2 and Table 1, Jan 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00277}}). Llama 3's rate was about 3.8 per 1,000 servers per day derived (419 / 54 days / 2,048 servers). Read the same paper with a calculator: it also says that "with 100K GPUs, there is a failure every 18 minutes", which its own rate does not give. 100,000 GPUs at 8 per server are 12,500 servers; 12.5 × 2.3 = 28.75 interruptions a day, one about every 50 minutes derived. (18 minutes would need about 6.4 per 1,000 servers per day, close to the 6.5 per 1,000 node-days Meta reported for an older research cluster: {{Kokolis et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680}}.)

How runs survive: checkpoint and restart. Every so often all ranks save the model, optimiser and data-loader state (a checkpoint); after a failure the job restarts from the last one. You lose the work since that checkpoint plus the restart time, and you pay for writing checkpoints even when nothing fails. Too frequent wastes time writing, too rare wastes time redoing; Young's classic answer is an interval of about √(2 · C · M), with C the time to write one checkpoint and M the job's MTBF ({{Young 1974|https://doi.org/10.1145/361147.361115}}; refined by {{Daly 2006|https://doi.org/10.1016/j.future.2004.11.016}}). The {{hardware reliability page|n:3f05c17b0d0d8143851cfc3ae14c3c22}} derives it and shows where it breaks.

derived: MTBF = 3.09 h × 16,384 / N (Llama 3's rate); interval τ = √(2CM); expected loss ≈ C/τ + (τ/2 + R)/M, the first-order approximation, which overstates the loss once τ nears M. The {{Failure lab|#t-fail}} has the full goodput simulator.

The other defences, each answering one part of the arithmetic above:

  • Make checkpoints cheap. Asynchronous checkpointing copies state to host memory in seconds and uploads it while training continues; DeepSeek checkpoints "typically every 5 minutes" at over 10 GiB/s per node, so a failure loses at most 5 minutes published ({{Fire-Flyer §VII-A|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.14158}}). Google went further for Gemini Ultra: no periodic checkpoints to storage, but "redundant in-memory copies of the model state", which raised the largest job's goodput "from 85% to 97%" published ({{Gemini §3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11805}}).
  • Make restarts fast. Keep hot spares (Gemini held back a few TPU cubes per SuperPod "for hot standbys and rolling maintenance"), cache container images on nodes, and restart only the failed part when the framework allows it.
  • Keep bad nodes out. A health check runs before or between jobs; a node that fails is drained (Slurm) or cordoned and tainted (Kubernetes) so nothing new lands on it. Ai2's Beaker runs a tensor multiplication before each workload and on failure "cordons the associated host and reschedules the workload" published ({{OLMo 2 §6.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656}}); OpenAI's nodes join Kubernetes with a "preflight" taint that only passing GPU tests remove.
  • Shrink instead of stopping. Elastic training (torchrun with a minimum and maximum node count) continues with fewer workers; it changes the global batch or the data order unless the code compensates, so most large pre-training runs restart at full size instead.
  • Watch for slow, not just dead. A straggler (a GPU running hot and throttled, a flapping link) slows every synchronous step to its pace without failing anything.

5. Failures and how runs survive them

A synchronous job is a chain as strong as its weakest GPU: when one GPU, cable, switch, host or process fails, every rank stops at the next collective. Parts fail rarely, but a big job holds many of them. Meta's Llama 3 405B pre-training had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days on up to 16,384 H100s, about 78% of them confirmed or suspected hardware, and still kept "higher than 90% effective training time" ({{Llama 3 §3.3.4, Table 5|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}) published: one stop every 3.1 hours derived: 54 × 24 / 419. If each GPU (with its share of host and network) fails independently, a job's mean time between failures (MTBF) falls as 1/N:

Meta's 2026 report on a recurring job of about 32K GPUs counts 678 interruptions with GPU HBM3 memory now the largest cause, 2.3 interruptions per 1,000 servers per day and 95 to 97% effective training time ({{arXiv 2602.00277|https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00277}}) published; {{Fault tolerance at scale|n:3fc5c17b0d0d815786d1c1dddb311a9b}}, section 1, sets the two tables side by side and checks the paper's own "every 18 minutes at 100K GPUs" against its rate.

Checkpoint and restart is the base defence: every so often all ranks save model, optimiser, data-loader and random-number state (a checkpoint); after a failure the job restarts from the last one, losing the work since then plus the restart time, and paying for every write even when nothing fails. How often to write is a trade-off with a classic answer (Young's interval, about √(2 · C · MTBF) for a write time C), derived with its exact form and a simulator on {{Power, cooling, reliability|n:3f05c17b0d0d8143851cfc3ae14c3c22}}, section 6. Each other defence removes one part of the lost time:

  • Make checkpoints cheap. Asynchronous checkpointing copies state to host memory in seconds and writes it out while training continues; DeepSeek checkpoints "typically every 5 minutes" ({{Fire-Flyer §VII-A|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.14158}}); Google kept "redundant in-memory copies of the model state" for Gemini Ultra and raised the largest job's goodput "from 85% to 97%" ({{Gemini §3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11805}}) published.
  • Make restarts fast. Hot spares (Gemini held back TPU cubes "for hot standbys and rolling maintenance"), images cached on nodes, restarting only the failed part where the framework allows.
  • Keep bad nodes out. A health check runs before or between jobs; a failing node is drained (Slurm) or cordoned and tainted (Kubernetes) so nothing new lands on it. Ai2's Beaker "cordons the associated host and reschedules the workload" ({{OLMo 2 §6.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656}}); OpenAI's nodes join with a "preflight" taint that only passing GPU tests remove.
  • Shrink instead of stopping. Elastic training continues on fewer workers, which changes the global batch or the data order unless the code compensates; most large pre-training restarts at full size.
  • Watch for slow, not just dead. A straggler (a hot, throttled GPU, a flapping link) slows every synchronous step to its pace without failing anything.

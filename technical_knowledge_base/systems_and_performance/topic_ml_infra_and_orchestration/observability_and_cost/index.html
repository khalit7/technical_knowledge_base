1. Observability and cost in one screen

A training cluster is paid for by the GPU-hour whether the GPUs do anything or not. Observability is the set of meters that tell you what those hours bought; cost is what you do with the answer. Three meters, read together, are the whole subject:

1. The scheduler's accounting

Who held which GPUs, from when to when. Slurm's sacct, Kueue's Workload conditions, Ray's state API. It is the bill. It cannot tell you whether anything ran.

2. The device's counters

What the hardware did: on NVIDIA GPUs, DCGM's counters (GPU utilisation, SM activity, tensor activity, memory, power, errors). They say how busy the chip was, not whether the work was useful.

3. The training job's log

What the model got: steps, tokens per second, loss, MFU, time since the last good checkpoint. Only this says whether the run advanced.

Every failure on this page is one meter looking healthy while another does not. A hung job holds every GPU (meter 1 full) at 100% GPU utilisation (meter 2 full) and makes no steps (meter 3 empty). A slow node shows nothing on either of the first two. A cluster whose scheduler reports 92% allocated can be running half its jobs at a fifth of the chip.

What this page measured on the topic's local clusters (Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM, shared machine; fake GPUs, real schedulers): a Prometheus scraping kind and Slurm while the running example (the topic's tiny GPT, 4 ranks over gloo) went through a healthy run, a slow node, a hang and two queues. The hang alert fired 33 s after a rank froze, while the process-group timeout took 83 s to end the job; the waiting ranks' CPU fell to 0.01 cores, the opposite of what a GPU reports in the same situation; the slow node made the healthy pod look exactly as idle as the slow one; Kueue left 8 of 12 GPUs idle with work waiting because memory quota, not GPUs, ran out. All of it replays on the Cluster dashboard tab.

What it takes from published work: the same LLM clusters read 97% to 99% median GPU utilisation and about 40% median SM activity (Shanghai AI Lab, section 4); goodput has three definitions that do not agree (section 6); and owning a GPU fleet costs about $1.40 per H100 per calendar hour on stated assumptions, against $3.99 to $12.29 per GPU-hour in published rental list prices (read 2026-10-16), with every assumption shown (section 12 and the Capacity planner).

2. The words, from zero

Metric, time seriesA named number sampled over time, with labels that say which thing it describes: container_cpu_usage_seconds_total{pod="oc-hang-node-0-1"}. Each distinct set of labels is its own time series.
Counter, gaugeA counter only goes up (CPU seconds used, steps done); you read its rate. A gauge goes up and down (memory in use, GPUs allocated); you read its value.
Exporter, scrapeAn exporter is a small program that serves current values over HTTP at /metrics; Prometheus fetches (scrapes) every exporter on a fixed interval and stores the samples. dcgm-exporter does this for GPUs, cAdvisor (inside the kubelet) for containers.
CardinalityThe number of distinct time series. Labels multiply it: one metric per GPU on 1,000 GPUs is 1,000 series; add a per-job label and every job makes new ones. It is what a metrics system's cost grows with.
SM, warp, occupancyA GPU is made of streaming multiprocessors (SMs: 132 on an H100 SXM). Threads run in groups of 32 called warps; an SM holds up to 64 resident warps. Occupancy is resident warps over that maximum. Taught on GPU architecture.
DCGMNVIDIA's Data Center GPU Manager: the library and daemon that read a GPU's counters, run health checks and diagnostics, and feed dcgm-exporter.
MFU, HFUModel FLOPs Utilisation: the FLOPs the model needs per step (about 6 per parameter per token) divided by what the GPUs could do at their datasheet peak in that time. Hardware FLOPs Utilisation also counts recomputed work, so it is never lower. Section 5.
Goodput, badputThe share of time (or chip-time) that becomes kept training progress; badput is the rest, broken into named causes. Three published definitions in section 6.
Scheduling efficiencyGPU-hours allocated to jobs over GPU-hours the cluster could have given out. The rest is idle or fragmented. Section 10.
Showback, chargebackShowback reports each team's usage; chargeback bills it against a budget. Both need the scheduler's accounting and an agreed rate. Section 11.
Capacity planningChoosing how many GPUs to have, and whether to own or rent them, from the work you must do and how well you can keep them busy. Section 12.

3. GPU counters: what each one measures

The problem. "Is the GPU busy?" has at least five different answers, and the one every dashboard shows by default is the weakest. How it works: the driver keeps one coarse duty-cycle counter, and DCGM reads finer profiling counters from the chip's performance monitors. The definitions, quoted from NVIDIA's documentation (read 2026-10-16):

Three facts that change what you deploy
The default exporter does not ship SM activity. Its two lines are commented out in default-counters.csv (lines 105 and 106 at 4.8.4); you must enable them, and profiling fields need the exporter to run with SYS_ADMIN.
The profiling fields were renamed. In DCGM 4.6.1, 1002 is DCGM_FI_PROF_SM_UTIL_RATIO and 1004 DCGM_FI_PROF_TENSOR_UTIL_RATIO; the old names stay as aliases (dcgm_fields.h lines 3821 to 3825). Dashboards written for one name may need both.
They are sampled, and multiplexed. DCGM samples at 1 Hz by default (100 ms minimum); counters that cannot be read together are sampled in turns, and "collection at higher frequencies will result in zeroes returned". dcgm-exporter asks DCGM for a new value every 30 s by default (--collect-interval 30000). A 2-second stall between steps is invisible at that resolution; the training log sees it.

How to read them together. SM activity says how much of the chip has work; occupancy says how full each busy SM is (low is normal for large matrix multiplies); tensor activity says how much matmul work is done, which is the closest counter to MFU; DRAM activity says whether the phase is memory-bound. NVIDIA's own rule of thumb for SM activity: "A value of 0.8 or greater is necessary, but not sufficient, for effective use of the GPU. A value less than 0.5 likely indicates ineffective GPU usage." The Metrics explainer runs seven situations (healthy matmuls, too few blocks, launch gaps, an input stall, a memory-bound phase, an exposed all-reduce, a hung collective) through all the definitions at once, before and after each fix.

Predict: a kernel is launched with 26 thread blocks on a 132-SM GPU and runs for the whole one-second sample. What do GPU utilisation and SM activity read?
A kernel was executing the whole time, so the duty-cycle counter reads 100%. Only 26 of 132 SMs have a resident warp, so SM activity is 26/132 = 0.20. DCGM's documentation gives the same example with N/5 blocks. The opposite case, all SMs busy for a fifth of the window, also reads 0.2 SM activity, but 20% utilisation: SM activity alone cannot tell them apart; the pair can.

4. The gap between "utilisation" and real work

The problem. Three different numbers are all called GPU utilisation: the share of GPUs allocated to jobs, the share of time any kernel ran, and the share of SMs with work. Each is an upper bound on the next, and published fleets show how far apart they sit. Before the table, guess.

Predict: in the Shanghai AI Lab's two LLM-development clusters (4,704 A100s, March to August 2023), the median GPU utilisation (nvidia-smi's duty cycle) was 97% and 99%. What was the median DCGM SM activity on the same clusters?
"The median SM activity in both clusters is approximately 40%", against 97% and 99% median GPU utilisation (Hu et al., NSDI 2024, operator). The duty cycle sees that a kernel is running; it cannot see that most SMs are idle or waiting inside it.

The local clusters have no GPUs, so the device meter is the training pods' CPU time (cAdvisor) for what was measured, and the GPU counters derived from their definitions for what a GPU would show. What transfers. The CPU result is the reverse of the GPU one, and both come from how the collective waits. Gloo blocks in the kernel on a socket, so a waiting rank uses no CPU; NCCL keeps a kernel resident on the GPU, polling for data, so a waiting GPU reads 100% utilisation (NCCL's behaviour from its design; not run here). Either way, the device meter alone cannot tell a hang from work or from idleness. How to check it: put the step counter (meter 3) on the same dashboard as the device counters, and alert on it (section 9).

5. MFU and HFU in the training loop

The problem. The only counter of useful work is the job's own: tokens processed per second, turned into FLOPs and divided by the peak. How it works: model FLOPs per step = 6 x parameters x tokens in the step (plus an attention term at long context); MFU = model FLOPs per step / (step time x GPUs x peak FLOP/s). HFU uses the FLOPs the hardware actually ran, including activation recomputation, so it rises when you recompute and is not comparable across runs. The arithmetic, its conventions and published values are on Performance math; here is how to log it, done on the running example.

# in the training loop: time a step the way the hardware sees it
t0 = time.perf_counter()
loss = model(x).loss; loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()
torch.cuda.synchronize()        # on a GPU: or the clock stops before the kernels finish
dt = time.perf_counter() - t0
flops = 6 * n_params * tokens_per_step          # + 12 * layers * ctx * d_model * tokens at long context
mfu = flops / (dt * world_size * PEAK_FLOPS)    # PEAK: dense BF16 datasheet value, never the sparse one
log({"step": step, "tokens_per_s": tokens_per_step / dt, "mfu": mfu})

Measured here (src/scripts/oc_mfu.py, one process, one thread, in the mi-train:1 container capped at 1 CPU, 3 runs): the "peak" is a 1,024 x 1,024 fp32 matrix multiply on that core, because a container's CPU share has no datasheet. measured on Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM, shared machine.

What transfers: the method, and the fact that the convention changes the number (here attention adds about 15% to the FLOPs at context 64). What does not: the value; a 112,577-parameter model on one CPU core says nothing about an H100. Published values for real runs: Llama 3 405B 38% to 43% BF16 MFU (Llama 3, sec. 3.3.2, operator); ByteDance MegaScale 55.2% on 12,288 GPUs for a 175B model (MegaScale, operator of its own system).

Four ways to log a wrong MFU
Timing without synchronising (the CPU clock stops before the GPU finishes, MFU too high). Dividing by the 2:4-sparse peak (twice the dense one, MFU half what it should be). Counting padding tokens as tokens. Averaging over a window that includes evaluation, checkpoint saves or the first step's compilation, which then hides as "low MFU" instead of showing as badput.

Two findings while wiring it. What observability costs, sized

8. Logs and traces of a training job

The problem. Metrics are numbers on a fixed interval; when a 4,096-rank job dies, you need what each rank was doing and in which order. How it works on the running example: every rank writes one JSON object per line (ev, rank, step, loss, gloss, ts, rss_mb), rank 0 also writes checkpoints, and the launcher's environment goes in the first line. That is what the exporter on this page turned into metrics, and what let section 6 rebuild the badput.

  • Per-rank, structured, timestamped. Rank 0's log alone hides the straggler and the rank that crashed first; one line per rank per N steps is cheap (here 47 lines for rank 0 over 400 steps, checkpoints included).
  • Correlate by job and attempt. Put the scheduler's job id and the restart count in every line (the running example records SLURM_JOB_ID, JOB_COMPLETION_INDEX, TORCHELASTIC_RESTART_COUNT), so the logs of a requeued job do not mix with its earlier attempt.
  • Traces for the step itself come from profilers, not from the log: the PyTorch profiler or Nsight Systems for a few steps on demand (Profiling, benchmarking and correctness, Making training fast), and for collectives the NCCL flight recorder, which keeps the last collectives of each rank in memory and dumps them on a timeout (sibling page Launching distributed training).
  • Mind the volume. At 1,000 ranks, one line per step at 1 step per second is 86 million lines a day. Log every N steps, and keep per-step detail in metrics or a profiler window.

9. Alerting on hangs and stragglers

The problem. The defaults detect a hang late. A collective that never completes ends at the process-group timeout: 10 minutes for NCCL and 30 minutes for gloo in torch 2.14.1 (sibling page Fault tolerance at scale, from ProcessGroupNCCL.hpp and Backend.hpp). For all that time every GPU of the job is held. A straggler is never detected by default at all: the job just runs slower.

How it works: alert on the training job's own progress, and use device counters to find the cause. The rules that ran (src/scripts/prom/oc_rules.yml):

# a hang: no step logged for 20 s by a run that has not ended (scale 20 s to a few step times)
- alert: TrainingStalled
  expr: (time() - max by (system, run) (oc_train_last_step_timestamp_seconds)) > 20
        and on (system, run) oc_train_ended == 0
  for: 10s
# a straggler or slow node: under 60% of this run's own best rate in the last 3 minutes
- alert: TrainingSlowdown
  expr: oc:train_steps_per_second < 0.6 * max_over_time(oc:train_steps_per_second[3m])
        and on (system, run) oc_train_ended == 0
  for: 10s
# the node behind it (on GPUs: clock event reasons, Xid, NVLink errors)
- alert: PodThrottled
  expr: oc:pod_throttled_ratio > 0.5
  for: 10s
# quota held and nothing trains
- alert: GpusHeldNoProgress
  expr: sum(oc_kueue_usage{resource="example.com/gpu"}) > 0
        and on () (sum(oc:train_steps_per_second) or vector(0)) == 0
  for: 20s
Event (recorded)AlertSeconds after the eventDefault that would have acted

measured; from the ALERTS series at 5-second resolution. Time to page is at most threshold + for + scrape interval + evaluation interval (20 + 10 + 5 + 5 s here, 33 s measured).

The cost of the threshold. GpusHeldNoProgress went pending at the start of every job here: quota admitted, no step yet (startup took about 7 s). Its 20-second for: is what kept it from firing. On a real job startup takes minutes (image pull, data, compile, checkpoint load), so a progress alert needs a grace period after start, or it pages at every launch; and a stall threshold shorter than the longest legitimate pause (a checkpoint save, an evaluation) pages on healthy jobs. Set both from the job's own step and checkpoint times.

Stragglers. The slow-node test is worth replaying on the Cluster dashboard: after the pod holding ranks 2 and 3 was capped at 0.2 CPU, the healthy pod's CPU fell to the same 0.17 cores, because synchronous training makes everyone wait for the slowest. Utilisation could not name the slow node; the throttling counter of its cgroup could. On GPUs the equivalents are the clock event reasons, power and temperature per GPU, and per-rank timing of the same work: NVIDIA's NVRx straggler detector scores each rank's section and kernel times against the best rank (the sibling page Fault tolerance at scale replays it), and ByteDance's MegaScale reports finding about 0.5% straggler machines with CUDA-event timers (MegaScale sec. 5.1, operator).

10. Scheduling efficiency: the cluster's own loss

The problem. Before a job can waste a GPU-hour, the cluster has to hand it out. Scheduling efficiency is allocated GPU-hours over capacity; what is left is idle (nobody asked) or fragmented (somebody asked, but the free GPUs were the wrong shape). The parent's Scheduling lab and Cost lab measured backfill and gang-scheduling losses; this page measured the two kinds of fragmentation a dashboard has to catch.

Kueue: a resource other than GPUs binds

A ClusterQueue with 12 GPUs, 4 CPUs and 3 GiB; three jobs of 4 GPUs, 2 CPUs and 1,800 MiB. Memory admits one at a time: couldn't assign flavors to pod set node: insufficient unused quota for memory in flavor default-flavor, 528Mi more needed. GPU quota used: 4 of 12, the whole time. Admission waits . measured

Slurm: the free GPUs are the wrong shape

Two nodes each have 2 of 4 GPUs taken by a 2-node job, the third node is full. A 1-node 4-GPU job waits with reason Resources for 66 s while 4 GPUs are free. Allocated over total read 67%, which is what the community Slurm exporters report as "GPU utilization". measured

What to put on the dashboard: allocated over capacity per GPU type and partition; pending jobs by reason and their age (Kueue's kueue_pending_workloads and kueue_admission_wait_time_seconds, Slurm's pending reasons); the largest free block (GPUs free on one node, nodes free in one rack), because that is what decides whether the next big job can start; and an alert for work waiting while capacity is free (PendingWithFreeGpus fired for about a minute in the Kueue test, SlurmPendingWithFreeGpus for about a minute in the Slurm test). The fix is in the scheduler, not the dashboard: job shapes that tile the nodes (whole nodes for multi-node jobs), quotas that match the node shape, topology-aware placement, backfill with honest time limits (the sibling pages Slurm and Kubernetes for ML).

At scale, published: Meta's RSC clusters averaged 83% and 85% cluster utilisation (allocation) over 11 months; Google reports scheduling goodput above 95% for TPU jobs of all sizes thanks to preemption settings (both operator, section 4's table and section 6). A high allocation number is a good sign for the scheduler and says nothing about the jobs: Philly's GPUs were mostly allocated and their duty cycle averaged 52%.

11. Chargeback and quotas

The problem. A shared cluster is a budget split between teams; without a bill, the team that queues most wins. Showback reports each team's GPU-hours; chargeback bills them. Both are computed from the scheduler's accounting, and the main design choice is to charge for allocated GPU-hours, not used ones: allocation is what the others could not have, and it is the only number the scheduler knows exactly. Charging for SM activity would reward a team for shrinking its hang detection instead of its allocation.

How Slurm does it. Each partition has TRESBillingWeights; a job's billing is the weighted sum of what it was allocated, used for fairshare and for limits, and reported by sreport. The local cluster bills a GPU at 1.0 and a CPU at 0.25. Its accounting for the Slurm test, read with sacct -X -P -o JobID,User,Account,...,ElapsedRaw,AllocTRES:

JobUser (account)SecondsAllocTRESGPU-secondsBilling x seconds

Two details from the documentation explain the table. Billing "is stored as an integer and truncated if needed" (Slurm TRES): bob's 4 GPUs and 1 CPU should be 4.25, and sacct shows billing=4. And PriorityFlags=MAX_TRES_GRES sums billable GPUs but takes the maximum of the other resources, so a job cannot pay twice for CPUs and memory that come with its GPUs. The monthly chargeback query: sreport -t hours -T gres/gpu cluster AccountUtilizationByUser start=2026-09-01 end=2026-10-01 (composed from the documented options; sreport rolls usage up hourly, so it shows nothing for the last few minutes, as the sibling page Slurm found).

Kubernetes: quotas are Kueue ClusterQueues (nominal quota per flavor), budgets between teams are cohorts with borrowing and lending limits, and the bill is the Workload's admitted resources times its Admitted-to-Finished time, which no tool computes by default: export it (the sibling page Kubernetes for ML covers cohorts and fair sharing). Ray has no accounting of its own beyond its state API; on KubeRay the Kubernetes path applies.

12. Capacity planning, and owning against renting

The problem. How many GPUs does the lab need next year, and should it buy them? How it works, in three steps the Capacity planner computes:

  1. The run. GPUs to meet a deadline, with goodput falling as the job grows (more GPUs, more failures per hour). With the defaults (Llama 3.1 405B, 15.6T tokens, H100, 40% MFU, Llama 3's failure rate, 90 days) the answer is GPUs at goodput.
  2. The fleet. The year's demand: the main runs plus everything else (ablations, smaller models, fine-tunes, evaluation: an assumption, defaulted to half again), divided by 8,760 hours and a scheduling efficiency (85%, Meta's level). With one main run a year, the deadline sets the fleet, and the year's hours fill only of it: a lab sized for its biggest run has spare hours the rest of the year, which is why labs share clusters between pretraining and everything else.
  3. Own or rent. Owning one GPU costs its price (and network and storage on top) spread over its depreciation life, plus energy (kW x average draw x PUE x hours x price per kWh), plus staff and space. Defaults: $33,600 per H100 (Epoch AI, from a 2023 analyst figure), 5.5 years (Meta's server life in its 10-K for 2025), 10.2 kW per 8-GPU DGX H100 (NVIDIA), PUE 1.54 (Uptime Institute 2025 survey average; Google's fleet is 1.09), 9.77 cents per kWh (EIA, US industrial, July 2026). That is per GPU per calendar hour, against $5.97 for an AWS Capacity Block and $6.88 on demand (read 2026-10-16).

Reading it: owned and reserved GPUs cost the same every hour whether busy or not, so their cost per useful hour is the hourly cost divided by the useful share; on-demand costs nothing when idle but rarely offers a thousand interconnected GPUs at once. Owning beats on-demand list prices once more than of the hours are used, on these assumptions. The answer moves most with the purchase price, the depreciation life (Amazon shortened a subset of its servers from six to five years in 2025, citing "the increased pace of technology development, particularly in the area of artificial intelligence and machine learning"), and the rental price you can actually negotiate: Epoch AI estimates effective 2024 market rents of $1.50 to $2.50 per H100-hour (independent), which makes renting competitive with owning. derived

What the calculation leaves out
Owning also means a building with the power (a 16,384-GPU fleet at 1.275 kW per GPU is about 21 MW before cooling), lead times of months, cash up front, and the risk that the next GPU generation makes the old one worth less per FLOP before it is paid off. Renting means capacity you may not get when you need it. Large buyers negotiate prices that are not published on either side.

Common mistakes

Alerting on GPU utilisation
A hung NCCL collective reads 100%; a job with a fifth of its SMs busy reads 100%. Alert on the job's step counter and tokens per second; use DCGM's SM and tensor activity to explain, not to page.
Deploying dcgm-exporter with its defaults and expecting SM activity
# DCGM_FI_PROF_SM_ACTIVE, gauge, The ratio of cycles an SM has at least 1 warp assigned. is commented out in default-counters.csv at 4.8.4. Enable it (and occupancy) in your counters file, and run the exporter with the privileges profiling needs.
Looking for throttling in the wrong cgroup
The slow-node test's first rule read the container cgroup and returned 0 while the pod was throttled 70% of its periods: the cap was on the pod cgroup, and throttling is counted where the limit is hit. Measured here; check which cgroup (or, on GPUs, which clock reason) carries the limit before trusting a zero.
A progress alert without a start-up grace period
GpusHeldNoProgress went pending at the start of every job (admitted, no step yet) and was saved only by its 20-second for:. Real jobs take minutes to start: exclude the first steps or alert on "no step for N x the job's own median step time after the first step".
Expecting a gauge that is off by default
kueue_cluster_queue_resource_usage and kueue_cluster_queue_nominal_quota exist only with enableClusterQueueResources: true; the shipped config has it commented out. The same goes for Slurm: no metrics endpoint before 25.11.
Reading "GPU utilization" from a Slurm exporter as device utilisation
The community exporter's "GPU utilization" is "allocation ratio (alloc/total) per GPU type" (vpenso/prometheus-slurm-exporter README); it read 67% in the fragmentation test while a job waited and some allocated GPUs were idle.
Fragmentation hidden by GPU-only quotas
Kueue said insufficient unused quota for memory in flavor default-flavor, 528Mi more needed with 8 of 12 GPUs free. Size every quota resource to the job shape, or the cheapest resource decides how many GPUs you use.
Per-GPU series with per-job labels
Every job id on every GPU counter makes new series each run; over a 15-day retention that is the cardinality explosion the Observability page warns about. Put job identity on a few job-level series and join at query time.
Billing that silently truncates
Slurm stores billing as an integer: 4 GPUs and 1 CPU at weights 1.0 and 0.25 bill as 4, not 4.25. Use weights that give whole numbers for your job shapes, or bill from AllocTRES directly.

Interview questions

A 64-GPU job's dashboard shows 100% GPU utilisation everywhere, but no step was logged for 15 minutes. What is happening and how do you confirm it?
Most likely a hung collective: NCCL's kernel is resident and polling, which counts as a kernel executing. Confirm with SM and tensor activity (near zero), NVLink and network bytes (near zero), the step counter, then the stacks (py-spy) and the NCCL flight recorder to find the rank that never joined. Kill and drain that node rather than wait for the 10-minute NCCL timeout.
Why is SM activity a better meter than GPU utilisation, and why is it still not enough?
GPU utilisation is a duty cycle (any kernel running); SM activity averages over all SMs, so it sees a kernel that fills a fifth of the chip. But warps waiting on memory count as active, so a memory-bound job reads high SM activity with idle tensor cores. MFU from the training log is the measure of useful work; tensor activity is the nearest counter.
Define goodput. How do Google's and Meta's definitions differ?
The share of time that becomes kept training progress. Google multiplies scheduling goodput (all resources available), runtime goodput (checkpointed progress over allocated time) and program goodput (MFU). Meta's ETTR is productive runtime over available wall-clock time, including queueing, and leaves MFU out. Same idea, different boxes: always say which.
How do you find a straggler in a 2,048-GPU synchronous job?
Not with utilisation: everyone waits for the slowest, so all ranks look alike (measured here: the healthy pod dropped to the throttled pod's CPU). Time the same work per rank (CUDA events around forward and backward, as MegaScale and NVRx do) and compare each rank with the best; then look at that node's clock event reasons, power, temperature, Xid and link errors.
How big is the Prometheus for a 1,024-GPU cluster?
dcgm-exporter's 25 default series per GPU is about 25,600 series; container and node metrics add about 950 per node, so 128 nodes add about 122,000. At a 30-second scrape that is about 4,900 samples per second, and at 2 bytes per sample about 12.7 GB for 15 days. The section 7 calculator does it for any inputs. Then watch labels: per-job labels multiply it.
Charge teams for allocated or used GPU-hours?
Allocated. It is what the others could not use, the scheduler knows it exactly, and it gives teams a reason to release GPUs, fix hangs and size jobs well. Show utilisation next to it as a quality signal, not as the bill.
Design question: observability and cost controls for a 1,000-GPU training lab.
Meters: dcgm-exporter on every node with SM activity, tensor activity, clock reasons, Xid and NVLink errors enabled; node and container metrics; scheduler metrics (Kueue or Slurm 25.11's endpoint) with pending reasons and admission waits; every training job exporting step, tokens per second, MFU, last checkpoint and per-rank step time. Storage: Prometheus sharded or a long-term store, 30-second scrape for GPU counters, job labels on job-level series only. Alerts: no step for N step times after start-up, step rate below a fraction of the run's own best, a node with throttled clocks or Xid, GPUs admitted with no progress, work pending with GPUs free. Accounting: chargeback on allocated GPU-hours per team from sacct or Kueue Workloads, monthly; quotas per team with borrowing. Planning: quarterly forecast from the run plan (tokens, deadlines, failure rate) with the busy share of the fleet as the key efficiency number, and an own-versus-rent comparison redone whenever prices or depreciation assumptions change.
Your cluster is 95% allocated but throughput is down 30% this month. Where do you look?
Allocation is not work. Per job: tokens per second and MFU against last month; jobs holding GPUs with no steps (hangs); failure and restart counts (goodput); checkpoint stall time. Per node: low SM or tensor activity on a few GPUs (stragglers, throttling). Then the queue: did job shapes change and fragmentation rise?

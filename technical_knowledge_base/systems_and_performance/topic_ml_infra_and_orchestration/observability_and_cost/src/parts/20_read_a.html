The idea

A GPU-hour is paid for whether or not it trains, and each meter between the bill and the training log usually reads at most what the meter above it reads; so page and bill on the job's own progress and the scheduler's allocation, and use the device counters to explain the gap.

One GPU-hour, read by seven meters: each usually reads at most the one above it

Bar lengths are illustrative. The order is not a strict law: tensor activity and MFU measure differently (busy cycles against model FLOPs), so either can read higher; section 4 says why. each label on the right is a published or measured reading from a different fleet, so they are not one chain to multiply. The colour of the meter name says who reports it: the scheduler, the GPU, the training job. The famous gap is rungs 3 to 4: Shanghai AI Lab's LLM clusters read 97% and 99% median GPU utilisation and about 40% median SM activity on the same GPUs (section 3). The last rung is the Capacity planner's default run: 40% MFU times 85.5% goodput, so 34% of the paid peak becomes kept model FLOPs.

New terms

DCGM: NVIDIA's Data Center GPU Manager, the library and daemon that read a GPU's counters and feed dcgm-exporter.
GPU utilisation: the share of time any kernel was running (a duty cycle). The default meter, and the weakest.
SM activity: the share of time an SM had at least one warp resident, averaged over all SMs (132 on an H100 SXM).
Occupancy: resident warps over the 64 an SM can hold; low is normal for big matrix multiplies.
Tensor activity: the share of cycles the tensor (matrix) pipes were busy; the counter closest to MFU.
Goodput, badput: the share of allocated time that becomes progress saved in a checkpoint; badput is the rest, by cause.
Scheduling efficiency: GPU-hours allocated to jobs over GPU-hours the cluster had; the rest is idle or fragmented.
Showback, chargeback: showback reports each team's GPU-hours; chargeback bills them against a budget.

1. Three meters, and a hung job on all of them

Three kinds of meter read a training cluster. The scheduler's accounting (Slurm's sacct, Kueue's Workload conditions, Ray's state API) says who held which GPUs from when to when: it is the bill, and it cannot see whether anything ran. The device's counters (DCGM on NVIDIA GPUs: utilisation, SM and tensor activity, memory, power, errors) say how busy the chip was, not whether the work was useful. The training job's log (steps, tokens per second, loss, MFU, time since the last good checkpoint) is the only one that says whether the run advanced. Every failure on this page is one meter looking healthy while another does not.

Predict: one rank of a 4-rank job freezes inside a collective. Which meters notice?
Only the log. The job still holds all its GPUs, and a GPU waiting in an NCCL collective keeps a kernel resident, polling, which counts as a kernel running. Step through the recording below to see each meter.

The local clusters have no GPUs, so the device row is the pods' CPU time (cAdvisor, measured) plus what a GPU's utilisation would read by its definition (derived). The steps-per-second row is a 30-second rate window, so it takes 30 s to fall to zero after the freeze, which is why the hang alert in section 7 uses the time since the last step instead.

What it shows. The waiting ranks' CPU fell to 0.01 cores, the opposite of what a GPU reports in the same situation: gloo blocks on a socket and spins nothing, while NCCL keeps a kernel resident on the GPU polling for data, so a waiting GPU reads 100% utilisation (NCCL's behaviour by design; not run here). Either way the device meter cannot tell a hang from work or from idleness. The alert on the step counter fired 33 s after a rank froze; the process-group timeout (45 s here) ended the job 83 s after the freeze.

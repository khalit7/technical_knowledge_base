10. Scheduling efficiency: the cluster's own loss

The problem. Before a job can waste a GPU-hour, the cluster has to hand it out. Scheduling efficiency is allocated GPU-hours over capacity; what is left is idle (nobody asked) or fragmented (somebody asked, but the free GPUs were the wrong shape). The parent's {{Scheduling lab and Cost lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b5925bfd1d8665dd4b}} measured backfill and gang-scheduling losses; this page measured the two kinds of fragmentation a dashboard has to catch.

Two nodes each have 2 of 4 GPUs taken by a 2-node job, the third node is full. A 1-node 4-GPU job waits with reason Resources for 66 s while 4 GPUs are free. Allocated over total read 67%, which is what the community Slurm exporters report as "GPU utilization". measured

What to put on the dashboard: allocated over capacity per GPU type and partition; pending jobs by reason and their age (Kueue's kueue_pending_workloads and kueue_admission_wait_time_seconds, Slurm's pending reasons); the largest free block (GPUs free on one node, nodes free in one rack), because that is what decides whether the next big job can start; and an alert for work waiting while capacity is free (PendingWithFreeGpus fired for about a minute in the Kueue test, SlurmPendingWithFreeGpus for about a minute in the Slurm test). The fix is in the scheduler, not the dashboard: job shapes that tile the nodes (whole nodes for multi-node jobs), quotas that match the node shape, topology-aware placement, backfill with honest time limits (the sibling pages {{Slurm|n:3c65c17b0d0d81889c32cd050bbbadd3}} and {{Kubernetes for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cf9417de52108cfbce}}).

At scale, published: Meta's RSC clusters averaged 83% and 85% cluster utilisation (allocation) over 11 months; Google reports scheduling goodput above 95% for TPU jobs of all sizes thanks to preemption settings (both operator, section 4's table and section 6). A high allocation number is a good sign for the scheduler and says nothing about the jobs: Philly's GPUs were mostly allocated and their duty cycle averaged 52%.

11. Chargeback and quotas

The problem. A shared cluster is a budget split between teams; without a bill, the team that queues most wins. Showback reports each team's GPU-hours; chargeback bills them. Both are computed from the scheduler's accounting, and the main design choice is to charge for allocated GPU-hours, not used ones: allocation is what the others could not have, and it is the only number the scheduler knows exactly. Charging for SM activity would reward a team for shrinking its hang detection instead of its allocation.

How Slurm does it. Each partition has TRESBillingWeights; a job's billing is the weighted sum of what it was allocated, used for fairshare and for limits, and reported by sreport. The local cluster bills a GPU at 1.0 and a CPU at 0.25. Its accounting for the Slurm test, read with sacct -X -P -o JobID,User,Account,...,ElapsedRaw,AllocTRES:

JobUser (account)SecondsAllocTRESGPU-secondsBilling x seconds

Two details from the documentation explain the table. Billing "is stored as an integer and truncated if needed" ({{Slurm TRES|https://slurm.schedmd.com/tres.html}}): bob's 4 GPUs and 1 CPU should be 4.25, and sacct shows billing=4. And PriorityFlags=MAX_TRES_GRES sums billable GPUs but takes the maximum of the other resources, so a job cannot pay twice for CPUs and memory that come with its GPUs. The monthly chargeback query: sreport -t hours -T gres/gpu cluster AccountUtilizationByUser start=2026-09-01 end=2026-10-01 (composed from the documented options; sreport rolls usage up hourly, so it shows nothing for the last few minutes, as the sibling page {{Slurm|n:3c65c17b0d0d81889c32cd050bbbadd3}} found).

Kubernetes: quotas are Kueue ClusterQueues (nominal quota per flavor), budgets between teams are cohorts with borrowing and lending limits, and the bill is the Workload's admitted resources times its Admitted-to-Finished time, which no tool computes by default: export it (the sibling page {{Kubernetes for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cf9417de52108cfbce}} covers cohorts and fair sharing). Ray has no accounting of its own beyond its state API; on KubeRay the Kubernetes path applies.

12. Capacity planning, and owning against renting

The problem. How many GPUs does the lab need next year, and should it buy them? How it works, in three steps the {{Capacity planner|#t-cap}} computes:

  1. The run. GPUs to meet a deadline, with goodput falling as the job grows (more GPUs, more failures per hour). With the defaults (Llama 3.1 405B, 15.6T tokens, H100, 40% MFU, Llama 3's failure rate, 90 days) the answer is GPUs at goodput.
  2. The fleet. The year's demand: the main runs plus everything else (ablations, smaller models, fine-tunes, evaluation: an assumption, defaulted to half again), divided by 8,760 hours and a scheduling efficiency (85%, Meta's level). With one main run a year, the deadline sets the fleet, and the year's hours fill only of it: a lab sized for its biggest run has spare hours the rest of the year, which is why labs share clusters between pretraining and everything else.
  3. Own or rent. Owning one GPU costs its price (and network and storage on top) spread over its depreciation life, plus energy (kW x average draw x PUE x hours x price per kWh), plus staff and space. Defaults: $33,600 per H100 (Epoch AI, from a 2023 analyst figure), 5.5 years (Meta's server life in its 10-K for 2025), 10.2 kW per 8-GPU DGX H100 (NVIDIA), PUE 1.54 (Uptime Institute 2025 survey average; Google's fleet is 1.09), 9.77 cents per kWh (EIA, US industrial, July 2026). That is per GPU per calendar hour, against $5.97 for an AWS Capacity Block and $6.88 on demand (read 2026-10-16).

Reading it: owned and reserved GPUs cost the same every hour whether busy or not, so their cost per useful hour is the hourly cost divided by the useful share; on-demand costs nothing when idle but rarely offers a thousand interconnected GPUs at once. Owning beats on-demand list prices once more than of the hours are used, on these assumptions. The answer moves most with the purchase price, the depreciation life (Amazon shortened a subset of its servers from six to five years in 2025, citing "the increased pace of technology development, particularly in the area of artificial intelligence and machine learning"), and the rental price you can actually negotiate: Epoch AI estimates effective 2024 market rents of $1.50 to $2.50 per H100-hour (independent), which makes renting competitive with owning. derived

What the calculation leaves out
Owning also means a building with the power (a 16,384-GPU fleet at 1.275 kW per GPU is about 21 MW before cooling), lead times of months, cash up front, and the risk that the next GPU generation makes the old one worth less per FLOP before it is paid off. Renting means capacity you may not get when you need it. Large buyers negotiate prices that are not published on either side.

4. MFU: the job's own meter of useful work

The only counter of useful work is the job's: tokens per second, turned into FLOPs and divided by the peak. MFU = 6 x parameters x tokens per step / (step time x GPUs x peak FLOP/s), plus an attention term at long context; HFU counts the FLOPs the hardware actually ran, recomputation included, so it rises when you recompute and is not comparable across runs. The arithmetic, its conventions and published values are on {{Performance math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81faad1ef390b0e54d08}}; what belongs here is logging it in the loop:

# in the training loop: time a step the way the hardware sees it
t0 = time.perf_counter()
loss = model(x).loss; loss.backward(); opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()
torch.cuda.synchronize()        # on a GPU: or the clock stops before the kernels finish
dt = time.perf_counter() - t0
flops = 6 * n_params * tokens_per_step          # + 12 * layers * ctx * d_model * tokens at long context
mfu = flops / (dt * world_size * PEAK_FLOPS)    # PEAK: dense BF16 datasheet value, never the sparse one
log({"step": step, "tokens_per_s": tokens_per_step / dt, "mfu": mfu})
Four ways to log a wrong MFU
Timing without synchronising (the CPU clock stops before the GPU finishes: MFU too high). Dividing by the 2:4-sparse peak (twice the dense one: MFU half what it should be). Counting padding tokens as tokens. Averaging over a window that includes evaluation, checkpoint saves or the first step's compilation, which then hides as "low MFU" instead of showing as badput.

Tensor activity is the counter closest to MFU, not a bound on it. It counts the cycles the tensor pipes were busy, whatever the work; MFU counts the model's FLOPs against one chosen peak. Recomputed activations keep the pipes busy but are not model FLOPs, so they raise tensor activity and not MFU; a busy pipe is not a pipe at its peak, so tensor activity usually reads above MFU; and a busy cycle does about twice the work at FP8 that it does at BF16, so a run doing FP8 matrix multiplies but quoting MFU against the BF16 peak can read MFU above tensor activity. Read them side by side, not as one chain.

Published values for real runs: Llama 3 405B 38% to 43% BF16 MFU ({{Llama 3, sec. 3.3.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}, operator); ByteDance MegaScale 55.2% on 12,288 GPUs for a 175B model ({{MegaScale|https://arxiv.org/html/2402.15627v1}}, operator of its own system).

The same logging run on the running example (one CPU core)

src/scripts/oc_mfu.py, one process, one thread, in the mi-train:1 container capped at 1 CPU, 3 runs. The "peak" is a 1,024 x 1,024 fp32 matrix multiply on that core, because a container's CPU share has no datasheet. The ceiling came out at 87.0, 83.6 and 87.8 GFLOP/s; the model's step took a median 46.1 ms for 2,048 tokens (44,408 tokens/s), so MFU against that ceiling was 34.4%, 36.5% and 34.2% by the 6N convention and 39.5%, 41.8% and 39.1% with the attention term. measured on Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM, shared machine.

What transfers: the method, and the fact that the convention changes the number (here attention adds about 15% to the FLOPs at context 64). What does not: the value; a 112,577-parameter model on one CPU core says nothing about an H100.

5. Goodput: metering the time that was kept

MFU measures the steps that ran; goodput measures how much of the allocated time became progress you kept. Google's version multiplies scheduling goodput, runtime goodput and program goodput (MFU); Meta's ETTR leaves MFU out and counts queueing. The definitions, the badput split per failure and which recovery design removes which part are owned by {{Fault tolerance at scale|n:3fc5c17b0d0d815786d1c1dddb311a9b}} (section 9, "Goodput: name every lost second", and section 5, "Recovery designs"). This section is the metering side: what to record so the number can be computed. Here it is from two recorded runs, using Kueue's Workload conditions (Admitted, Finished) and rank 0's log; runtime goodput is seconds of progress saved in a checkpoint over seconds allocated.

The hung run held its GPUs for 108 s and kept 13 s of it: runtime goodput 84% healthy, 12% hung

measured, seconds of allocation, 4 fake GPUs; Kueue's timestamps have one-second resolution. Healthy, 400 steps: allocated 74 s, start-up 7.6 s, progress kept 62.0 s, after the last step 4.5 s. Hung at step 150: allocated 108 s, start-up 7.1 s, progress kept 13.0 s (checkpoint at step 100), progress lost 6.4 s (steps 100 to 150), and 81.6 s after the freeze: the hang. Scheduling goodput is 100% for both (admitted the moment they were submitted); program goodput cannot be measured without a GPU.

How to record it: log the badput categories directly instead of inferring them afterwards, as Google's open-source {{ml-goodput-measurement|https://github.com/AI-Hypercomputer/ml-goodput-measurement}} library does (device initialisation, program start-up, data loading, checkpoint save and restore, wasted progress from disruptions): a timestamped event per phase, per attempt, plus the step of the last saved checkpoint.

Published goodput numbers, and why one point is worth a lot

Google's paper ({{ML Fleet Efficiency, arXiv 2502.06982|https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06982}}, operator) reports scheduling goodput above 95% for all job sizes; Meta ({{Kokolis et al., HPCA 2025|https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21680}}, operator) reports ETTR 0.85 to 0.9 for its largest jobs; the Llama 3 report calls its 405B run's "effective training time" "higher than 90%" without defining it further. Google's own example of the stakes: "a 1% improvement in ML Goodput can translate to more than a million dollars in cost savings" for 20,000 GPUs over 8 weeks ({{Google Cloud blog, 2024-04-10|https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/goodput-metric-as-measure-of-ml-productivity}}, vendor).

6. Prometheus for a GPU cluster: exporters, dashboards, and what they cost

Every node runs exporters; one Prometheus (or a few, sharded) scrapes them on an interval, evaluates recording and alerting rules and sends firing alerts to Alertmanager; Grafana draws dashboards from its queries. PromQL, storage, histograms and cardinality are taught on {{Observability|n:3ef5c17b0d0d811e8490c54dd42c2b9b}}; what is specific to a training cluster is which exporters and which labels:

Two dashboards. A node dashboard, per GPU: SM and tensor activity, DRAM activity, power, temperature, clock event reasons, Xid and remapped rows, NVLink errors and bytes. Two findings while wiring it. Kueue's per-ClusterQueue usage and quota gauges (kueue_cluster_queue_resource_usage, ..._nominal_quota) were absent: they need enableClusterQueueResources: true, which is commented out in the shipped configuration (the same pattern as SM activity in dcgm-exporter; Slurm has no endpoint at all before 25.11); LocalQueue usage and pending counts were on, and the exporter read the ClusterQueue's .status.flavorsUsage instead. And the endpoints are mostly not about your jobs: Kueue's served 2,516 samples per scrape, 299 of them kueue_*; an idle kind worker's cAdvisor about 950.

What the metrics system costs

Prometheus's own capacity rule: "Prometheus stores an average of only 1-2 bytes per sample", and disk = retention x samples per second x bytes per sample ({{Prometheus storage docs|https://prometheus.io/docs/prometheus/latest/storage/}}; default retention 15 days, default scrape 1 minute). The whole local setup held about 1,500 series.

Predict: on a 1,024-GPU cluster with dcgm-exporter's defaults, which dominates the series count: the GPU counters or the per-node container metrics?
The container metrics: 25 series per GPU is 25,600 for the GPUs, while about 950 per node over 128 nodes is about 122,000. The calculator's defaults are exactly this case.

At the defaults that is about 4,900 samples per second and about 12.7 GB for 15 days at 2 bytes per sample. Every label added to a GPU series (job name, user, pod) multiplies the series by the number of values it takes over the retention window: keep job identity on a handful of job-level series and join at query time, not on every per-GPU counter.

Logs: per rank, with the attempt

Metrics are numbers on a fixed interval; when a 4,096-rank job dies you need what each rank was doing and in which order. So every rank writes structured lines with the scheduler's job id and the restart count, every N steps (one line per step at 1 step per second on 1,000 ranks is 86 million lines a day). Traces of the step itself come from profilers (PyTorch profiler or Nsight Systems for a few steps on demand: {{Profiling, benchmarking and correctness|n:3c65c17b0d0d813da895ffee55b0457c}}, {{Making training fast|n:3c65c17b0d0d810d9e1fdf9ec942c20d}}), and for collectives from the NCCL flight recorder, which keeps each rank's last collectives and dumps them on a timeout ({{Distributed PyTorch|n:3c65c17b0d0d815e85aff8decd8f35ab}}, section 11; using it on a real hang is {{Launching distributed training|n:3fc5c17b0d0d81b3a8a5c58224bff9fa}}, section 8).

What the running example logs

One JSON object per line per rank (ev, rank, step, loss, gloss, ts, rss_mb), with the launcher's environment in the first line; that is what this page's exporter turned into metrics and what rebuilt the badput above. Rank 0's log alone hides the straggler and the rank that crashed first; one line per rank per N steps is cheap (here 47 lines for rank 0 over 400 steps, checkpoints included). The job id and restart count come from SLURM_JOB_ID, JOB_COMPLETION_INDEX and TORCHELASTIC_RESTART_COUNT, so a requeued job's logs do not mix with its earlier attempt.

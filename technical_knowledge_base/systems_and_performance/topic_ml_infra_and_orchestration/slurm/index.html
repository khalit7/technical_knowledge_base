Slurm in one screen

Slurm is the program that owns a cluster's machines and hands them out. You describe what your job needs (2 nodes, 4 GPUs each, 10 hours); Slurm queues the request, decides when it may run and on which nodes, starts it there, watches it, kills it at its time limit and records what it used. Most large GPU training clusters run it: the root page's Tool map has the dated evidence of who uses what.

Four ideas carry the whole page. Read them once now; every section below is one of them in detail.

1. One controller decides

A single daemon, slurmctld, holds every job and node in memory and makes every scheduling decision. Each compute node runs slurmd, which only does what the controller says. There is no negotiation between nodes.

2. A job is an allocation

A job is a reservation of resources (nodes, CPUs, memory, GPUs) for a time limit. It gets all of them at once or stays pending, so a multi-node job never starts with half its nodes: gang scheduling comes free.

3. Steps run inside it

Inside the allocation, srun launches job steps: the processes that do the work, one per task. A job can run many steps, one after another or side by side.

4. Policy is a priority number

Who goes first is one integer per job, a weighted sum of factors (fairshare, age, size, partition, QOS). Limits (QOS, accounts, partitions) can hold a job whatever its priority, and preemption can take nodes back.

1. The daemons: who does what

The problem. Hundreds or thousands of machines, many users, and one rule: no GPU may be given to two jobs at once. Slurm's answer is a single brain. Every decision is made in one process that holds the whole cluster in memory, and the machines are its hands.

In 24.11 that restarts the controller in place; its own log says so (measured, 05:36:39 to 05:36:40 UTC): Reconfigure signal (SIGHUP) received, Saving all slurm state, slurmctld version 24.11.5 started on cluster mi, Recovered state of 3 nodes, Relinquishing control to new slurmctld process. Why it matters. Declaring less than the hardware is safe (some is left for the OS). Declaring more is not: a node that registers with fewer CPUs, less memory or less temporary disk than declared is taken out of service "to avoid scheduling jobs on them" (slurm.conf, node configuration; the state shows as INVAL, an invalid registration). This cluster hit it while being built: with no device files for its fake GPUs a node went INVAL with gres/gpu count reported lower than configured (0 < 4) (root page, src/data/README.md). So a typo in slurm.conf takes nodes out of service, and production sites declare a little less memory than slurmd -C reports.

Predict: the controller crashes on day 2 of a 3-day training job and is restarted an hour later. What happened to the job?
It kept training. The processes run under slurmstepd on the compute nodes and do not need the controller while they run; the controller's state (jobs, allocations) is on disk in StateSaveLocation, and on restart it logs Recovered state of N nodes and Recovered information about N jobs, as it did here at every reconfigure. What stops for that hour is everything new: submissions, starts, and steps (srun must ask the controller for each step, so a script that launches a new step every epoch would stall).

2. The life of a job, from the controller's own log

The problem. "My job is not running" can mean a dozen things: it is waiting its turn, waiting for nodes, waiting for a limit, its nodes are booting, its prolog is running, its first step has not launched, it finished and is cleaning up. Each is a different state in Slurm, and knowing them tells you where to look.

Job states

The states squeue and sacct print (short codes in brackets). Click one. The main line is what the running example did here; the side exits are the ways a job ends badly.

Watching it happen. The running example was submitted while another job (bob's, 12 GPUs, time limit 1 minute, sleeping 25 s) held every GPU, with the controller at debug log level and the Backfill debug flag on, a prolog and epilog that log a line on each node, and squeue polled every second measured. Read it as the job's life:

  1. Submit. sbatch sends the script and its options to the controller, which validates them against partitions, limits and node configurations (a request no node could ever satisfy is refused here, with Requested node configuration is not available), gives the job an id and an initial priority, and returns. The script is stored by the controller; editing the file afterwards changes nothing.
  2. Pending. The job waits with a reason, here Resources: it is first in line and the resources are busy. The backfill scheduler planned a start at 05:37:53, which is the blocker's time limit (started 05:36:53, limit 1 minute), not its real end: Slurm plans with limits because it cannot know runtimes.
  3. Allocate. The blocker actually ended at 05:37:18.890. Ending a job is an event that triggers the main scheduler, which allocated c1 and c2 to job 79 22 ms later, 34 s before the plan. A job's planned start is an upper bound that usually moves earlier.
  4. Prolog. With PrologFlags=Alloc, slurmd runs the site's prolog as root on every node of the allocation before anything else (5 ms later here). Sites check GPU health here; a prolog that exits non-zero drains the node and requeues the job.
  5. Batch step. The script runs on the first node of the allocation (c1) as the user. This is step 79.batch.
  6. Job steps. Each srun inside the script asks the controller for a step; slurmd on each node starts a slurmstepd, which starts the tasks. The running example's one srun started one torchrun per node: step 79.0.
  7. Completing. When the script exits, the job is COMPLETING while every node kills leftovers and runs the epilog; only then are the nodes free for the next job.
  8. Completed, and recorded. The accounting record goes to slurmdbd; sacct shows the job and each step:
$ sacct -j 79 --format=JobID,JobName,State,Start,End,Elapsed,NodeList,AllocTRES

Where the 10 seconds went. Of the job's 10 s of elapsed time, the training loop itself ran about 6.5 s (the ranks' own wall_s); the rest is the chain every job pays: prolog, starting the batch shell, the step request, torchrun and Python imports, rendezvous, and the epilog. On a real cluster the prolog (GPU health checks), image pulls and the rendezvous of hundreds of nodes make this chain minutes long, and it is paid again on every restart. The root's Launch lab splits it by stage. measured on Apple M1 Pro, Docker Desktop VM, shared machine

Predict: squeue --start said job 79 would start at 05:37:53. Why did it start at 05:37:18?
The plan uses time limits; the blocker asked for 1 minute and used 25 s. When it ended, _job_complete was followed 21 ms later by Running job scheduler for default depth and the allocation: the main scheduler runs on events (a job submitted or ending, a node coming back) and also periodically, so it does not wait for the next backfill cycle. Nothing was preempted: both jobs were in partition batch, and preemption here works only across partition tiers (section 9).

3. Resources: what a job can ask for, and what it gets

The problem. A GPU node is not a box of GPUs: it is CPUs, memory and GPUs that must be handed out together, and a job that takes all the CPUs of a node leaves its GPUs unusable by anyone. Slurm tracks every kind of thing it hands out as a TRES (trackable resource): cpu, mem, node, gres/gpu (GRES, "generic resources", is how Slurm names devices such as GPUs), and billing, a weighted sum used for fairshare.

How a request becomes an allocation

The selection plugin select/cons_tres (the default) allocates individual cores, memory and GPUs, so several jobs can share a node. SelectTypeParameters says what is consumable: here CR_Core (cores only); production GPU clusters use CR_Core_Memory so memory is counted too. The request options combine per job, per node or per task:

Predict: the running example asks --nodes=2 --ntasks-per-node=1 --cpus-per-task=2 --gres=gpu:2, and this cluster bills a GPU 1.0 and a CPU 0.25. What does sacct show as its AllocTRES?
billing=5,cpu=4,gres/gpu=4,node=2 (section 2's sacct): --gres and --ntasks-per-node are per node, so 2 nodes give 2 x 2 CPUs and 2 x 2 GPUs; billing is 4 x 1.0 + 4 x 0.25 = 5. The batch step (79.batch) shows only the first node's share, cpu=2,gres/gpu=2.
Stranded GPUs: a request that wastes a node
The root's Scheduling lab hit this on this cluster: sbatch --gpus=6 --cpus-per-gpu=1 on two nodes of 4 CPUs and 4 GPUs got NumCPUs=8, every CPU of both nodes (one task per node takes all its CPUs), so the 2 GPUs left on the second node could not be used by anyone. Writing it as tasks (--ntasks=6 --gpus-per-task=1 --cpus-per-task=1) gave 6 CPUs. The same happens with memory under CR_Core_Memory: a job without --mem on a cluster whose default is the whole node's memory takes all of it. Check AllocTRES in scontrol show job and scontrol show node after submitting anything new.

Which GPU is mine? Binding and cgroups

Counting GPUs is half the job; the other half is making sure a process uses only its own. On a real node gres.conf maps each GPU to its device file (Name=gpu File=/dev/nvidia[0-7], or AutoDetect=nvml to ask the NVIDIA driver), and for every job and step Slurm sets CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES to the GPUs it was given. With cgroups (TaskPlugin=task/cgroup, ProctrackType=proctrack/cgroup and in cgroup.conf ConstrainCores, ConstrainRAMSpace, ConstrainDevices, each "no" by default, cgroup.conf), the kernel enforces it: the job's processes cannot see other GPUs, cannot run on other cores, and are killed when they exceed their memory (state OUT_OF_MEMORY). Cgroups are the Linux kernel's resource groups; Containers and isolation (Topic: operating-systems) owns how they work.

This lab cluster has neither: its GPUs are counted placeholders and its cgroups are off (inside a container without systemd, slurmd 24.11 stops with fatal: systemd scope for slurmstepd could not be set, root setup). What a task sees, from a step of four tasks measured:

$ srun -l bash -c 'echo rank=$SLURM_PROCID local=$SLURM_LOCALID node=$(hostname) gpus_on_node=$SLURM_GPUS_ON_NODE cuda=${CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES:-unset}'
0: rank=0 local=0 node=c1 gpus_on_node=2 cuda=unset
1: rank=1 local=1 node=c1 gpus_on_node=2 cuda=unset
2: rank=2 local=0 node=c2 gpus_on_node=2 cuda=unset
3: rank=3 local=1 node=c2 gpus_on_node=2 cuda=unset

On a real GPU node cuda= would print the GPU indices; here there are no devices to name. SLURM_PROCID is the global rank, SLURM_LOCALID the rank on the node (which picks the GPU when srun launches one task per GPU).

Topology. Where the nodes sit matters for all-reduce speed: topology/tree (switches) and topology/block (blocks of nodes such as NVL72 racks; both plugins are in 24.11, and 25.05 added several topologies in one topology.yaml, topology docs) let Slurm keep a job inside one switch or block, and --switches=1@01:00:00 asks to wait up to an hour for that. This cluster has no switches to model; the sibling page Cluster anatomy covers the networks.

4. Job steps: the processes inside an allocation

The problem. An allocation is just a reservation; something has to start the processes on every node, give each its rank, and know when they all ended. That is srun. Each srun in a script is one job step: it asks the controller for a slice of the allocation, slurmd on each node starts a slurmstepd, and that starts the tasks and collects their exit codes. A job can run steps one after another (preprocess, train, evaluate) or side by side (a sweep of small runs inside one big allocation).

The trap. By default a step may use every CPU of the job on each of its nodes ("By default, all non-GRES resources on each node in the step allocation will be used", srun --exact), so steps you meant to run side by side queue behind each other. --exact gives a step only what it asks for. Here are the same four one-task steps, launched together inside the same 2-node allocation, without and with it:

Four steps side by side, without and with --exact measured

Each bar is a step as sacct recorded it (start, end, node, CPUs and GPUs it held). The allocation holds 2 CPUs and 2 GPUs on each of c1 and c2. Jobs 129 (without) and 126 (with), 16 Oct 2026; one-second resolution.

Without --exact each step took both CPUs of its node, so the third and fourth waited 6 s for the first two (srun says only srun: Step created for StepId=129.4 when it finally gets in). With it each took one CPU and all four ran at once. A subtlety measured here: a job submitted with --cpus-per-task exports SLURM_CPUS_PER_TASK, and srun treats that as an explicit -c, which implies --exact (srun's -c entry: "Explicitly requesting this option implies --exact"): the same steps without --exact inside a job with --cpus-per-task=1 (job 130) ran side by side with 1 CPU each. So the behaviour of a step depends on how its job was submitted: write --exact when you mean it.

Overlap. Steps never share CPUs unless asked: srun --overlap lets a step run on resources another step holds, which is how you attach a debugger or py-spy to a running training step (srun --overlap --jobid=N --pty bash). For training: the usual layouts are one step that starts everything (one torchrun per node, as the running example does, or one task per GPU with ranks from SLURM_PROCID); the sibling page Launching distributed training compares them, and Distributed PyTorch owns torchrun at the PyTorch level.

5. Partitions, QOS and associations: who may use what

The problem. A shared cluster needs rules beyond "first come, first served": one team may not take every GPU, debug jobs must start fast, a huge job may run only at night. Slurm has three places to put rules, and a pending job's reason names the one holding it.

Partition

A named set of nodes (a queue) with its own rules: MaxTime, MaxNodes, default time, who may submit, PriorityJobFactor (a priority bonus) and PriorityTier (an order between partitions, used for preemption). Partitions may overlap: here batch, urgent and this page's sllow all cover c[1-3]. In slurm.conf.

Association

A (cluster, account, user) triple in the accounting database: alice in teama, bob in teamb. Accounts form a tree (root, teama, teamb), each with fairshare shares and optional limits such as GrpTRES=gres/gpu=64 (the whole team at once) or MaxJobs. In slurmdbd, set with sacctmgr.

QOS

"Quality of service": a named bundle of a priority, limits (MaxTRESPerUser, MaxWall, GrpTRES) and preemption rules that a job selects with --qos. Typical: debug (high priority, 1 node, 30 minutes), normal, scavenger (lowest priority, preemptible). In slurmdbd.

When several define the same limit, the first one found wins, in this order: partition QOS, job QOS, user association, account associations up the tree, root, partition (resource limits). And none of it applies unless AccountingStorageEnforce includes limits and qos: the default is none, and this lab cluster runs with none, so for this page's runs it was switched on, with two QOS and one limit measured:

$ sacctmgr -i add qos sl-high Priority=100
$ sacctmgr -i add qos sl-small Priority=20 MaxTRESPerUser=gres/gpu=4
$ sacctmgr -i modify user where name=alice,bob,carol set qos+=sl-high,sl-small
$ sacctmgr -i modify user where name=carol set GrpTRES=gres/gpu=2

6. The scheduler: a main loop and a backfill loop


The problem. Thousands of pending jobs, a cluster that changes every second. Testing every job against every node on every change would be too slow, and starting jobs in strict priority order wastes nodes that a big job at the head cannot use yet. Slurm runs two loops inside the controller.




The main loop (fast, strict)
Runs on every event (a job submitted or ending, a node returning) and every sched_interval (60 s by default, ). It walks the pending jobs in priority order and starts each one that fits now. On an event it tests at most default_queue_depth jobs (100 by default). And the moment one job of a partition cannot start, it starts nothing else in that partition or on its nodes in that pass: strict priority order.


The backfill loop (slower, planning)
Every bf_interval (30 s by default, 2 s here) it builds a timeline from every running job's time limit, gives each waiting job, in priority order, the earliest start where it fits (a reservation), and starts any lower-priority job that fits in a gap and ends before the reservations it would cross. Slurm's version reserves for every job it tests (conservative) and reserves whole nodes.




Source: the main loop stops a partition at its first blocked job


and on an event it tests a limited depth


That rule is where the two most common reasons come from: the first blocked job of a partition shows Resources, and every job behind it shows Priority ("a job ahead of me is waiting"). Backfill is the only way a job jumps that line, and it needs your time limit to do it. The root's Scheduling lab runs the same eight-job queue under FIFO and backfill on this cluster and shows where Slurm's whole-node reservations depart from the textbook; this page does not repeat it.

7. Priority: one number per job


The problem. The main loop walks jobs in priority order, so the whole policy of who goes first reduces to how that number is computed. With PriorityType=priority/multifactor it is a weighted sum of factors, each between 0 and 1:


priority = Wage·age + Wfs·fairshare + Wsize·jobsize + Wpart·partition + Wqos·qos + Σ Wtres·tres − nice




age
time eligible (since AccrueTime) over PriorityMaxAge, capped at 1. A job held by a dependency or a begin time does not accrue: in the gallery, jobs 84 and 85 showed AccrueTime=Unknown.


fairshare
with Fair Tree (the default), the user's rank in the account tree divided by the number of users: heavy recent use, decayed with PriorityDecayHalfLife, means a low factor. The root's Scheduling lab derives it with a calculator.


jobsize
by default favours large jobs: (nodes / cluster nodes + CPUs / cluster CPUs) / 2.


partition
the partition's PriorityJobFactor over the largest one.


qos
the QOS's priority over the largest QOS priority.


tres
each TRES the job asks for over the partition's total, with its own weight (PriorityWeightTRES=GRES/gpu=500).


nice
subtracted as is (--nice=1500); users may only lower their own priority.




Source: the sum, then truncation to an integer, and 0 reserved for held jobs


The other factors are computed in set_priority_factors() in the same file: age , job size , partition , QOS , TRES . Defaults: PriorityDecayHalfLife 7 days and PriorityCalcPeriod 5 minutes (); this cluster uses 10 minutes and 1 minute so a lab can watch them move.


Measured. For the gallery run the weights were set to age 1,000, fairshare 10,000, job size 1,000, partition 1,000, QOS 2,000 and GPU TRES 500 (the cluster's normal configuration uses only fairshare and age; this page's variant is src/scripts/sl_slurm.conf). Second, the result is truncated: job 82's parts were 86.67 + 5,000 + 500 + 1,000 + 333.33 = 6,920.0, printed 6,920, while sprio -l rounds each part (87, 333) so the printed parts do not always add up to the total.







8. Preemption: taking nodes back


The problem. The cluster is full of long training jobs and an urgent job arrives: an evaluation before a deadline, or a team whose guaranteed share is being used by others. Waiting days is not acceptable. Preemption lets the scheduler stop running jobs to make room.


SettingChoices


PreemptType: who may preempt whompreempt/partition_prio: a job in a partition with a higher PriorityTier preempts jobs of lower tiers (this cluster: urgent tier 10 over batch tier 1). preempt/qos: each QOS lists the QOS it may preempt (sacctmgr modify qos high set preempt=scavenger). preempt/none.


PreemptMode: what happens to the victimCANCEL (killed), REQUEUE (killed and put back in the queue, needs --requeue; otherwise cancelled), SUSPEND (paused in memory, resumed later; needs GANG at cluster level), GANG (time slicing between jobs on the same resources), OFF (preemption docs).


GraceTimethe victim is sent SIGCONT and SIGTERM at once and SIGKILL only after GraceTime (default 0), which is the window to write a checkpoint.




Source: in partition_prio, a tier strictly higher is the test 


The root's Scheduling lab preempted bob's 12-GPU batch job with a 4-GPU urgent job on this cluster: preempted JobId=39 has been requeued to reclaim resources for JobId=40, the urgent job was allocated 0.6 s after it was submitted, and the victim could not restart for 121 s (a requeued job's begin time is pushed past the job credential's lifetime, 120 s by default, plus 1). For training, REQUEUE without a checkpoint loses everything since the last save; GPU-hours lost per preemption are the time since the last checkpoint times the GPUs, the trade-off the root's Failure lab turns into a checkpoint interval.


Checkpoint before the limit: --signal


The same machinery serves time limits. --signal=B:USR1@25 asks Slurm to send SIGUSR1 to the batch shell 25 s before the time limit; the script traps it, saves, and requeues itself, and the next start resumes. A real run measured:

13. Slurm on Kubernetes described, not run here


The problem. Many organisations want one pool of GPUs for both batch training (which researchers like to drive with sbatch, fairshare and backfill) and long-running services (inference, notebooks, pipelines) that Kubernetes runs well. Splitting the GPUs between two clusters leaves one idle while the other queues. Two projects run Slurm inside Kubernetes:


    

  • Slinky (SchedMD's own): a Kubernetes operator that "contains a Kubernetes operator to deploy and manage certain components of Slurm clusters". Workers are NodeSets, "A set of homogeneous Slurm workers (compute nodes)", run as a StatefulSet or a DaemonSet of slurmd pods, each pod's status reflecting its Slurm node state; the controller is a pod too, with high availability through a ReadWriteMany volume. Its compatibility table lists releases 1.0 to 1.3, each needing Slurm 25.11 or later (slurm-operator README); the latest release when this page was written was v1.2.3 (30 September 2026, root page's Tool map).
    • 

  • SUNK (CoreWeave): "Each Slurm compute node is a Pod running slurmd, placed one-to-one on a Kubernetes Node"; a SUNK Pod Scheduler places Kubernetes pods "through Slurm's own scheduling logic, on the same Nodes as Slurm jobs", and a Syncer "mirrors state between Slurm nodes and their Pods in both directions" (SUNK docs, vendor).
    • 

  • Managed Slurm in clouds: AWS SageMaker HyperPod launched with Slurm in November 2023 (root page's Tool map).
    • 



What you get, and what it costs. Slurm's semantics stay intact (an allocation is still all-or-nothing, fairshare and backfill still decide), while Kubernetes handles node lifecycle, images, monitoring and the services next to the jobs. The cost is two control planes that must agree about every node, a slurmd per node that now lives in a pod (its cgroups, devices and network are whatever the pod is given), and failure modes from both. Choosing between Slurm, Kubernetes with Kueue, and Ray is the root page's comparison; the sibling page Kubernetes for ML covers the Kubernetes side.

15. Common mistakes, with the messages they produce


Asking for far more time than the job needs
Backfill plans with your limit (section 6): a 2-hour job asking for 24 hours fits no gap and never jumps the queue, and before a maintenance window it shows ReqNodeNotAvail, Reserved for maintenance while nodes sit free (section 10). Ask for the expected time plus a margin, and checkpoint.


Reading (Priority) or the "DOWN, DRAINED" message as a broken cluster
Priority means a job ahead of yours is waiting. Nodes required for job are DOWN, DRAINED or reserved for jobs in higher priority partitions appeared here with every node healthy: a higher-priority job in an overlapping partition was waiting (section 6). Check sinfo and squeue --start first.


A job that can never start, accepted without complaint
QOSMaxGRESPerUser, AssocGrpGRES and PartitionTimeLimit jobs were all accepted here and would wait forever (section 5). A limit reason on a job that alone exceeds the limit is permanent: resubmit smaller, or ask for DenyOnLimit and EnforcePartLimits so such jobs are refused at submission.


Expecting the submit-time error to explain itself
Requested node configuration is not available covers 5 GPUs on a 4-GPU node, 4 nodes on a 3-node partition, 8,000 MB on a 4,000 MB node and 5 CPUs per task on a 4-CPU node alike (section 5). Compare your request with scontrol show node and the partition's limits; an administrator can read the cause in the controller log (never runnable in partition batch).


Side-by-side srun steps that run one after another
Without --exact a step may take every CPU of the job on its node (section 4); the measured case took 12 s instead of 6. Whether it happens depends on whether the job was submitted with --cpus-per-task. Write --exact, and -N1 for one-task steps in a multi-node job (srun: warning: can't run 1 processes on 2 nodes, setting nnodes to 1).


Stranding GPUs with CPU or memory requests
A job that takes all CPUs or all memory of a node leaves its other GPUs unusable (section 3). Tie CPUs and memory to GPUs (--gpus-per-task, --cpus-per-gpu, --mem-per-gpu) or take whole nodes with --exclusive.


Relying on --signal timing to the second
The signal "may be sent up to 60 seconds earlier than specified"; here it came 23 s early (section 8). And a requeued job waits about two minutes before it can start again. Both belong in the checkpoint budget.


Leaving dependency chains to rot
A job whose afterok parent failed sits as DependencyNeverSatisfied forever (section 9). Use --kill-on-invalid-dep=yes, or afterany plus a check in the script.


Reading sprio as live, or sreport as complete
sprio shows the factors of the last recalculation (every PriorityCalcPeriod, 5 minutes by default); sreport reads hourly rollups and showed nothing for the last few minutes (sections 7 and 11). Use sacct for recent jobs.


Assuming a job is fenced in
Without task/cgroup and ConstrainDevices=yes, a process can open every GPU on the node and run on any core (as on this lab cluster). On a shared node that is one user's job slowing another's. Check scontrol show config | grep -i -E 'TaskPlugin|ProctrackType' and cgroup.conf.







16. Interview questions


Why does Slurm not need gang scheduling as an add-on, while Kubernetes does?
A Slurm job is one allocation: the controller gives all its nodes at once or none, and only then starts processes. Kubernetes' default scheduler places pods one at a time and knows nothing of the job they belong to, so partial allocations (and deadlocks between two half-placed jobs) are possible without Kueue, Volcano or a coscheduling plugin; the root's Scheduling lab reproduces the deadlock.


A job has shown (Priority) for hours on a cluster with idle GPUs. Walk through what you check.
squeue --start and sprio for the jobs ahead in the same partition (the main loop stops a partition at its first blocked job); whether the idle GPUs come with free CPUs and memory (scontrol show node, AllocTRES); its time limit against the gap before the next reservation, including maintenance reservations; overlapping partitions; limits on its QOS or account (a limit reason would name them); fairshare (sshare).


Explain multifactor priority and what dominates it in practice.
A weighted sum of factors in [0, 1] (age, fairshare, job size, partition, QOS, TRES) minus nice, truncated to an integer. The weights decide; most sites make fairshare dominant, so a team's recent usage decides its place, as in this page's run where one team's 12-GPU job moved its users from first to last in 51 s. Age stops starvation; QOS gives fast lanes such as debug.


What is the difference between Resources and Priority as reasons?
Resources: the job is the first blocked job of its partition and waits for resources to free. Priority: a higher-priority job ahead of it is waiting, so the main loop did not try it. Only backfill can start a Priority job early, if its time limit fits a gap.


How do you run a 30-day training run on a cluster whose maximum job time is 2 days?
As a chain: --requeue plus --signal=B:USR1@600 and a trap that checkpoints and requeues, or a chain of afterany jobs; each start resumes from the latest checkpoint. Budget the dead time per hop (here about two minutes before a requeued job may start, plus queue wait and startup), and checkpoint often enough that a crash loses little.


Preemption: REQUEUE, CANCEL or SUSPEND for training jobs?
SUSPEND keeps GPU memory occupied and is rarely practical for GPU jobs; CANCEL loses the run; REQUEUE with a GraceTime long enough to write a checkpoint (the victim gets SIGTERM at once and SIGKILL after GraceTime) is the usual choice, with the low-priority work in a preemptible QOS or partition.


What happens to running jobs if slurmctld dies? If slurmdbd dies? If a node's slurmd stops answering?
Controller: running jobs continue (nothing new starts, srun cannot create steps) and its saved state is recovered on restart or by a backup controller. Database: the controller caches records and keeps scheduling, limits cannot be changed. slurmd: after SlurmdTimeout (300 s by default) the node is DOWN and its jobs are requeued (if requeueable) or killed; the root's Failure lab measured it.


Design: the scheduling setup for a 1,000-GPU lab (125 nodes of 8 GPUs) shared by three teams, with research jobs, production training runs and evaluation.
One controller with a backup on a second host and slurmdbd on its own host; configless nodes; cgroups with device and memory confinement; GPUs per node as GRES with NVML autodetection; a health-check prolog and HealthCheckProgram that drain bad nodes. Partitions: one main partition of all nodes (whole-node jobs, --exclusive default for multi-node), a small interactive or debug partition, and topology modelled (blocks or switches) so big jobs stay in one network domain. Accounts per team with fairshare shares matching what each funds, Fair Tree, decay half-life of a week; QOS normal, high (production runs, limited GPUs per team, can preempt), debug (1 node, 1 hour, high priority) and scavenger (any idle GPUs, preemptible with REQUEUE and a GraceTime of a few minutes). Reservations for a production run that needs 512 GPUs for a week, and for maintenance. Backfill on, with bf_max_job_user so one user's thousand jobs cannot fill the backfill budget. Accounting in GPU-hours per team, reported weekly. Trade-offs to say: whole-node allocation wastes capacity on small jobs (hence the shared debug partition), preemption costs the scavenger jobs their work since the last checkpoint, and strict fairshare can make a team with one huge run wait behind many small jobs of others (hence a reservation or high QOS for planned runs).


What does scontrol reconfigure do in Slurm 24.11?
It makes the controller save its state and start a fresh controller process that reads the new configuration and recovers the state (logged here: "Relinquishing control to new slurmctld process"); configless nodes fetch the new files. Running jobs are not touched. Test configuration changes on a copy first: a node declared with more than it has goes out of service at once (section 1).

Why pending? Decode a reason


Every pending job carries one reason, the first check it failed. Slurm 24.11.5 knows  reason strings (src/common/job_state_reason.c at slurm-24-11-5-1), and  of them are accounting limits of an association or a QOS. The green ones below were produced on the lab cluster for this page (or by the root's labs); click one, or type any reason you have seen.









How to read a limit name


The 170 limit reasons follow one grammar, and the decoder above splits any of them: who (Assoc: your user or account association; QOS: the job's QOS; ...PerAccount/...PerUser without a prefix: a QOS limit counted per account or user), kind (Grp: the sum over all running jobs of that association or QOS; Max: one job, or one user or node, as the suffix says; Min: a minimum the job does not meet), resource (Cpu, Mem/Memory, Node, GRES (GPUs), Billing, Energy, License, BB (burst buffer), Jobs, SubmitJobs, Wall), and measure (nothing: the amount held now; Minutes: resource-minutes used, including finished jobs; RunMinutes: resource-minutes still to run for jobs running now, from their time limits). 


The three questions to ask of any reason


    

  1. Will it ever start? A limit the job exceeds on its own (QOSMaxGRESPerUser on an 8-GPU job when the limit is 4), PartitionTimeLimit, DependencyNeverSatisfied and JobHeldAdmin do not clear by waiting.
    2. 

  2. Is it about me or the cluster? Resources, Priority, ReqNodeNotAvail are the cluster; Assoc*, QOS* and Dependency are your own usage, limits and chain.
    3. 

  3. What does the plan say? squeue --start -j N gives the backfill plan, an upper bound that usually moves earlier (Job lifecycle tab); N/A means no plan at all.

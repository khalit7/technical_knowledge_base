The idea in one screen

Slurm is one controller that gives each job its whole allocation at once or nothing, tries waiting jobs in the order of one priority number, and lets a lower job start early (backfill) only when its time limit fits a gap; steps, limits, preemption and reservations are refinements of that one loop.

One job's path: queue, allocation, steps

The topic's lab cluster with the running example (job 79) as it ran: 1 sbatch queues it in slurmctld; 2 the main loop or backfill grants c1 and c2 together (gang scheduling comes free); 3 the script runs as step 79.batch on c1, and its srun starts step 79.0 on both nodes; 4 the order of waiting jobs is one integer per job, fed by usage that slurmdbd records. Section numbers below follow the same path.

You need: nothing beyond Linux processes and a training job. Why clusters need a scheduler at all, and Slurm against Kubernetes and Ray, are on the parent Topic: ml-infra-and-orchestration; how torchrun and NCCL start the ranks inside a step is on Launching distributed training.

Allocation (job)
nodes, CPUs, memory and GPUs held for a time limit, granted all at once.
Job step
one srun inside an allocation: the processes (tasks) that do the work.
Partition
a named set of nodes with its own rules (a queue).
Association, QOS
a user in an account, and a named bundle of priority and limits; both live in the accounting database.
TRES, GRES
anything Slurm counts (cpu, mem, gres/gpu, billing); GRES are devices such as GPUs.
Backfill
starting a lower-priority job in a gap that its time limit fits, without delaying higher jobs.
Fairshare
a priority factor that falls as a user's or team's recent usage rises.
Reason
the one word squeue prints for a pending job: the first check it failed.

1. The daemons: one brain, many hands

No GPU may be given to two jobs at once, on thousands of machines with many users. Slurm's answer is a single brain: every decision is made in one process that holds the whole cluster in memory (the hero's top box), and the machines only do what it says.

Declared, not detected. Slurm schedules what slurm.conf declares for a node, not what the hardware has. Here each node declares 4 CPUs and 4,000 MB while slurmd -C sees 5 CPUs and 9,951 MB, and Slurm hands out exactly the declared amounts. Declaring less is safe; declaring more is not: a node that registers with fewer CPUs, memory or temporary disk than declared is taken out of service "to avoid scheduling jobs on them" (state INVAL, slurm.conf). This cluster hit it while being built: gres/gpu count reported lower than configured (0 < 4). So a typo in slurm.conf takes nodes out of service, and sites declare a little less memory than slurmd -C reports.

Predict: the controller crashes on day 2 of a 3-day training job and is restarted an hour later. What happened to the job?
It kept training. The processes run under slurmstepd on the compute nodes and do not need the controller while they run; the controller's state is on disk in StateSaveLocation, and on restart it logs Recovered state of N nodes and Recovered information about N jobs, as it did here at every reconfigure. What stops for that hour is everything new: submissions, starts and steps (srun asks the controller for each step, so a script that launches a new step every epoch would stall).

The states squeue and sacct print


Main line: PENDING (PD), CONFIGURING (CF, nodes booting or a slow prolog), RUNNING (R), COMPLETING (CG, about 1 s here), COMPLETED (CD). Bad ends: FAILED (script exited non-zero; not requeued; under torchrun the code Slurm records is torchrun's 1, not the failed rank's, as Launching distributed training, section 3, shows), TIMEOUT (SIGTERM at the limit, SIGKILL after KillWait), CANCELLED, NODE_FAIL (with --requeue the job is requeued instead), OUT_OF_MEMORY (needs ConstrainRAMSpace), PREEMPTED. Back to the queue or paused: REQUEUED, SUSPENDED (keeps its memory, GPU memory included). Click a state for its note.


Where the 10 seconds went. The training loop ran about 6.5 s (the ranks' own wall_s); the rest is the chain every job pays: prolog, batch shell, step request, torchrun and imports (the ranks began 2.3 s after the step launch), rendezvous, epilog. On a real cluster GPU health checks, image pulls and the rendezvous of hundreds of nodes make this chain minutes long, paid again on every restart; the root's Launch lab splits it by stage.

3. Resources: what a job asks for, and what it gets


A GPU node is not a box of GPUs: it is CPUs, memory and GPUs that must be handed out together. Slurm counts every kind of thing it hands out as a TRES (trackable resource): cpu, mem, node, gres/gpu, and billing, a weighted sum used for fairshare. The selection plugin select/cons_tres (the default) allocates individual cores, memory and GPUs, so several jobs can share a node; SelectTypeParameters says what is consumable (here CR_Core, cores only; GPU clusters use CR_Core_Memory so memory is counted too).

Measured in the root's Scheduling lab on this cluster (scontrol -d show job, 16 Oct 2026): the first form got NumCPUs=8 (c1 CPUs 0-3 and GPUs 0-3, c2 CPUs 0-3 and GPUs 0-1), so the next 2-GPU job sat in (Resources) while sinfo showed GPUs free. The second form got exactly 6 CPUs; its split across nodes was not recorded, so the drawing's split is illustrative.


The rule behind it: a GPU is schedulable only with the CPUs and memory that come with it. One task per node takes all the node's CPUs, and under CR_Core_Memory a job without --mem on a cluster whose default is the whole node takes all its memory. Check AllocTRES in scontrol show job against what you meant after submitting anything new.


Predict: the running example asks --nodes=2 --ntasks-per-node=1 --cpus-per-task=2 --gres=gpu:2, and this cluster bills a GPU 1.0 and a CPU 0.25. What does sacct show as its AllocTRES?




billing=5,cpu=4,gres/gpu=4,node=2 (section 2's sacct): --gres and --ntasks-per-node are per node, so 2 nodes give 2 x 2 CPUs and 2 x 2 GPUs; billing is 4 x 1.0 + 4 x 0.25 = 5. The batch step 79.batch shows only the first node's share, cpu=2,gres/gpu=2.


Which GPU is mine? On a real node gres.conf maps each GPU to its device file (Name=gpu File=/dev/nvidia[0-7], or AutoDetect=nvml), and Slurm sets CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES for every job and step to the GPUs it was given. Only cgroups make that a fence: with TaskPlugin=task/cgroup, ProctrackType=proctrack/cgroup and ConstrainCores, ConstrainRAMSpace, ConstrainDevices in cgroup.conf (each "no" by default, cgroup.conf), the job's processes cannot see other GPUs or use other cores, and are killed past their memory (state OUT_OF_MEMORY). Without them a process can open every GPU on the node, as on this lab cluster, where cgroups are off (inside a container without systemd, slurmd 24.11 stops with fatal: systemd scope for slurmstepd could not be set). How cgroups work is owned by Containers and isolation.


Topology. Where the nodes sit decides all-reduce speed: topology/tree (switches) and topology/block (blocks of nodes such as NVL72 racks; both in 24.11, and 25.05 added several topologies in one topology.yaml, topology docs) keep a job inside one switch or block, and --switches=1@01:00:00 waits up to an hour for that. The networks themselves are Cluster anatomy's.

4. Job steps: the processes inside an allocation


An allocation is only a reservation; srun starts the processes. Each srun in a script is one job step: it asks the controller for a slice of the allocation, slurmd on each node starts a slurmstepd, and that starts the tasks, gives each its rank and collects their exit codes. Steps run one after another (preprocess, train, evaluate) or side by side (a sweep of small runs inside one big allocation).


The trap. By default a step may use every CPU of the job on each of its nodes ("By default, all non-GRES resources on each node in the step allocation will be used", srun --exact), so steps meant to run side by side queue behind each other. Predict how long four 6-second one-task steps take in a 2-node allocation of 2 CPUs per node, then step through:


Four steps side by side: 12 s without --exact, 6 s with it measured






Each bar is a step as sacct recorded it (start, end, node, CPUs). The allocation holds 2 CPUs and 2 GPUs on each of c1 and c2. Jobs 129 (without) and 126 (with), 16 Oct 2026; one-second resolution.


Without --exact each step took both CPUs of its node (AllocTRES cpu=2), so the third and fourth waited 6 s (srun prints only srun: Step created for StepId=129.4 when it finally gets in); with it each took one CPU and all four ran at once. A subtlety: a job submitted with --cpus-per-task exports SLURM_CPUS_PER_TASK, and srun treats that as an explicit -c, which "implies --exact" (srun's -c entry): the same steps without --exact inside a job with --cpus-per-task=1 (job 130) ran side by side. So a step's behaviour depends on how its job was submitted: write --exact when you mean it, and -N1 for one-task steps in a multi-node job.

Overlap. Steps never share CPUs unless asked: srun --overlap --jobid=N --pty bash runs a step on resources another step holds, which is how you attach a debugger or py-spy to a running training step. For training the usual layout is one step that starts everything: one torchrun per node (the running example, section 14), or one task per GPU with ranks from SLURM_PROCID. Launching distributed training compares the launchers, and Distributed PyTorch owns torchrun at the PyTorch level.






5. Partitions, QOS and associations: who may use what


A shared cluster needs rules beyond first come, first served: one team may not take every GPU, debug jobs must start fast, a huge job may run only at night. Slurm has three places to put a rule, and a pending job's reason names the one holding it.


Each limit lives at one level, and the reason names that level




The three jobs this page submitted to break one rule each, while every GPU was busy (squeue at 05:38:07 UTC, 16 Oct 2026). Partition: a named set of nodes with MaxTime, MaxNodes, a default time, who may submit, PriorityJobFactor and PriorityTier, in slurm.conf; partitions may overlap (here batch, urgent and sllow all cover c[1-3]). Association: a (cluster, account, user) triple in slurmdbd; accounts form a tree with fairshare shares and limits such as GrpTRES=gres/gpu=64 (the whole team at once) or MaxJobs, set with sacctmgr. QOS: a named bundle of a priority, limits (MaxTRESPerUser, MaxWall, GrpTRES) and preemption rules that a job selects with --qos; typical are debug (high priority, 1 node, 30 minutes), normal and scavenger (lowest, preemptible).


All three were accepted and will never start: 8 GPUs exceed sl-small's 4 per user, 4 GPUs exceed carol's 2, and 120 minutes exceed batch's MaxTime=60. A job that breaks a limit on its own pends forever unless its QOS has the DenyOnLimit flag (then it is refused at submission), and a job over a partition's time limit is accepted unless EnforcePartLimits is set. Carol's priority, 6500, was the highest of the three: priority and limits are separate questions. squeue --start printed N/A for job 88 but a start time for 92 and 93, so a start time is not a promise that a job can run.


When several places define the same limit, the first found wins, in this order: partition QOS, job QOS, user association, account associations up the tree, root, partition (resource limits). And none of it applies unless AccountingStorageEnforce includes limits and qos: the default is none, as on this lab cluster, so for these runs it was set to associations,limits,qos.

6. The scheduler: a main loop and a backfill loop


Testing every job against every node on every change is too slow, and starting jobs in strict priority order wastes nodes that a big job at the head cannot use yet. So the controller runs two loops. The main loop runs on every event (a job submitted or ending, a node returning) and every sched_interval (60 s by default, ); it walks pending jobs in priority order, starts each one that fits now, tests at most default_queue_depth jobs on an event (100 by default), and the moment one job of a partition cannot start, starts nothing else in that partition or on its nodes in that pass. The backfill loop runs every bf_interval (30 s by default, 2 s here): it builds a timeline from every running job's time limit, reserves the earliest start for each waiting job in priority order, and starts a lower job that fits a gap and ends before any reservation it would cross. Slurm's version reserves for every job it tests (conservative) and reserves whole nodes.


The main loop stops at the first blocked job; backfill fills the gap it leaves

That rule is where the two most common reasons come from: the first blocked job of a partition shows Resources, and every job behind it shows Priority ("a job ahead of me is waiting"). Backfill is the only way a job jumps that line, and it needs your time limit to do it: a 2-hour job asking for 24 hours fits no gap. Either loop may start any job, and the log says which (sched: Allocate for the main loop, sched/backfill: _start_job for backfill): on the idle cluster job 125 was started by backfill 444 ms after sbatch, because this cluster's sched_min_interval=500000 (0.5 s) keeps the main loop from running more than twice a second and the backfill cycle came first.

with Fair Tree (the default), the user's rank in the account tree divided by the number of users: heavy recent use, decayed with PriorityDecayHalfLife, means a low factor. The root's Scheduling lab has a Fair Tree calculator.

by default favours large jobs: (nodes / cluster nodes + CPUs / cluster CPUs) / 2.


partition
the partition's PriorityJobFactor over the largest one.


qos

tres

8. Preemption and requeue: taking nodes back


The cluster is full of long training jobs and an urgent job arrives (an evaluation before a deadline, a team whose guaranteed share others are using). Preemption lets the scheduler stop running jobs to make room; the same requeue machinery serves time limits.


SettingChoices


PreemptType: who may preempt whompreempt/partition_prio: a job in a partition with a strictly higher PriorityTier preempts jobs of lower tiers (this cluster: urgent tier 10 over batch tier 1). preempt/qos: each QOS lists the QOS it may preempt (sacctmgr modify qos high set preempt=scavenger). preempt/none.


PreemptMode: what happens to the victimCANCEL (killed), REQUEUE (killed and put back in the queue, needs --requeue; otherwise cancelled), SUSPEND (paused in memory; needs GANG at cluster level, and keeps GPU memory occupied, so it is rarely practical for GPU jobs), GANG (time slicing on the same resources), OFF (preemption docs).


GraceTimethe victim gets SIGCONT and SIGTERM at once and SIGKILL only after GraceTime (default 0): the window to write a checkpoint.




For training the usual choice is REQUEUE with a GraceTime long enough to checkpoint, with low-priority work in a preemptible QOS or partition. The root's Scheduling lab preempted bob's 12-GPU batch job with a 4-GPU urgent job on this cluster: preempted JobId=39 has been requeued to reclaim resources for JobId=40, the urgent job was allocated 0.6 s after submission, and the victim could not restart for 121 s. GPU-hours lost per preemption are the time since the last checkpoint times the GPUs, the trade-off the root's Failure lab turns into a checkpoint interval.


Checkpoint before the limit, then two minutes of dead time: job 128 measured




--time=1 --signal=B:USR1@25 --requeue: Slurm should signal the batch shell 25 s before the 60 s limit; the script traps SIGUSR1, saves, and runs scontrol requeue; the next start resumes. Two lessons for a checkpoint budget. The signal "may be sent up to 60 seconds earlier than specified" because of how Slurm resolves events (sbatch --signal), so ask for more warning than a save takes, plus a minute. And a requeued job's begin time is pushed past the job credential's lifetime (120 s by default) plus 1 s, so the old run's credentials and I/O are gone before the new run starts: budget two minutes of dead time per requeue, for preemption and for time limits alike. SLURM_RESTART_COUNT tells the script it is a restart; the running example instead finds its latest checkpoint pointer and resumes from it. A 30-day run on a cluster whose maximum job time is 2 days is a chain of such hops (or of afterany jobs, section 9).

10. Reservations: holding nodes for a time or a team


Some capacity must be guaranteed in advance: a maintenance window, a week of 512 GPUs promised to one team for a big run, a demo. A reservation is a named block of nodes (or cores) for a time window, usable only by the users or accounts it names (scontrol create reservation, by an administrator). A maintenance window on all three nodes, starting in 3 minutes for 5 minutes, then three jobs measured:

The 2-minute job fitted before the window and ran; the 4- and 10-minute jobs could not finish before it and pended with ReqNodeNotAvail, Reserved for maintenance, planned for 05:47:57, its end. This is the most common surprise before a maintenance: ask for less time, or wait. After scontrol delete ReservationName=sl_maint the 10-minute job started at once. The source sets that reason when a job's time window overlaps a MAINT reservation .


A team reservation, node c3 for alice for 10 minutes: alice's job naming --reservation=sl_team ran on c3, her job without it on c1; bob's 3-node job pended with ReqNodeNotAvail, May be reserved for other job and his 2-node job with Resources. Only a job that names the reservation can use it. Flags change the rules: FLEX lets a job use nodes inside and outside, MAINT marks the time as maintenance in accounting, IGNORE_JOBS creates it even if running jobs overlap. For a training team the useful pattern is a recurring or long reservation on a block of nodes in one network domain, so a large job never waits behind fragmentation.

12. Containers on Slurm described, not run here


A training job needs an exact stack (CUDA, NCCL, PyTorch, your code) on nodes that share one OS image, and nobody gets root. The common answer on GPU clusters is Pyxis with enroot (NVIDIA): Pyxis is "a SPANK plugin for the Slurm Workload Manager" (SPANK: Slurm's plugin interface for job options) that adds --container-image, --container-mounts and --container-name to srun, so srun --container-image=nvcr.io#nvidia/pytorch:<tag> --container-mounts=/data:/data python train.py runs every rank in the image; enroot imports a Docker image into a squashfs file and runs it without root, and pyxis requires enroot 3.1.0 or later (pyxis README). Slurm on Kubernetes: Slinky and SUNK (described, not run here)


Organisations that want one GPU pool for batch training (driven with sbatch, fairshare and backfill) and long-running services that Kubernetes runs well can run Slurm inside Kubernetes rather than split the GPUs between two clusters, one idle while the other queues.


    

  • Slinky (SchedMD's own): a Kubernetes operator that "contains a Kubernetes operator to deploy and manage certain components of Slurm clusters". Workers are NodeSets, "A set of homogeneous Slurm workers (compute nodes)", run as a StatefulSet or a DaemonSet of slurmd pods, each pod's status reflecting its Slurm node state; the controller is a pod too, with high availability through a ReadWriteMany volume. Its compatibility table lists releases 1.0 to 1.3, each needing Slurm 25.11 or later (slurm-operator README); the latest release when this page was written was v1.2.3 (30 September 2026, root's Tool map). SchedMD open-sourced it in November 2024 (Apache-2.0) and reached 1.0 in late 2025; a second Slinky project, slurm-bridge, goes the other way and lets Slurm be the scheduler for Kubernetes pods so both kinds of work share one GPU pool (Slinky docs). Not run on the topic's kind cluster: it needs Slurm 25.11+ images and more memory than the topic's budget.
    • 

  • SUNK (CoreWeave): "Each Slurm compute node is a Pod running slurmd, placed one-to-one on a Kubernetes Node"; a SUNK Pod Scheduler places Kubernetes pods "through Slurm's own scheduling logic, on the same Nodes as Slurm jobs", and a Syncer "mirrors state between Slurm nodes and their Pods in both directions" (SUNK docs, vendor). A CoreWeave product since 2023 with no public repository: Slurm accounting in MySQL, node lifecycle tied to CoreWeave's node health checks, available only on CoreWeave Kubernetes Service.
    • 

  • Which: Slinky on any Kubernetes cluster, SUNK when renting from CoreWeave; neither if researchers are content with Kubernetes-native jobs.
    • 

  • Managed Slurm in clouds: AWS SageMaker HyperPod launched with Slurm in November 2023 (root's Tool map).
    • 



What you get, and what it costs. Slurm's semantics stay intact (all-or-nothing allocations, fairshare, backfill) while Kubernetes handles node lifecycle, images, monitoring and the services beside the jobs. The cost is two control planes that must agree about every node, a slurmd that lives in a pod (its cgroups, devices and network are whatever the pod is given), and failure modes from both. Choosing between Slurm, Kubernetes with Kueue, and Ray is the root's comparison; Kubernetes for ML owns the Kubernetes side.

Design rehearsal: a 1,000-GPU lab shared by three teams (optional)


125 nodes of 8 GPUs; research jobs, production training runs and evaluation.


One controller with a backup on a second host and slurmdbd on its own host; configless nodes; cgroups with device and memory confinement; GPUs per node as GRES with NVML autodetection; a health-check prolog and HealthCheckProgram that drain bad nodes. Partitions: one main partition of all nodes (whole-node jobs, --exclusive default for multi-node), a small interactive or debug partition, and topology modelled (blocks or switches) so big jobs stay in one network domain. Accounts per team with fairshare shares matching what each funds, Fair Tree, decay half-life of a week; QOS normal, high (production runs, limited GPUs per team, can preempt), debug (1 node, 1 hour, high priority) and scavenger (any idle GPUs, preemptible with REQUEUE and a GraceTime of a few minutes). Reservations for a production run that needs 512 GPUs for a week, and for maintenance. Backfill on, with bf_max_job_user. Accounting in GPU-hours per team, reported weekly.


Trade-offs to say: whole-node allocation wastes capacity on small jobs (hence the shared debug partition), preemption costs the scavenger jobs their work since the last checkpoint, and strict fairshare can make a team with one huge run wait behind many small jobs of others (hence a reservation or high QOS for planned runs).


And why Slurm needs no gang-scheduling add-on while Kubernetes does: a Slurm job is one allocation, all nodes or none, before any process starts; Kubernetes' default scheduler places pods one at a time and knows nothing of the job they belong to, so two half-placed jobs can deadlock without Kueue, Volcano or a coscheduling plugin (the root's Scheduling lab reproduces it).

Why pending? Decode a reason


Every pending job carries one reason, the first check it failed. Slurm 24.11.5 knows  reason strings (src/common/job_state_reason.c at slurm-24-11-5-1), and  of them are accounting limits of an association or a QOS. The green ones below were produced on the lab cluster for this page (or by the root's labs); click one, or type any reason you have seen.









How to read a limit name


The 170 limit reasons follow one grammar, and the decoder above splits any of them: who (Assoc: your user or account association; QOS: the job's QOS; ...PerAccount/...PerUser without a prefix: a QOS limit counted per account or user), kind (Grp: the sum over all running jobs of that association or QOS; Max: one job, or one user or node, as the suffix says; Min: a minimum the job does not meet), resource (Cpu, Mem/Memory, Node, GRES (GPUs), Billing, Energy, License, BB (burst buffer), Jobs, SubmitJobs, Wall), and measure (nothing: the amount held now; Minutes: resource-minutes used, including finished jobs; RunMinutes: resource-minutes still to run for jobs running now, from their time limits). 


The three questions to ask of any reason


    

  1. Will it ever start? A limit the job exceeds on its own (QOSMaxGRESPerUser on an 8-GPU job when the limit is 4), PartitionTimeLimit, DependencyNeverSatisfied and JobHeldAdmin do not clear by waiting.
    2. 

  2. Is it about me or the cluster? Resources, Priority, ReqNodeNotAvail are the cluster; Assoc*, QOS* and Dependency are your own usage, limits and chain.
    3. 

  3. What does the plan say? squeue --start -j N gives the backfill plan, an upper bound that usually moves earlier (Reading, section 2); N/A means no plan at all.
    4.

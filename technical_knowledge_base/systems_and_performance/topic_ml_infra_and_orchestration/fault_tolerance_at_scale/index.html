The idea

A synchronous job on thousands of GPUs fails every few hours, so how much of it trains is decided less by the failure rate than by what each failure costs: the time until something notices, the time to get running again, and the work redone since the last checkpoint. Every design on this page shrinks one of those parts; slow and silent failures never start that clock and need detectors of their own.

One failure on a 16,384-GPU job: about 47 minutes lost with defaults, about 3 with the best designs

1. What fails, and how often

Meta's Llama 3 405B pre-training on up to 16,384 H100s was interrupted 419 times unexpectedly in 54 days, once every 3.1 hours, yet kept "higher than 90% effective training time" published (Llama 3 sec. 3.3.4). Meta's 2026 table for a recurring 32K-GPU job counts 678 interruptions and reports 95% to 97% effective training time published (arXiv 2602.00277). The failure rate barely moved (2.3 interruptions per 1,000 servers per day in 2026, against 3.8 for Llama 3 by our arithmetic) calculation; the recovery got faster.

Predict: in the Shanghai AI Lab's LLM clusters (4,704 A100s, six months of 2023), user script errors (a missing file, a type error, a bad import) were what share of failed jobs, and what share of the GPU time lost to failures?
73.4% of the 2,575 failed jobs and 3.2% of the GPU time published (Hu et al., NSDI 2024, Table 3; the table's arithmetic reproduces exactly). Script errors fail in the first minutes of small jobs. Infrastructure failures were 10.9% of failed jobs but 82.1% of the GPU time: they hit long, big jobs, after hours of work. Count failures to fix your users' tooling; weight them by GPU time to fix your platform.

Same company, two years apart: HBM overtook the GPU compute rows published

Sources: Llama 3, Table 5; Training LLMs with Fault Tolerant HSDP on 100,000 GPUs, Table 1 (arXiv v1, 30 Jan 2026). All eleven tables are in the Failure taxonomies tab.

  • Big jobs fail on hardware; clusters fail on code. In one long job on thousands of GPUs, GPUs and their memory dominate (64% of Llama 3's interruptions by count). In a multi-tenant cluster most failed jobs are user errors (Philly 2019, Acme 2024, ByteRobust 2025), while the hardware failures cost most of the GPU time: at Meta's research clusters hardware ended 0.2% of jobs but 18.7% of GPU runtime published (Kokolis et al., HPCA 2025, Fig. 3).
  • The leading cause moves. In Meta's 2026 table "'Faulty GPU Compute' used to be the top cause, but is now reduced to the fourth (7.4%) ... HBM issues rose to become the top issue" (22.9%), and PCIe devices are second (18.0%). Fleet work pays off cause by cause; the total does not go to zero.
  • A large share is never attributed. Watchdog timeouts and "unknown" are 10% of the 2026 table; job hangs 9.9% of ByteRobust's incidents; NODE_FAIL with no cause is the largest hardware signal at Meta's research clusters. For these the cost is mostly detection time (section 3).
  • Real jobs fail more often than independent servers predict. If servers fail independently, a job on N servers fails N times as often as one (the reliability page, section 5, draws it): at Meta's research-cluster rate of 6.50 failures per thousand node-days, a 1,024-GPU job (128 servers) should run 28.8 hours between failures calculation, yet the same paper measured 7.9 hours for 1,024-GPU jobs (Kokolis et al., HPCA 2025) published. Software, the network and the job's own code fail too; the 1/N law is a floor on the failure rate, not a forecast.
Older records, and the tables' own arithmetic checked (depth)

The same order of magnitude in older and smaller records. Meta's OPT-175B ran on 992 80GB A100s, and "hardware failures contributed to at least 35 manual restarts and the cycling of over 100 hosts over the course of 2 months", plus an estimated "70+ automatic restarts" (OPT, section 2.5) published. Per GPU that is at least 1.8e-3 restarts per GPU-day against Llama 3's 4.7e-4 interruptions per GPU-day, roughly 3.7 times as often calculation; the two count different things (restarts against interruptions, "2 months" taken as 60 days) on different hardware two years apart, so read it as "the order of magnitude is stable", not as a trend. The Gemini checkpointing paper (Rice and AWS, SOSP 2023) puts OPT-175B's failures at about 178,000 GPU-hours wasted (Wang et al., section 1) published, about 12% of 992 GPUs for 61 days calculation.

2. Five ways to fail, and the clock each one starts

Sorting failures by cause (GPU, network, software) tells you what to fix in the fleet. Sorting them by how they look to the job tells you how they are noticed, and that decides what each one costs.

The further down the table, the longer until anything notices

With every default untouched, a crash is caught in a fraction of a second (the root's Failure lab measured about 0.15 s on all three systems), but a frozen GPU is caught by the NCCL watchdog only after 10 minutes, during which every other GPU in the job does nothing; a dead node on Slurm costs 5 minutes before the controller acts; and no layer at all watches for a slow rank or a wrong number. 3. Detection: every layer has a timer, set for safety

Each layer of the stack watches with its own timer, and the defaults were chosen so that a slow checkpoint or a long first compilation does not kill a healthy job: torchrun polls its workers every 0.1 s, the NCCL watchdog waits 10 min, gloo 30 min, Slurm 300 s, Kubernetes 50 s plus 300 s, Ray about 30 s. Shorten each to just above your longest legitimate pause.

Health checks: keep bad machines out of the next restart

Timeouts catch a failure after it has stopped a job; health checks catch the machine before it is given one. Three moments matter: before a node joins (burn-in; OpenAI's nodes join Kubernetes with a "preflight" taint that only passing GPU tests remove, per the root page), before or between jobs (Ai2's Beaker multiplies two tensors on every GPU before each workload and cordons the host on failure; Slurm's HealthCheckProgram), and after a failure, to decide whether the machine goes back into the pool. NVIDIA's DCGM diagnostics run in "< 2.5 Seconds" at level 1 (fits before every job), minutes at level 2 (between jobs or after a failure) and tens of minutes to hours at levels 3 and 4 (burn-in). A drain or taint after a failed check is what keeps the next restart from landing on the same bad machine, the trap OPT-175B's logbook records when its cloud replacement returned the same bad host.

Restarting onto the same broken machine just fails again, so every system has a way to say "schedule nothing new here": on Slurm a node is drained (running jobs finish, nothing new starts); on Kubernetes it is cordoned (unschedulable) or tainted (a taint repels every pod that does not tolerate it; a NoExecute taint also evicts running ones). Slurm runs HealthCheckProgram as root every HealthCheckInterval seconds and ignores its exit code: the script must drain the node itself; the lab's drained its node within 1.4 to 7.3 s in four tries measured.

The Kubernetes trap. A node that goes silent becomes NotReady after 50 s and gets the taint node.kubernetes.io/unreachable:NoExecute; pods tolerate it for 300 s by default, then are marked for deletion. But a pod is only gone when its kubelet confirms it, and a dead kubelet never does, so the pod stays Terminating and anything waiting for it waits forever. The fix is to tell the cluster the node is really down:

kubectl taint node mi-kind-worker2 node.kubernetes.io/out-of-service=nodeshutdown:NoExecute

measured: 2 s after this taint the stuck pod was force-deleted, the JobSet restarted on two healthy workers and the run resumed from step 100 (root page, src/fail/runs/fl-k3). Remove the taint once the node is repaired. Clusters automate this with a node-health controller rather than a person.

4. Checkpointing: what each strategy stops training for

A checkpoint is the job's state saved so a restart can continue: weights, optimiser moments, the learning-rate step, the data position, the random-number generators. Its price is the pause C, the time training stands still per save, and that pause sets how often you can afford to save: the best interval grows as √(2CM) for a job MTBF M (Young; derived on the reliability page, section 6). A cheaper pause means more frequent saves, so less rework per failure.

5. Recovery designs: which part of the lost time each one removes

After a failure the job loses the time to detect it, to wait for the scheduler, to get a healthy machine, to start (processes, rendezvous, communicator set-up), to load the checkpoint, and then to redo the work since that checkpoint; on top, it pays the checkpoint pause on every save, failure or not. Every design attacks some of these and leaves the rest.

Predict: a 16,384-GPU job at Llama 3's failure rate (one failure every 3.1 hours). Which single change raises goodput most: cutting detection from 10 minutes to 10 seconds, adding hot spares so no machine is provisioned, or making checkpoints asynchronous (a 0.78 s pause instead of 5 minutes)?
Asynchronous checkpoints, by far, in this model calculation: from 68.9% (all defaults) to 72.7% with fast detection, 77.1% with spares as well, and 97.0% once checkpoints pause for 0.78 s instead of 5 minutes, because a cheap checkpoint can be taken every couple of minutes, which shrinks both the pauses and the rework. The other two only shorten the recovery itself, which here is a smaller part of the loss than the 40-minute optimal interval's pauses and rework. How to read the numbers
The model is the one built for the root page's Failure lab (its curve against the checkpoint interval is section 6 of the reliability page): failures arrive at random with job MTBF M = 1 / (GPUs × failure rate per GPU); a checkpoint pauses training for C every τ of work; each failure costs the recovery time R plus the work since the last checkpoint; expected goodput = τ / (M eR/M (e(τ+C)/M − 1)) (Daly 2006), with τ at its optimum unless the design fixes it. For replica groups only the failed group's share of R is lost (R × (1 − kept share)), our approximation. calculation Inputs are published figures from different systems and hardware, combined per design, so each design row is illustrative as a whole even where every input is sourced: no single source reports these exact combinations. The two bottom designs are this topic's own measured runs of the tiny job, for scale. Not modelled: storage bandwidth (frequent checkpoints of a large model must fit the storage fabric), the window in which an asynchronous checkpoint is still being written (a failure then falls back to the previous one), queue time, and failures during recovery beyond the formula's own.

Elastic training and a hot spare, measured with torchrun

Elastic training lets a job continue with fewer machines instead of stopping: torchrun --nnodes=1:2 accepts any number of nodes between 1 and 2. Each node runs an agent; the agents meet at a rendezvous (the c10d backend: a small key-value store that one agent hosts) to agree who is in the job, then start the workers. When a worker fails, every agent stops its workers, meets again with whoever is still there, and restarts them all from the latest checkpoint with the new world size.

Choosing, in one paragraph
Measure your failure rate and your current R and C first (section 9). Asynchronous checkpoints are the cheapest large win at almost any scale. Hot spares and fast detection matter once recovery is a large share of the MTBF, roughly from thousands of GPUs. In-memory checkpoints and replica groups matter when the MTBF falls to tens of minutes (about 30 minutes at 100,000 GPUs at Llama 3's rate), where even a fast storage restart is a large share of every interval. Elastic resizing and redundant computation fit preemptible capacity better than a reserved cluster. Every design needs its restart path exercised before the real run needs it.

6. A restart is only as good as the checkpoint it finds

Every recovery design ends the same way: some process loads some saved state and continues. Four traps found on this topic's own clusters show that "we have checkpoints" and "we can restart" are different claims, and each has a check you can run before the real run needs it.

1. The restart path itself fails

Two-node torchrun --max-restarts=2 with PyTorch 2.14.1's defaults failed to restart twice in a row on Slurm (Gloo connectFullMesh failed ... Connection refused): the restarted ranks dialled addresses from the previous attempt. TORCH_DISABLE_SHARE_RDZV_TCP_STORE=1 gives each restart a fresh store and the same crash then resumed in 3.4 s measured (root's Failure lab; the elastic runs are in section 5). Check: kill a rank in a test job and watch it come back.

2. The restart has a single point of failure

With torchrun's c10d rendezvous, one agent hosts the key-value store every agent meets at. Kill that agent's node and the survivors die too: RendezvousConnectionError: The connection to the C10d store has failed measured. Check: put the endpoint on the most stable machine, or use an external store (etcd), and kill the store's host in a test.

3. The newest checkpoint on disk is not the one restored

Ray Train 2.59.0 restored step 50 in 3 of 3 runs although rank 0 had written step 100 to storage before the crash measured (the Ray child page). The controller registers a checkpoint only once it has collected that report from every worker, by polling every 2 s (report_handler.py lines 40 and 63 @ ray-2.59.0: "Wait for all workers to report training results", if not all(self._training_report_queues): return); a worker that died before the poll took its report with it. Check: restore from what the framework says is registered, treat other directories as possibly incomplete, and count the lost interval in your goodput.

4. The resumed run is not the same run

Resuming the running example with 4 ranks changed the loss in the last bit at 2 of 10 logged steps (2.4e-7), identically on Slurm, Kubernetes and Ray measured: DDP rebuilds its gradient buckets after a process's first step, so a restarted process sums in a different order. Harmless here, but it means "bit for bit" needs care, and it is why this page's replay switches the rebuild off. Check: compare the loss of the first steps after a resume with the original run; a last-bit difference is arithmetic, a jump is missing state (optimiser moments, learning-rate step, data position, random-number generators).

7. Silent data corruption: one bit, measured

A silent data corruption (SDC) is a wrong result that raises no error: a marginal transistor in a multiplier, a bit flipped in a register or a cache line that ECC does not cover. ECC protects data at rest in memory; it does not check arithmetic, so the run keeps going on wrong numbers. At Gemini's scale Google expected "SDC events to impact training every week or two"; Llama 3 attributes 6 of its 419 interruptions (1.4%) to SDC, the ones that were caught published. The hardware side and the CPU fleet studies are on the reliability page, section 7; this section is what one flipped bit does to a data-parallel run and which detector catches it.

The experiment. We took the running example (4 data-parallel replicas, 200 steps, deterministic), and in six runs flipped exactly one bit at step 120 on replica 2: either in its local gradient just before the all-reduce (the corruption happened in the backward pass), or in one of its weights just after the optimiser step (the corruption happened in the update, or in memory on that one chip). Three bit positions each: a low mantissa bit, an exponent bit and the top exponent bit. Every step logged the global loss, the global gradient norm, a checksum of every replica's weights (the sum of their bit patterns, compared across replicas), and a fingerprint of each replica's local gradients. A clean run is the reference; a replay from the step-100 checkpoint recomputes steps 101 to 150 on healthy hardware.

  • Most flips are invisible in the loss. In five of the six runs the global loss never moved by more than 1e-4, far below its step-to-step noise; only the top exponent bit of a weight made noise, by overflowing to NaN. A loss-curve alarm catches the loud tail, not SDC in general.
  • A huge gradient did not blow up training. Flipping the top exponent bit of the largest gradient element made it about 1.6e37 and the gradient norm about 3.9e36, yet the final loss moved only in the fourth decimal. AdamW divides each update by the square root of a running mean of squared gradients; our reading is that the square overflowed to infinity in float32, so this one weight's update collapsed to almost nothing and stayed there for the rest of the run, leaving one weight silently frozen our reading. A one-weight AdamW fed the same gradient shows exactly that: its update falls from about 1e-3 to under 1e-6 per step and the squared-gradient average stays infinite (src/scripts/recompute.py). A gradient-norm monitor is the only alarm that fired.
  • Before or after the all-reduce decides which detector works. A gradient corrupted before the all-reduce is averaged into every replica: they stay identical, so comparing replicas finds nothing, in all three gradient runs. A weight corrupted after it lives on one replica: the checksum comparison caught all three weight flips at the step they happened, including the low bit that changed the loss by at most 4.8e-7.
  • Deterministic replay caught every case, and named the chip. Because the running example is deterministic (same batches, fixed reduction order, no dropout), recomputing steps from the last checkpoint on healthy hardware reproduces every logged fingerprint except the corrupted one: replica 2 at step 120 for the gradient flips, replica 2 at step 121 (the first computation that used the bad weight) for the weight flips. This is the method Google describes for Gemini; it needs bit-for-bit determinism, which is why this page switched off DDP's one-time bucket rebuild (the root's Failure lab showed it changes the last bit after a restart).
What transfers to a real cluster, and what does not
Transfers: where the corruption enters relative to the all-reduce, and which comparison can see it; NaN spreading to every replica within one step; Adam's normalisation hiding a large gradient. Does not transfer directly: bf16 and fp8 training have fewer mantissa and exponent bits, so the same flip lands differently; GPU kernels are often nondeterministic, so replay needs deterministic algorithms or a tolerance; and the checksum costs a reduction over every weight, which large runs do periodically, not every step.

8. Stragglers: the slow rank waits least

A straggler costs no restart and raises no alarm, so it is easy to underestimate. Every synchronous step waits for the slowest rank, so a rank that is 10% slow makes the whole job about 10% slower. The one large published study says it is the common case: in ByteDance's clusters, "42.5% of the jobs are at least 10% slower" than they would be without stragglers, and "10.4% of the allocated GPU hours are wasted" published (Lin et al., arXiv 2505.05713v2, five months of 2024, 3,079 jobs of 128 GPUs or more).

The root page's lab made rank 3 of the running example (2 nodes x 2 ranks, Slurm) sleep 50 ms or 200 ms after every step measured. Every rank then reported the same step time (22.1, 70.5 and 221.8 ms), so a step-time log cannot tell you who is slow. 9. Goodput: name every lost second

Goodput is the share of a job's elapsed time spent computing useful new steps: Gemini's definition, "the time spent computing useful new steps over the elapsed time of the training job" (Gemini 1.0 sec. 3). Its complement, the time lost, is badput. One headline number is not enough to act on; accounting splits badput into named parts, each owned by someone.

The published numbers are not on one scale: "90% effective training time" (Llama 3), "97% goodput" (Gemini) and "97% ETTR" (ByteRobust) differ in whether queue time and MFU are inside the number, which changes it by more than the differences between them (definitions in the closed block below). Google's open-source ml-goodput-measurement library names the badput buckets a platform should log: TPU (accelerator) initialisation, training preparation, program start-up, synchronous data loading, unproductive checkpoint save and restore time, "wasted progress from disruption", "infrastructure recovery from disruption", and custom events; for asynchronous saves only the blocking copy counts as badput (AI-Hypercomputer/ml-goodput-measurement).

Recovery time matters more than the interval. At Llama 3's failure rate a 16,384-GPU job fails every 3.1 hours. With a 1-minute blocking checkpoint and 10 minutes to recover, the best interval is about 19 minutes of work and goodput is about 85% calculation. Cutting recovery from 10 minutes to 1 raises it to about 89%, more than the whole difference between a 10-minute and a 40-minute interval (84% and 83% against the optimum of 85%). Near the optimum the curve is flat in the interval (the reliability page's section 6 draws it); it is steep in the pause and in recovery. That is why Gemini kept model replicas in memory and spare cubes, why DeepSeek checkpoints "typically every 5 minutes" asynchronously, and why the measured recovery of the tiny job (seconds for an in-job restart, 2.5 minutes for a Slurm requeue) matters more at 16,000 GPUs than at 4.

What to log, so the accounting is possible. A per-step record with a timestamp (the running example's ts), a record at every start with the step it resumed from and the checkpoint it loaded, a record for every checkpoint save with its blocking time, and the scheduler's events (submitted, eligible, started, failed, requeued) with their times. With those, every second of a job's wall-clock falls into exactly one bucket, and a failure's cost can be split into its detection, waiting, start-up, load and rework, which is what section 5's model splits. Common mistakes

Quoting one failure share without its unit.
"Hardware causes 78% of failures" (Llama 3, one job's interruptions) and "user code causes 73% of failures" (Acme, a cluster's failed jobs) are both right. Say what was counted, and weight by GPU time when the question is cost.
Copying a table's headline without checking it.
Llama 3's 58.7% GPU share inherits a misprinted row (64.0% by counts); ByteRobust's text and Table 1 disagree by 1,451 explicit failures; Meta's 2026 "a failure every 18 minutes" at 100K GPUs does not follow from its own rate. Recompute before you quote.
Leaving the NCCL timeouts at their defaults on a big job.
A frozen GPU then costs every other GPU 10 minutes before the watchdog acts (Watchdog caught collective operation timeout: ... ran for 600000 milliseconds before timing out.), and a wedged watchdog 8 more minutes (ProcessGroupNCCL's watchdog got stuck for 480 seconds without making progress in monitoring enqueued collectives.). Shorten them to just above your longest legitimate pause, and add health inspections that catch a hung GPU in seconds.
Ranking ranks by collective time to find a straggler.
The slow rank waits least. On the replay, the "long waits" rule flagged the three healthy ranks hundreds of times and the slow one never. Compare compute time across ranks (relative score) or against the rank's own history (individual score).
Treating a flat loss curve as proof of healthy hardware.
Five of the six bit flips measured here moved the loss by less than 1e-4, and one froze a weight for the rest of the run. Silent corruption needs checksums across replicas or deterministic replay, not a loss alarm.
Comparing replicas to catch every corruption.
A gradient corrupted before the all-reduce is averaged into every replica, so replicas still agree (all three gradient flips passed the checksum). Replica comparison catches corruption after the all-reduce; replay catches both.
Restoring the newest checkpoint directory on disk.
It may be incomplete or never registered: Ray Train restored step 50 with step 100 on disk, 3 runs of 3. Restore what the framework says is complete; write atomically and move a "latest" pointer last.
Putting effort into the checkpoint interval before the checkpoint pause.
Near its optimum the goodput curve is flat in the interval; it is steep in the pause. At 16,384 GPUs, 0.78 s asynchronous pauses instead of 5-minute blocking ones take the model from 77% to 97%; tuning the interval of a 5-minute checkpoint gains a point or two.
Assuming hot spares replace a health check.
A spare that was never tested is a bad node in waiting, and a replacement can be the same bad machine back (OPT-175B's logbook). Self-test spares while they wait (ByteRobust keeps them "self-checked"), and drain or taint every node that fails, so the restart lands elsewhere.
Shipping a job spec with no retries.
failurePolicy.maxRestarts: 0 (JobSet), FailureConfig(max_failures=0) (Ray Train) and plain torchrun (default --max-restarts=0) all turn one crashed rank into a dead run: measured on all three in the root page's Failure lab. Slurm never retries a job that failed by itself; resubmit it from a wrapper or let torchrun restart inside the allocation.
Reading torchrun's "Root Cause" as the culprit in a hang.
It names the first process that exited with an error, which in a hang is a healthy rank that timed out (section 3, "A hang, measured"). The root page's Launch lab saw the same on Kubernetes: each pod's summary named a waiting rank (rank 0 on pod 0, rank 2 on pod 1) while the stalled rank 3 appeared only as exitcode: -15 (SIGTERM), killed by its own agent. Find the rank everyone waited for, and dump its stack.
Assuming Kubernetes recovers from a dead node by itself.
A pod on a node whose kubelet is gone stays Terminating; a Job waiting for it never fails over (measured: stuck for over 7 minutes until the node.kubernetes.io/out-of-service taint). Automate the taint with a node-health controller.
A health check that only exits non-zero.
Slurm ignores the exit code of HealthCheckProgram; the script must run scontrol update ... state=drain itself (section 3, health checks).

Interview questions

Design the failure handling for a 16,384-GPU pre-training run. What do you build first?
Start from the numbers: at Llama 3's rate the job fails every 3.1 hours. Write down C (checkpoint pause) and R (detect + wait + replace + start + load). Build in this order: (1) asynchronous checkpoints to host memory with a background write, sized to the storage fabric, so checkpoints can be every few minutes; (2) health inspection with seconds-level detection of hung or lost GPUs and NICs, and collective timeouts shortened to just above the longest legitimate pause, with the flight recorder on; (3) hot spares inside the allocation, self-tested, so a restart never waits for the scheduler or provisioning; (4) automatic drain or taint of failed nodes, with DCGM level 2 or 3 before they return; (5) a tested restart path (kill a rank and a node in a rehearsal); (6) per-rank compute timing with a relative score for stragglers; (7) goodput accounting by bucket. In-memory peer checkpoints and replica groups come next if the measured R still dominates. In the model, defaults to async plus spares goes from about 69% to 97% goodput.
Your job's goodput is 80%. How do you find out why?
Split the lost 20% into buckets from logs: queue and scheduling, start-up, checkpoint pauses, rework after failures, recovery time per failure (detect, wait, replace, start, load), and slow steps (stragglers). Multiply rates by costs: failures per day times R and rework. The largest bucket decides the next project; often it is the checkpoint pause or a slow, manual recovery step.
All GPUs show 0% utilisation except one, and the job has printed nothing for 20 minutes. What happened and what do you do?
A hang: every rank waits in a collective for one that is stuck (the busy GPU may be the one spinning). Dump stacks and the flight recorder on all ranks, find the rank whose last collective differs, check that node (Xid in the kernel log, ECC, NVLink, NIC), drain it, restart from the last checkpoint. Afterwards shorten the timeouts, because 20 minutes of a whole cluster is the real cost.
How would you detect silent data corruption in a training run?
Know where it can enter: corruption after the all-reduce shows up as replicas that should be identical disagreeing (periodic weight checksums across data-parallel replicas); corruption before it is averaged into everyone and needs recomputation: deterministic replay of suspicious steps on other machines and comparison of per-replica gradient fingerprints (Gemini's method; measured here, it named the replica and the step in every case). Add gradient-norm and loss alarms for the loud cases and offline stress tests of suspect machines. Replay needs determinism.
Slurm, Kubernetes or Ray: which recovers a lost node best out of the box?
Measured on the topic's lab clusters: Slurm requeued automatically and resumed in 166 s once its heartbeat timeout fired; Ray declared the node dead in 30 s and then waited, because its worker group needed capacity that no longer existed; Kubernetes with a silent node waited until an operator applied the out-of-service taint (445 s). None of them recovers quickly by default; each needs spares and tuned timeouts. Details in the root's Failure lab.
Why does a slow GPU hurt more than a dead one, sometimes?
A dead GPU stops the job and triggers recovery; a slow one silently sets the pace of every synchronous step and triggers nothing. Lin et al. found 42.5% of large jobs at least 10% slower and 10.4% of GPU hours wasted, more than many failure budgets.
Elastic training or restart at full size?
Elastic keeps going on fewer machines but changes the per-GPU batch, the parallel layout or the data each step sees; most pre-training runs restart at full size on spares instead. Elastic fits preemptible capacity and jobs whose optimisation tolerates batch changes (or that keep the global batch with gradient accumulation).
A 2,048-GPU job at Llama 3's failure rate, checkpoints take 30 s to write. How often does it fail and how often should it checkpoint?
MTBF scales as 1/N: 3.09 h x 16,384 / 2,048 = about 24.7 h. Young: τ = √(2 x 30 s x 89,100 s) = about 2,310 s, roughly every 39 minutes of work; checkpoint overhead C/τ is about 1.3% and expected rework τ/2M about 1.3% calculation. Then say what would change it: async checkpointing shrinks C, slow recovery adds R/M per failure.
Your resumed run's loss differs from the original in the 7th digit. Bug?
Probably not, if it is a last-bit difference with no jump: the reduction order changed (here DDP rebuilds its buckets after a process's first step; on GPUs, nondeterministic kernels). A jump at the resume step is a bug: optimiser state, LR schedule step, data position or RNG state was not restored.

Before comparing: the units differ
A share of interruptions of one big job (Llama 3, Meta 32K, C4) is dominated by hardware, because only a long synchronous job on thousands of GPUs sees thousands of hardware faults. A share of failed jobs in a multi-tenant cluster (Philly, Acme, ByteRobust) is dominated by user code, because most jobs are small and most bugs show up in the first minutes. Both are true; they answer different questions. Where a report also gives the GPU time each cause cost, switch to it: Acme's script errors are 73.4% of its failed jobs and 3.2% of the GPU time lost. What the tables agree on

  • In big synchronous jobs, GPUs and their memory lead. GPU compute plus GPU memory is 64% of Llama 3's interruptions by count and 37% of the 2026 Meta 32K job's, where HBM is now the largest single row (22.9%) and PCIe devices second (18.0%); C4's one 4,096-GPU job: 40% (CUDA plus ECC/NVLink), with the network the larger share.
  • In multi-tenant clusters, users' code leads by count, hardware by cost. Philly and Acme: most failed jobs are programming errors that fail in minutes; the rare infrastructure failures hit long, big jobs and take most of the GPU time (Acme: infrastructure 10.9% of failed jobs, 82.1% of the GPU time).
  • Hangs and unknowns are a large, stubborn share. NCCL watchdog timeouts are 9.0% of the Meta 32K table; ByteRobust's job hangs 9.9%; NODE_FAIL with no attribution is the largest hardware signal at Meta's research clusters. Detection, not repair, is where those cost time.
  • Network faults are the largest share where the paper looks at the network (Unicron 48.6%, C4 47.5%), and small where it does not (Llama 3: switches and cables 8.4%, NICs 1.7%). A cause table reflects its authors' instrumentation as much as the cluster.

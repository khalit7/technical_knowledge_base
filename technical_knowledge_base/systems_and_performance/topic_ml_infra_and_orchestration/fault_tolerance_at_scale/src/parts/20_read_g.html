8. Stragglers: how common, and how production systems find them

A straggler costs no restart and raises no alarm, so it is easy to underestimate. The one large published study says it is the common case: in ByteDance's clusters, "42.5% of the jobs are at least 10% slower" than they would be without stragglers, and "10.4% of the allocated GPU hours are wasted" published (Lin et al., {{arXiv 2505.05713v2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05713}}, five months of 2024, 3,079 jobs of 128 GPUs or more).

Two disagreements worth knowing. Lin et al. find stragglers mostly in compute; FALCON's production cases at four nodes were mostly network congestion (42 of 43). Both can be right: they measure different clusters and different job mixes, and a job's own imbalance (pipeline stages, sequence lengths) looks like a straggler to a detector that only compares ranks. That is the practical point: a detector that names a rank still needs a second step that tells a slow machine (drain it) from a slow role in the job (fix the code).

Every one of these detectors compares compute time across ranks or across time, never collective time alone. The reason is the inversion measured in the root's Failure lab and replayed in the {{Straggler replay|#t-str}} tab: the slow rank arrives last and waits least, so ranking ranks by time spent in the all-reduce names its victims. On the replay's real timings, the relative score at NVIDIA's 0.75 flags the planted sustained straggler after 4 steps with a 10-step median window and raises no false alarm on the run without stragglers measured; the wait-based rule never names it.

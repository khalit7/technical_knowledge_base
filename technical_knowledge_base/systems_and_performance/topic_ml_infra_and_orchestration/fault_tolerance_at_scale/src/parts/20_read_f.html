7. Silent data corruption: one bit, measured

A silent data corruption (SDC) is a wrong result that raises no error: a marginal transistor in a multiplier, a bit flipped in a register or a cache line that ECC does not cover. ECC (error-correcting codes) protects data at rest in memory; it does not check arithmetic. So the run keeps going, on wrong numbers. The {{hardware reliability page|n:3f05c17b0d0d8143851cfc3ae14c3c22}} explains the hardware side and the fleet studies of CPUs; this section is about what one flipped bit does to a data-parallel training run and which detector would have caught it.

What has been published about it

Suspect machines are located by replaying in groups, machines grouped one way (by x divided by m) and then another (x modulo n), so the faulty machine is the one in both failing groups. Across 19 jobs of 9,600 GPUs or more, "only 1.23% of failures required dual-phase replay" authors' figures.
Ma et al. (Harvard and AWS, {{arXiv 2502.12340|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12340}}, Feb 2025)Ran training in lock-step on 15 nodes flagged unhealthy and 15 healthy ones and compared every value: mismatches were rare but could be huge (one backward-pass value 9.63e+12), the gradient noise small (at worst 5.1% of the gradient norm), pre-training loss curves "identical" while the parameters drifted to "different optima"; in fine-tuning, loss spikes, one of them ending in zero test accuracy on a benchmark published.

The experiment

We took the running example (4 data-parallel replicas, 200 steps, deterministic), and in six runs flipped exactly one bit at step 120 on replica 2: either in its local gradient just before the all-reduce (the corruption happened in the backward pass), or in one of its weights just after the optimiser step (the corruption happened in the update, or in memory on that one chip). Three bit positions each: a low mantissa bit, an exponent bit and the top exponent bit. Every step logged the global loss, the global gradient norm, a checksum of every replica's weights (the sum of their bit patterns, compared across replicas), and a fingerprint of each replica's local gradients. A clean run is the reference; a replay from the step-100 checkpoint recomputes steps 101 to 150 on healthy hardware.

Detectors, each run over the logged values by src/scripts/make_data.py: loss spike, the global loss is non-finite or more than 6 median absolute deviations from the median of the previous 20 steps; gradient norm, non-finite or more than 10 times the median of the previous 20 steps; checksum, the replicas' weight checksums differ; replay, a replica's local-gradient fingerprint differs from the replayed one. The loss-spike rule also fired once on the clean run (step 84): a real bump in the loss, a false alarm.

What it shows

  • Most flips are invisible in the loss. In five of the six runs the global loss never moved by more than 1e-4, far below its step-to-step noise; only the top exponent bit of a weight made noise, by overflowing to NaN. A loss-curve alarm catches the loud tail, not SDC in general.
  • A huge gradient did not blow up training. Flipping the top exponent bit of the largest gradient element made it about 1.6e37 and the gradient norm about 3.9e36, yet the final loss moved only in the fourth decimal. AdamW divides each update by the square root of a running mean of squared gradients; our reading is that the square overflowed to infinity in float32, so this one weight's update collapsed to almost nothing and stayed there for the rest of the run, leaving one weight silently frozen our reading. A one-weight AdamW fed the same gradient shows exactly that: its update falls from about 1e-3 to under 1e-6 per step and the squared-gradient average stays infinite (src/scripts/recompute.py). A gradient-norm monitor is the only alarm that fired.
  • Before or after the all-reduce decides which detector works. A gradient corrupted before the all-reduce is averaged into every replica: they stay identical, so comparing replicas finds nothing, in all three gradient runs. A weight corrupted after it lives on one replica: the checksum comparison caught all three weight flips at the step they happened, including the low bit that changed the loss by at most 4.8e-7.
  • Deterministic replay caught every case, and named the chip. Because the running example is deterministic (same batches, fixed reduction order, no dropout), recomputing steps from the last checkpoint on healthy hardware reproduces every logged fingerprint except the corrupted one: replica 2 at step 120 for the gradient flips, replica 2 at step 121 (the first computation that used the bad weight) for the weight flips. This is the method Google describes for Gemini; it needs bit-for-bit determinism, which is why this page switched off DDP's one-time bucket rebuild (the root's Failure lab showed it changes the last bit after a restart).
What transfers to a real cluster, and what does not
Transfers: where the corruption enters relative to the all-reduce, and which comparison can see it; NaN spreading to every replica within one step; Adam's normalisation hiding a large gradient. Does not transfer directly: bf16 and fp8 training have fewer mantissa and exponent bits, so the same flip lands differently; GPU kernels are often nondeterministic, so replay needs deterministic algorithms or a tolerance; and the checksum costs a reduction over every weight, which large runs do periodically, not every step.

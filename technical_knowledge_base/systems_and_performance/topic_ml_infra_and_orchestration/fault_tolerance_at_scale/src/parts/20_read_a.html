1. Fault tolerance in one screen

A large training job is one program running in lockstep on thousands of GPUs: every step, every GPU must finish its share before any of them can take the next step. So the job is only as healthy as its least healthy part, and with enough parts something is always failing. Fault tolerance is everything that keeps such a job making progress anyway: noticing that a part failed, getting the job running again on healthy parts, and losing as little work as possible in between.

The root page's {{Failure lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b5925bfd1d8665dd4b}} broke the topic's running example (a tiny GPT trained by 4 processes, the same job on Slurm, Kubernetes and Ray) and recorded what each system did. This page goes a level deeper on the same subject and asks the questions a platform engineer is asked: what actually fails at scale, how quickly each kind of failure can be noticed, which recovery design removes which part of the lost time, how to catch the failures that raise no error at all, and how to account for the time lost.

1. Prevent

Burn-in and health checks keep bad machines out of jobs: drain a Slurm node, taint a Kubernetes node. Section 4.

2. Detect

Every layer has a timer: the launcher in 0.1 s, a collective in 10 to 30 minutes, the scheduler in tens of seconds to minutes. Slow and silent failures have no default detector at all. Sections 3, 4, 7, 8.

3. Recover

From "requeue and reload from storage" (tens of minutes) through hot spares, asynchronous and in-memory checkpoints, to replica groups where only the failed part restarts. Section 5 and the {{Recovery designs|#t-rec}} tab.

4. Verify and account

The restart must find the right checkpoint and continue the same run; the time lost is broken into named parts so you know what to fix next. Sections 6 and 9.

The numbers that frame the page. Meta's Llama 3 405B pre-training on up to 16,384 H100s was interrupted 419 times unexpectedly in 54 days, once every 3.1 hours, yet kept "higher than 90% effective training time" published ({{Llama 3 sec. 3.3.4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}). Its 2026 successor table, for a recurring 32K-GPU job, counts 678 interruptions and reports 95% to 97% effective training time published ({{arXiv 2602.00277|https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00277}}). Getting from one figure to the other is the subject of this page: the failure rate barely moved (2.3 interruptions per 1,000 servers per day in 2026, against 3.8 for Llama 3 by our arithmetic) calculation; the recovery got faster.

How the evidence on this page was made
Measured here: two new experiments on the running example, run natively on the Mac (4 processes, gloo, CPU) by src/scripts/ft_lab.py on 2026-10-16: one bit flipped in a gradient or a weight, with four detectors (section 7), and stragglers planted on a schedule with per-rank compute and wait times ({{Straggler replay|#t-str}} tab). Labelled "measured on Apple M1 Pro, natively (macOS), shared machine". Reused: the root's Failure lab's twelve recorded failures on the Slurm, Kubernetes and Ray lab clusters (Docker Desktop VM), and its goodput model. No cluster workload was run for this page. Published: every failure table we could find, read on 2026-10-16 and checked for arithmetic (extracts in src/inputs/), labelled published, or vendor / authors' figure when the source is the system's own builder. Source code quoted at PyTorch v2.14.1, Slurm 24.11.5, Ray 2.59.0, with file and line.

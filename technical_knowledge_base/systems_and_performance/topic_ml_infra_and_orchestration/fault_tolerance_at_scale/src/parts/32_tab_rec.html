Recovery designs: where the time per failure goes, and what it does to goodput

Every recovery design is an answer to one question: which part of the time lost per failure does it remove? This tab splits that time into the same five parts for every design (detect, wait for the scheduler, get a healthy machine, start up, load the checkpoint), adds the rework since the last checkpoint, and runs the root page's goodput model for any job size.

How to read the numbers
The model is the one built for the root page's Failure lab and drawn on its own in the last card of this tab (Goodput against the checkpoint interval): failures arrive at random with job MTBF M = 1 / (GPUs × failure rate per GPU); a checkpoint pauses training for C every τ of work; each failure costs the recovery time R plus the work since the last checkpoint; expected goodput = τ / (M eR/M (e(τ+C)/M − 1)) (Daly 2006), with τ at its optimum unless the design fixes it. For replica groups only the failed group's share of R is lost (R × (1 − kept share)), our approximation. calculation Inputs are published figures from different systems and hardware, combined per design, so each design row is illustrative as a whole even where every input is sourced: no single source reports these exact combinations. The two bottom designs are this topic's own measured runs of the tiny job, for scale. Not modelled: storage bandwidth (frequent checkpoints of a large model must fit the storage fabric), the window in which an asynchronous checkpoint is still being written (a failure then falls back to the previous one), queue time, and failures during recovery beyond the formula's own.

Goodput against the checkpoint interval

The cards above fix the interval at each design's optimum. This one moves it. Young's interval of work between checkpoints is τ ≈ √(2CM), with C the blocking checkpoint write and M the job's mean time between failures; Daly refined it to √(2C(M+R)) + C when the recovery R is not small. {{Power, cooling, reliability|n:3f05c17b0d0d8143851cfc3ae14c3c22}} derives both from zero; here the new input is R, the recovery the root page's lab measured, and the point is how much the recovery path matters next to the interval. The model is the root lab's, moved here on 2026-10-19; the {{measured-run accounting|#t-read}} (Reading, section 9) checks its bookkeeping on three real runs.

calculation: failures arrive at random (exponential) with job MTBF M = 1 / (N x per-GPU rate); per-GPU rates from Llama 3 (419 / (16,384 x 54 days)) or OPT (at least 105 restarts / (992 x 60 days)). Goodput = τ / (M eR/M (e(τ+C)/M − 1)), the exact expected value when failures can also strike during checkpoints and recovery (Daly 2006; the same model as the reliability page with all downtime lumped into R). The simulation replays 3,000 random failures with a fixed seed and must land on the curve.

Reading the calculator. At Llama 3's failure rate a 16,384-GPU job fails every 3.1 hours. With a 1-minute blocking checkpoint and 10 minutes to recover, the best interval is about 19 minutes of work and goodput is about 85% calculation. Cutting recovery from 10 minutes to 1 raises it to about 89%, more than the whole difference between a 10-minute and a 40-minute interval (84% and 83% against the optimum of 85%). Near the optimum the curve is flat; recovery time is not. That is why Gemini kept model replicas in memory and spare cubes, why DeepSeek checkpoints "typically every 5 minutes" asynchronously, and why the measured recovery presets (seconds for an in-job restart, 2.5 minutes for a Slurm requeue) matter more at 16,000 GPUs than at 4.

5. Recovery designs: which part of the lost time each one removes

After a failure the job loses the time to detect it, to wait for the scheduler, to get a healthy machine, to start (processes, rendezvous, communicator set-up), to load the checkpoint, and then to redo the work since that checkpoint; on top, it pays the checkpoint pause on every save, failure or not. Every design attacks some of these and leaves the rest.

Predict: a 16,384-GPU job at Llama 3's failure rate (one failure every 3.1 hours). Which single change raises goodput most: cutting detection from 10 minutes to 10 seconds, adding hot spares so no machine is provisioned, or making checkpoints asynchronous (a 0.78 s pause instead of 5 minutes)?
Asynchronous checkpoints, by far, in this model calculation: from 68.9% (all defaults) to 72.7% with fast detection, 77.1% with spares as well, and 97.0% once checkpoints pause for 0.78 s instead of 5 minutes, because a cheap checkpoint can be taken every couple of minutes, which shrinks both the pauses and the rework. The other two only shorten the recovery itself, which here is a smaller part of the loss than the 40-minute optimal interval's pauses and rework. How to read the numbers
The model is the one built for the root page's Failure lab (its curve against the checkpoint interval is section 6 of the {{reliability page|n:3f05c17b0d0d8143851cfc3ae14c3c22}}): failures arrive at random with job MTBF M = 1 / (GPUs × failure rate per GPU); a checkpoint pauses training for C every τ of work; each failure costs the recovery time R plus the work since the last checkpoint; expected goodput = τ / (M eR/M (e(τ+C)/M − 1)) (Daly 2006), with τ at its optimum unless the design fixes it. For replica groups only the failed group's share of R is lost (R × (1 − kept share)), our approximation. calculation Inputs are published figures from different systems and hardware, combined per design, so each design row is illustrative as a whole even where every input is sourced: no single source reports these exact combinations. The two bottom designs are this topic's own measured runs of the tiny job, for scale. Not modelled: storage bandwidth (frequent checkpoints of a large model must fit the storage fabric), the window in which an asynchronous checkpoint is still being written (a failure then falls back to the previous one), queue time, and failures during recovery beyond the formula's own.

Elastic training and a hot spare, measured with torchrun

Elastic training lets a job continue with fewer machines instead of stopping: torchrun --nnodes=1:2 accepts any number of nodes between 1 and 2. Each node runs an agent; the agents meet at a rendezvous (the c10d backend: a small key-value store that one agent hosts) to agree who is in the job, then start the workers. When a worker fails, every agent stops its workers, meets again with whoever is still there, and restarts them all from the latest checkpoint with the new world size.

Lose one of two nodes, then add one back

measured in one container capped at 2 CPUs and 2.2 GB: two torchrun agents (A, B) of 2 ranks each, c10d rendezvous on localhost, 400 steps, checkpoints every 50; B and its ranks are killed with SIGKILL (root page, src/fail/scripts/fl_elastic.sh, fl_spare.sh; runs el3, el4).

Where the 44 seconds go. Agent A saw its ranks fail 1.5 s after the kill. It then waited for the rendezvous to forget B, whose keep-alive stops counting after 5 s x 3 attempts, and for the last call: once the minimum number of nodes is present the rendezvous still waits 30 s for more before closing. With a spare already waiting, the job was full again at once and only paid detection and restart: 18.1 s.

Shrinking counts as a restart; growing does not. The restart counter went to 1 when B was lost and stayed at 1 when C joined: "membership changes do not count as retries" (elastic/agent/server/api.py line 958). And the shrunk run is not the same computation: the global batch stays 32 sequences, but each of 2 ranks now averages 16 rows instead of 8, so the sums happen in a different order and the loss differs in the last bits at 7 of the 15 logged steps run at world size 2 (by up to 4.8e-7) against an uninterrupted 4-rank run measured. With a batch that does not divide evenly, or a data loader that shards by rank, it would also change which data each step sees.

Most large pre-training runs do not shrink: they restart at full size on spare capacity, as Gemini Ultra did with "a small number of cubes per superpod" kept for hot standbys ({{Gemini 1.0, section 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11805}}) published; the spare run above is that pattern at toy scale. The store-host single point of failure and the shared-store restart bug these runs also hit are traps 1 and 2 of section 6; the rendezvous mechanism in full is on {{Launching distributed training|n:3fc5c17b0d0d81b3a8a5c58224bff9fa}}.

Google reports a 1,024-GPU job going from about 80% to over 90% goodput, the largest gain from asynchronous checkpoints (+3 to 4 points) and the rest from more frequent checkpoints, faster recovery and elastic scale-down vendor: the same order as the prediction above, make checkpoints cheap first.

Managed versions, and Google's gains point by point (depth)

Managed versions exist and package several rows: {{AWS SageMaker HyperPod|https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sagemaker/latest/dg/sagemaker-hyperpod-resiliency-slurm-auto-resume.html}} replaces nodes found by its health agent and deep checks (DCGM level 4, EFA, NCCL tests) and resumes with srun --auto-resume=1 (what the managed platforms take off your hands, SageMaker jobs against HyperPod included, is {{ML platforms and workflows|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b9943cf6c72600f96b}}, section 10); its December 2025 {{"checkpointless" training|https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/checkpointless-training-on-amazon-sagemaker-hyperpod-production-scale-training-with-faster-fault-recovery/}} reports recovery cut from "15 to 30+ min" to under 2 minutes (under 90 s at 2,304 GPUs) vendor. Google reports a 1,024-GPU (A3 Mega) job going from about 80% to over 90% goodput by stacking asynchronous checkpoints (+3 to 4 points), checkpoints every 20 minutes instead of hours (+2 to 3), recovery from 20 to about 2 minutes (+1 to 2) and elastic scale-down (+1 to 2) vendor ({{Google Cloud blog, May 2025|https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/elastic-training-and-optimized-checkpointing-improve-ml-goodput}}). The order of those gains is the same as the prediction above: make checkpoints cheap first.

Choosing, in one paragraph
Measure your failure rate and your current R and C first (section 9). Asynchronous checkpoints are the cheapest large win at almost any scale. Hot spares and fast detection matter once recovery is a large share of the MTBF, roughly from thousands of GPUs. In-memory checkpoints and replica groups matter when the MTBF falls to tens of minutes (about 30 minutes at 100,000 GPUs at Llama 3's rate), where even a fast storage restart is a large share of every interval. Elastic resizing and redundant computation fit preemptible capacity better than a reserved cluster. Every design needs its restart path exercised before the real run needs it.

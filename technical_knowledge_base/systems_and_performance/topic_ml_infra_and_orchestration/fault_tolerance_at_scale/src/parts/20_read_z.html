10. Common mistakes

Quoting one failure share without its unit.
"Hardware causes 78% of failures" (Llama 3, one job's interruptions) and "user code causes 73% of failures" (Acme, a cluster's failed jobs) are both right. Say what was counted, and weight by GPU time when the question is cost.
Copying a table's headline without checking it.
Llama 3's 58.7% GPU share inherits a misprinted row (64.0% by counts); ByteRobust's text and Table 1 disagree by 1,451 explicit failures; Meta's 2026 "a failure every 18 minutes" at 100K GPUs does not follow from its own rate. Recompute before you quote.
Leaving the NCCL timeouts at their defaults on a big job.
A frozen GPU then costs every other GPU 10 minutes before the watchdog acts (Watchdog caught collective operation timeout: ... ran for 600000 milliseconds before timing out.), and a wedged watchdog 8 more minutes (ProcessGroupNCCL's watchdog got stuck for 480 seconds without making progress in monitoring enqueued collectives.). Shorten them to just above your longest legitimate pause, and add health inspections that catch a hung GPU in seconds.
Ranking ranks by collective time to find a straggler.
The slow rank waits least. On the replay, the "long waits" rule flagged the three healthy ranks hundreds of times and the slow one never. Compare compute time across ranks (relative score) or against the rank's own history (individual score).
Treating a flat loss curve as proof of healthy hardware.
Five of the six bit flips measured here moved the loss by less than 1e-4, and one froze a weight for the rest of the run. Silent corruption needs checksums across replicas or deterministic replay, not a loss alarm.
Comparing replicas to catch every corruption.
A gradient corrupted before the all-reduce is averaged into every replica, so replicas still agree (all three gradient flips passed the checksum). Replica comparison catches corruption after the all-reduce; replay catches both.
Restoring the newest checkpoint directory on disk.
It may be incomplete or never registered: Ray Train restored step 50 with step 100 on disk, 3 runs of 3. Restore what the framework says is complete; write atomically and move a "latest" pointer last.
Putting effort into the checkpoint interval before the checkpoint pause.
Near its optimum the goodput curve is flat in the interval; it is steep in the pause. At 16,384 GPUs, 0.78 s asynchronous pauses instead of 5-minute blocking ones take the model from 77% to 97%; tuning the interval of a 5-minute checkpoint gains a point or two.
Assuming hot spares replace a health check.
A spare that was never tested is a bad node in waiting, and a replacement can be the same bad machine back (OPT-175B's logbook). Self-test spares while they wait (ByteRobust keeps them "self-checked"), and drain or taint every node that fails, so the restart lands elsewhere.

11. Interview questions

Design the failure handling for a 16,384-GPU pre-training run. What do you build first?
Start from the numbers: at Llama 3's rate the job fails every 3.1 hours. Write down C (checkpoint pause) and R (detect + wait + replace + start + load). Build in this order: (1) asynchronous checkpoints to host memory with a background write, sized to the storage fabric, so checkpoints can be every few minutes; (2) health inspection with seconds-level detection of hung or lost GPUs and NICs, and collective timeouts shortened to just above the longest legitimate pause, with the flight recorder on; (3) hot spares inside the allocation, self-tested, so a restart never waits for the scheduler or provisioning; (4) automatic drain or taint of failed nodes, with DCGM level 2 or 3 before they return; (5) a tested restart path (kill a rank and a node in a rehearsal); (6) per-rank compute timing with a relative score for stragglers; (7) goodput accounting by bucket. In-memory peer checkpoints and replica groups come next if the measured R still dominates. In the model, defaults to async plus spares goes from about 69% to 97% goodput.
Your job's goodput is 80%. How do you find out why?
Split the lost 20% into buckets from logs: queue and scheduling, start-up, checkpoint pauses, rework after failures, recovery time per failure (detect, wait, replace, start, load), and slow steps (stragglers). Multiply rates by costs: failures per day times R and rework. The largest bucket decides the next project; often it is the checkpoint pause or a slow, manual recovery step.
All GPUs show 0% utilisation except one, and the job has printed nothing for 20 minutes. What happened and what do you do?
A hang: every rank waits in a collective for one that is stuck (the busy GPU may be the one spinning). Dump stacks and the flight recorder on all ranks, find the rank whose last collective differs, check that node (Xid in the kernel log, ECC, NVLink, NIC), drain it, restart from the last checkpoint. Afterwards shorten the timeouts, because 20 minutes of a whole cluster is the real cost.
How would you detect silent data corruption in a training run?
Know where it can enter: corruption after the all-reduce shows up as replicas that should be identical disagreeing (periodic weight checksums across data-parallel replicas); corruption before it is averaged into everyone and needs recomputation: deterministic replay of suspicious steps on other machines and comparison of per-replica gradient fingerprints (Gemini's method; measured here, it named the replica and the step in every case). Add gradient-norm and loss alarms for the loud cases and offline stress tests of suspect machines. Replay needs determinism.
Slurm, Kubernetes or Ray: which recovers a lost node best out of the box?
Measured on the topic's lab clusters: Slurm requeued automatically and resumed in 166 s once its heartbeat timeout fired; Ray declared the node dead in 30 s and then waited, because its worker group needed capacity that no longer existed; Kubernetes with a silent node waited until an operator applied the out-of-service taint (445 s). None of them recovers quickly by default; each needs spares and tuned timeouts. Details in the root's Failure lab.
Why does a slow GPU hurt more than a dead one, sometimes?
A dead GPU stops the job and triggers recovery; a slow one silently sets the pace of every synchronous step and triggers nothing. Lin et al. found 42.5% of large jobs at least 10% slower and 10.4% of GPU hours wasted, more than many failure budgets.
Elastic training or restart at full size?
Elastic keeps going on fewer machines but changes the per-GPU batch, the parallel layout or the data each step sees; most pre-training runs restart at full size on spares instead. Elastic fits preemptible capacity and jobs whose optimisation tolerates batch changes (or that keep the global batch with gradient accumulation).

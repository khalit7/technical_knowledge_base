Interview questions

Design: shard Llama 3 70B for pre-training on a full TPU v5p pod. What mesh, and why?

State first: 70.55B parameters at 16 bytes each is 1.13 TB of weights, gradients and Adam state (derived), so no form of pure data parallelism fits in 95 GiB chips; the state must be sharded. The scaling book takes 8,960 chips (16x20x28) and a 4M-token batch (1,024 sequences of 4,096), that is 468 tokens per chip: below the 2,550 that pure FSDP needs on v5p and above the about 113 that mixed FSDP plus tensor parallelism needs (α²/(MXMYF) with F = 28,672, MXMY = 2). The best FSDP degree is √(B/F · MX/MY · N) ≈ 1,619 with MX/MY = 2, and tensor parallelism must stay below F/2,550 ≈ 11. The book's answer: "roughly 2048-way FSDP and 4-way tensor parallelism", with tensor parallelism on the axis with the fewest hops; its takeaway box phrases the same budget as "data parallelism (1024-way), sequence parallelism (2-way), and tensor parallelism (4-way)". At 40% MFU it estimates "about 44 days" ({{scaling book, part 6|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/applied-training/}}). In JAX: a mesh ('fsdp', 'tensor') made with jax.make_mesh over the slice, parameters sharded by logical rules, activations constrained at block boundaries, jax.checkpoint per layer, and orbax for sharded checkpoints. Mention what you would check first: the compiled HLO for unplanned all-gathers, and memory_analysis() against HBM.

GSPMD and Shardy: what is the difference, and does it change your code?

Both spread a few annotations through the program and hand the result to the same SPMD partitioner. GSPMD represents a sharding as a tile assignment over devices and propagates with operator priorities over repeated forward and backward passes; Shardy represents it per dimension with named mesh axes (open or closed), describes each op with a factor-based rule, and resolves conflicts through an explicit hierarchy (user priorities, op priorities, aggressive, basic). Code does not change, but the result can: measured here, the same tensor-parallel train step compiled to 18 collectives under Shardy and 167 under GSPMD, and the unconstrained FSDP step to 210 under Shardy and 148 under GSPMD. Constraining activations makes both agree.

Your FSDP step is far slower than data parallel at the same batch. What do you look for?

All-gathers of activations rather than weights: attribute the collectives in compile().as_text() to source lines and look for [batch, sequence, hidden]-shaped gathers (in the root lab's unconstrained FSDP case, 33.5 MB of MLP-hidden all-gathers in the forward and the same again in the backward). Fix with with_sharding_constraint on the residual stream (or Explicit mode). Then check the per-chip batch against C/Wici, whether weights are re-gathered in the backward pass, and whether the gradient reduction is a reduce-scatter (on TPU it should be).

Why can tensor parallelism not just go to 64-way?

Its traffic is activations per layer, independent of how many devices share it, while the compute per device falls as 1/Y; past Y ≈ F/2,550 on v5p it is communication-bound. It must also divide the number of (KV) heads: measured here, 8-way tensor parallelism over 4 heads cost 1.33 times the predicted bytes.

Ring attention or all-gather context parallelism?

Same bytes per device; the all-gather version is simpler and holds all keys and values for a moment (273 MB of temp per device against 123 MB at 8,192 tokens, measured here), the ring keeps only one block but needs N sequential steps whose transfers must overlap compute. Both need a load-balanced token order under a causal mask, or the last device does N times the first one's work.

Why is pipeline parallelism less common on TPU than on GPU clusters?

Inside a slice, ICI connects every chip on a torus at high bandwidth, so FSDP and tensor parallelism reach thousands of chips; the bubble ((S−1)/(MR+S−1)) and the memory for microbatches in flight are costs you only pay when the network forces you to. GPU clusters hit slow inter-node links at 8 GPUs, which is where pipelining pays. The scaling book says the same in its pipelining section.

What does jax.distributed.initialize() do, and what breaks if one host skips a step?

It connects the processes through a coordinator so that jax.devices() is global and compiled programs span every host. Every process must then run the same jitted computations in the same order; one that branches differently hangs the others in a collective. Reading a global array with np.asarray fails on purpose ("spans non-addressable (non process local) devices"); use process_allgather or addressable_shards.

An MoE layer: why write the dispatch with shard_map instead of trusting propagation?

Routing is data-dependent and the efficient collective is an all-to-all that propagation finds only sometimes: measured here, 1 of 6 configurations got all-to-all from the GShard-style einsum code, the other 5 got all-gathers and an all-reduce. Written by hand it is always exactly two all-to-alls forward. Production code goes further with ragged_all_to_all (MaxText's moe.py, ) to avoid padding to a fixed capacity.

A pipeline gives each group of devices a run of consecutive layers and streams microbatches through them. Here it is written in JAX by hand, run on the running example, and checked against one device.

The problem. When a model's layers do not fit, or the links between groups of devices are slow, you can cut the model by depth: stages of consecutive layers, each on its own devices, passing activations forward and gradients back. To keep stages busy the batch is cut into microbatches. Stages still idle at the start and the end: the bubble. The general theory and the GPU schedules (1F1B, interleaved, zero bubble, DualPipe) are on {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}} and the {{Distributed PyTorch|n:3c65c17b0d0d815e85aff8decd8f35ab}} page's "Pipeline schedules" tab; this tab is about how JAX does it.

How JAX does it. No propagator invents a pipeline: it is a loop over time. The GSPMD paper reduced it to sharding ({{section 3.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.04663}}): keep a buffer with one slot per stage, run every stage's layer at once with vmap, and shift the buffer by one each iteration; shard the stage dimension and "GSPMD will turn the buffer shifting into cross-device communication via CollectivePermute". MaxText's pipeline is this design: a jax.vmap over stages () and shard_map rotations with ppermute over the stage axis (). The version below writes the per-device loop directly in shard_map, which is easier to read, and lets jax.grad differentiate the whole loop: the backward pass is the forward schedule run in reverse (GPipe's order: all forwards, then all backwards).

Three details carry the design. The stage index comes from jax.lax.axis_index("stage"), so one program serves every stage: whether a device reads a new microbatch or the buffer is a jnp.where, not Python control flow. rot sends stage j's output to j+1 and the last stage's back to stage 0: for circular schedules that is the path to the next repeat (stage 0 keeps it in circ until its turn). And the result is handed to every stage with a psum over stage of the last stage's outputs, an all-reduce you could avoid by computing the loss on the last stage only.

Circular schedules: why repeats shrink the bubble


With R repeats each stage holds R non-consecutive blocks of layers (stage 0 of 2: layers 0 and 2; stage 1: layers 1 and 3) and every microbatch goes round the ring R times. A tick now costs 1/R of a GPipe tick, and the S−1 bubble ticks are 1/R as long: on the running example, 2 stages with 4 microbatches, the bubble falls from 1/5 (GPipe, ticks of 2 layers) to 1/9 (circular, ticks of 1 layer). The price is R times as many transfers (16 collective-permutes against 8) and a ring back from the last stage to the first. MaxText's config notes the trade-off: more layers per stage "the easier it is to hide the pipeline communication behind the compute", more repeats a smaller bubble. With M = S (the 2-microbatch run) the circular schedule needs no storage at stage 0: the last stage's output arrives exactly when stage 0 needs it.


Not run here: 1F1B or zero-bubble orderings, which need the backward of each microbatch scheduled by hand (for example with jax.vjp per stage) instead of differentiating the whole loop; and timing, which on simulated CPU devices would mean nothing.

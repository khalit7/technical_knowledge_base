Sharding and scale: Mesh, GSPMD, shard_map and multi-host

In JAX you write the step for one device and give a few arrays a layout over a named grid of devices; someone then lays out every other array (the compiler's propagation, the type system, or you inside shard_map), and the communication is whatever those layouts force. Communication is a consequence of layout, so you read it off the compiled program, never off the Python.

Three annotations in, one program per device out, with the collectives the layouts force

1. You annotate a few arrays
mesh = jax.make_mesh((2, 4), ('data', 'model'))
w = jax.device_put(w, NamedSharding(mesh, P('data', 'model')))
x = jax.device_put(x, NamedSharding(mesh, P('data', None)))
loss, grads = jax.jit(step)(w, x)

The running example's fc1 weight w [128, 512] and a batch x [32, 128], on 8 devices arranged as a (data 2, model 4) mesh. Nothing else in step mentions devices.

You know how PyTorch spreads a model over GPUs: you pick a wrapper (DDP, FSDP2, a tensor-parallel plan) or a DTensor placement, and it owns the communication. JAX works the other way round: change one PartitionSpec and a different set of collectives appears in the compiled program, while the Python code does not change.

You need JAX core (jit, tracing, vmap), the parallelism forms from Distributed Training (this page shows only JAX's API and behaviour for each), and HLO dumps from XLA and compilation.

measured means run here on an Apple M1 Pro CPU with 8 simulated devices (XLA_FLAGS=--xla_force_host_platform_device_count=8, jax 0.11.2): the compiled programs, the collectives and their sizes, the memory figures and the losses are real and deterministic; speeds are not (a "collective" between simulated devices is a memory copy). TPU numbers are published by Google; arithmetic on them is derived.

1. Mesh, PartitionSpec and jit with shardings

The objects. jax.make_mesh((2, 4), ('data', 'model')) () arranges the 8 devices as a 2 × 4 grid with named axes. A PartitionSpec says, for each dimension of an array, which mesh axes split it; a mesh axis the spec does not name holds copies. A NamedSharding(mesh, spec) joins the two. Take the running example's first MLP weight, fc1, [128, 512] in float32 (262,144 bytes), and change only its spec:

One weight, six PartitionSpecs: which block each device holds

Predict before you click P(None, 'model'): how many devices hold each block of the weight?

Spec

The rule (derived from the definition, and matching what visualize_array_sharding printed for P('data', 'model'): (64, 128) per device): the size each device holds along dimension i is sizei divided by the product of the sizes of the mesh axes named for i; every element is held by as many devices as the product of the axes named nowhere. A tuple such as ('data', 'model') splits one dimension over both axes, data major. An axis may appear only once per spec: P('data', 'data') is refused because the spec "has duplicate entries" ().

Where shardings enter a program. jax.device_put(x, NamedSharding(mesh, spec)) places an array. jax.jit(f, in_shardings=..., out_shardings=...) fixes the layout of a function's inputs and outputs (the multi-host run in section 12 uses in_shardings=(rep, rep, xs, xs)); without them, jit takes the layouts its arguments already have. Inside, jax.lax.with_sharding_constraint(y, spec) () pins one intermediate. Every other array is left to whoever decides layouts along each mesh axis (section 2). JAX turns a NamedSharding into GSPMD's or Shardy's own notation (, ) when it hands the program to XLA.

2. Who decides the layout: Auto, Explicit, Manual

The problem. A train step creates hundreds of intermediate arrays (activations, gradients, optimizer updates). You annotate a handful: the parameters, the batch. Every other array needs a sharding too (which device holds which piece), and every place where an operation needs its inputs laid out differently from how they are is a collective. Something has to fill in the rest.

How JAX answers. Each named axis of a Mesh has an axis type (jax.sharding.AxisType), and the type says who decides along that axis (the hero's third panel):

Explicit when you want every layout decision visible and checked, and accept writing out_sharding= in the places JAX asks. Manual for patterns no propagator invents: ring attention, pipelines, custom MoE dispatch, overlapping a collective with compute.






3. How sharding propagation decides


The problem. Assigning a layout to every array so that total communication is smallest is a search over an exponential space; production compilers do not solve it. Both of XLA's propagators are heuristics that spread your annotations through the program and never invent a new split you did not ask for. Knowing their rules is how you predict where they will go wrong. First see one at work: a transformer block of the running example (forward and backward), every sharding read from the HLO that XLA dumped right after its propagation pass.

The propagation rules in their authors' words (3 min)


GSPMD (2021): priorities and iterations


The design is in Xu et al., GSPMD, arXiv 2105.04663, section 3.5 (v2, December 2021). Its rules, in order of importance:


    

  1. Preserved dimensions pass through. "XLA operators typically preserve some dimensions from inputs to outputs. We simply propagate sharding of such a dimension from inputs to outputs, and vice versa." A batch split on the input therefore reaches every activation.
    2. 

  2. Compatible shardings merge. A matmul's output takes its row split from one operand and its column split from the other when they use different mesh axes (the paper's Figure 3: AyB times BCx gives AyCx).
    3. 

  3. Iterate to a fixed point, refining only. The pass runs "in multiple iterations, and alternates between forward propagation (input to output) and backward propagation (output to input)"; it "changes the sharding on a tensor only when it finds a more fine-grained sharding", which guarantees it stops.
    4. 

  4. Priorities break ties. "Elementwise operators are assigned the highest priority to propagate through in both directions ... Operators like Dot, which add or remove dimensions are assigned lower priority", and a broadcast propagates backward before forward. Without priorities, the visiting order decides and "may result in mismatched sharding specifications around an elementwise operator" (Figure 4).
    5. 



The paper is explicit about the limit: "We decided to keep the sharding propagation simple, so it does not try to create new sharding patterns on dimensions. The propagation result may not always be optimal", and the remedy it gives is the one you will use: "the user can explicitly annotate the output to precisely control the sharding decision."


Shardy: axes, factors and a hierarchy


Shardy is the MLIR-based successor that JAX switched to by default in 0.7.0 ("JAX is migrating from GSPMD to Shardy by default"). The Shardy propagation doc and sharding representation doc (both read 2026-10-15) describe three differences:


    

  • Axis-based, not device-list-based. A sharding names mesh axes per dimension, like a PartitionSpec; GSPMD's HloSharding is a tile assignment over a device list. A dimension can be open (?: "open for propagation to further shard it along additional axes") or closed.
    • 

  • One rule per operation, in factors. Each op gets an einsum-like sharding rule, a matmul being (i, k), (k, j) -> (i, j); reshapes use compound factors such as ((ij), k). "Shardy propagates sharding axes along factors instead of dimensions": project the operands' shardings onto factors, merge, project back.
    • 

  • An explicit hierarchy of strategies, "interpreted as nested for loops": user priorities ({"x"}p1), then operation priorities ("pass-through" ops such as elementwise and reshape first, dot and reduce later, the same idea as GSPMD's), then aggressive propagation (resolves conflicts), then basic propagation ("propagates axes that are compatible between all operands and results", no conflict resolution). Loops, conditionals and shard_map regions are crossed through "data flow edges".
    • 





Both propagators hand their result to the same SPMD partitioner, which rewrites the program per device and inserts the collectives: XLA's CPU pipeline adds either sdy::ShardyXLA () or ShardingPropagation (), then StatefulRngSpmdPartitioner.

4. Explicit mode on the whole running example


The problem it solves. In Auto mode a bad layout costs you nothing at trace time and a lot at run time: the compiler quietly inserts whatever collective makes the program correct. Explicit mode moves the decision to trace time. Each operation has a rule mapping input shardings to an output sharding; when the rule cannot decide, or would need communication you did not ask for, tracing stops with a ShardingTypeError. The JAX guide states the intent: the rules "prefer to error and require annotation rather than falling back to arbitrarily chosen defaults", to "get important parallelism decisions in your face, rather than hide them" (JAX docs, Distributed arrays and automatic parallelization, read 2026-10-15).


What it costs, measured. The running example's loss and gradient, traced on an Explicit mesh exactly as written for Auto mode, then with the smallest change each error asks for. Three lessons from the ladder. First, even plain data parallelism needs one annotation: the embedding lookup tok_emb[idx] is a gather whose output layout JAX will not guess, so you write tok_emb.at[idx].get(out_sharding=P('data', None, None)) (). Second, FSDP in Explicit mode is literally written out: jax.reshard(w, P()) before each use is the all-gather, and the gradient comes back with the weight's own sharding (float32[128@data,512]), because the transpose of a reshard is a reshard. Adding arrays split on the same axis along different dimensions is refused for the same reason, with the same fix. Third, Megatron's row-parallel matmul is the textbook ambiguous case: both operands are split along the summed dimension, so the result is a partial sum on every device, and you must say whether you want it summed and where (jnp.einsum(..., out_sharding=P('data', None, None)), ). After the fixes, data parallel compiles to the same single all-reduce of 3,305,476 bytes as the root lab's Auto-mode layout, tensor parallel to 17 all-reduces (loss and gradient only, no optimizer here), and every loss is within 4.8e-7 of one device.


JAX also has unreduced types, where "the logical value equals the distributed sum of the physical shards' values along that axis", for delaying a reduction (shown as f32[8,16]{U:X} in the guide). Not run here: the guide marks the details as still to come.

7. Context parallelism: ring attention against all-gather


The problem. At long context the activations of one sequence no longer fit on one device, and splitting the batch does not help when the batch is one sequence. Context parallelism splits the sequence itself: device d holds tokens dT/N to (d+1)T/N − 1 of every sequence. Everything except attention is per-token and needs no communication. Attention needs every query to meet every earlier key. The three traffic patterns and the causal load balancing are Distributed Training's section 8; here, what JAX compiles for each.


Three ways, run here. The running example's block-0 attention (q, k and v [32, 4 heads, 128 tokens, 32], real weights, causal) with the 128 tokens split 16 per device over 8 devices (src/runs/ss_cp.py):


    

  • Let propagation do it: annotate q, k, v as sequence-split and jit ordinary attention. The compiler all-gathers K and V.
    • 

  • All-gather by hand in shard_map: gather K and V, attend your own queries with the causal mask shifted by your offset. This is the variant Llama 3 used (Llama 3 paper, arXiv 2407.21783, section 3.3.2) and MaxText's default (context_parallel_strategy: "all_gather", ).
    • 

  • Ring attention (Liu, Zaharia and Abbeel 2023, arXiv 2310.01889): pass K/V blocks round the ring with ppermute, N steps, accumulating an online softmax (running max, denominator and numerator), so no device ever holds all keys or a full score matrix.
    •

What the numbers say. All three give the one-device result (largest difference 7.5e-8 for the ring; gradients within 2.9e-6 for all three). Per device the ring sends exactly what a ring all-gather of K and V would (7 blocks of each: 3,670,016 bytes, against the all-gather's 4,194,304-byte output of which 7/8 arrives from other devices). The gain is memory: at 8,192 tokens the compiled temp memory is 123 MB per device for the ring against 273 MB for the all-gather version, because nobody holds all keys and values. The cost is N sequential steps, each needing its transfer to overlap with compute. The schedule also shows the known flaw of a plain causal ring: device 0 has work in 1 of 8 steps and device 7 in all 8. Load-balanced orderings (each device holds a chunk from the start and one from the end of the sequence) fix that; MaxText's context_parallel_load_balance: true is the default (). Not run here: the load-balanced ordering, and DeepSpeed-Ulysses, which uses all-to-all to swap the sequence split for a head split (arXiv 2309.14509; MaxText's "ulysses" option).


Megatron's sequence parallelism is a different thing (30 s)
Megatron's sequence parallelism (Korthikanti et al. 2022, arXiv 2205.05198) is a different thing despite the name: inside a tensor-parallel group it splits the LayerNorm and dropout activations along the sequence, turning each all-reduce into a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather of the same total size. On CPU the reduce-scatter cannot be formed (no ReduceScatterCreator in the pipeline), so it is not shown separately here.






8. Pipeline parallelism, written by hand


A pipeline gives each group of devices a run of consecutive layers (a stage) and streams microbatches through them; stages idle at the start and the end, the bubble. The theory and the GPU schedules (1F1B, interleaved, zero bubble, DualPipe) are Distributed Training's section 6 and the Distributed PyTorch page's section 8 (pipelining: stages and schedules); this section is how JAX does it.


No propagator invents a pipeline: it is a loop over time. The GSPMD paper reduced it to sharding (section 3.3): keep a buffer with one slot per stage, run every stage's layer at once with vmap, and shift the buffer by one each iteration; shard the stage dimension and "GSPMD will turn the buffer shifting into cross-device communication via CollectivePermute". MaxText's pipeline is this design: a jax.vmap over stages () and shard_map rotations with ppermute over the stage axis (). The version run here writes the per-device loop directly in shard_map, which is easier to read, and lets jax.grad differentiate the whole loop: the backward pass is the forward schedule run in reverse (GPipe's order: all forwards, then all backwards).

Three details carry the design. The stage index comes from jax.lax.axis_index("stage"), so one program serves every stage: whether a device reads a new microbatch or the buffer is a jnp.where, not Python control flow. rot sends stage j's output to j+1 and the last stage's back to stage 0: for circular schedules that is the path to the next repeat (stage 0 keeps it in circ until its turn). And the result is handed to every stage with a psum over stage of the last stage's outputs, an all-reduce you could avoid by computing the loss on the last stage only.


Circular schedules: why repeats shrink the bubble. With R repeats each stage holds R non-consecutive blocks of layers (stage 0 of 2: layers 0 and 2; stage 1: layers 1 and 3) and every microbatch goes round the ring R times. A tick now costs 1/R of a GPipe tick, and the S−1 bubble ticks are 1/R as long: on the running example, 2 stages with 4 microbatches, the bubble falls from 1/5 (GPipe, ticks of 2 layers) to 1/9 (circular, ticks of 1 layer). The price is R times as many transfers (16 collective-permutes against 8) and a ring back from the last stage to the first.

9. Expert parallelism: two all-to-alls you write yourself


In a mixture-of-experts layer each token goes to a few of many expert MLPs (the form is Distributed Training's section 7). Put one expert per device and tokens must travel to their experts and back: two all-to-alls per layer. Routing is data-dependent, so propagation finds that pattern only sometimes; here it is written with shard_map.


The setup (src/runs/ss_moe.py): the 4,096 tokens of the running example's batch 0, taken after block 0 and the next LayerNorm ([4,096, 128]), 512 on each of 8 devices; a router [128, 8] and 8 expert MLPs (128 → 256 → 128, exact GELU), one per device. The router and experts are new seeded weights, illustrative, not trained: the routing is lopsided (expert 6 is wanted by 120 to 150 of each device's 512 tokens, expert 1 by 21 to 35), which makes capacity visible. Each device routes its own tokens (Switch Transformer's groups); capacity C = ⌈capacity factor × 512 × k / 8⌉ tokens per expert per device; tokens over capacity are dropped (the layer outputs 0 for them, the residual stream carries them). Top-1 is Switch's routing (Switch Transformers paper page), top-2 GShard's and Mixtral's (Mixtral paper page).

11. Choosing a mesh for a model and a TPU slice


The question. Given a model, a global batch and a slice (say 256 v5e chips or 512 v5p chips), how many ways to split for each strategy, and which physical torus axes should carry which? Three constraints decide it.


    

  1. Memory. Parameters, gradients and optimizer state (16 bytes per parameter for float32 weights, float32 gradients and two float32 Adam moments, 4 + 4 + 8; bfloat16 weights and gradients plus a float32 master copy also come to 16) divided by the FSDP and tensor degrees, plus activations divided by the batch-splitting and tensor degrees, must fit in HBM (16 GB per v5e chip, 95 GiB per v5p chip, Google Cloud TPU docs, last updated 2026-10-13).
    2. 

  2. Communication hidden under compute. The scaling book's conditions (part 5, How to parallelize a transformer for training): data parallelism and FSDP stay compute-bound while the batch per chip exceeds C/Wici, which for v5p is "2,550" tokens (C = 4.6e14 FLOP/s, Wici = 1.8e11 B/s bidirectional); spreading FSDP over all three axes of a v5p pod lowers it to about 850. Tensor parallelism "becomes communication bound when Y > MY · F / 2550", "for most models ... between 8 and 16-way". Mixing the two, the best FSDP degree is Xopt = √(B/F · MX/MY · N), which lets the batch per chip fall to about 100.
    3. 

  3. Topology. A logical mesh axis should sit on physical axes whose neighbours are one ICI hop apart, and its size must be a product of physical axis sizes. The physical torus is TPU programming and performance's subject; what matters here is how JAX maps mesh axes onto it. TPU programming and performance (section 7 and its Slice planner tab, which owns the placement) ran JAX's own create_device_mesh on mock slices: on a v5p 4x4x4 cube, a (16, 4) mesh puts the 4 on one physical axis (1 hop) and the 16 on the other two (2 hops between logical neighbours), while (8, 8) is refused outright with  unless you allow splitting physical axes.
    4. 



Across slices the network is the data-centre network (DCN), about 6.25 GB/s per chip in the book's v5p numbers, so the batch per slice must exceed C/Wdcn ≈ 73,440 tokens; the standard answer is FSDP and tensor parallelism inside the slice and pure data parallelism (or HSDP) across slices. MaxText encodes exactly this: 12 mesh axes (), each with an ici_*_parallelism and a dcn_*_parallelism degree, defaults ici_fsdp_parallelism: -1 # recommended ICI axis to be auto-sharded and dcn_data_parallelism: -1, and comments marking dcn_tensor_parallelism "never recommended" (). Model code never names a mesh axis: it names logical axes ('embed', 'heads', 'activation_batch_attn'), and logical_axis_rules () map them to mesh axes, so one config change re-lays out the whole model.


Try it

12. Many hosts: one program, many controllers


The model. A TPU slice larger than one host (one VM with a few chips attached) runs one Python process per host, all executing the same script: JAX is multi-controller. The JAX guide: "All processes (usually) run the same Python script", and "it's crucial that all processes with participating devices run the same computation in the same order", a rule that is "mostly unchecked", so a mismatch hangs (JAX docs, multi-controller JAX, read 2026-10-15). The PyTorch picture is close: torchrun starts one process per GPU and you call init_process_group; JAX starts one process per host and you call jax.distributed.initialize() (), which on Cloud TPU, Slurm or Kubernetes needs no arguments. After it, jax.devices() lists every chip of every host (the global devices) and jax.local_devices() this host's (also called addressable). A jitted function over a global array is still one SPMD program; the difference from one host is only that a process can touch the shards on its own devices and nothing else.


Run here. JAX runs the same thing on CPU with several processes on one machine, so this page does it for real: 2 processes, each owning 4 simulated devices, connected by jax.distributed over localhost (collectives between processes use gloo, the default CPU implementation, ). Optional: design questions that combine several sections, and what changed from the old Notion page.


Design: shard Llama 3 70B for pre-training on a full TPU v5p pod. What mesh, and why?


State first: 70.55B parameters at 16 bytes each is 1.13 TB of weights, gradients and Adam state (derived), so no form of pure data parallelism fits in 95 GiB chips; the state must be sharded. The scaling book takes 8,960 chips (16x20x28) and a 4M-token batch (1,024 sequences of 4,096), that is 468 tokens per chip: below the 2,550 that pure FSDP needs on v5p and above the about 113 that mixed FSDP plus tensor parallelism needs (α²/(MXMYF) with F = 28,672, MXMY = 2). The best FSDP degree is √(B/F · MX/MY · N) ≈ 1,619 with MX/MY = 2, and tensor parallelism must stay below F/2,550 ≈ 11. The book's answer: "roughly 2048-way FSDP and 4-way tensor parallelism", with tensor parallelism on the axis with the fewest hops; its takeaway box phrases the same budget as "data parallelism (1024-way), sequence parallelism (2-way), and tensor parallelism (4-way)". At 40% MFU it estimates "about 44 days" (scaling book, part 6). In JAX: a mesh ('fsdp', 'tensor') made with jax.make_mesh over the slice, parameters sharded by logical rules, activations constrained at block boundaries, jax.checkpoint per layer, and orbax for sharded checkpoints. Mention what you would check first: the compiled HLO for unplanned all-gathers, and memory_analysis() against HBM.


Your FSDP step is far slower than data parallel at the same batch. What do you look for?
All-gathers of activations rather than weights: attribute the collectives in compile().as_text() to source lines and look for [batch, sequence, hidden]-shaped gathers (in the root lab's unconstrained FSDP case, 33.5 MB of MLP-hidden all-gathers in the forward and the same again in the backward). Fix with with_sharding_constraint on the residual stream (or Explicit mode). Then check the per-chip batch against C/Wici, whether weights are re-gathered in the backward pass, and whether the gradient reduction is a reduce-scatter (on TPU it should be).


Why is pipeline parallelism less common on TPU than on GPU clusters?
Inside a slice, ICI connects every chip on a torus at high bandwidth, so FSDP and tensor parallelism reach thousands of chips; the bubble ((S−1)/(MR+S−1)) and the memory for microbatches in flight are costs you only pay when the network forces you to. GPU clusters hit slow inter-node links at 8 GPUs, which is where pipelining pays. The scaling book says the same in its pipelining section.

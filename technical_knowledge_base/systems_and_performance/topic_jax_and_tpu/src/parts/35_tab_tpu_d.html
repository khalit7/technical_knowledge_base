8. Write a kernel, call one, or let XLA do it

  • Let XLA do it for matmuls, elementwise chains, reductions and normalisation: it fuses them, picks tiles and lays out memory, and its result is portable across generations. Most of a training step should stay here.
  • Call a ready-made kernel for the well-known algorithms XLA cannot derive. The pinned jax ships TPU kernels in jax/experimental/pallas/ops/tpu/: flash_attention, splash_attention (block-sparse attention with masks), paged_attention and ragged_paged_attention (serving), megablox (grouped matmuls for mixture-of-experts) and matmul. MaxText imports splash_attention from there (layers/attention_op.py:29, main @ 1a95d1e5) and also uses kernels from {{tokamax|https://github.com/openxla/tokamax}}, OpenXLA's library of ready-made Pallas kernels (0.0.16 on PyPI, 2026-10-09; the {{Tool map|#t-map}} dates it).
  • Write your own when the algorithm is yours: a new attention variant, a fused quantised matmul, a collective overlapped with compute (Pallas TPU kernels can copy to another chip over ICI with pltpu.make_async_remote_copy). Start in interpret mode against a jnp reference, then lower for TPU on your laptop, then tune block sizes on the chip.
Predict: your Pallas kernel matches jnp to 1e-7 in interpret mode on your laptop. What do you know about its speed on a v5e?
Nothing. Interpret mode runs the kernel body as ordinary XLA:CPU code inside a while loop; it does not model DMAs, pipelining, VMEM limits or the MXU. Section 6 also shows it accepts a block shape the TPU compiler rejects. Speed is measured on the chip, with the profiler, against the XLA version you are trying to beat.

11. Common mistakes and interview questions

Head dimensions or vocabularies that are not multiples of 128
Each MXU pass is a full 128 × 128 (or 256 × 256) tile; a head dimension of 32 uses a quarter of it on v5e and a sixteenth on v6e (section 2). Pad vocabularies to a multiple of 128; choose head dimensions of 128.
Expecting small-batch decoding to use the chip
A 7B-class model decoding one sequence reaches 0.2 to 0.6% of bf16 peak on any generation (section 3): every weight byte is read for one multiply-add. Batch requests until the token count approaches the ridge point.
Mesh axes in the wrong order
create_device_mesh gives the most physical axes to the last logical axis. Put tensor parallelism last; a mesh written ("model", "data") gives the chatty axis the worst placement.
Forgetting to zero an accumulator, or zeroing it on the wrong grid step
Pallas outputs start uninitialised; both interpreters fill them with NaN, which is how the bug shows (section 6). Initialise under pl.when(pl.program_id(k_axis) == 0).
Marking a reduction axis "parallel"
The plain interpreter ignores dimension semantics and gives the right answer; the TPU interpreter, with two cores, gave an error of 0.17 (section 6). Only axes whose output tiles differ may be parallel; Pallas's rule of thumb is "parallel, unless the output window does not vary".
Leaving every axis "arbitrary" on v4 or v5p
The default semantics are all arbitrary, so a megacore chip runs the kernel on one of its two cores. Mark independent output axes parallel.
Trusting interpret mode for shapes and speed
It accepted a 100-row block the TPU compiler rejects, and it clamps out-of-range tiles silently. Lower for "tpu" with jax.export, and use InterpretParams() for index checks.

Interview questions

Why do TPUs want shapes in multiples of 128, and what changed with v6e?
The MXU is a 128 × 128 systolic array (256 × 256 on v6e and TPU7x) holding one weight tile; any dimension is padded up to a whole tile, and the VPU's registers are 8 × 128, so the last two dimensions of arrays and Pallas blocks want multiples of 8 and 128. On v6e the tile is 256, so a model of 128-wide matrices uses a quarter of each pass.
Your 7B model decodes at 60 tokens per second on one v5e. Is something wrong?
No: that is the memory bound. 13.5 GB of bf16 weights at 819 GB/s is 16 ms per step, 62 tokens per second for a batch of one. Raise the batch (throughput grows almost linearly until about 240 tokens per step, the ridge), quantise the weights, or shard the weights over more chips.
On a 4 × 4 × 4 v5p slice, how would you lay out FSDP 16 and tensor parallelism 4, and why?
jax.make_mesh((16, 4), ("fsdp", "model")): the model axis, which communicates every layer, gets one whole physical axis with wraparound (neighbours 1 hop apart); FSDP, which gathers weights once per layer and can overlap with compute, gets the other two (y and z), so its all-gathers use two axes of bandwidth. (8, 8) is not placeable without splitting axes.
What does a BlockSpec do that a Triton kernel does by hand?
It states which tile each grid point needs; Pallas then generates the HBM to VMEM DMAs, double-buffers them so loads overlap compute, and skips copies of a tile that did not change. In Triton you compute pointers and masks and the hardware cache and the compiler's pipelining do the rest.
How do you develop a TPU kernel without a TPU?
Write it with a jnp reference; run it with interpret=True for correctness, then with interpret=pltpu.InterpretParams(detect_races=True, num_cores_or_threads=2) for index, ordering and race bugs; lower it with jax.export(..., platforms=["tpu"]) to catch tiling errors; then measure on a real chip. All four steps but the last ran on this page.
When is an all-reduce over ICI latency-bound?
When the bytes per hop are small: the scaling book puts the per-hop floor at about 1 µs, so on v5e (4.5e10 B/s one way) anything under about 45 kB per hop is latency-bound. The running example's 3.3 MB of float32 gradients over 16 chips is not: all-reduced over a 16-chip v5e axis with wraparound it takes about 73 µs, about a sixth of the step's matmul bound (section 3).

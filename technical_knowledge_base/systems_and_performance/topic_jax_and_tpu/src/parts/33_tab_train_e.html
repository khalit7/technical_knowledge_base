Why they drift, and why it does not matter

Both frameworks compute the same function, but their matrix-multiply kernels, fusions and reductions add the same numbers in different orders, so every step differs in the last bits of float32. Training is a chaotic iteration: each step's output is the next step's input, and small differences grow. The difference in training loss first exceeds 1e-5 at step and 1e-4 at step , and never reaches 1e-3 in steps (largest , in the last 500), yet the validation losses at the end are (PyTorch), (plain JAX) and (NNX). Plain JAX and NNX lower to nearly the same XLA program, so they stay closer to each other (largest gap ) than either does to PyTorch. The validation loss bottoms out at near step and then creeps up in all three: by step 4,000 each character of the 1 MB training text has been seen about 16 times (4,000 x 4,096 / 1,003,855 derived), the start of overfitting, identical in both frameworks.

The key biases are a lesson in Adam. The bias of the key projection has a true gradient of exactly zero: adding the same vector b to every key adds the same number q·b to every attention score in a row, and softmax ignores a constant added to a whole row. Both frameworks compute about 2e-11 of float noise for it. Adam divides each update by the running root-mean-square of the gradient, so pure noise is normalised into steps as large as the learning rate, in a random direction that differs between frameworks. That is why the key biases are the weights furthest apart for the first 2,000 steps or so, while being unable to change the outputs. Later the ordinary weights catch up as the chaos compounds: by step 4,000 they differ by up to (after moving up to from their initial values) and the logits by on a largest logit of . Two runs of one model end at different weights that are equally good: what to compare across frameworks is the loss, not the weights.

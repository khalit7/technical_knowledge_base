3. The roofline, with the running example on it

The {{Reading tab|#t-read}} gave the rule in one line: an operation whose arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte it moves to or from HBM) is below the chip's ridge point waits on memory. Below, every distinct matmul of the running example's training step sits on the roof of the generation you pick, beside the weight matmuls of a 7B-class model (Llama 2 7B as configured in MaxText's pinned configs/models/llama2-7b.yml: D 4,096, MLP 11,008, 32 layers, 6.74 billion parameters) at the number of tokens you choose. The derivation (FLOPs, bytes, the time bound) is on the Hardware topic's {{Performance math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81faad1ef390b0e54d08}} page; this is the calculator.

Bytes = inputs read once plus output written once, at 2 bytes per number (bf16) or 4 (float32); time = max(FLOPs ÷ bf16 peak, bytes ÷ HBM bandwidth). The 7B point is one forward pass of its weight matmuls (attention scores and the KV cache left out), so at 1 token it is a decode step with a batch of 1, at 4,096 a training micro-batch's forward. Real kernels reach 50 to 80% of these bounds at best.

Predict: on a v6e, how many of the running example's 12 matmul shapes are limited by memory rather than by the MXUs?
All 12, on every generation. The highest intensity in the step is 99.9 FLOPs per byte (the MLP matmuls, in bf16), below even v5p's ridge of 166. The reason is the model, not the batch: with D = 128, each weight matrix is tiny, so the activations (4,096 rows of them) dominate the bytes, and in bf16 the intensity of an m × k by k × n matmul is 1 ÷ (1/m + 1/k + 1/n), always below the smallest of the three; here that is 128 or 512. Width, not batch, is what a small model lacks.

What the running example would cost on a TPU calculated. Its 99 matmuls do 22.75 GFLOP per step (XLA's cost_analysis() on the CPU compile counts 23.69 GFLOP for the whole forward and backward, the rest being elementwise work). At bf16 peak a v6e would do them in 25 µs, but because every one is memory-bound the bound is 236 µs; v5e 473 µs, v5p 140 µs, TPU7x 52 µs. The whole step measured 0.102 s here (the shared setup's 500-step run, measured on Apple M1 Pro (CPU), shared machine, 4 threads, low priority). In practice a step this small on a TPU is dominated by fixed costs the roofline ignores (launching the program, the host loop, the optimizer's many small elementwise ops), which is why nobody trains 826,368 parameters on a TPU.

The 7B-class model calculated. Its bf16 weights are 13.5 GB, so a v5e's 16 GB holds them with little room for anything else, and one decode step for one user must read all of them: about 16 ms on v5e (62 tokens per second at best), 8.1 ms on v6e (124), 1.8 ms on TPU7x (558), whatever the MXUs could do. At 256 tokens per step v5e reaches 97% of its bf16 peak and v6e only 42%: the faster the MXUs relative to HBM, the larger the batch needed to use them. That is the ridge point turned into a batching rule.

4. The mesh meets the torus

The {{Sharding lab|#t-shard}} built meshes on 8 simulated devices, where every device is as close as every other. On a real slice the chips sit on a torus (section 1's table), and two neighbours along one ICI link are much cheaper to talk to than two chips five hops apart. So the question a JAX programmer faces is: when I write jax.make_mesh((16, 16), ("data", "model")), which physical chips form each "model" group? JAX answers it in mesh_utils.create_device_mesh, which jax.make_mesh calls. That function only reads each device's coordinates, so it runs on this CPU against mock TPU devices built the way JAX's own tests build them.

jax's own device placement, run on mock slices measured

Run with jax._src.mesh_utils.create_device_mesh at jax-v0.11.2 (src/tpu/tp_torus.py). "Hops" is the largest ICI distance between neighbours along a logical axis, counting the wraparound link where the slice has one. The picture shows the chips of the bottom layer (z = 0), coloured by their index along the last logical axis, the one JAX treats as most communication-heavy.

What the runs show:

  • The last axis wins. The docstring says the mesh shape is "ordered by increasing network-intensity", and the loop at mesh_utils.py:321 assigns axes from last to first, giving each the largest set of whole physical axes still free. So list your axes least to most chatty, ("data", "fsdp", "model"). MaxText does exactly this: mesh_axes in its configs/base.yml:684 runs from data through fsdp to tensor and expert, and it builds the mesh with this same mesh_utils.create_device_mesh (maxtext_utils.py:2086, main @ 1a95d1e5).
  • Logical sizes must be products of physical ones. On a 4 × 4 × 4 v5p cube, (16, 4) and (4, 16) work; (8, 8) raises NotImplementedError because 8 is not a product of whole 4-chip axes. jax.make_mesh lets v5e (and only v5e: sharding_impls.py:962) split a physical axis instead, at the price shown in the "split" result: neighbours up to 9 hops apart.
  • TPU7x adds a fourth axis, the two TensorCores of a chip, which the source treats as the fastest link (priority_map, mesh_utils.py:316), so a "model" axis of 2 lands on the two cores of one chip, 0 hops apart.

How long a collective takes on ICI calculated

The scaling book's model for a ring over one torus axis of X chips: an all-gather or reduce-scatter of V bytes takes V ÷ Wbidi with a wraparound link (independent of X) or V(X − 1)/X ÷ Wone-way without one; an all-reduce costs twice that; spanning n physical axes divides the time by n; and nothing goes faster than about 1 µs per hop (Tmin × X ÷ 2). What each collective moves is on the {{Sharding lab|#t-shard}}.

Wraparound

Per-link bandwidths from the scaling book (2025-02-04): one-way 4.5e10 B/s on v4 and v5e, 9.0e10 on v5p, v6e and TPU7x; bidirectional twice that. Its own worked example: an all-gather of 34 MB over a 4-chip v5e axis, 377 µs with wraparound, about 560 without (measured in its profile: about 680).

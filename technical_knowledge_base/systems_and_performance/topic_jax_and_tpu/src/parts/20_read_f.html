6. TPUs from the programmer's side, and Pallas

A GPU is about a hundred small processors running thousands of threads. A TPU chip has one or two big TensorCores and no threads: think "one wide core with a matrix engine and explicitly managed memory", scheduled almost entirely by XLA. The parts a JAX programmer meets:

  • MXU: a systolic array of 128 × 128 multiply-accumulate cells (256 × 256 on v6e and TPU7x), bf16 in, fp32 accumulation; peak only when matmul dimensions are multiples of its size. VPU: the slower vector unit for everything elementwise.
  • VMEM: a software-managed on-chip scratchpad that tiles are copied into from HBM, the chip's main memory; no hardware cache does it for you.
  • ICI: direct chip-to-chip links forming a 2D torus (v5e, v6e) or a 3D torus (v4, v5p, Ironwood). A pod is the largest set joined this way, a slice the part you rent; Multislice joins slices over the slower data-centre network. v5p's pod is 8,960 chips, but the largest slice Cloud schedules is 6,144 (16 × 16 × 24), and Multislice reaches 18,432 (vendor, Cloud TPU v5p page, last updated 2026-10-13).
  • A JAX device is not always a chip: on v4 and v5p the two TensorCores are fused into one device ("megacore"); on TPU7x each core is a device, so a 4-chip Ironwood host shows 8 (jax/_src/tpu_info.py at jax-v0.11.2).

Two notes on the figures

Ironwood's ridge uses its bf16 peak. v5e's bandwidth is ambiguous: Cloud's page says "800 GiBps" (859 GB/s, ridge 229), the scaling book and JAX's own tpu_info.py 819 GB/s, used here. The specifications come from the Cloud TPU documentation and Google's posts; the hardware page also records where they disagree.

The roofline in one line. A matmul of B tokens against a D × F weight does 2BDF FLOPs and moves about 2DF bytes of bf16 weights, so when B is much smaller than D and F its arithmetic intensity is about B FLOPs per byte. Training with thousands of tokens per chip clears every ridge above; decoding one token at a time for 8 users does not.

Predict: a 7B model decodes for a batch of 8 users on one v6e chip. What fraction of the chip's bf16 peak can its weight matmuls reach at best?
About 8 ÷ 560 = 1.4% derived. Each weight byte read from HBM is used for only about 8 multiply-adds, and v6e needs about 560 per byte to keep its matrix units busy. That is why inference batches requests, and why the same chip can look superb in training and poor in small-batch decoding. The {{TPU and Pallas lab|#t-tpu}} places the running example's own matmuls on this roofline; {{TPU programming and performance|n:3c65c17b0d0d8129b0a5fd82e44ecf6e}} section 3 gives the batch each generation needs.

Pallas. XLA writes almost every kernel, and for most training that is enough. When it is not (attention variants, mixture-of-experts routing, quantised or ragged matmuls), Pallas is JAX's kernel language, the counterpart of Triton: a Python function over refs (buffers in on-chip memory), launched on a grid, with a BlockSpec per input saying which tile each grid step loads. It lowers through Mosaic on TPU and through Triton or Mosaic GPU on GPU ({{Pallas docs|https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/pallas/index.html}}); interpret=True runs a kernel on the CPU, checking indexing and correctness but not speed.

Two kernels run here in interpret mode, and corrections to the old page

The TPU and Pallas lab wrote two kernels and ran them interpreted measured: a tiled matmul on the running example's MLP shape (4,096 × 512 times 512 × 128, blocks of 512 × 128 × 128 on an 8 × 1 × 4 grid) matches jnp to 2.5e-7, and a causal flash-attention kernel (64 × 64 blocks) matches to 8.9e-8 while skipping 128 of its 512 blocks above the diagonal. Interpreted, the matmul took about 49 ms against 4.7 ms for XLA's own, which says nothing about a TPU. A stricter interpreter that simulates the TPU's cores caught a bug the plain one hid: marking the reduction axis of the grid "parallel" gave an error of 0.17.

Corrections to the old page
The old table gave Ironwood as "GA Apr 2026"; Google's 2025-11-06 post said it "will be generally available in the coming weeks", and the exact date is unconfirmed. Its "more than 4x Trillium per chip" is Google's headline: the bf16 peaks give 2,307 ÷ 918 = 2.5x, and only the fp8 peak (which Trillium cannot run faster than bf16) gives 4,614 ÷ 918 = 5.0x derived. Read a vendor's "x times faster" by asking which precision and which workload.

0. In one screen

Pallas is JAX's language for writing your own kernels, the counterpart of Triton (and, one level lower, of CUDA C++) in the PyTorch world. You write one Python function that works on refs (mutable views of small tiles sitting in fast on-chip memory), say how the big arrays are cut into tiles and which tile each step of a loop gets (the grid and the BlockSpecs), and pl.pallas_call turns it into a TPU kernel (through Mosaic) or a GPU kernel (through Triton or Mosaic GPU). The root page introduced the five ideas and ran a tiled matmul and the flash attention forward in its {{TPU and Pallas lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ecbc04eea6aa45ea1a}}; this page builds every other kind of kernel you will meet, on the running example's real tensors, and reads the ones Google ships.

Every error is measured here, on the running example (Apple M1 Pro CPU, Pallas interpret mode, jax 0.11.2), against a plain jax.numpy reference; float32 throughout unless the row says bf16. Timings appear only once, in section 9, and only to show they mean nothing for a TPU.

The four things to take away
  • Write a kernel only for what XLA cannot derive: an algorithm that changes what is stored (flash attention never writes the T × T scores), data-dependent tiles (paged and ragged attention, mixture-of-experts), or block sparsity. For a matmul or a normalisation, Google's own guide shows the best Pallas matmul only matching XLA (section 1).
  • The grid is a sequential loop on a TPU, so a block that several steps share stays in VMEM and can accumulate (sections 4 and 8). On a GPU the same grid is parallel programs, which is why the GPU kernels split such reductions into a second kernel.
  • Gradients are yours to write: jax.grad of a pallas_call fails; wrap forward and backward kernels in jax.custom_vjp (sections 4 and 5).
  • Interpret mode proves correctness, not speed or legality: test with the TPU interpreter and lower for TPU on your laptop before paying for a chip (section 9).

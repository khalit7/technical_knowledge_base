0. In one screen

Pallas is JAX's language for writing your own kernels, the counterpart of Triton (and, one level lower, of CUDA C++) in the PyTorch world. You write one Python function that works on refs (mutable views of small tiles sitting in fast on-chip memory), say how the big arrays are cut into tiles and which tile each step of a loop gets (the grid and the BlockSpecs), and pl.pallas_call turns it into a TPU kernel (through Mosaic) or a GPU kernel (through Triton or Mosaic GPU). The root page introduced the five ideas and ran a tiled matmul and the flash attention forward in its TPU and Pallas lab; this page builds every other kind of kernel you will meet, on the running example's real tensors, and reads the ones Google ships.

Every error is measured here, on the running example (Apple M1 Pro CPU, Pallas interpret mode, jax 0.11.2), against a plain jax.numpy reference; float32 throughout unless the row says bf16. Timings appear only once, in section 9, and only to show they mean nothing for a TPU.

The four things to take away
  • Write a kernel only for what XLA cannot derive: an algorithm that changes what is stored (flash attention never writes the T × T scores), data-dependent tiles (paged and ragged attention, mixture-of-experts), or block sparsity. For a matmul or a normalisation, Google's own guide shows the best Pallas matmul only matching XLA (section 1).
  • The grid is a sequential loop on a TPU, so a block that several steps share stays in VMEM and can accumulate (sections 4 and 8). On a GPU the same grid is parallel programs, which is why the GPU kernels split such reductions into a second kernel.
  • Gradients are yours to write: jax.grad of a pallas_call fails; wrap forward and backward kernels in jax.custom_vjp (sections 4 and 5).
  • Interpret mode proves correctness, not speed or legality: test with the TPU interpreter and lower for TPU on your laptop before paying for a chip (section 9).

1. Why write a kernel at all, and when not to

The problem. In PyTorch you reach for a custom CUDA or Triton kernel when the framework's kernels move too much memory. In JAX the compiler, XLA, already writes most kernels for you and fuses chains of elementwise work into one pass (how it decides, and the softmax compiled three ways, are on XLA and compilation, section 4). What a compiler cannot do is change the algorithm. Four kinds of work need one:

  • Store less than the maths suggests. Attention written as softmax(q kT) v materialises a T × T score matrix in memory (HBM, the chip's large and slow memory). Flash attention computes the same result one tile at a time and never stores it. For the running example's layer 0 that matrix is 8.4 MB of float32 (32 sequences × 4 heads × 128 × 128), and the backward of the plain version keeps the probabilities of the same size as a residual; the flash backward keeps one number per row instead, 66 KB measured here. For a 7B-class model at 8,192 tokens with 32 heads, the scores are 32 × 8,1922 × 2 bytes = 4.3 GB per sequence per layer in bf16 derived.
  • Data-dependent tiles. Serving keeps the KV cache in fixed-size pages scattered through memory; mixture-of-experts sends a different number of tokens to each expert. The tile a step needs depends on a table that only exists at run time (section 7).
  • Skip work that is known to be zero. Causal or block-sparse attention masks whole tiles; a kernel can skip computing them and even skip copying them (sections 5 and 7).
  • Overlap communication with compute by hand, for example a matmul that pulls the next shard from a neighbouring chip while multiplying the current one (Pallas TPU kernels can copy between chips with pltpu.make_async_remote_copy; not run here: it needs several chips).

When not to. Google's own Pallas TPU matmul guide measured, on a TPU v5e, a plain Pallas matmul against jnp.matmul (XLA). The best Pallas block size reached the compiler; small blocks were 15 times worse. Published outputs committed with the guide at jax-v0.11.2 Google, published:

The same guide's sparse chapter, on the same kind of chip, is the counter-example: a block-sparse × dense matmul (16,384 square, blocks of 512) took 8.136 ms against 46.953 ms for the dense XLA reference Google, published, because the kernel never touches the zero blocks. That is the general rule: a kernel wins by doing less, not by doing the same thing better than the compiler.

What the two lowerings show is the core difference between the backends. In Mosaic the refs arrive as memref<512x128xf32, #tpu.memory_space<vmem>>: the copy from HBM already happened, decided by the BlockSpec, and the body is vector.load, two vector.multi_reductions and a tpu.vector_store. In Triton the refs arrive as raw pointers (!tt.ptr<f32>), and the lowering builds a 16 × 128 tensor of addresses (tt.make_range, tt.addptr) before tt.load: the BlockSpec became pointer arithmetic, which is what a Triton programmer writes by hand. Triton functions and their tuning are on Triton and Gluon.


Transformations. A pallas_call sits inside jax.jit like any primitive. jax.vmap of it adds a leading grid axis (the design note's rule, docs/pallas/design/design.md:542): the softmax kernel vmapped over 4 score matrices of 1,024 rows became one call with grid (4, 2) and matched to 1.5e-8 measured here (jax's own GPU LayerNorm is written this way, one row per program, then vmapped). jax.grad needs a custom_vjp (section 4). Inside shard_map a kernel sees each device's shard; the sharding pages own that.






3. Row kernels: elementwise, softmax, cross-entropy


The simplest useful kernels cut an array into bands of rows and process each band on its own: a one-dimensional grid, one BlockSpec per operand, axis marked "parallel". Three of them, each on a real tensor of the running example (code exactly as it ran, from src/runs/pk_kernels.py):








Bias + GELU on the MLP pre-activation of layer 0 (layer 0 MLP pre-activation x W1 [4,096 x 512] and bias b1 [512]) matches jax.nn.gelu(x + b, approximate=False) to 7.5e-8 and the model's own hidden activations to the same measured here. Two details matter beyond the arithmetic. The bias BlockSpec returns block (0, 0) at every step, so Pallas copies it once, not eight times, and the TPU lowering marks it #tpu.pipeline_mode<synchronous> (single-buffered): "Force single-buffering pipelining for trivial windowing in VMEM" (mosaic/lowering.py:1220), a trivial window being a block equal to the whole array with an index map of zeros. And jax.lax.erf lowers to Mosaic's math.erf, so the exact GELU the model uses costs nothing extra; the tanh approximation (JAX's default) would differ by 1.9e-4 here, a porting trap the running example sets explicitly.


Fused softmax on the scaled, causally masked attention scores of all 128 heads (layer 0 attention scores, scaled and causally masked, all 128 heads [16,384 x 128]): whole rows fit in a 512 × 128 block, so the max, the exponentials and the sum stay in VMEM and each element is read once and written once. Error 6.0e-8, row sums within 2.4e-7 of 1. 49.6% of the entries are -inf and come out as exact zeros, which works only because every row keeps its diagonal: a row with every entry masked computes -inf - (-inf) and returns NaN (tried here: that row came out all NaN, yes, the other 511 rows untouched), the reason the flash kernel and the paged kernel below skip empty tiles with pl.when instead. With bf16 inputs the kernel (which upcasts to float32 inside) is off the float32 answer by 2.0e-3; XLA's jax.nn.softmax on the same bf16 input by 3.6e-3 measured here: inside a kernel the precision of the arithmetic is yours to choose.


Fused cross-entropy on the whole model's logits at initialisation (logits of the whole model at initialisation, batch 0 [4,096 x 65], with the targets): each row's loss is its logsumexp minus the logit of the target, picked with broadcasted_iota and a compare instead of a gather, and the softmax is never stored. Mean 4.21168, identical to the model's loss_fn (difference 0), and close to ln 65 = 4.174 as an untrained model should be. The block is 512 × 65, legal only because 65 is the whole row (the root lab showed the (8, 128) rule rejecting a 100-row block). Legal is not free: VMEM is laid out in rows of 128 lanes, so each 65-wide row occupies 128 lanes and 49% of the vector work and memory is padding derived. The production flash attention kernel accepts the same cost on purpose: it stores its running max and sum 128 lanes wide, one value repeated (flash_attention.py:675).


Predict: the bias + GELU kernel runs 8 grid steps. How many times is the 512-float bias copied from HBM into VMEM?




Once. Pallas copies a block only when its block index differs from the previous step's, and the bias's index map always returns (0, 0). The Grid and BlockSpec tab counts it: x 8 copies, b 1, o 8. On TPU the lowering also gives it a single buffer, since there is nothing to prefetch.







4. LayerNorm, forward and backward, joined by custom_vjp


The problem. A pallas_call is opaque to JAX's autodiff in reverse mode. Measured here: jax.grad of a function that calls the softmax kernel fails with ValueError: Linearization failed to produce known values for all output primals. This is typically caused by attempting to differentiate a function uses an operation that does not support reverse-mode autodiff., and through the flash kernel (scratch buffers, pl.when) with a bare AssertionError. Forward mode (jax.jvp) of the softmax kernel does work. The Pallas design note explains why reverse mode is not the default path: transposing a kernel turns parallel reads into overlapping writes ("slow when done atomically"), and it names jax.custom_vjp "a viable escape hatch" (docs/pallas/design/design.md:553). So every training kernel comes as a pair, a forward and a backward, joined by jax.custom_vjp (the rule itself is explained on JAX core, section 6); PyTorch's counterpart is a torch.autograd.Function with its own backward.




src/runs/pk_kernels.py, the layer_norm section, as it ran.


How it works. The forward normalises a band of 256 rows and also writes two numbers per row, the mean and 1/√(var + ε). Those are the residuals: the backward recomputes x̂ = (x − μ) · rstd from them instead of storing x̂, so the saved state is x plus 2,130,432 bytes in all for this input, of which only 32 KB are statistics. The backward has two kinds of output. dx is per row, like the forward. dscale and dbias are sums over all 4,096 rows: their BlockSpec maps every grid step to the same (1, 128) block, so the block stays in VMEM, the kernel zeroes it at step 0 and adds each band's contribution, and Pallas writes it back once at the end.


Evidence. On layer 0's input with the trained LayerNorm (scale between 0.935 and 1.037 after the 500-step run), the forward matches the model's layer_norm to 2.4e-7, and against jax.vjp of it the gradients agree to a relative 1.1e-7 (dx), 1.7e-7 (dscale) and 1.0e-7 (dbias) measured here: float32 rounding.


The accumulation forces "arbitrary", and the plain interpreter will not tell you


Mark the backward's row axis "parallel" and a megacore chip may split the rows between its two cores, each accumulating into its own copy of the shared block. The plain interpreter ignores dimension semantics and returns the right answer (error 3.1e-5 on values up to 176, rounding). The TPU interpreter with two simulated cores, three seeds:  measured here. With "arbitrary" the same two-core run is right again (3.1e-5).


How production does it on a GPU. The Triton-style LayerNorm that ships with jax (deprecated in favour of tokamax, section 10) cannot accumulate across programs, because GPU programs run at the same time. It computes dscale and dbias in a second kernel, layer_norm_backward_kernel_dw_db (ops/gpu/layer_norm.py:192), with one program per block of columns looping over all rows with masked loads. Same maths, a different decomposition, forced by the grid semantics.

The root's lab ran the causal flash attention forward and showed it matching the model. 6. Pipelining: what a BlockSpec does for you, written by hand


The problem. A TensorCore computes only on data in VMEM; the arrays live in HBM. Copying a tile in, computing, and copying the result out, one after the other, leaves the matrix unit idle during every copy. Every BlockSpec kernel on this page is pipelined automatically: while step s computes, Pallas's generated code is already copying the blocks step s+1 needs into a second buffer (double buffering), and writes each output block back once it is final. The default is two buffers per operand ("The default buffer count is 2 for all inputs and outputs", the Pallas TPU pipelining guide, docs/pallas/tpu/pipelining.md:140).


One matmul, copies and compute serial against double-buffered














Calculated, not measured: every copy at v5e's published 819 GB/s and every block matmul at its 197 TFLOP/s bf16 peak (the figures the Pallas TPU matmul guide uses), bf16 inputs, float32 outputs, a copy issued only for blocks whose index changed. No DMA latency, issue cost or pipeline overhead is modelled, so real times are longer; the shape of the timeline is the point. derived


The two cases show the two regimes. The running example's matmul moves more bytes than it computes, so even perfectly overlapped it takes as long as its copies (9.05 µs against 8.96 µs of copies); the 7B-class matmul with 512 × 1,024 × 1,024 blocks computes longer than it copies, and overlap hides almost every copy (73.7 µs against 119.2 serial for the first 12 steps). Overlap can only bring the time down to the larger of the two lanes; block sizes decide which lane that is (section 11).


Written by hand. Put an operand in pl.ANY and Pallas copies nothing; you allocate VMEM slots and DMA semaphores as scratch and issue the copies with pltpu.make_async_copy(src, dst, sem) (mosaic/primitives.py:703), a descriptor with .start() and .wait(). The bug the TPU interpreter exists for
Move the .wait() to after the matmul that uses the slot. In the plain interpreter a DMA completes the moment it starts, so the result is still exact (error 0). On a TPU the matmul would read a half-copied buffer. The TPU interpreter executes DMAs only when the kernel waits on their semaphore (dma_execution_mode="on_wait", the default, interpret/params.py:178) and fills fresh buffers with NaN, so 1,024 of the 4,096 output rows came out NaN measured here.






7. Scalar prefetch: tiles chosen by data


The problem. An index map is a traced function of the grid indices, run to decide which block to copy before the step runs. Serving needs more: the KV cache of each sequence lives in fixed-size pages scattered through a shared pool, and which physical page holds tokens 32 to 47 of sequence 3 is in a page table known only at run time. Scalar prefetch solves it: pltpu.PrefetchScalarGridSpec(num_scalar_prefetch=n, ...) (mosaic/core.py:390) copies the first n arguments, small integer arrays, into SMEM before the grid starts and passes them as extra arguments to every index map and to the kernel (as SMEM refs, :407). (Why pages: the PagedAttention paper page simulates the KV cache.) In vLLM's CUDA paged attention the block table is just a kernel argument that each thread block reads; in Triton you load the entry and compute the pointer. Here the BlockSpec still does the copy, so the pipelining of section 6 still applies.




The test. Eight (sequence, head) pairs of the running example decode one token each: query = the q row at position n − 1, keys and values 0 to n − 1, with lengths 37, 128, 64, 90, 1, 17, 113, 70. Their K and V are cut into pages of 16 tokens and scattered over a pool of 40 pages (3 unused) in a shuffled order. Causal attention's row n − 1 sees exactly keys 0 to n − 1, so the kernel's output must equal the model's own attention output at that row: it does, to 3.7e-8, in both interpreters measured here.


The page table of the test, and where each logical page lives




Rows: the 8 sequences; columns: logical pages 0 to 7; each cell is the physical page in the pool (a dash: unused slot). Hover or tap a cell to see its page in the pool on the right. Lengths and the shuffle are those of src/runs/pk_run.py (NumPy seed 0).


Lowered for TPU, the table and lengths become memref<8x8xi32, #tpu.memory_space<smem>> and memref<8xi32, ...smem> arguments and the function is tagged scalar_prefetch = 2 : i64; the index maps become transform_ functions that read SMEM with memref.load. The index map also clamps the page index to the sequence's last page, so the steps past the end, which do no work, also copy nothing new: 43 K-page copies without the clamp, 37 with it, exactly the 37 pages that hold tokens.


Stale table entries
Unused table slots must still hold a valid page index. With 9,999 in them and no clamp, the plain interpreter silently clamps the out-of-range block (error 3.7e-8: the step's arithmetic is skipped anyway); the TPU interpreter refuses: Out-of-bounds block index (9999, 0, 0) for input "k_ref" in iteration (0, 3) on device 0 (core 0): reading [(9999, slice(0, 16, 1), slice(0, 32, 1))] but input has shape (40, 16, 32). On a chip that copy would read outside the pool. With −1 in the slots neither interpreter raised measured here, so do not rely on the interpreter for negative indices.


Who uses it in production (all at jax-v0.11.2, section 10): paged attention passes lengths and page indices this way (paged_attention_kernel.py:640); the megablox grouped matmul for mixture-of-experts prefetches group metadata, which group and which row tile each step works on (gmm.py:530, built by make_group_metadata:79); run here on the running example's MLP rows split into four "experts" of 1000, 0, 2600, 496 rows, one of them empty, it matched per-group matmuls to 2.7e-7. Splash attention precomputes, for every (head, query block, key block), the next key block worth fetching, data_next, and keeps it "in TPU scalar-memory", a "scarse resource" its comment says, shrinking the dtype to int8 or int16 where it can (splash_attention_mask_info.py:32).







8. Dimension semantics, megacore and the order of the grid


Each grid axis is "parallel" (any order is fine, so the axis may be split between cores) or "arbitrary" (must run in order, as an accumulation must). With no dimension_semantics, every axis is arbitrary (mosaic/lowering.py:778). The split matters on megacore chips, v4 and v5p, where two TensorCores appear as one JAX device and share HBM but each has its own VMEM; "Only parallel dimensions can be partitioned over cores" (the Pallas TPU design notes, docs/pallas/tpu/details.rst:180). On TPU7x each core is its own device (the root's TPU lab, from tpu_info.py), and splitting work between them is sharding's job, not the grid's.


The rule: an axis may be "parallel" only if no output block is visited by two different indices along it. The matmul's K axis, the flash kernels' inner key or query loop, and the LayerNorm backward's row axis (because of dscale) all revisit an output block, so they are "arbitrary"; everything else on this page is "parallel".


Predict: the TPU interpreter runs the flash forward (one head, 4 query blocks × 4 key blocks, semantics ("parallel", "parallel", "arbitrary")) on one simulated core. In what order does it visit the query blocks?




Shuffled: recorded with grid_point_recorder, the query blocks came in the order 3, 1, 0, 2, each with its key blocks 0 to 3 in order. The interpreter randomises "parallel" axes on purpose (random_seed, interpret/params.py:144) so a kernel that silently depends on their order fails in testing; the "arbitrary" LayerNorm backward came out in order (yes). A real single-core TPU runs the grid lexicographically.


The root's lab showed the classic failure, a matmul whose K axis was marked parallel, wrong by 0.17 under two simulated cores; section 4 above showed the same for an accumulation across rows. Both are invisible to interpret=True.







9. Testing a kernel without a TPU, and what it cannot tell you


The ladder at the top of the page is the whole method. Each rung catches a different class of bug, measured on this page and on the root's lab:

Tolerances. Compare with numpy.testing.assert_allclose against the jnp reference, with tolerances set by dtype, not by hope. On this page float32 kernels land at a relative 1e-7 (rounding), a long float32 sum such as dscale over 4,096 rows at a few e-5 absolute on values near 176, and bf16 inputs at a few e-3. A kernel 1e-4 off in float32 has a bug.


What interpret mode cannot tell you. It runs the kernel body as ordinary XLA:CPU code inside a loop over the grid, so it knows nothing about the MXU, VMEM capacity, DMA bandwidth or how Mosaic will schedule the code. Its timings say nothing about TPU speed (and not much about CPU speed either):

11. Choosing block sizes


The cost model. For a matmul tiled (bm, bk, bn), the Pallas TPU guide counts HBM traffic as (m k n / bn + k n m / bm + m n) × bytes per element: A is re-read once per column of blocks, B once per row of blocks (docs/pallas/tpu/matmul.md:341). Its advice: "to be compute bound, make the blocks as big as possible", limited by VMEM and by pipeline bubbles (with few grid steps, the first copy and last write-back are not hidden); and in practice "sweep over a large set of candidate block sizes, profile the kernel, and pick the best one". The root's lab tabulates that traffic for the running example and a 7B-class matmul; the Kernel playground adds the VMEM each of this page's kernels needs at each block size, against each generation's VMEM.


What production does. Three strategies, from the source:


    

  • One default for everything: the flash attention kernel's 128 everywhere, with its TODO.
    • 

  • A checked-in table: ragged paged attention looks its block sizes up in TUNED_BLOCK_SIZES (tuned_block_sizes.py:37), 668 entries for "TPU v5" and 668 for "TPU v6", keyed by dtypes, heads per block, head dimension, page size, batched tokens and maximum model length, each giving (KV pages per block, queries per block). The two generations share every key but disagree on 237 of them: tuned values do not transfer between chips.
    • 

  • Search at run time: tokamax's ops declare a search space and tokamax.autotune measures it, for example ragged_dot's row tiles from 128 to 1,024 ("we found that there is no need on current TPU generations to search for larger tiles for m", ops/ragged_dot/pallas_mosaic_tpu.py:453). Its README warns that "autotuning is fundamentally non-deterministic" and that different configs "can lead to different numerics": serialise the result and reuse it.
    •

Common mistakes, with the evidence


Calling jax.grad on a function that contains a pallas_call
Fails: ValueError: Linearization failed to produce known values for all output primals for the softmax kernel, a bare AssertionError for the flash kernel (section 4). Forward mode works; reverse mode needs jax.custom_vjp with a backward you write (and test against jax.vjp of the reference).


Forgetting sm_scale on the production flash attention
It defaults to 1.0. On the running example the unscaled output is off by 0.198 (section 5). No error is raised: the model just trains differently.


Accumulating into an output block along a "parallel" axis
Right in interpret=True, wrong or NaN under the TPU interpreter with two cores, and wrong on a megacore chip (section 4; the root lab's K-parallel matmul for the same bug). Rule: "parallel, unless the output window does not vary" along that axis.


Using a DMA's buffer before waiting on it
The plain interpreter completes copies instantly, so it hides the bug (exact result); the TPU interpreter executes copies at the wait and fills fresh buffers with NaN (1,024 NaN rows, section 6). Always test hand-written DMAs under pltpu.InterpretParams().


Leaving stale or out-of-range entries in a prefetched table
The plain interpreter clamps silently; the TPU interpreter raises "Out-of-bounds block index (9999, 0, 0) for input "k_ref" ..."; negative entries were not flagged by either (section 7). Fill unused slots with a valid index and clamp in the index map.


A row with every entry masked
max is -inf, -inf - (-inf) is NaN, and the row comes out NaN (section 3, tried here). Skip empty tiles with pl.when or guarantee at least one valid entry per row (decoding at length 0 is the usual way to hit it).


Asking the pallas_call pipeline for three buffers
pl.Buffered(3) runs in interpret mode and fails at TPU lowering: "Only single (1) and double (2) buffering are supported. Got 3." (section 6). Deeper buffering and lookahead belong to emit_pipeline.


Expecting emit_pipeline to run on a laptop
At jax 0.11.2 it needs the TPU generation to pick a tiling and stops with "Unsupported TPU device kind: cpu" in both interpreters; lowering with jax.export under an abstract TPU device works (section 6).


Raising vmem_limit_bytes alone
The docstring says it "must be used in conjunction with the --xla_tpu_scoped_vmem_limit_kib=N flag with N*1kib > vmem_limit_bytes" (mosaic/core.py:97). Not run here (needs a TPU).


Reading interpret-mode timings as performance
The flash kernel took 150 ms in interpret mode against 2.0 ms for XLA's attention on this CPU (section 9). That ratio says nothing about a TPU.


An output dtype that rounds the accumulator
The old version of this page showed a matmul kernel with out_shape in the input dtype and x_ref[...] @ y_ref[...]. It is correct, but with bf16 inputs the result is stored in bf16: off the float32 answer by 9.0e-4 against 7.1e-4 with a float32 output (preferred_element_type=jnp.float32), measured here. Accumulate and, where the next op allows, store in float32.







Interview questions, with short answers


When would you write a Pallas kernel instead of trusting XLA?
When the win comes from doing less, which a compiler cannot derive: not storing an intermediate (flash attention), data-dependent tiles (paged attention, MoE grouped matmuls), skipping known-zero blocks (block-sparse masks), or overlapping a collective with compute. Not for a matmul or a normalisation: Google's own guide has the best Pallas matmul at 91.5% of v5e peak against XLA's 92.3%. Check the profile first, then look for a ready-made kernel, then write one.


Why can a TPU kernel accumulate dscale across grid steps in one block, when a GPU kernel needs a second kernel or atomics?
On a TPU core the grid is a sequential loop, so a block that consecutive steps map to stays in VMEM and is written back once; the axis must be "arbitrary" so it is not split between megacore cores. On a GPU the grid's programs run concurrently with no ordering, so a shared block would race: jax's Triton LayerNorm computes dscale and dbias in a separate kernel, and FlashAttention-2's backward adds into dQ with atomics.


Walk through the flash attention backward. What is saved, what is recomputed, and why two kernels?
Saved: Q, K, V, O and one logsumexp per row (66 KB here against 8.4 MB for the probabilities). Computed once: D = rowsum(dO ∘ O). Per tile: P = exp(S − L) recomputed, dV += PTdO, dP = dO VT, dS = P ∘ (dP − D), dK += dSTQ, dQ += dS K (both scaled). dK and dV sum over query blocks and dQ over key blocks; each must be owned by one grid row to accumulate in VMEM, so one kernel loops queries for each key block, the other keys for each query block. The cost is recomputing QKT in each.


What is scalar prefetch, and how would you use it for paged attention?
Small integer arrays copied into SMEM before the grid starts and passed to every index map and to the kernel. For paged decode attention: grid (sequence, logical page); the page table and lengths are prefetched; the K and V index maps return table[s, min(p, last page)], so each step's BlockSpec copies the right physical page and steps past the end copy nothing; the kernel masks the tail of the last page and runs the online softmax across pages.


Design: a fused grouped matmul for mixture-of-experts on a TPU. What are the grid, the BlockSpecs, the semantics and the test plan?
Inputs: tokens sorted by expert (m × k), expert weights (E × k × n), group sizes. Precompute on the host or in jnp a metadata list mapping each grid index to (group, row tile), with tiles that straddle a group boundary listed once per group; pass it by scalar prefetch. Grid (tiles, n blocks, k blocks); lhs map (row tile, k), rhs map (group, k, n block), out map (row tile, n block); semantics ("parallel", "parallel", "arbitrary") with the k loop accumulating in a float32 scratch; mask rows outside the group on straddling tiles. Backward: a transposed variant for dW (per group) and the same kernel for dX, under custom_vjp. Tests: per-group jnp matmuls as the reference, uneven and empty groups, bf16 tolerances, the TPU interpreter with two cores, lowering for TPU; then tile sizes swept on the chip. This is megablox's design (ran here: error 2.7e-7).


How do you test a TPU kernel without a TPU?
A jnp reference; interpret=True for values and gradients (assert_allclose with dtype-based tolerances); interpret=pltpu.InterpretParams(detect_races=True, num_cores_or_threads=2) for ordering, DMA and out-of-bounds bugs; jax.export(..., platforms=["tpu"]) to catch illegal block shapes and unsupported ops. Then the chip, for speed and VMEM.


Your Pallas matmul reaches 6% of a v5e's peak on 8,192-square inputs. What is the first thing to check?
Block sizes. With 128-cubed blocks the guide measured 6.1%; with 512 × 1,024 × 1,024 the same kernel reached 91.5%. Small blocks re-read A and B from HBM many times, and each step does too little arithmetic to hide its copy.


Why does the production flash kernel store its running max and sum 128 values wide?
VMEM and vector registers are laid out in 8 × 128 tiles; an array whose last dimension is 1 occupies a full 128-lane row anyway, and keeping the statistic replicated across lanes makes broadcasting it against a 128-wide score tile free. The same padding is why a 65-wide vocabulary wastes 49% of the lanes.


Pallas against Triton in one minute?
Same idea, a Python kernel over tiles. Pallas on TPU: sequential grid, BlockSpecs that generate double-buffered DMAs into VMEM, (8, 128) tiling, custom_vjp for gradients, interpreters and AOT lowering for testing. Triton: parallel programs, pointer arithmetic and masked loads, power-of-two blocks, @triton.autotune. A generic Pallas kernel lowers to Triton too; one that uses TPU scratch, DMAs or semaphores does not.

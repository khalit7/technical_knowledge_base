4. optax, transformation by transformation

The model. An optax GradientTransformation is a named tuple of two pure functions: init(params) -> state and update(grads, state, params) -> (updates, new_state) (). optax.chain(t1, t2, ...) feeds each one's output updates into the next and keeps a tuple of their states (). Every optimiser you know is a chain: optax.adamw is scale_by_adam, then add_decayed_weights, then scale_by_learning_rate (). The root's Training lab lets you build chains and checks its JavaScript against optax; here we open one chain at one step.

One real step through the running example's chain

The running example uses chain(clip_by_global_norm(1.0), adamw(lr_schedule, b1=0.9, b2=0.95, eps=1e-8, weight_decay=0.1, mask=decay_mask)). The script recomputes every stage by hand on the real gradients of the first 12 steps and checks the result against tx.update: the largest difference was measured, float rounding in the bias correction. The stages, with PyTorch's names:

g → g·min(1, c/‖g‖) → m = β₁m + (1−β₁)g, v = β₂v + (1−β₂)g² → m̂/(√v̂ + ε) → + λ·p·mask → ×(−ηₜ)
c
clip threshold 1.0, applied to the global norm of all gradients (clip_grad_norm_)
m, v
Adam moments (exp_avg, exp_avg_sq); m̂ = m/(1−β₁ᵗ), v̂ = v/(1−β₂ᵗ)
λ
weight decay 0.1, only where the mask is true (25 weight matrices)
ηₜ
learning rate at step t: linear warmup to 1e-3 over 50 steps

The {{Optimiser lab|#t-opt}} animates these stages for eight tracked numbers, step by step. Three things it shows that a formula hides: at step 0 the gradient norm was , so clipping scaled every gradient by ; at step 0 the Adam direction m̂/√v̂ is exactly ±1 for every coordinate (m̂ = g and v̂ = g²), so the first update is the sign of the gradient times the learning rate, whatever the gradient's size; and the key bias, whose gradient is pure float noise of about , gets almost nothing, because that is far below ε = 1e-8: here ε, not the gradient, sets the step (its update at step 0 was against for a weight). ε is what stops Adam from normalising pure noise into full-size steps.

Changing hyperparameters during training: inject_hyperparams

In PyTorch you change optimizer.param_groups[0]["lr"]. An optax optimiser closes over its hyperparameters, so they are constants in the compiled step. optax.inject_hyperparams(optax.adamw)(learning_rate=1e-3, ...) moves them into the optimiser state, where they are traced arrays you can overwrite (). Measured: four steps with the learning rate set to 1e-3, 1e-3, 5e-4 and 2.5e-4 in state.hyperparams traced the step time; rebuilding optax.adamw(lr) with a new Python float traced it times for three values measured. A schedule function (the running example's warmup) needs neither: it is evaluated inside the step from the step count in the state. Use injection for changes decided at run time (a plateau, a manual intervention), and it also makes the current learning rate visible in the checkpoint.

Parameter groups: masks and partition

Two tools replace PyTorch's parameter groups. A mask (a pytree of booleans, or a function of the params returning one) switches a single transformation on and off per leaf: adamw(mask=...) decays only the masked leaves. optax.partition sends each leaf to a different optimiser by label (); optax.multi_transform is now a backwards-compatible alias of it (). Measured with three labels on the running example: AdamW with decay on the weight matrices ( numbers), Adam on the biases and norms, and set_to_zero() on the two embeddings (frozen: unchanged after 5 steps, ). Freezing this way also saves memory: the optimiser state held numbers against for AdamW on everything, because set_to_zero keeps no moments.

Gradient accumulation: MultiSteps

optax.MultiSteps(tx, every_k_schedule=4) wraps an optimiser: for three calls it adds the gradient to an accumulator and returns zero updates; on the fourth it passes the mean to tx (). Measured on 4 micro-batches of 8 sequences against one step on the batch of 32: the update norms were , and the parameters after the real step differed from the big-batch step by at most measured. Not zero: the mean of four micro-batch gradients equals the batch gradient in exact arithmetic but not in float32, and Adam's first step turns any small difference on a near-zero coordinate into a sign. The cost: the accumulator is one more copy of the parameters (state numbers against ). Two traps: the step count and every schedule now advance once per four calls, and a loop that logs the "step" must count real updates (state.gradient_step).

Skipping bad steps: apply_if_finite

optax.apply_if_finite(tx, max_consecutive_errors=3) checks the gradients and, if any is NaN or infinite, returns zero updates and leaves the inner state untouched (). With a NaN injected into one entry at step 3 measured: . After max_consecutive_errors bad steps in a row it applies the update anyway, so persistent NaNs still surface. This is the JAX counterpart of the skip that PyTorch's GradScaler performs on overflow.

Schedules are functions of the step

A schedule is any function from the step count to a number. The old page's example, warmup_cosine_decay_schedule(0.0, 3e-4, warmup_steps=2000, decay_steps=100000, end_value=3e-5), evaluated by optax: at step 2,000, at step 51,000 (halfway through the cosine, (3e-4 + 3e-5)/2 = 1.65e-4 derived) and at 100,000. Note that decay_steps includes the warmup (). Warmup-stable-decay (WSD), common in recent LLM recipes, is join_schedules of three linear or constant pieces; both are drawn in the {{Optimiser lab|#t-opt}}.

Lion and Muon

Lion (Chen et al., 2023) keeps one moment and steps by its sign: every coordinate moves by exactly the learning rate. Measured on one step at lr 1e-4: of the updates had magnitude 1e-4, the rest were exact zeros (zero gradients) measured. Its state is one tree ( numbers, half of AdamW's), and its default weight decay is 1e-3 (); its authors recommend a learning rate 3 to 10 times smaller and decay 3 to 10 times larger than AdamW's (arXiv 2302.06675, section 5).

Muon (Jordan, 2024) applies momentum and then replaces each weight matrix's update by an approximately orthogonal one: the same singular vectors, singular values pushed towards 1, by five iterations of a quintic Newton-Schulz map X ← aX + b(XXᵀ)X + c(XXᵀ)²X with (a, b, c) = (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315) (, ). It is in optax.contrib, which is where optax keeps newer optimisers (alongside schedule-free, Prodigy, SOAP-style and others). On the running example's real first-layer MLP gradient () the singular values spanned a ratio of ; after normalisation and five iterations of the 128 singular values ended between 0.5 and , close to the near-uniform 0.5 to 1.5 band the optax docstring describes, while the smallest, before normalisation, stayed near zero (): a direction with essentially no gradient is not amplified in five iterations. The {{Optimiser lab|#t-opt}} animates the spectrum iteration by iteration.

optax's Muon applies to every 2-D parameter by default
By default optax.contrib.muon labels every two-dimensional leaf "muon" and everything else "adam" (). In the running example that includes the token and position embeddings and the output head, which Muon's authors keep on AdamW. Pass muon_weight_dimension_numbers returning None for them.

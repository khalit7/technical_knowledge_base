9. Large scale: DeepSeek on 96 GPUs and on a rack

SGLang's large-scale recipes target mixture-of-experts models such as DeepSeek-V3/R1 (671 billion parameters, 37 billion active per token, 256 routed experts per layer, multi-head latent attention with one compressed KV vector per token). Five pieces, each explained as a mechanism on {{Distributed and disaggregated serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d8170bd7cdf47af0ec07b}}, with SGLang's switch for each:

DP attentionWith MLA there is a single KV head, so splitting attention by heads would copy the whole KV cache to every GPU. SGLang runs attention data-parallel (--enable-dp-attention), each GPU holding different requests' KV, and gathers only around the expert layers; v0.4 reported 1.9 times decode throughput from it vendor.
Expert parallelism with DeepEPEach GPU holds a slice of the experts; tokens go to the GPUs holding their experts and back (all-to-all), with DeepEP's normal mode for prefill and low-latency mode for decode (--moe-a2a-backend deepep, {{EP docs|https://docs.sglang.io/docs/advanced_features/expert_parallelism}}).
EPLBDeepSeek's expert-parallel load balancer replicates hot experts into redundant slots (256 plus 32 = 288), which also allows 12 or 72 GPUs instead of powers of two.
Two-batch overlapSplit the batch in two so one half's all-to-all runs while the other half computes; on GB200 NVL72's faster links SGLang moved to finer single-batch overlap.
Prefill/decode disaggregationPrefill and decode on different GPU pools, the KV moving between them over a transfer backend: mooncake (the default), nixl, mooncake_tcp, mori, ascend, and fake for tests ({{choices.py L120-127|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/v0.5.21/python/sglang/srt/arg_groups/choices.py#L120}}, {{PD docs|https://docs.sglang.io/docs/advanced_features/pd_disaggregation}}); v0.5.21 lets an instance switch role without a restart ({{#28403|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/28403}}).

The headline numbers are the team's own and each measured one thing vendor: 22,282 decode tokens/s per node of 8 H100 for DeepSeek-V3 with prefill and decode measured separately (May 2025); 3.8 and 4.8 times that per GPU on GB200 NVL72, up to 1.8 and 1.9 times of it from lower precision (September 2025); and "up to 25x" for GB300 NVL72 against H200 (February 2026), measured at 50 tokens/s per user, where the older GPU is weakest; the same post says that without the latency constraint H200 reaches similar throughput to its earlier figures. None of this was run here: it needs dozens of datacenter GPUs.

10. SGLang or vLLM?

What independent data says. Which engine is faster depends on the model, the GPU, the precision, the prompt length, the per-user speed you promise and the date of the build. The one place both engines are run by a third party on the same model and GPUs is SemiAnalysis InferenceX, a continuously running benchmark ({{repository|https://github.com/SemiAnalysisAI/InferenceX}}, {{dashboard|https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/inference}}) independent. Of the models it lists, only DeepSeek-V4-Pro has single-node runs of both (no speculative decoding, builds from June to September 2026, read from its public API on 2026-10-08). Neither engine dominates: at matched concurrency with 1,024-token prompts, SGLang's throughput per GPU ran from 0.83 to 1.37 times vLLM's on B200 FP4, 0.50 to 1.01 times on B300 FP4 and 0.83 to 1.04 times on H200 FP8; only on MI355X did one engine lead at every matched concurrency (SGLang, 1.85 to 3.32 times, against a vLLM ROCm nightly build).

Runs at the same concurrency, and what this comparison can and cannot say (table and caveats)

  • Different recipes. Each engine ran with its own tensor, expert and data-parallel choices (TP and EP columns), so a row at the same concurrency can compare different numbers of GPUs per replica; the envelopes, which take each engine's best configuration, are the fairer reading.
  • One model. DeepSeek-V4-Pro is a 1.6-trillion-parameter mixture of experts with a long-context attention design; results on a dense 8B or 70B model can differ completely.
  • No prefix reuse. These are single-turn random prompts, so nothing here exercises the radix cache or cache-aware scheduling, where section 3 found the two caches equal.

The old version of this page said SGLang "leads by about 20 to 30% on prefix-heavy small and mid-size models" and that "at 70B+ dense scale differences shrink to a few percent" (2026 folklore, no source given): no measurement found supports either figure, so they are dropped.

11. Running SGLang on a Mac, and the test that caught a wrong-answer bug

With SGLANG_USE_MLX=1 the scheduler, radix cache and API are SGLang's own and the forward pass runs in Apple's MLX ({{Apple Metal docs|https://docs.sglang.io/docs/hardware-platforms/apple_metal}}); that is what made this page's real-engine measurements possible on a laptop (from v0.5.22 Apple silicon also runs on the standard PyTorch backend, without MLX). v0.5.21 on the M1 Pro, installed from source, broke in four ways:

  1. It did not start (AttributeError: 'MlxModelRunnerStub' object has no attribute 'kv_cache_configurator'); fixed upstream one day after the tag ({{PR #41314|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/41314}}).
  2. It answered the wrong question: send A, an unrelated B, then A again, all greedy, and the second A came back as unrelated text with 25 of its 26 prompt tokens reported cached. Open issue {{#42415|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/issues/42415}}; the open pull request {{#42542|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/42542}} fixes it.
  3. Not a bug: even patched, the cached A diverged from the uncached A after eight tokens, at a near-tie of two candidates. Prefix caching is exact in arithmetic, not in floating point.
  4. Crashes ([METAL] Command buffer execution failed: Insufficient Memory, with gigabytes free) whenever the cache had to evict, a prefill batch mixed a chunked prompt with other requests, or a burst followed /flush_cache.
The habit this teaches
Before trusting a cache, run the same greedy request with it on and off, and an unrelated request in between. Identical text is not guaranteed (item 3), but a different topic is a bug (item 2). All measurements on this page were taken with both fixes applied and the crashes avoided.
The four problems in detail, with the reproduction (evidence)

1. It did not start. A refactor of the token-capacity code had not been carried to the Apple path; the fix ({{PR #41314|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/41314}}, merged 2026-09-30) landed one day after the release's tag commit. Applying its seven-line property let the server start.

2. It answered the wrong question. The second A came back as "a) the number of employees in the company...". Disabling the radix cache made it go away, which pointed at the cache. Issue #42415 was reported on v0.5.20; PR #42542 traces it to chained decode steps writing a finished request's KV into slots the tree still points at. With that patch the repeat answered A again, every time. Fresh server, cache on, greedy: A, B, A, B, A:

3. Cached and uncached answers differ. The cached A diverged after eight tokens ("is used to store" against "represents the hierarchical"). MLX-LM alone, with no SGLang, does the same when the prompt is computed in two pieces instead of one: at the ninth generated token, where the texts part, the gap between the two best candidates was 0.000, 0.125 and 0.125 in logit in the three runs. Computing in a different order rounds differently, and a near-tie flips.

4. Crashes. Every time the radix cache had to evict (a 3,000-token pool and 840-token prompts one at a time, with and without the patch of item 2); every time a prefill batch mixed a chunked prompt (512-token chunks) with other requests, inside _async_extend_batch, with 6 GB free, while the same bursts of 8 to 32 requests ran cleanly with chunks of 4,096 (not reported upstream as of this writing); on a burst of requests sent after /flush_cache (seen in two runs, not isolated further); in every run of the overlap experiment (section 5); and in every dfs-weight run. Open pull requests on the Apple backend touch neighbouring code (per-request buffers released late, {{#39251|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/39251}}; retracted requests' state, {{#38179|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/38179}}; no device-memory figure on Apple, {{#39675|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/issues/39675}}); none was confirmed as the cause here. The runs avoid all of them: a pool large enough never to evict, chunks of 4,096 and no flushes.

The set-up on the M1, and what transfers to a datacenter GPU (provenance)
  • Machine: Apple M1 Pro, 16 GB unified memory, shared with other jobs (load average 3.9 to 27.2 at the start of the runs; every configuration repeated, spread shown). Measured on 2026-10-08 and 2026-10-09.
  • SGLang v0.5.21 (tag commit e00930c5, 2026-09-29), installed from source (no macOS wheel on PyPI: the v0.5.21 wheels are Linux only) with the srt_mps extra and Rust extensions off (SGLANG_BUILD_RUST_EXTS=none): PyTorch 2.13.0, MLX 0.32.3, MLX-LM 0.32.0, XGrammar 0.2.7, Transformers 5.12.1.
  • Two patches from upstream, both in src/m1/: the start-up fix of PR #41314 (merged after the tag) and the cache fix of PR #42542 (open).
  • Model: Qwen3-0.6B (Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B at commit c1899de2) quantized here to 4 bits, group size 64, with MLX-LM 0.32.0, from the root's model store.
  • Server: SGLANG_USE_MLX=1 python -m sglang.launch_server --model-path <model> --disable-cuda-graph --port 8120 --enable-metrics plus --chunked-prefill-size 4096 --max-running-requests 8 and the flags of each experiment; a fresh server per run. The backend gives each running request its own contiguous KV buffer of 4,096 tokens per layer (448 MiB for this model, {{model_runner.py L258|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/v0.5.21/python/sglang/srt/hardware_backend/mlx/model_runner.py#L258}}), so at most 8 ran at once. Client: src/m1/exp.py over the native /generate endpoint with token ids cut from WikiText-2, greedy, streaming; time to first token (TTFT) is the arrival of the first streamed chunk. Scripts: src/m1/run_m1.sh (one server per configuration), exp.py, sgclient.py, summarise.py; raw results in src/m1/results/.
  • Transfers: which tokens are cached and reused (the scheduler and tree are the same code), the ordering effects of the queue policies, the correctness test, the fact that a grammar adds per-step CPU work.
  • Does not transfer: the absolute times. A 0.6B model on a laptop GPU makes the forward pass short and the Python scheduler's share large, so CPU-side effects (overlap, grammar masks) look bigger here than on an H100 serving a 70B model; prefill costs far more per token here than on a GPU, so cache hits save more seconds here. The MLX backend also lacks paged attention: it copies a cached prefix out of the pool into a per-request cache ({{model_runner.py|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/v0.5.21/python/sglang/srt/hardware_backend/mlx/model_runner.py}}).
Interview rehearsal: three design questions (optional)

Design a router for 8 replicas of a chat model with long system prompts. Route by prefix affinity (approximate per-replica trees or consistent hashing of the prefix) but fall back to least-loaded when the load is imbalanced beyond a threshold; measure hit rate, TTFT percentiles and per-replica load. SGLang's gateway does exactly this with a 0.3 match threshold and 64/1.5 imbalance thresholds by default; check that the imbalance thresholds can fire at your real concurrency.

Why does DeepSeek serving use data-parallel attention with expert parallelism? MLA has one compressed KV per token, so tensor-parallel attention would replicate the cache on every GPU; data-parallel attention keeps each request's cache on one GPU, and expert parallelism spreads the experts, with all-to-all exchanges around each MoE layer.

How would you check that prefix caching is working on a production server? Per-request cached-token counts or the hit-rate metric, TTFT split by hit and miss, and a greedy A/B/A correctness test with the cache on and off.

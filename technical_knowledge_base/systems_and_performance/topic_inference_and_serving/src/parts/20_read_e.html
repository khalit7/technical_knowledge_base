A serving engine is the program in the middle of the hero: an OpenAI-compatible HTTP front end, a tokenizer, the scheduler, the cache manager and a model runner that calls GPU kernels. Choosing. Start from the hardware and the model, not from a benchmark chart:

  • Datacenter GPUs, a popular open model, an API this afternoon: vLLM, the widest model and chip support; most cloud stacks wrap it ({{vLLM: inside the default engine|n:3c65c17b0d0d8113b976d8bcaf2c0722}}).
  • A large MoE across many GPUs, or agents resending long prefixes: SGLang or vLLM; SGLang published the most detailed expert-parallel recipe, DeepSeek-V3 on 96 H100s (vendor), and which engine is faster depends on the workload ({{SGLang|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e691e4ce9f9b5d1fad}}, sections 9 and 10).
  • NVIDIA only, one model, every last percent: TensorRT-LLM (or NIM containers on it), at the price of lock-in ({{Distributed and disaggregated serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d8170bd7cdf47af0ec07b}}, section 8).
  • A laptop, desktop, phone or CPU server: llama.cpp or Ollama; on a Mac also MLX. Measured here, the two decode alike alone (196 and 199 tokens per second at 4 bits), but under overload mlx_lm.server ran out of GPU memory and its generation thread died while llama-server queued and slowed measured here ({{Local and on-device inference|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f7a144fe25d799e811}}, sections 6 to 8).

New request shapes and release notes (decision-model routes added in October 2026, for example) are tracked in tech news, {{2026-10-19|n:3fe5c17b0d0d81828721e0f0f189eb76}}.

8. Cheaper tokens: fewer bytes, or more tokens per read

Since decode is limited by bytes read, a token gets cheaper in two ways: read fewer bytes per token (quantisation), or get more than one token per read (speculative decoding). What matters for serving is which numbers are made smaller and what the hardware does with them:

QuantisedFormatsSpeeds upWatch
Weights onlyINT4, INT8 with a scale per small group (AWQ, GPTQ, GGUF k-quants), unpacked to 16 bits inside the kerneldecode, 2 to 4 times fewer bytes; not prefillquality, larger on small models
Weights and activationsFP8; FP4 on Blackwell (NVFP4, MXFP4), on tensor cores at two or four times BF16 speeddecode and prefillneeds the hardware
KV cacheFP8, INT8, INT4more users per GPUquality per model: on Qwen3-1.7B an 8-bit cache left WikiText-2 perplexity at 15.18 and a 4-bit one raised it to 19.42 ({{Batching|n:3c65c17b0d0d819db53bd2c48909e5b2}}, section 8)

Measured here on Qwen3-0.6B (llama.cpp, M1 Pro GPU): generation went from 113 tokens per second at F16 to 140 at Q8_0 and 196 at Q4_K_M, while prompt processing fell from 3,618 to 3,240; WikiText-2 perplexity went from 20.88 to 20.86 at Q8_0 and 22.59 at Q4_K_M measured here ({{Engine bench|#t-bench}}, "Quantization: speed and quality"). Eight bits is close to free; four bits is a real trade. Number formats and the methods (GPTQ, AWQ, rotations) are owned by {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}; what a GGUF type name means and every type measured, by {{Local and on-device inference|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f7a144fe25d799e811}}, sections 4 and 5.

Speculative decoding uses decode's idle arithmetic: a cheap draft proposes k tokens, the target model scores all of them in one pass, keeps the longest run it agrees with and adds one of its own, and a modified rejection-sampling rule keeps the output distribution exactly the target's ({{Leviathan et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192}}). It pays only while checking k tokens costs little more than producing one, so the gain shrinks as the batch grows; on this laptop a Qwen3-4B pass over 4 tokens cost 2.2 times a pass over 1, so a 0.6B draft made llama.cpp slower on free text and at best 1.32 times faster on a highly predictable list measured here (a llama.cpp Metal effect of the Q4_K weights; on Qwen3-1.7B at Q8_0 a pass over 4 tokens cost 1.25 times one token) ({{Engine bench|#t-bench}}, "Speculative decoding"). The step, the acceptance rule and why it is exact, the worked example, every kind of drafter and the batch-size crossover are on {{Speculative decoding|n:3f35c17b0d0d81e086fde85940ebac1c}}; good small drafts on {{Distillation and small language models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}.

Kernels and graphs remove the overhead around the bytes: FlashAttention computes attention in on-chip memory ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e4a3e8e7a2c4771381}}; split-KV decode kernels on {{Writing kernels|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b9ab89c1bb7238cb6c}}), paged attention kernels read keys and values through the block table, and CUDA graphs record a decode step's hundreds of launches once and replay them ({{Streams, CUDA graphs and multi-GPU|n:3f05c17b0d0d81b19a6acab65c370b75}}; compilers on {{Compiler stack|n:3f05c17b0d0d81ddbc00fac9e8a2ff89}}).

9. More than one GPU

One GPU stops being enough for three different reasons, each with its own fix: the weights do not fit, the cache for enough users does not fit, or one GPU is too slow for the latency target. The running example shows the second: Llama 3.1 70B in BF16 is 141 GB, so on H100s (80 GB) it needs two GPUs, and the two then have room for the cache of only 2 conversations {{Capacity planner|#t-plan}}, TP 2 on H100 in BF16.

Expert parallel (EP)some of a mixture-of-experts model's expertsall-to-all: tokens to their experts and backhuge MoE models; big batches per expertload imbalance across experts; network heavy5

Sections of {{Distributed and disaggregated serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d8170bd7cdf47af0ec07b}}, whose hero draws the five splits and what crosses per token; the parallelism forms themselves (Megatron's split, all-reduce, all-to-all) are owned by {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}}, the links by {{Interconnects and scaling|n:3c65c17b0d0d81808563fd41fb163af0}}.

Disaggregated serving puts prefill and decode on different GPUs because they want different things (compute against bandwidth, section 2) and because a prefill in a decode step stalls every stream. The prefill GPU sends the finished cache to a decode GPU: 2,000 tokens of the running example are 625 MiB, about 13 ms over a 400 Gb/s network link or under 1 ms over NVLink 2,000 × 320 KiB / link speed. vLLM's own docs say "Disaggregated prefill DOES NOT improve throughput": it buys control over TTFT and tail inter-token latency ({{disagg_prefill.md|https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/v0.31.0/docs/features/disagg_prefill.md}}). It is worth its complexity at scale with strict TPOT targets and long prompts; on a few GPUs, chunked prefill gets much of the benefit. When it pays, with the published claims and their settings, is that page's section 7.

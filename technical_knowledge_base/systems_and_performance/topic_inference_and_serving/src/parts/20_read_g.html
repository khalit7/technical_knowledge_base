6. Making each token cheaper

Since decode is limited by bytes read, there are two ways to make a token cheaper: read fewer bytes per token (quantization), or get more than one token per read (speculative decoding). Kernels and graphs remove the overheads around both.

Quantization for serving: fewer bytes per weight

Quantization stores numbers in fewer bits (the formats and the maths are on {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}). For serving, what matters is which numbers and what the hardware does with them:

Weight-only (INT4, INT8)4 or 8 bits with a scale per small group, unpacked to 16 bits inside the kernel (AWQ, GPTQ, GGUF k-quants). Decode reads 2 to 4 times fewer bytes and speeds up; prefill does not.
Weights and activations (FP8, FP4)Both sides of the multiply in 8 bits (4 on Blackwell's NVFP4 and MXFP4), on tensor cores two or four times BF16 speed: faster decode and prefill, on Hopper, Blackwell or MI300-class chips.
KV cache (FP8, INT8, INT4)Half or a quarter of the cache: more users per GPU. Check the quality cost per model.

Measured here on Qwen3-0.6B (llama.cpp, M1 Pro GPU): generation went from 113 tokens per second at F16 to 140 at Q8_0 and 196 at Q4_K_M (prompt processing fell, 3,618 to 3,240); WikiText-2 perplexity went from 20.88 to 20.86 at Q8_0 and 22.59 at Q4_K_M, and the top token matched F16's 97.8% and 83.0% of the time measured here ({{Engine bench|#t-bench}}, "Quantization: speed and quality"). Eight bits is close to free; four bits is a real trade, larger on small models. AWQ ({{Lin et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00978}}) and GPTQ ({{Frantar et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.17323}}) are the two common recipes for 4-bit weights.

Speculative decoding: guess several tokens, check them in one pass

A decode step at small batch leaves the arithmetic idle, so checking 5 tokens costs about the same as producing 1 (it is a tiny prefill). Speculative decoding uses that: a cheap draft proposes the next k tokens, the big target model scores all of them in one pass, keeps the longest run it agrees with, and adds one token of its own. A modified rejection-sampling rule keeps the output distribution exactly the target's ({{Leviathan et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192}}, {{Chen et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01318}}). With acceptance rate a per token, each target pass yields (1 − ak+1) / (1 − a) tokens on average: 2.77 at a = 0.7 and k = 4.

The draft can be a small model of the same family, extra prediction heads on the target (Medusa: 2.2 to 3.6 times faster in its authors' tests, {{Cai et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.10774}}), a light layer that predicts the target's own features (EAGLE: 2.7 to 3.5 times lower latency on LLaMA2-Chat 70B, {{Li et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15077}}), the model's own multi-token prediction heads (DeepSeek-V3), or no model at all: n-gram or prompt lookup, which copies a continuation that already appears in the prompt and works well for code edits and retrieval answers. The newest drafters propose a whole block at once with a small diffusion model: DFlash2 for Qwen3.8-27B reports 3.43 times the output throughput of plain decoding at concurrency 1 on GSM8K, behind the same exact verifier ({{model card|https://huggingface.co/incoai/Qwen3.8-27B-DFlash2}}, the authors). vLLM v0.31.0 offers all of these ({{config/speculative.py|https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/v0.31.0/vllm/config/speculative.py#L74}}).

It is not always faster, and not always byte-identical
It pays only when checking k tokens costs little more than producing one, and when the draft guesses well. At large batch the GPU is no longer idle, so verification is not free and the gain shrinks. On this laptop a Qwen3-4B pass over 4 tokens cost 2.2 times a pass over 1, so a 0.6B draft made llama.cpp slower on free text and at best 1.32 times faster on a highly predictable list prompt; MLX was slower at every k measured here. The output distribution is the target's in exact arithmetic, but in floating point a batched check can round a near-tie differently from one-at-a-time decoding: at k = 6, MLX's greedy output differed from its own baseline on two prompts ({{Engine bench|#t-bench}}, "Speculative decoding"). Test acceptance rate and speed on your own traffic and batch size.

Kernels and graphs: removing the overhead around the bytes

Three pieces of engineering every serious engine uses, each owned by another page: FlashAttention computes attention in on-chip memory without writing the full score matrix ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e4a3e8e7a2c4771381}}; split-KV decode kernels on {{Writing kernels|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b9ab89c1bb7238cb6c}}); paged attention kernels read keys and values through the block table; CUDA graphs record a decode step's hundreds of small kernel launches once and replay them, removing launch overhead that otherwise dominates small batches ({{Streams, CUDA graphs and multi-GPU|n:3f05c17b0d0d81b19a6acab65c370b75}}; compilers on {{Compiler stack|n:3f05c17b0d0d81ddbc00fac9e8a2ff89}}). The price of portability is now measured: vLLM's hardware-agnostic model layers came within 3.4% of the native implementation's total throughput on H100 (geometric mean over three models; {{PyTorch blog, 2026-09-22|https://pytorch.org/blog/hardware-agnostic-models-in-vllm/}}), against the tens of percent a hand-fused megakernel buys at batch 1 (section 1).

Common mistakes

"It does 3,000 tokens per second."
Per user or for the whole server? Input, output or both? At what latency? The running example does 46 tokens per second for one user alone and 2,982 for the GPU at 141 users, with each of them then at 21. A throughput figure without its concurrency and its TPOT is not a capacity.
Sizing by the weights alone
"70B in BF16 fits on two H100s" is true and useless: what is left holds the cache of 2 conversations (section 7). Size by weights plus cache for the concurrency you need, plus activations and the engine's reserve.
Reading GPU utilization as load
nvidia-smi's utilization says a kernel was running, not how much of the chip it used; memory-bound decode shows near 100% while the arithmetic units idle. Watch queue length, KV cache usage, TTFT and ITL.
Means instead of percentiles
The measured request of section 4 had a mean TPOT of 43.9 ms and an 803 ms stall. Users notice the stalls. Report p50, p90 and p99 of TTFT and ITL, and goodput at a stated SLO.
Benchmarking with a closed loop, fixed lengths and a warm cache
N users who each wait for their answer slow down with the server and hide overload; identical prompts measure the prefix cache. Use open-loop (Poisson) arrivals at several rates, real length distributions, and say what was cached.
Comparing engines at their defaults
Defaults differ: llama-server picks 4 slots on its own, mlx_lm.server decodes 32 at once, vLLM's token budget per step depends on the GPU, TensorRT-LLM admits only what is guaranteed to fit. Set the same batch limits, cache size and precision before comparing.
"Speculative decoding is a free 2 to 3 times"
The published speed-ups are at batch 1 on GPUs where checking k tokens is nearly free. At large batch, or where verification costs more (2.2 times for 4 tokens on the laptop measured here), it can slow decoding down; and floating-point outputs can differ from plain decoding.
"4-bit is free" and "4-bit is faster"
Weight-only 4-bit speeds up decode (fewer bytes) but not prefill (same arithmetic, plus unpacking: 3,240 against 3,618 tokens per second measured here), and costs quality that grows as models shrink (perplexity 20.88 to 22.59 on Qwen3-0.6B). Measure on your task.
Round-robin in front of prefix caching
With N replicas, a returning conversation lands on a replica without its cache (N − 1) / N of the time, and its history is prefilled again. Route by prefix.

Interview questions, with short answers

Why is decode memory-bound and prefill compute-bound?
Each weight does 2 operations per token. Decode processes one token per sequence per step, so it reads every weight for about 2 operations per byte (in FP8); an H200 can do 412 operations per byte read. Prefill processes thousands of prompt tokens per read of the weights, far above 412. Batching moves decode up the same axis.
How much KV cache do 100 users at 4K context need on Llama 3.1 70B?
2 × 80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 × 2 bytes = 320 KiB per token; × 4,096 × 100 = 125 GiB in BF16, more than one H200 has left after the weights; FP8 halves it. Then say what you would do: FP8 cache, TP 2, or fewer users per GPU.
What did continuous batching fix, and what did PagedAttention fix?
Continuous batching (Orca) re-forms the batch every step, so finished requests leave and new ones join at once, instead of waiting for the slowest answer. PagedAttention (vLLM) allocates the cache in small blocks through a block table instead of one slab per request for its maximum length, so far more requests fit, which is where the throughput comes from; it also makes prefix sharing natural.
TTFT is fine at low load but its p99 explodes at peak. Why, and what do you do?
Queueing: requests wait for cache room or for prefill budget. Check waiting requests, KV usage and preemptions. Fixes: more replicas or bigger cache (FP8 KV, TP), chunked prefill with a sensible token budget, prefix caching and prefix-aware routing, admission control that rejects early instead of timing out late.
TPOT is spiky. Why?
Steps that also carry someone's prefill. Chunked prefill caps it; disaggregating prefill and decode removes it.
When would you split prefill and decode onto different GPUs?
At scale, with strict TPOT targets and long prompts, when the two phases want different parallelism or hardware. The price is moving the cache (625 MiB per 2,000-token prompt of a 70B model) and running two pools. On a few GPUs, chunked prefill first.
When does speculative decoding not help?
At large batch (the GPU is no longer idle, so verification costs real time), with a poor draft (low acceptance), or where a k-token pass costs much more than a 1-token pass. Measure acceptance and speed at your batch size.
Design: a chat assistant on a 70B model, 20 requests per second at peak, p99 TTFT 2 s, TPOT 50 ms.
Per request: 2,000 tokens in, 300 out. FP8 weights (73 GB) fit one H200, leaving about 54 GB of cache: 141 conversations with a shared system prompt. Single-user decode 21.6 ms per token, prefill 0.42 s. Find the load per GPU that holds both targets (2.11 requests per second in the planner), so 10 GPUs, about $45 an hour; replicas behind a prefix-aware router; chunked prefill; autoscale on queue and TTFT with warm spares; compare against an API at average load.
How do you route a multi-turn chat across replicas?
By prefix: hash the conversation's prefix (or ask the replicas what they hold) and send it where its cache lives, unless that replica is overloaded. Round-robin forces re-prefill.
Self-host or API?
Compute dollars per million tokens at your average utilization, not at peak throughput. APIs win at low or bursty volume; self-hosting at high steady volume, with heavy prefix reuse, custom models or data that cannot leave. Add the engineers' time.

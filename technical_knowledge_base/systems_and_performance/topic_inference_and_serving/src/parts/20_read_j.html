9. Running it in production

Routing: send a conversation back to its cache

With several replicas, something in front must pick one for each request. A web load balancer's round-robin spreads the load evenly but ignores the cache: the second turn of a conversation lands on a replica that never saw the first, and its whole history is prefilled again. Prefix-aware routing (also called cache-aware or KV-aware) sends a request to the replica most likely to hold its prefix, weighed against how busy each replica is.

Eight requests, three replicas: round-robin against prefix-aware routing

illustrative traffic: four conversations (A to D) of two turns, arriving in order; first turns are the running example's 1,000-token system prompt plus 1,000 tokens; a second turn resends those 2,000, the 300-token answer and 200 new tokens. Prefill cost per token is the running example's derived 0.21 ms. The prefix-aware router here scores each replica by the tokens of the prefix it holds minus 1,000 per request already sent to it, a toy version of the load-and-cache scoring real routers use; even so replica 1 ends with four requests.

Published gains are vendor or partner measurements: llm-d reports 3 times the output throughput and half the TTFT of round-robin for Llama 3.1 70B on four MI300X ({{llm-d README|https://github.com/llm-d/llm-d}}); NVIDIA Dynamo's KV-aware router, twice as fast a TTFT on Baseten's Qwen3-Coder 480B deployment ({{Dynamo README|https://github.com/ai-dynamo/dynamo}}). Gateways sit above this: {{Gateways and routing|n:3f15c17b0d0d81e4a964c129a3e520ae}}. Client-side prompt caching with API providers is on {{Context engineering|n:3f15c17b0d0d811292cee46514370431}}.

Autoscaling and cold starts

Scale on what the users feel and what runs out: queue length, KV cache usage, TTFT. Not on GPU utilization, which reads near 100% during memory-bound decode whether the server is comfortable or drowning. A new replica must load its weights (72.66 GB here: about 36 s at an assumed 2 GB/s from local NVMe 72.66 / 2, minutes from remote storage), profile memory and capture CUDA graphs. Engines now attack start-up directly: vLLM v0.30.0 cut CUDA graph capture from 12 s to 2 s and engine start from 28.9 s to 8.2 s on an H200, and keeps quantized weights resident in a per-GPU daemon across restarts (vllm preload in v0.31.0) ({{release notes|https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/releases/tag/v0.30.0}}); SGLang v0.5.18 overlaps loading with graph capture, 35.6 s against 84.8 s for Qwen3-32B on an H100 against its plain default ({{release notes|https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/releases/tag/v0.5.18}}); both are the projects' own measurements. Keep warm replicas, cache weights locally, and scale ahead of known peaks (ServerlessLLM, {{Fu et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.14351}}, reports 10 to 200 times faster starts from local, loading-optimised checkpoints).

Observability and benchmarking honestly

Watch per replica: running and waiting requests, KV cache usage, preemptions, prefix cache hit rate, TTFT and ITL histograms, tokens in and out. Most engines publish these at /metrics for Prometheus. When you benchmark, a number is only meaningful with its conditions:

  • Open-loop arrivals (Poisson, as MLPerf's Server scenario) for a service; a closed loop (N users who each wait for their answer) hides overload, because the users slow down with the server.
  • Real length distributions, not one fixed prompt and output length; state whether end-of-sequence was ignored to force lengths.
  • Prefix cache state: repeated identical prompts measure the cache, not the model; and single-turn chat traffic hides what agents do (long multi-turn contexts, heavy prefix reuse, bursts of sub-agents), which is why SemiAnalysis replays real agentic traffic in its AgentX comparisons ({{2026-09-14|https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/vera-rubin-nvl72-agentic-inference}}, partly paywalled).
  • Percentiles and goodput at a stated SLO, across a sweep of load to find the knee; warm-up excluded; several runs with their spread; exact versions, flags and hardware. The {{Engine bench|#t-bench}} records every run with its command line and the machine's load, because this laptop was shared.

10. Production: routing, scaling and honest numbers

With several replicas, something in front picks one for each request. A web load balancer's round-robin spreads load evenly but ignores the cache: with N replicas, a returning conversation lands on one without its cache (N − 1) / N of the time and its whole history is prefilled again. Prefix-aware routing (cache-aware, KV-aware) sends a request to the replica most likely to hold its prefix, weighed against how busy each replica is. {{LLM serving in production|n:3f35c17b0d0d818aa406da1f7a784c62}}, section 2, steps eight requests through three real replicas under every routing rule (round-robin computed 13,000 prompt tokens; the rules that follow the prefix computed as few as 5,804) and replays a real multi-turn trace; how Dynamo and llm-d score workers is on {{Distributed and disaggregated serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d8170bd7cdf47af0ec07b}}, section 2, and SGLang's router code on {{SGLang|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e691e4ce9f9b5d1fad}}, section 8.

Depth: mistakes and interview rehearsal

Common mistakes (each points to the section that shows why)
  • "It does 3,000 tokens per second." Per user or for the server, at what latency? The running example does 46 for one user alone and 2,982 for the GPU at 141 users, each of them then at 21. A throughput without its concurrency and TPOT is not a capacity (sections 1 and 5).
  • Sizing by the weights alone. "70B in BF16 fits on two H100s" is true and useless: what is left holds the cache of 2 conversations (section 9). Size by weights plus cache for the concurrency you need.
  • Reading GPU utilisation as load. nvidia-smi's utilisation says a kernel was running, not how much of the chip it used (section 10).
  • Means instead of percentiles. The measured request of section 6 had a mean TPOT of 43.9 ms and an 803 ms stall.
  • Comparing engines at their defaults. llama-server picks 4 slots on its own, mlx_lm.server decodes 32 at once, vLLM's token budget per step depends on the GPU, TensorRT-LLM admits only what is guaranteed to fit. Set the same batch limits, cache size and precision before comparing.
  • "4-bit is faster." Weight-only 4-bit speeds up decode but not prefill (3,240 against 3,618 tokens per second measured here), and costs quality that grows as models shrink (section 8).
Interview: why is decode memory-bound and prefill compute-bound?
Each weight does 2 operations per token. Decode processes one token per sequence per step, so it reads every weight for about 2 operations per byte in FP8; an H200 can do 412 per byte read. Prefill processes thousands of tokens per read, far above 412. Batching moves decode up the same axis.
Interview: how much KV cache do 100 users at 4K context need on Llama 3.1 70B?
320 KiB per token × 4,096 × 100 = 125 GiB in BF16, more than one H200 has left after the weights; FP8 halves it. Then say what you would do: FP8 cache, TP 2, or fewer users per GPU.
Interview: TTFT is fine at low load but its p99 explodes at peak. Why, and what do you do?
Queueing: requests wait for cache room or prefill budget. Check waiting requests, KV usage and preemptions. Fixes: more replicas or a bigger cache (FP8 KV, TP), chunked prefill with a sensible token budget, prefix caching and prefix-aware routing, admission control that rejects early instead of timing out late. If TPOT is spiky instead, steps are carrying someone's prefill: chunked prefill caps it, disaggregation removes it.
Interview: design a chat assistant on a 70B model, 20 requests per second at peak, p99 TTFT 2 s, TPOT 50 ms.
Per request 2,000 tokens in, 300 out. FP8 weights (73 GB) fit one H200, leaving about 54 GB of cache: 141 conversations with a shared system prompt. Single-user decode 21.6 ms per token, prefill 0.42 s. Find the load per GPU that holds both targets (2.11 requests per second in the planner), so 10 GPUs, about $45 an hour; replicas behind a prefix-aware router; chunked prefill; autoscale on queue and TTFT with warm spares; compare against an API at average load.

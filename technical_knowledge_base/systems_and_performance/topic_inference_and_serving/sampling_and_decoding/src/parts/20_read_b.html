1. Search: greedy and beam

Greedy decoding takes the argmax token at every step: what temperature 0 means. It is locally optimal and, for open-ended text, repetitive and dull. Beam search keeps the B highest-probability partial sequences: each is extended by its best next tokens, all candidates are scored by total log-probability, and the top B survive. Beam search is the standard for machine translation and summarisation, where outputs are short and closed-ended; chat LLMs essentially do not use it, and greedy is fine only for short factual answers.

In Fig. 2 both run on GPT-2 small continuing a sentence from Jerome K. Jerome's {{Three Men in a Boat|https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/308}} (1889); every score is the model's own log-probability measured here. Each column is one step; a box's height is its sequence's total log-probability (taller is more probable), and faded boxes are hypotheses the decoder dropped.

  1. One hypothesis. The prompt ends "It made me awfully wild,". Greedy keeps a single sequence: each step appends the most probable token and never looks back.
  2. Step 1: " and". Log-probability −0.68, ahead of " but" (−2.23) and " so" (−3.08); the other two are never revisited.
  3. Step 2: " I". −0.73; running total −1.41.
  4. Step 3: " was". −2.12 (total −3.53), ahead of " had" (−2.76) and " couldn" (−3.31).
  5. Step 4: " very". " very" (−2.64) edges out " so" (−2.66) by 0.02. Greedy commits; whatever follows must live with it.
  6. Step 5: " angry". −2.43; running total −8.61.
  7. Step 6: done. "and I was very angry." Six tokens, total log-probability −9.44 (probability 7.9e-5). Switch to beam search to see a more probable path greedy could not find.
  1. Three hypotheses at once. With B = 3, every beam is extended by its 3 best tokens at each step, the 9 candidates are ranked by total log-probability, and the best 3 survive. In each column the candidates are sorted by that total, best at the front.
  2. Step 1. The three best first tokens become the three beams: " and" (−0.68), " but" (−2.23), " so" (−3.08).
  3. Step 2. Every beam continues with " I"; the totals keep "and I" (−1.41) well ahead of "but I" (−3.16) and "so I" (−3.24).
  4. Step 3. All three survivors extend beam 1 ("and I was", "and I had", "and I couldn"): the "but" and "so" branches die out. Beam search often collapses to one prefix like this.
  5. Step 4. Greedy's path "and I was very" scores −6.17 and is dropped: "and I couldn't" (−4.72), "and I had to" (−4.85) and "and I had no" (−6.10) score higher. Greedy could not see this because " was" looked best one step earlier.
  6. Step 5. "had no" followed by " idea" (−0.62) overtakes the others (total −6.72): a weak step can pay off when the next token is near-certain.
  7. Step 6. Best beam: "and I had no idea what", total −7.54, against greedy's −9.44: 6.7 times more probable. More probable is not better text: see the 80-token runs below.

Try: step to 4 in beam mode. Greedy's "was very" is still a candidate but scores below three other branches, so beam search drops it. After six tokens the best beam is 6.7 times more probable than greedy's text measured here (exp(−7.54 + 9.44); recompute.py).

More probable is not better. Run both for 80 tokens on the same prompt measured here: both searches fall into a loop the model rates as highly probable; the sample wanders but does not repeat.

Holtzman et al. named this: "using likelihood as a decoding objective leads to text that is bland and strangely repetitive", and natural text's per-token probability is "on average, much lower than text generated by beam search" (§4.3). On 5,000 WebText continuations with GPT-2 Large (762M) they measured perplexity 12.38 for human text against 1.50 for greedy and 1.48 for beam search with 16 beams, and 73.66% of greedy texts repeating (Table 1, {{Holtzman et al., 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.09751}}). Section 3 reruns that comparison on twelve decoders.

Correction: temperature 0 is greedy, but not reproducible on a server
Greedy is deterministic for a fixed computation; a served model is not one, because the batch it shares with other requests changes the kernels' reduction order. Thinking Machines sent the same prompt 1,000 times to Qwen3-235B at temperature 0 and got 80 different completions, diverging at token 103; batch-invariant kernels made all 1,000 identical ({{He, September 2025|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/}}; how a server reshapes that batch every step is on {{Batching, KV cache and scheduling in depth|n:3c65c17b0d0d819db53bd2c48909e5b2}}). Anthropic says the same of its API: "temperature = 0 ... never guaranteed identical outputs" ({{Opus 5.5 migration guide|https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/models/opus-5-5/migration-guide}}), and its newer models and API version no longer accept a temperature at all (section 6).

In one screen

A large model generating text spends most of each step reading its weights, not doing arithmetic. Speculative decoding lets a cheap drafter guess the next few tokens and has the large target model check all the guesses in one forward pass, keeping the ones it agrees with. A probabilistic acceptance rule makes the result exactly what the target alone would have produced, only sooner.

Why it can work

On this laptop one Qwen3-4B forward pass over 5 tokens took times as long as a pass over 1 token, not 5 times measured here. Checking guesses is cheap while the chip waits on memory.

Why it is exact

Accept a guessed token x with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)); on rejection, draw from what the target wanted and the draft under-proposed. Tested here on real models with 20,000 samples per rule: the exact rules pass, a one-line bug fails by a chi-square of .

What it gives

Tokens per target pass = (1 − αk+1)/(1 − α), with α the chance a guess is accepted and k guesses per step. Published: 2 to 3x at batch 1 with a good drafter on datacenter chips (authors' and vendors' numbers).

When it stops paying

As the batch grows a step becomes compute-bound and every extra checked token costs real time. Measured on a real server here: Engines now switch it down or off by batch size.

1. Why decode leaves the chip idle

The problem. A language model produces one token per forward pass, and each pass depends on the token before it. During decode (generating the answer) each pass processes one new token per user, yet it has to read every weight of the model from memory. Qwen3-4B at 4-bit (Q4_K_M) is a GB file: every token of the answer reads those bytes once. On the M1 Pro that pass took ms, about tokens per second measured here.

How much arithmetic is that? About 2 operations per weight per token (one multiply, one add), so roughly 8 billion operations per token for a 4B model. At tokens per second that is under 0.4 trillion operations per second, on a GPU that did trillion per second while processing a 512-token prompt (the same weights, read once, used for 512 tokens). The arithmetic units are mostly idle; the step waits for memory. This is the memory-bound regime, worked out with a roofline on Performance math.

Predict: one Qwen3-4B pass over 5 tokens (positions processed in parallel, as when checking 4 guesses plus one) versus a pass over 1 token, on the M1 Pro GPU. How much longer?

Time of one forward pass against the number of tokens in it

What this means. If the pass time stayed flat as tokens were added, checking guesses would be free. On a datacenter GPU it nearly is: in the derived H100 curve a pass stays within 10% of the one-token time up to about tokens, because the H100 does about 590 FP8 operations per byte it reads (1,979 TFLOP/s over 3.35 TB/s) and a few extra tokens use a sliver of that. On the laptop the measured curve is a staircase instead: 2 tokens cost x one token, 5 tokens x, yet 16 to 32 tokens cost only times. The steps are kernels, not physics. llama.cpp's Metal backend multiplies a quantized weight matrix by 1 token with a matrix-vector kernel; it has a small-batch kernel for 2 to 8 tokens, but for the Q4_K and Q6_K formats in this file only from 4 tokens; and above 8 tokens it switches to a matrix-matrix kernel (ggml-metal-ops.cpp L2442 to L2470, ggml-metal-common.cpp L28 to L35). The same measurement on Qwen3-1.7B at Q8_0, which does get the small-batch kernel from 2 tokens, gives x for 5 tokens (toggle above).

What transfers from the laptop
The shape transfers: a pass over a few tokens costs much less than that many passes, because the weights are read once. Where it bends does not, and it depends on hardware, weight format and kernels as much as on the model. The M1 Pro's GPU does about 5 trillion operations per second on this workload against roughly 150 GB/s of effective bandwidth (the parent's Engine bench), about 35 operations per byte, so it runs out of idle arithmetic within tens of tokens even with ideal kernels; an H100 after a hundred or more. Before trusting any speed-up, measure t(k + 1)/t(1) on your own engine and weights.

2. Draft, then verify

How it works. Each speculative step has two halves.

  1. Draft. A cheap drafter proposes the next k tokens (k is the draft length, typically 2 to 8). Here the drafter is Qwen3-0.6B, run k times in a row, each run a fast small forward pass.
  2. Verify. The target runs once over the last accepted token plus the k guesses. Because a transformer computes a next-token distribution at every position of its input, this one pass gives the target's distribution after each prefix: after the context, after guess 1, after guesses 1 and 2, and so on, k + 1 distributions in all.
  3. Accept. Walk the guesses left to right and keep each one the target agrees with (section 3 says exactly what "agrees" means). At the first disagreement, replace that guess with a token drawn from the target and throw away the rest. If every guess survives, take one more bonus token from the target's last distribution.

So one target pass yields between 1 token (the first guess was wrong: no worse than plain decoding, apart from the time spent drafting) and k + 1 tokens. The draft's and the target's KV caches (the stored attention keys and values of earlier tokens) are rolled back past any rejected guesses. 3. The acceptance rule, and why it is exact

The problem. If the target samples with temperature (not always the most likely token), "agree" cannot mean "the guess equals the target's top token". We want the accepted text to be distributed exactly as if the target had sampled it alone. Write p for the target's next-token distribution and q for the draft's, at the same position.

accept x ~ q with probability min(1, p(x) / q(x)); otherwise draw from (p − q)+ / Σ(p − q)+
p, q
target and draft probabilities after the same prefix (after temperature, top-k and so on, the same settings on both)
(p − q)+
max(0, p(x) − q(x)): the residual, the probability the draft under-proposed

Why it is exact (Leviathan et al. 2023, Chen et al. 2023, found independently). The chance of outputting x through acceptance is q(x) · min(1, p(x)/q(x)) = min(p(x), q(x)). The chance of a rejection is β = 1 − Σ min(p, q), and the residual's total mass is Σ(p − q)+ = Σ(p − min(p, q)) = β too. So the chance of outputting x through rejection is β · (p − q)+(x)/β = (p(x) − q(x))+. Add them: min(p, q)(x) + (p − q)+(x) = p(x). The draft only changes how often we get the token cheaply, never which token we get. The probability that a guess is accepted is

α = Σx min(p(x), q(x)) = 1 − TV(p, q)
TV
total variation distance, half the summed absolute difference between p and q

The picture: overlay the two distributions. The overlap is what is accepted; the part of p sticking out above q is the residual. Here is a real position from a story the target wrote (Qwen3-1.7B target, Qwen3-0.6B draft, temperature 1), and the same 16 draft guesses judged by two exact rules.

measured here p and q are the real distributions at that position (top 50 tokens kept on both; the 8 tokens with the largest p + q shown, guesses can fall outside them). The 16 guesses and coin flips are drawn with a fixed seed and reused by both rules, so the only difference is the rule.

The second rule is also exact, and slower. llama.cpp and mlx-lm verify by sampling the target's own token at each position and keeping the guess only if the two are equal (llama.cpp common/sampling.cpp L678 to 705). Every output token is then a genuine sample from p, so the distribution is exact, but a guess survives with probability Σ p(x) q(x), which is lower than Σ min(p, q) whenever the target is uncertain. With a greedy draft (q puts all its mass on the draft's top token, which is what llama.cpp and vLLM's default do), both rules accept with probability p(draft token). Over the target's own story text, the mean per-guess acceptance was under min(1, p/q) with a sampled draft, with a greedy draft and under sample-and-compare with a sampled draft measured here.

Testing exactness on real models

A proof is a proof, but implementations have bugs. The Acceptance rule lab runs the test properly; the result in brief. For the story prompt, 20,000 four-token openings were drawn with each rule (k = 3, temperature 1, top-k 50, so steps include rejections, residual draws and bonus tokens), and each set was compared against the target's exact probabilities for all openings with an expected count of at least 5, plus one "other" bin (a chi-square goodness-of-fit test).

The three exact rules are indistinguishable from sampling the target directly; the "bug" (on rejection, draw from p instead of the residual) over-produces the tokens both models like, and the test catches it at once. With 20,000 samples a rule that shifted the opening distribution by more than a few percent of total variation would fail.

Temperature 0 and floating point

At temperature 0 the rule reduces to "keep the guess while it equals the target's top token", and the output should be identical to plain greedy decoding. In exact arithmetic it is. In floating point, the target's logits for a position depend slightly on how the computation was batched. Computing a sequence's logits in blocks of 7 tokens (as a k = 6 verifier does) instead of one token at a time moved individual logits by up to on the 4-bit Qwen3-1.7B in MLX (which computes in bfloat16, about 3 significant digits), and changed the top token at of positions across four prompts, each one a near-tie where the top two logits were at most apart measured here. That is why the parent's Engine bench saw mlx-lm's k = 6 output differ from its own plain output on two prompts. Neither output is wrong; greedy decoding is only as deterministic as the arithmetic underneath it (the same holds for any change of batch size, with or without speculation).

4. How many tokens does a step give?

If every guess is accepted independently with the same probability α, a step keeps i guesses with probability αi(1 − α) (for i < k), always adds one target token, and so yields on average

E[tokens per step] = 1 + α + α2 + … + αk = (1 − αk+1) / (1 − α)
α
acceptance probability of one guess, Σ min(p, q), averaged over positions
k
guesses per step

Two consequences are worth remembering. The yield is capped at 1/(1 − α) however long the draft: at α = 0.7 that is 3.33, and k = 4 already gets 2.77 of it. And the benefit is very non-linear in α: at k = 4, α = 0.5 gives 1.94 tokens per step, α = 0.9 gives 4.10.

Why it works, and its catch. The formula assumes independent coin flips with one α, and real acceptance is anything but uniform: near 1 inside a word or a repeated pattern, near 0 where the text could go many ways (the first word of a story). Yet with α measured as the mean over the guesses actually examined, it predicted the measured tokens per step with a median error of % (largest %) over all combinations of prompt, temperature and k run here measured here. The catch is that α itself cannot be predicted: it was on the list and on the story with the same two models. Measure it on your own traffic; engines report it (section 8). Because acceptance depends on the text, text can also be written to lower it: an adversarial suffix appended to attacker-controlled content (a retrieved page, a shared document) makes drafter and target disagree more often, in some cases until speculative decoding is slower than plain decoding; the suffixes survive sampling and transfer across drafters, and regularising them to keep output quality gives up most of the slowdown (Speedbumps (arXiv 2610.10929)). Per-request acceptance is worth monitoring for that reason too.

5. What it costs, and the batch-size crossover

The cost model. A step costs k draft passes plus one target pass over k + 1 tokens. Measure everything in units of one ordinary target step, t(1):

speed-up = E[tokens per step] × t(1) / ( k · tdraft + t(k + 1) )
t(n)
time of one target pass over n tokens per sequence (section 1's curve)
tdraft
time of one draft pass; Leviathan et al. call tdraft/t(1) the cost coefficient c

Leviathan et al. assume t(k + 1) = t(1), which gives their speed-up (1 − αk+1) / ((1 − α)(kc + 1)), with c below 0.05 for their T5 drafts (though their own Table 4 gives 0.11 for T5-large). The batch-size crossover. Now serve many users at once (how an engine forms and schedules those batches is on Batching, KV cache and scheduling in depth). With B users in a batch, the target's verification pass carries B × (k + 1) tokens. The weights are still read once per step, so plain decoding gets cheaper per token as B grows (the reason batching exists), but the flat part of section 1's curve gets used up by the batch itself: once B tokens already fill the arithmetic, every extra guessed token costs real compute, and rejected guesses are pure waste. Speculation trades idle arithmetic for latency, and a full batch has no idle arithmetic.

The Batch crossover lab rebuilds this from the measured cost curve and lets you move α, k, the drafter's cost and the hardware; it gets the shape right and the laptop's absolute numbers wrong (it leaves out llama-server's per-step overhead), and says by how much.

What the published GPU results say
The same crossover appears in every measured source, further out because an H100 stays memory-bound longer. EAGLE-3 in SGLang on one H100 (Llama 3.1 8B, MT-Bench): 1.81x throughput at batch 2, 1.48x at 16, 1.38x at 64; the older EAGLE fell below 1x from batch 24 (EAGLE-3, Table 3, measured by the SGLang team). DFlash on one B200 with Qwen3-4B: 4.8x at concurrency 1, 2.9x at 32 (DFlash, Table 3, authors). vLLM's own blog: 1.5x to 2.8x at QPS 1, but 1.4x to 1.8x slower at high QPS on Llama 3 70B, 4x H100 (2024-10-17, vendor). On an L40S at QPS 16, even k = 1 cost 14% (TurboSpec, vLLM authors). No benchmark across batch sizes by a party that is neither vendor nor author was found.

Two things move the crossover out. Long contexts: every sequence's KV cache must also be read each step, and that read grows with batch size times context length, so at long context a big batch is still memory-bound and verification is cheap again; MagicDec reports up to 2.51x for Llama 3.1 8B at batch 32 to 256 on 8x H100 with long contexts (Sadhukhan et al., ICLR 2025, authors). The drafter needs no long context of its own: one that attends over the full prefix inherits the target's context cost and its speedup erodes, then reverses, at ultra-long context, but target-conditioned drafters (EAGLE-style heads) already read the target's hidden states over the whole prefix. Frozen short-context drafters with a bounded attention cache and rotary positions re-based into their trained range kept their short-context speedup flat from 4k to two million tokens across three target-drafter pairs and five corpora, at a cost of at most 0.16x of speedup at short length (Long-Context Drafting Does Not Require Long-Context Drafters (arXiv 2610.20865), authors). Cheaper drafts and adaptive length: a drafter whose cost does not grow with k (block diffusion), and choosing k per step from the load (TurboSpec, SGLang's adaptive table, vLLM's batch-size schedule, DSpark's per-request verification length) keep the gain at low load and avoid the loss at high load.

6. Drafters: where the guesses come from

Everything so far works with any drafter that proposes tokens and (for sampling) says how likely it thought each one was. The drafters differ in three things: how well they guess (acceptance), what a guess costs (the cost ratio c), and what you have to train or ship. Why feature-level drafters guess better

A separate 0.6B model has to work out what the 4B model is thinking from the tokens alone. EAGLE's head is handed the 4B model's own hidden state (its summary of everything so far), so its job is much easier: predict what the next feature, and so the next token, will be. That is why a single extra layer can out-guess a separate model several times its size: on Qwen3-4B, the AngelSlim EAGLE-3 head (one layer) reports a mean accepted length of 2.08 with 2 draft tokens on one H20 in vLLM, where plain decoding gives 1 (model card, vendor). The cost: the head only works with the exact target it was trained for, and the engine must pass hidden states from the target to the drafter every step.

MTP takes this one step further: the prediction module is trained with the model, so the model's maker ships the draft. DeepSeek-V3 trained one MTP module (depth 1) to improve pre-training and noted it can be "repurposed ... for speculative decoding" (DeepSeek-V3 paper page has a calculator for what 85 to 90% acceptance implies). With one MTP token, a step yields at most 2 tokens; engines chain the module to draft 2 to 4.

Block diffusion attacks the other term, the drafting cost. An autoregressive drafter needs k sequential passes for k tokens, so c grows with k; a diffusion drafter writes the block in one pass, so a 16-token draft costs about as much as a 1-token draft. Its weakness is that block positions are guessed without seeing each other (the "suffix decay" DSpark's Markov head tries to fix). The same idea used as the whole model rather than as a drafter is on the DiffusionGemma paper page.

Not every acceleration in this family is exact
Medusa-2's typical acceptance accepts a guessed token when the target gives it probability above min(ε, δ·e−H) (H the entropy of the target's distribution); it is faster but no longer samples from the target (Cai et al. 2024). TensorRT-LLM's "relaxed acceptance" for DeepSeek-R1's thinking phase is lossy in the same way (accept if in the target's top 10 within a probability gap; TensorRT-LLM blog). Exactness is a property of the verification rule, not of the drafter. And a lossy rule's acceptance overstates its speed: across twelve target-drafter pairs, at each training-free relaxed rule's most relaxed setting, verification accepted 1.34x as many tokens per round as the exact rule but completions came out 1.54x longer, mostly longer reasoning, which largely cancelled the gain; the rules that cut verifier rounds typically lost accuracy substantially (Acceptance Is Not Speed (arXiv 2610.23818)). Under a lossy rule, report completion length and verifier rounds beside acceptance length.

7. Trees: checking several guesses in one pass

A chain draft dies at its first wrong token. If the draft was unsure between two words, it could propose both and let the target pick: a draft tree. All branches are checked in the same forward pass using a tree attention mask (each candidate token attends only to its own ancestors), introduced by SpecInfer and Medusa, and made adaptive in EAGLE-2, which grows the tree where the draft's confidence (the product of its probabilities along the path) is highest. The target keeps the longest root path it agrees with.

The trade: a tree spends its verification budget on breadth instead of depth. With a budget of M tokens checked per pass, a chain of M goes deep on one guess; a tree of M nodes covers the likely alternatives near the root, where most chains fail.

Accepted length = the longest root path equal to the target's own greedy continuation.

That is why engines use trees at low concurrency and short chains, or none, at high concurrency.

Common mistakes

"Speculative decoding changes the model's answers"
With an exact rule (min(1, p/q) plus the residual, or sample-and-compare) the output distribution is the target's; the Acceptance rule lab tests it on real models. What can change outputs is floating point (batched verification rounds differently) and lossy variants (typical acceptance, relaxed acceptance), which are a choice, not the method.
Quoting the acceptance rate as tokens per step
"Acceptance rate 0.7" and "acceptance length 2.8" are different numbers. Engines report one or the other (vLLM and SGLang show acceptance length, llama.cpp drafted and accepted counts, from which a per-drafted-token rate follows but not α). Convert with the formula in section 4, and remember that per-drafted-token rates fall as k grows because tokens after the first rejection are wasted.
Benchmarking at batch 1 and deploying at batch 64
Every headline number in section 6 is at batch or concurrency 1 unless stated. Measure speed-up at your real concurrency, and turn speculation down or off above the crossover (section 5).
Measuring on one kind of prompt
Acceptance depends on the text: on this laptop the same draft pair accepted most tokens on a list and few on a story. n-gram drafting is near useless on fresh prose and excellent on code edits. Use a sample of real traffic.
Ignoring the drafter's own cost
A 0.6B draft for a 4B target is 15% of the parameters, so c is far from Leviathan's "less than 0.05", and k sequential draft passes add up. The speed-up formula has c in the denominator for a reason.
Using any small model as a draft
The draft must share the target's tokenizer exactly (vocabulary and ids), or be mapped (EAGLE-3 drafts often use a reduced vocabulary mapped back with a table). Same family and same chat template matter for acceptance.

Interview questions, with short answers

Why does checking 5 guessed tokens cost much less than generating 5?
At small batch a decode step is limited by reading the weights (and KV cache) from memory, not by arithmetic. Processing 5 positions reads the same weights once and does 5 times the arithmetic, which idle compute absorbs. On an H100 with an 8B model the derived ratio is about x; on the M1 here a 4B pass over 5 tokens cost x a pass over 1, because of a weak GPU and kernels that batch small token counts poorly. Always measure t(k + 1)/t(1).
State the acceptance rule and why it is exact.
Draft x from q; accept with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)); on rejection sample from max(0, p − q) normalised. P(output x) = min(p, q)(x) + (1 − Σ min(p, q)) · (p − q)+(x) / Σ(p − q)+, and Σ(p − q)+ = 1 − Σ min(p, q), so it equals min(p, q)(x) + (p − q)+(x) = p(x).
What is the acceptance probability of one drafted token?
α = Σx min(p(x), q(x)) = 1 − total variation distance between p and q. With a greedy (one-hot) draft it is p(draft token).
Expected tokens per target pass with k drafted tokens?
(1 − αk+1) / (1 − α), assuming the same α at every position: 2.77 at α = 0.7, k = 4. In practice α varies by position and text, so measure it.
When does speculative decoding make things slower?
When acceptance is low, when the draft is expensive (c large), and at large batch, where verification is no longer free because the step has become compute-bound. Long contexts move the crossover out (KV reads keep the step memory-bound; MagicDec). And under attack: adversarial suffixes in content the attacker controls can lower acceptance until it is slower than plain decoding (Speedbumps).
Draft model, EAGLE, Medusa, MTP, n-gram: how do you choose?
If the model ships MTP heads, use them. Otherwise an EAGLE-3 head for that exact model if one exists; a same-family small model if not; n-gram or suffix decoding added on top for copy-heavy work (code edits, RAG, agents). Measure acceptance length and throughput at real concurrency.
Why are tree drafts better at batch 1 and worse at batch 64?
A tree raises the expected accepted length per pass by covering likely alternatives, at the price of more verified tokens per pass. At batch 1 the extra tokens are free; at batch 64 every extra token is real compute.
Your greedy outputs changed after enabling speculation. Bug?
Not necessarily: verifying k tokens in one batched pass can round logits differently from one-at-a-time decoding, which flips argmax at near-ties. Check that the first difference is at a near-tie (here the top two logits were at most apart at each divergence), and compare against plain decoding with the same batch shapes.

1. Your own p and q

Five tokens. Set the target's p and the draft's q (each is normalised to sum to 1), then compare what each verification rule outputs. Exact rules reproduce p whatever q is; q only changes how often the cheap path is taken.

Rules: min(1, p/q) with residual (Leviathan et al., vLLM); sample and compare (draft samples from q, target samples from p, keep if equal: llama.cpp and mlx-lm at temperature > 0); greedy draft (draft proposes its top token, kept with probability p(token): llama.cpp's drafter and vLLM's default); bug: resample from p (on rejection draw from p instead of the residual); no verification (always keep the draft's token). Output distributions are computed exactly; the simulation draws with a fixed seed and runs a chi-square test against p.

3. The exactness test on real models

Method: for the story prompt, draw 20,000 sequences of the first 4 output tokens with each rule (draft Qwen3-0.6B, target Qwen3-1.7B, both 4-bit MLX; temperature 1 and top-k 50 on both; k = 3, so a step can end in a rejection, a residual draw or a bonus token, and a second step is needed when fewer than 3 guesses are kept). Every distribution was computed with a full forward pass over prompt plus prefix and cached by prefix, so each rule saw exactly the same p and q. The exact target probability of every 4-token opening with an expected count of at least 5 ( openings, of the probability) was computed from the same distributions, the rest pooled into one bin, and each rule's counts compared with a chi-square goodness-of-fit test. A rule that preserves the distribution gives a p-value spread uniformly between 0 and 1; a broken one gives a p-value near 0.

measured here Bars: observed count of each of the 12 most likely openings against the target's exact expected count (20,000 × probability). Acceptance per step counts guesses kept in the steps needed for 4 tokens, so it is lower than a full step's.

Why the gain shrinks as the batch grows, as a cost model you can move, checked against real llama-server runs at 1 to 16 users on the laptop, and carried to an H100 with the parent's calibrated step model.

speed-up(B) = E[tokens per step] × t(B, 1) / ( k · tdraft(B) + t(B, k + 1) )
B
sequences decoding together (users in the batch)
t(B, n)
one target pass over n tokens for each of B sequences
E[tokens per step]
(1 − αk+1)/(1 − α), or a measured acceptance length

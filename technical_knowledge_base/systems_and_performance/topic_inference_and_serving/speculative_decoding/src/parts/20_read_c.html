4. How many tokens does a step give?

If every guess is accepted independently with the same probability α, a step keeps i guesses with probability αi(1 − α) (for i < k), always adds one target token, and so yields on average

E[tokens per step] = 1 + α + α2 + … + αk = (1 − αk+1) / (1 − α)
α
acceptance probability of one guess, Σ min(p, q), averaged over positions
k
guesses per step

Two consequences are worth remembering. The yield is capped at 1/(1 − α) however long the draft: at α = 0.7 that is 3.33, and k = 4 already gets 2.77 of it. And the benefit is very non-linear in α: at k = 4, α = 0.5 gives 1.94 tokens per step, α = 0.9 gives 4.10.

5. What it costs, and the batch-size crossover

The cost model. A step costs k draft passes plus one target pass over k + 1 tokens. Measure everything in units of one ordinary target step, t(1):

speed-up = E[tokens per step] × t(1) / ( k · tdraft + t(k + 1) )
t(n)
time of one target pass over n tokens per sequence (section 1's curve)
tdraft
time of one draft pass; Leviathan et al. call tdraft/t(1) the cost coefficient c

Leviathan et al. assume t(k + 1) = t(1), which gives their speed-up (1 − αk+1) / ((1 − α)(kc + 1)), with c below 0.05 for their T5 drafts (though their own Table 4 gives 0.11 for T5-large). The batch-size crossover. Now serve many users at once (how an engine forms and schedules those batches is on {{Batching, KV cache and scheduling in depth|n:3c65c17b0d0d819db53bd2c48909e5b2}}). With B users in a batch, the target's verification pass carries B × (k + 1) tokens. The weights are still read once per step, so plain decoding gets cheaper per token as B grows (the reason batching exists), but the flat part of section 1's curve gets used up by the batch itself: once B tokens already fill the arithmetic, every extra guessed token costs real compute, and rejected guesses are pure waste. Speculation trades idle arithmetic for latency, and a full batch has no idle arithmetic.

What the published GPU results say
The same crossover appears in every measured source, further out because an H100 stays memory-bound longer. EAGLE-3 in SGLang on one H100 (Llama 3.1 8B, MT-Bench): 1.81x throughput at batch 2, 1.48x at 16, 1.38x at 64; the older EAGLE fell below 1x from batch 24 ({{EAGLE-3, Table 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01840}}, measured by the SGLang team). DFlash on one B200 with Qwen3-4B: 4.8x at concurrency 1, 2.9x at 32 ({{DFlash, Table 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06036}}, authors). vLLM's own blog: 1.5x to 2.8x at QPS 1, but 1.4x to 1.8x slower at high QPS on Llama 3 70B, 4x H100 ({{2024-10-17|https://vllm.ai/blog/2024-10-17-spec-decode}}, vendor). On an L40S at QPS 16, even k = 1 cost 14% ({{TurboSpec|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14066}}, vLLM authors). No benchmark across batch sizes by a party that is neither vendor nor author was found.

Two things move the crossover out. Long contexts: every sequence's KV cache must also be read each step, and that read grows with batch size times context length, so at long context a big batch is still memory-bound and verification is cheap again; MagicDec reports up to 2.51x for Llama 3.1 8B at batch 32 to 256 on 8x H100 with long contexts ({{Sadhukhan et al., ICLR 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11049}}, authors). Cheaper drafts and adaptive length: a drafter whose cost does not grow with k (block diffusion), and choosing k per step from the load (TurboSpec, SGLang's adaptive table, vLLM's batch-size schedule, DSpark's per-request verification length) keep the gain at low load and avoid the loss at high load.

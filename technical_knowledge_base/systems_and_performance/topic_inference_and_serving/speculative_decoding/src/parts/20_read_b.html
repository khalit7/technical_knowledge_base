2. Draft, then verify

How it works. Each speculative step has two halves.

  1. Draft. A cheap drafter proposes the next k tokens (k is the draft length, typically 2 to 8). Here the drafter is Qwen3-0.6B, run k times in a row, each run a fast small forward pass.
  2. Verify. The target runs once over the last accepted token plus the k guesses. Because a transformer computes a next-token distribution at every position of its input, this one pass gives the target's distribution after each prefix: after the context, after guess 1, after guesses 1 and 2, and so on, k + 1 distributions in all.
  3. Accept. Walk the guesses left to right and keep each one the target agrees with (section 3 says exactly what "agrees" means). At the first disagreement, replace that guess with a token drawn from the target and throw away the rest. If every guess survives, take one more bonus token from the target's last distribution.

So one target pass yields between 1 token (the first guess was wrong: no worse than plain decoding, apart from the time spent drafting) and k + 1 tokens. The draft's and the target's KV caches (the stored attention keys and values of earlier tokens) are rolled back past any rejected guesses. 3. The acceptance rule, and why it is exact

The problem. If the target samples with temperature (not always the most likely token), "agree" cannot mean "the guess equals the target's top token". We want the accepted text to be distributed exactly as if the target had sampled it alone. Write p for the target's next-token distribution and q for the draft's, at the same position.

accept x ~ q with probability min(1, p(x) / q(x)); otherwise draw from (p − q)+ / Σ(p − q)+
p, q
target and draft probabilities after the same prefix (after temperature, top-k and so on, the same settings on both)
(p − q)+
max(0, p(x) − q(x)): the residual, the probability the draft under-proposed

Why it is exact ({{Leviathan et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192}}, {{Chen et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01318}}, found independently). The chance of outputting x through acceptance is q(x) · min(1, p(x)/q(x)) = min(p(x), q(x)). The chance of a rejection is β = 1 − Σ min(p, q), and the residual's total mass is Σ(p − q)+ = Σ(p − min(p, q)) = β too. So the chance of outputting x through rejection is β · (p − q)+(x)/β = (p(x) − q(x))+. Add them: min(p, q)(x) + (p − q)+(x) = p(x). The draft only changes how often we get the token cheaply, never which token we get. The probability that a guess is accepted is

α = Σx min(p(x), q(x)) = 1 − TV(p, q)
TV
total variation distance, half the summed absolute difference between p and q

The picture: overlay the two distributions. The overlap is what is accepted; the part of p sticking out above q is the residual. Here is a real position from a story the target wrote (Qwen3-1.7B target, Qwen3-0.6B draft, temperature 1), and the same 16 draft guesses judged by two exact rules.

The second rule is also exact, and slower. llama.cpp and mlx-lm verify by sampling the target's own token at each position and keeping the guess only if the two are equal (llama.cpp {{common/sampling.cpp L678 to 705|https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/b11146/common/sampling.cpp#L678}}). Every output token is then a genuine sample from p, so the distribution is exact, but a guess survives with probability Σ p(x) q(x), which is lower than Σ min(p, q) whenever the target is uncertain. With a greedy draft (q puts all its mass on the draft's top token, which is what llama.cpp and vLLM's default do), both rules accept with probability p(draft token). Over the target's own story text, the mean per-guess acceptance was under min(1, p/q) with a sampled draft, with a greedy draft and under sample-and-compare with a sampled draft measured here.

Testing exactness on real models

A proof is a proof, but implementations have bugs. The {{Acceptance rule lab|#t-sdrule}} runs the test properly; the result in brief. For the story prompt, 20,000 four-token openings were drawn with each rule (k = 3, temperature 1, top-k 50, so steps include rejections, residual draws and bonus tokens), and each set was compared against the target's exact probabilities for all openings with an expected count of at least 5, plus one "other" bin (a chi-square goodness-of-fit test).

The three exact rules are indistinguishable from sampling the target directly; the "bug" (on rejection, draw from p instead of the residual) over-produces the tokens both models like, and the test catches it at once. With 20,000 samples a rule that shifted the opening distribution by more than a few percent of total variation would fail.

Temperature 0 and floating point

At temperature 0 the rule reduces to "keep the guess while it equals the target's top token", and the output should be identical to plain greedy decoding. In exact arithmetic it is. In floating point, the target's logits for a position depend slightly on how the computation was batched. Computing a sequence's logits in blocks of 7 tokens (as a k = 6 verifier does) instead of one token at a time moved individual logits by up to on the 4-bit Qwen3-1.7B in MLX (which computes in bfloat16, about 3 significant digits), and changed the top token at of positions across four prompts, each one a near-tie where the top two logits were at most apart measured here. That is why the parent's {{Engine bench|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}} saw mlx-lm's k = 6 output differ from its own plain output on two prompts. Neither output is wrong; greedy decoding is only as deterministic as the arithmetic underneath it (the same holds for any change of batch size, with or without speculation).

2. One step: draft, verify, accept

  1. Draft. The drafter proposes the next k tokens (typically 2 to 8). Here Qwen3-0.6B runs k times in a row, each a fast small forward pass.
  2. Verify. The target runs once over the last accepted token plus the k guesses. A transformer computes a next-token distribution at every input position, so this one pass gives the target's distribution after the context, after guess 1, after guesses 1 and 2, and so on: k + 1 distributions.
  3. Accept. Walk the guesses left to right and keep each one the target agrees with (section 3 says exactly what "agrees" means). At the first disagreement, replace that guess with a token drawn from the target and throw away the rest. If every guess survives, take one more bonus token from the target's last distribution.

One target pass therefore yields between 1 token (the first guess was wrong: no worse than plain decoding, apart from the drafting time) and k + 1. Both models' KV caches are rolled back past rejected guesses. Here is a real answer, step by step.

3. The acceptance rule, and why it is exact

When the target samples with temperature, "agree" cannot mean "the guess equals the target's top token": we want the kept text distributed exactly as if the target had sampled it alone. Write p for the target's next-token distribution and q for the draft's, at the same position. Overlay them: the overlap min(p, q) is what can be accepted cheaply; the part of p sticking out above q is what the draft under-proposed. Why it is exact ({{Leviathan et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192}} and {{Chen et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01318}}, found independently). The chance of outputting x through acceptance is q(x) · min(1, p(x)/q(x)) = min(p(x), q(x)). The chance of a rejection is β = 1 − Σ min(p, q), and the residual's total mass is Σ(p − q)+ = Σ(p − min(p, q)) = β too, so the chance of outputting x through rejection is β · (p − q)+(x)/β = (p(x) − q(x))+. Add them: min(p, q)(x) + (p − q)+(x) = p(x). The draft only changes how often we get the token cheaply, never which token we get. A guess is kept with probability

α = Σx min(p(x), q(x)) = 1 − TV(p, q)
TV
total variation distance, half the summed absolute difference between p and q

The second rule is also exact, and slower. llama.cpp and mlx-lm sample the target's own token at each position and keep the guess only if the two are equal (llama.cpp {{common/sampling.cpp L678 to 705|https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/b11146/common/sampling.cpp#L678}}). Every output token is a genuine sample from p, so the distribution is exact, but a guess survives with probability Σ p(x) q(x), lower than Σ min(p, q) whenever the target is uncertain. With a greedy draft (q all on the draft's top token, what llama.cpp and vLLM's default do), both rules keep a guess with probability p(draft token). Over the target's own story text, mean per-guess acceptance was under min(1, p/q) with a sampled draft, with a greedy draft and under sample-and-compare with a sampled draft measured here.

At temperature 0 the rule reduces to "keep the guess while it equals the target's top token", and the output should equal plain greedy decoding. In exact arithmetic it does; in floating point a batched verification pass rounds differently, and at near-ties the top token can flip (closed block). Neither output is wrong.

Greedy output can change with speculation: floating point at near-ties (measured)

The target's logits for a position depend slightly on how the computation was batched. Computing a sequence's logits in blocks of 7 tokens (as a k = 6 verifier does) instead of one token at a time moved individual logits by up to on the 4-bit Qwen3-1.7B in MLX (which computes in bfloat16, about 3 significant digits), and changed the top token at of positions across four prompts, each one a near-tie where the top two logits were at most apart measured here. That is why the parent's {{Engine bench|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}} saw mlx-lm's k = 6 output differ from its own plain output on two prompts. Greedy decoding is only as deterministic as the arithmetic underneath it (the same holds for any change of batch size, with or without speculation). If greedy outputs change after enabling speculation, check that the first difference is at a near-tie (here the top two logits were at most apart at each divergence), and compare against plain decoding with the same batch shapes.

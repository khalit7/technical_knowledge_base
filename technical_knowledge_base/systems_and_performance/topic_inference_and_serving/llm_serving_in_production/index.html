LLM serving in production: routing, autoscaling, cold starts, multi-LoRA, load testing

Reading: 30 min, plus 11 in closed details. What changes when one engine becomes a service: the latency promise and its bill, which replica gets each request, when to add replicas and how long they take to start, many fine-tunes on one model, the signals and alerts that matter, and load tests that do not fool you. Real replicas on an Apple M1 Pro, real traces, the routers' own code; published GPU results kept apart. Data as of October 2026.
The idea in one sentenceA production LLM service is many copies of one engine behind a router and an autoscaler, and its job is to keep a latency promise (time to first token and time per token, at a percentile) for the fewest GPU-hours: send each request where its prefix is already cached without piling up a queue, add replicas before the queue forms (they take tens of seconds to start), and prove it with load that does not wait for the server.

Every decision on this page sits on one request's path

A request goes from the client through a gateway (keys, rate limits) to a router, which picks one replica (a running copy of the engine with its own KV cache). Replicas export metrics; an autoscaler reads them and adds or removes replicas, each of which serves only after its cold start. The section that owns each box is printed on it; the promise (section 1) is measured where the user is, at the client.

You need: what happens inside one engine (prefill, decode, the KV cache, continuous batching), from the parent Topic: inference-and-serving, and prefix caching from Batching, KV cache and scheduling in depth. Running example (the parent's): a chat assistant on Llama 3.1 70B in FP8, one H200 per replica, 2,000-token prompts of which 1,000 are a shared system prompt, 300-token answers. Evidence: real replicas, a real trace and real engines run on an Apple M1 Pro, the routers' and engines' own code at pinned versions (llama.cpp build 11146, vLLM v0.31.0, SGLang v0.5.21), and published datacenter results kept apart and labelled.

SLO (service level objective): a latency target at a percentile over a window, "99% of first tokens within 2 s".

1. The promise and its bill: SLOs, goodput, capacity, cost per token

The promise, in numbers. The running example promises p99 TTFT within 2 s and mean TPOT within 50 ms. TTFT (time to first token) is queueing plus prefill, from request sent to first token received. TPOT (time per output token) is one request's mean gap after that, (end-to-end latency − TTFT) / (output tokens − 1); ITL (inter-token latency) is every individual gap, a distribution. Two targets, because they fail for different reasons: TTFT when requests queue or prompts are long, TPOT when batches are too large or a long prefill interrupts decoding.

Capacity is goodput, not peak throughput. DistServe's goodput is the maximum request rate at which a target fraction of requests (say 90%) meets the SLOs, per GPU (DistServe, OSDI 2024). Past that rate raw throughput stays about flat while goodput collapses, because every extra request only waits (the parent's Engine bench measures the bend). The parent's Capacity planner (its step model fitted to MLPerf runs) finds that one H200 serves 2.11 requests per second within both targets (at that load TTFT p99 1.05 s, TPOT 46.8 ms), so 20 requests a second at peak need ⌈20 / 2.11⌉ = 10 H200s. The 50 ms TPOT target, not memory, sets that number: relaxing it to 100 ms cuts the fleet to 8. derived by the parent's planner

Predict: the 10-GPU fleet is sized for the peak and paid for all day. An API charges $0.40 per million tokens. At what average traffic, as a share of the peak, does the fleet become as cheap as the API?
About 67%. The fleet costs the same every hour; the API's bill grows with traffic. At the peak the fleet serves 20 × 2,300 × 3,600 = 165.6 million tokens an hour, which the API would charge $66.24 for; the fleet's $44.70 equals the API's bill at 44.70 / 66.24 = 67% of the peak. derived Drag the slider below to see the crossing.

What moves the answer. At 50% of the peak the fleet is 35% dearer than the API per token; self-hosting wins with high and steady utilization, reserved or cheaper GPUs (DigitalOcean's 12-month H200 price is $3.40), traffic with heavy prefix reuse, a model or fine-tune no provider hosts, or data that cannot leave your network. An autoscaler (section 3) attacks the denominator differently: it stops paying for the night, so the same peak costs fewer GPU-hours. Measured numbers from the M1 Pro below do not transfer to these dollar figures; ratios and the shapes of the curves do.

Depth: where the 2.11 comes from, and what the planner varies

The parent's planner computes, for one copy of the model, the memory each GPU spends, how many sequences fit, how long each engine step takes at a given mix of prefill and decode, and the steady-state TTFT p99 and mean TPOT at each arrival rate; it takes the highest rate that meets both targets. Its two engine constants are fitted to published MLPerf runs, with the residuals shown on its tab. A simpler bound that this page's autoscaling simulator uses: a request holds one of 32 sequence slots for its prefill (0.42 s) plus 300 tokens at 46.8 ms (14.5 s), so one replica completes 32 / 14.5 = 2.2 requests a second at most; goodput (2.11) sits just below it because queueing near the limit pushes TTFT past the target. Change the model, GPU, precision, parallelism or targets in the Capacity planner.

2. Routing: which replica gets the request

For ordinary web servers any replica will do, so the classic routers are round robin (1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, ...) and least loaded (fewest requests in flight). An LLM replica is not interchangeable: it keeps a prefix cache of prompts it has already processed, and a request whose beginning is cached there skips that part of its prefill. A chat resends its whole history every turn, an agent its tools and growing context every step; send turn 2 to a different replica from turn 1 and the whole history is computed again.

Round robin, least loadedSpread the load evenly; ignore the cache. Least loaded reacts to slow requests; round robin does not.
Hash on the prefixHash the first N tokens (or a session key) to a replica with consistent or rendezvous hashing: the same prefix always lands on the same replica, and adding a replica moves only a share of the keys. No state, no load awareness.
Cache-aware (SGLang Model Gateway)Keep an approximate radix tree of what was sent where; follow the longest match if it covers more than cache_threshold (0.3) of the prompt, else go to the least loaded; switch to least loaded when the busiest replica has more than 64 requests and 1.5 times the idlest.
Weighted scorers (llm-d)Score every replica on several signals (prefix-cache match, queue depth, KV usage, LoRA affinity), each from 0 to 1 times a weight, and pick the highest total (llm-d scheduler). Red Hat's documented example weighs the prefix scorer 3 and the queue scorer 2 (OpenShift AI 3.5 docs).

The experiment. Four two-turn conversations, eight requests (system prompt 1,000 tokens, first turns 2,000, second turns 2,500 plus the generated answer); every policy repeated 3 times on fresh replicas, identical routing and token counts each time. Step through it; switch policy and scenario.

What it shows. Round robin computed 13,000 prompt tokens in every run, exactly what counting predicts. A toy prefix-aware router that charges a load penalty for every request already sent would compute 7,800, but real routers see only requests in flight, and requests arriving one at a time leave none. So least loaded, SGLang's rules, the weighted scorer and the hash all sent all eight to replica 1, and what that saved depended on memory: with a 256 MiB host cache (about one 2,300-token conversation at 112 KiB per token) 9,701 tokens were computed, with 1.5 GiB 5,804. When requests overlap, load becomes visible (third scenario, two bursts of four): the hash and SGLang's rules still sent all eight to replica 1, computing the fewest tokens (6,804) at a mean TTFT of 3.2 to 3.5 s; the weighted scorer's queue term spread them 3, 2, 3 and computed 10,103 with the lowest mean TTFT, 1.73 to 1.74 s; round robin and least loaded computed 14,000. The three replicas share one GPU, so spreading helps less than it would on three.

Following the prefix and watching the queue moves the whole TTFT curve left

Following the prefix nearly doubles the reuse: hashing the first two blocks (system prompt plus the start of the conversation), SGLang's rules and the weighted score found 17 to 19% of prompt tokens cached, round robin and least loaded 11 to 12%, and the replicas computed about 7% fewer prompt tokens. A bad key is worse than no routing: hashing only the first block, which every request shares (the system prompt), sent all 541 requests to one replica, found 9.9% cached and gave a median TTFT of 7.7 s. SGLang's imbalance rule never fired: no replica ever had more than 10 requests in flight against its threshold of 64, so the router followed the cache whenever the match exceeded 0.3 of the prompt (301 of 541 decisions in the first run) and otherwise picked the least loaded; on a small deployment its load protection is off unless you lower the thresholds. All policies delivered the same output tokens per second, because the arrival schedule is fixed; the difference is the work done and the wait.

What it costs, when it matters, how to check

Cost: a router that follows the cache concentrates traffic, so it needs a load signal or a bound; it also needs state (a tree per replica, or the engines' cache events, as llm-d's precise prefix scorer and Dynamo's KV-aware router read), and an approximate tree drifts from what the replica really kept. Stickiness concentrates harm as well as load: in Anthropic's August 2025 incident "some users were affected more severely, as our routing is 'sticky'", because a user routed to a misconfigured server kept going back to it (Anthropic postmortem, 17 Sep 2025). When it matters: requests sharing long prefixes (multi-turn chat, agents, a long system prompt or document) over more than one replica. How to check: each replica's prefix hit rate and prompt tokens computed, from its own counters, against what one replica with infinite cache would get on the same traffic; the busiest replica's share of the work; TTFT percentiles at the client.

Depth: other routers you will meet, and published gains

The vLLM production stack router (Helm chart vllm-stack-0.1.13, 29 Sep 2026) offers roundrobin, session, prefixaware, kvaware, loadaware and others; its load-aware rule is "score(i) = matched_tokens(i) / prompt_tokens - beta * relative_load(i)", so "a warm-but-saturated instance can lose to a cold-but-idle one", the same trade the weighted scorer made above (routing_logic.py at vllm-stack-0.1.13). Kubernetes' Gateway API Inference Extension (v1.6.2) replaces a plain Service with an InferencePool, "an extensible request scheduling algorithm that is kv-cache and request cost aware", whose endpoint picker now lives in llm-d (README at v1.6.2). project docs The simplest form is a sticky session: Character.AI routes every message of one dialogue to the same server so its cached KV state is reused between turns, and reports a 95% cache hit rate (Character.AI, 20 Jun 2024; a case file on Topic: swe-and-system-design).

Published gains are larger than the ones measured here and come from the projects themselves: llm-d reports 3 times the output throughput and half the TTFT of round robin for Llama 3.1 70B on four MI300X; Dynamo's KV-aware router, twice as fast a TTFT on Baseten's deployment (both quoted on the parent page). project measurements

Where this layer sits: routing between prefill and decode pools when the phases run on separate GPUs is on Distributed and disaggregated serving; the gateway above the router (API keys, rate limits, fallbacks between providers) on Gateways and routing; tenancy and degradation ladders around it on ML system design; the cache-aware router's Rust source read line by line on the SGLang page.

3. Autoscaling: what to scale on

Traffic follows the working day, and a launch or an agent fleet can triple it in minutes. A pool sized for the peak wastes GPUs most of the day (section 1); one sized for the average misses its SLO at the peak. An autoscaler is a control loop: read a metric, compare it with a target, change the replica count, wait, repeat. Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler (HPA), which most LLM deployments use directly or through KEDA, runs every 15 seconds and computes desired = ceil(current × metric / target); it ignores ratios within 10% of 1, adds at most 100% or 4 pods per 15 seconds, and only scales down to the highest recommendation of the last 300 seconds (HPA docs). Everything interesting is in the choice of metric.

GPU utilizationThe default instinct, and wrong for LLMs. NVIDIA's counter is the share of time at least one kernel was running (NVML). With continuous batching some request is almost always decoding, so it reads near 100% at a fraction of capacity.
Queue depthRequests waiting for a slot, per replica. It moves exactly when users start waiting, and is zero when there is headroom. vLLM exports it as vllm:num_requests_waiting, llama-server as llamacpp:requests_deferred.
KV cache usageThe share of KV memory in use: the resource that runs out first in decode. It saturates at 100%, so it says "full" but not "how far over".
A latency SLOScale when the p90 TTFT approaches its target. It measures what users feel, but reacts after they have felt it, and percentiles need enough requests per window to be stable.
Predict: one replica of the running example runs at 10% of the load it can carry. What does the GPU utilization gauge read?
About 96% derived. A request occupies the GPU for its whole life, prefill plus 300 decode steps: 0.42 + 300 × 0.0468 = 14.5 s. At 10% of the 2.2 requests per second a replica completes, 0.22 requests arrive per second, so on average 0.22 × 14.5 = 3.2 requests are in flight; with random (Poisson) arrivals the chance that none is running is e−3.2 = 4%. The gauge reads 96% while the replica could take ten times the load, so an HPA with a 70% utilization target scales to its maximum and stays there: in the comparison below it holds 16 replicas through the whole half hour, 7.66 replica-hours against 4.27 for a queue-depth autoscaler on the same traffic.

The cost of reacting. A scale-up decision helps only after the new replica's cold start (section 4). With a 44.5-second start the queue-depth autoscaler lets the p99 TTFT reach 29.8 s during the spike, and 89.5% of requests meet the 1.05 s target; the same autoscaler with a 6-second start keeps 96.1% within it. The faster a replica starts, the less headroom (capacity kept above demand) you must pay for: if a replica takes 3 minutes to start and traffic can grow 20% in 3 minutes, keep about 20% spare at all times. A short scale-down window flaps (the fourth comparison).

Three more signals that fail. CPU utilization barely moves, because a GPU server's CPU mostly tokenizes and schedules ("For inference workloads running on GPUs, we don't recommend CPU and memory utilization as the only indicators", Google's GKE guide); on ordinary servers it can even run backwards, as in Slack's January 2021 outage, where threads waiting on a congested network used less CPU and the autoscaler removed servers during the overload (a case file on Topic: swe-and-system-design). GPU memory is worse: engines reserve almost all of it at start, so it "only works for scaling up, and won't scale down when traffic decreases" (GKE, Best practices for autoscaling LLM inference workloads with GPUs, updated 2026-10-02). Requests per second ignores size: "A single request with a 32K-token prompt consumes orders of magnitude more compute than a short one" (Dynamo Planner overview, v1.5.0), so when replies grow during a spike the queue builds anyway. vendor guidance

One operational trap: metric names change between engine versions (vLLM renamed gpu_cache_usage_perc to kv_cache_usage_perc in 0.9.2), and an autoscaler querying a removed name reads zero and silently stops scaling. Scaling to zero needs an external trigger (KEDA, Knative or a gateway that holds the first request), because a pool with no replicas reports no metrics at all, and the first user after a quiet period waits the whole cold start: fine for an internal tool, wrong for a chat product.

Depth: what the guides recommend, with real thresholds

GKE: queue size "directly correlates to request latency" and is "largely workload-agnostic", so "use the growth point as a signal to initiate scale-up", starting with a threshold "between 3-5" waiting requests per replica; batch size (vllm:num_requests_running) reacts sooner for tighter latency but depends on request sizes. llm-d's KEDA blueprint scales each prefill or decode role on waiting requests (threshold 20) and KV cache use (0.95) (llm-d-autoscaling at 3c06c88). KServe (v0.21.0) hands autoscaling to KEDA; its documented example puts autoscalerClass: "keda" on an InferenceService and scales a vLLM server between 1 and 5 replicas on the Prometheus query vllm:num_requests_running with a target of 2, so "if our LLM receives 6 concurrent requests with our target of 2 requests per pod, the system will scale to 3 replicas" (KServe docs, LLM autoscaling with KEDA); the vLLM production stack's tutorial does the same on vllm:num_requests_waiting with a threshold of 5 (tutorial 20, at 014d070). KEDA polls every 30 s, and its 300 s cooldownPeriod applies only to scaling to zero (KEDA 2.21 ScaledObject spec). NVIDIA's Dynamo planner can "Target specific TTFT/ITL SLA values" and runs a predictive loop (default every 180 s) for the daily curve under a reactive one (every 5 s) for surprises. project docs

Depth: spot (preemptible) capacity

Spot GPUs are cheaper and can be taken back with little warning: two minutes on AWS (EC2 Spot interruption notices), 0 seconds by default or 120 seconds if configured on Google Cloud, which advertises "up to 91% discounts" (GCP Spot VMs), "up to 30 seconds" on Azure (Azure Spot VMs). Two minutes is less than most cold starts, so spot suits batch work first; for live serving, research systems mix spot across regions with on-demand fallback: SkyServe "reduces cost by 43% on average" against on-demand replicas (SkyServe, EuroSys 2025), and SpotServe reports "saving 54% monetary cost compared with only using on-demand instances" (SpotServe, ASPLOS 2024). authors' measurements

How to check

Replay a real day of arrivals against the autoscaler's rules before trusting it, and plot replicas, queue depth and p90 TTFT on one time axis, as above.

4. Cold starts: what a new replica does before its first token

A cold start sets how fast an autoscaler can react, how long a crashed replica is missing, and whether scaling to zero is tolerable. For the running example the weights alone are 72.66 GB. Four phases, each with its own fix:

1. Machine
seconds to minutes

A GPU node is provisioned (if none is free), the driver loads, the container image (often 10 GB or more) is pulled.

2. Weights
bytes / bandwidth

Read from local NVMe, network storage or a model hub into host memory and onto the GPU; 72.66 GB at an assumed 2 GB/s is 36 s, from remote storage minutes.

3. Engine init
seconds

Profile memory to size the KV cache, compile (torch.compile), capture CUDA graphs for each batch size, warm up.

4. Ready
health check passes

Only now does the router send traffic; the first requests may still pay for lazy initialization.

Cold runs take longer than warm ones by 0.77 s for the 4B file's 2.50 GB, an effective 3.2 GB/s from this laptop's SSD derived: extra time against file size, close for all three sizes. At that rate the running example's 72.66 GB would take 23 s before any engine work. The vLLM container, reading a 1.40 GiB checkpoint through Docker's file-sharing layer (the log names it: FUSE.GRPCFUSE) at about 40 MB/s, spent 36.2 and 37.3 s on weights of its 78 and 80 s start: where the weights come from can dominate, and the engine's log tells you which phase to fix. Its own initialization took 0.69 and 0.45 s (profile memory, allocate 6,016 and 5,760 tokens of KV cache, sized from the memory free at start; no CUDA graphs on the CPU backend). What does not transfer: llama.cpp maps the file and has no CUDA graphs or torch.compile, and it warms up before it reports healthy (first token after "ready" 64 ms for the 4B model); a GPU engine adds memory profiling, graph capture for each batch size and compilation, as the NVIDIA bar shows.

Mitigations, in the order they usually pay

Keep weights close: local NVMe or host memory rather than a hub or object store. Keep the engine alive: sleep mode or a weight daemon instead of a new process. Keep warm spare replicas sized to the cold start (section 3 shows the queue that builds while replicas boot). Scale ahead of known peaks on a schedule. Measure each phase from the engine's log on your own hardware. When one replica spans several GPUs or nodes, start-up also includes setting up the parallel groups: see Distributed and disaggregated serving.

5. Multi-LoRA: many fine-tunes on one set of weights

A LoRA adapter adds a small low-rank update to some weight matrices: instead of changing a d × d matrix W, it learns B (d × r) and A (r × d) with rank r of 8 to 64, and the layer computes x W + x AT BT scaled (how it trains: LoRA paper page). Rank 16 on the attention projections of Qwen3-0.6B is 4.6 million parameters, 9.2 MB in FP16, against 0.6 billion in the base (model card), so a provider can offer one per customer or per task on one copy of the base weights. The goal is requests for different adapters in the same batch, because batching is where serving efficiency comes from.

The base matrix multiply is shared; only the small adapter products differ per request

Why mixing works. Punica's kernel gathers each request's adapter and computes all the small products in one launch, and reported 12 times the throughput of serving systems of the time when serving many LoRA models, adding 2 ms per token (Chen et al. 2023). S-LoRA keeps all adapters in host memory, moves the active ones to the GPU on demand, and pages adapter weights and KV cache from one memory pool, serving thousands of adapters on one GPU with up to 4 times the throughput of HuggingFace PEFT and vLLM's then naive LoRA support (Sheng et al. 2023). authors' measurements vLLM now batches mixed adapters with its own kernels; how many it keeps on the device at once and their largest rank are its max_loras and max_lora_rank knobs (vLLM: inside the default engine, section 5). llm-d has a LoRA-affinity scorer that routes a request to a replica already holding its adapter.

Measured: llama-server with eight adapters. Eight rank-16 adapters made for this page (random weights, seeds 1 to 8, converted with llama.cpp's convert_lora_to_gguf.py: for measuring cost only), loaded with --lora-init-without-apply into one llama-server with 8 slots; 8 concurrent users each send 3 requests of 64 output tokens naming their adapter; two repeats. measured on Apple M1 Pro

With every request on the same adapter the server keeps 352 and 353 tokens/s, close to the 378 to 384 of the same server with no adapters. With two different adapters in flight it falls to 109 to 127, with four or eight to 83 to 90, no better than one user alone (88 to 100). The reason is one line of the server: a batch may only contain slots whose adapter sets are equal (can_batch_with, server-context.cpp L449-L455), because it applies one set of adapters to the whole context per step (common_set_adapter_lora, L2855), so different adapters take turns: the left drawing above. On such an engine, group traffic by adapter (a server or a route per adapter); otherwise use one with multi-adapter kernels (vLLM, SGLang, LoRAX-style servers).

A trap found on the way

A request that lists no adapter does not get the base model: llama-server then applies every loaded adapter at its load-time scale (launch_slot_with_task, L1708 to L1723, uses the server's default list). Here that meant all eight random adapters at once: garbage text and 206 and 217 tokens/s. Listing one adapter at scale 0 switches all of them off (365 and 382 tokens/s). Name the adapters explicitly in every request, or start the server without --lora for base traffic.

The same test on vLLM v0.31.0 (CPU backend in Docker, Qwen3-0.6B float32, --enable-lora --max-loras 4 --max-lora-rank 16, the same adapters in PEFT format, 8 users, 2 requests each): 21.1 and 24.6 tokens/s on one adapter and 20.1 and 24.1 on four different ones in the same batches, so mixing costs nothing extra; eight different adapters, more than --max-loras allows on the device, fell to 15.8 and 17.2 as adapters were swapped in and out. Without any adapter the same server gave 55.1 and 58.6 (machine load average 5 to 9): on this CPU backend the LoRA path itself is expensive, which says nothing about GPUs, where Punica-style kernels add little. vLLM counts requests per adapter in vllm:lora_requests_info (loggers.py L993). measured on Apple M1 Pro

6. Structured output under load

Tool calls and machine-read answers must be valid JSON matching a schema; constrained decoding compiles the schema into a grammar and masks every token that would break it before sampling (how: Sampling and decoding, section 5; inside SGLang's engine: the SGLang page). The serving questions are what it costs per token under load, and what it buys. On llama-server the answer was no consistent cost per token, and the schema decides validity: without it 0 of 50 answers parsed strictly (all wrapped in a Markdown code fence), with it 50 of 50 were bare, valid objects with exactly the four keys. measured on Apple M1 Pro In production, parse every response and count failures, and compare time per output token with and without the schema at the same output length and concurrency; the cost is a grammar compilation per new schema (cached after), a vocabulary-wide mask per step, and on some engines the lost overlap between CPU and GPU work; cache compiled grammars and use a fast backend (XGrammar).

Depth: the measurements

The same llama-server without adapters; Qwen3-0.6B (thinking off) invents a person as a JSON object with four keys, with and without response_format set to that schema; 1 user sending 5 requests, then 4 users sending 5 each; two repeats.

Median time per output token with the schema was 7.8 and 10.1 ms against 7.5 and 8.3 ms without it for one user, and 17.4 and 19.7 ms against 19.1 and 18.4 ms for four: within the noise. The grammar check runs on the CPU per sampled token; for a small vocabulary walk like this one it is cheap next to a decode step, and the SGLang page's measurement on its MLX backend (13.4 against 4.9 ms per token) shows it is not always so. On vLLM v0.31.0 (CPU, 4 users, structured-output backend auto, the default, which picks a grammar library per request: structured_outputs.py L21) the cost was inconclusive: 136 and 112 against 114 and 112 ms per output token in the two repeats, and again every unconstrained answer was fenced (0 of 24 strictly valid) while all 24 constrained answers were valid. Reasoning models are usually left unconstrained while thinking; a mask that overrides the model's choice can cost quality.

Percentiles, and how a histogram blurs them. An average TTFT hides the users who waited. Engines export histograms, and Prometheus's histogram_quantile interpolates linearly inside a bucket, so a percentile is located only to its bucket. vLLM's TTFT buckets jump from 1 to 2.5, 5 and 7.5 seconds (buckets.py L56): on this page's round-robin routing run (541 requests) the exact p99 TTFT was 4.10 s, while the same samples counted into vLLM's buckets give a histogram_quantile(0.99, ...) of 4.75 s. derived from measured samples Keep raw samples when the exact percentile matters, or choose buckets around your SLO.

Alert on the SLO, not on every metric. The 1% of requests a 99% SLO allows to miss is the error budget; the burn rate is how fast it is spent (a 1% miss rate is burn rate 1, 14.4% is 14.4). Google's SRE workbook pages when the burn rate exceeds 14.4 over both the last hour and the last 5 minutes (2% of a 30-day budget gone in an hour), and 6 over 6 hours and 30 minutes, with a ticket at 1 over 3 days and 6 hours (SRE workbook, Table 5-8); the short window ends the alert as soon as the problem does. For an LLM service, one such alert on the TTFT SLO and one on the TPOT SLO. The general method is on Observability; per-request traces of LLM calls on LLM observability.

Measure TTFT at the client too. The engine's clock starts when the request reaches it; queueing in a gateway or router before that is invisible to it.

8. Load testing without fooling yourself

A load test is meant to predict what users will see, and the way the load is generated decides the answer as much as the server does. A closed-loop generator simulates N users who each wait for their answer before sending the next request; an open-loop generator sends at times fixed in advance (Poisson arrivals at a rate), whatever the server is doing. Users of a public service are close to open loop. When a closed-loop server slows down, the generator slows with it and never sends the requests that would have queued: Gil Tene called this coordinated omission, and wrk2 corrects it by measuring each request from the moment it should have been sent (wrk2 README). The general theory (queueing, Little's law, test types) is on Capacity planning and performance.

Predict: the server freezes for 5 seconds in a 40-second test of 120 requests. How many requests will a closed-loop generator with 2 users record as slower than one second?
About 2: each user has one request stuck in the freeze and sends nothing more until it returns. The open-loop generator recorded 94 and 103 of 120 above one second in the two repeats, because the requests that kept arriving during the freeze queued and the backlog took most of the run to clear.

The same 5-second freeze: the closed loop stops asking, the open loop records the backlog

One llama-server, Qwen3-0.6B Q4_K_M, 4 slots, driven by the parent's loadgen.py; 120 requests of about 150 words, 48-token answers; the server process frozen with SIGSTOP 15 s after the start and resumed 5 s later, the kind of pause a garbage collection, a page-cache miss on the weights or a noisy neighbour causes. Each dot is a request at the time it was sent and its end-to-end latency (log scale); hollow circles are still waiting. First repeat shown. measured on Apple M1 Pro

With the freeze, the closed-loop run's median latency was 0.52 and 0.89 s in the two repeats and only 2 and 4 requests took over a second; the open-loop run's median was 4.76 and 1.81 s. Same server, same freeze. Applying wrk2's correction to the closed-loop samples (each slow sample also stands for the requests its user would have sent meanwhile) raises its p90 from 0.65 and 0.93 s to 1.48 and 2.42 s, closer to what the open loop saw, but it is a model of the missing requests, not a measurement. Open-loop arrivals at a stated rate are the honest default for a service; keep closed loops for "N agents in a loop" workloads, where the clients really do wait.

Depth: every request of all four runs, and every repeat with the correction

The rest of an honest test, each a mistake seen in published benchmarks:

  • Real lengths. A fixed 1,000-in, 100-out prompt hides prefill interference and the long tail of long prompts; replay a length distribution from your traffic (or a published one such as ShareGPT, BurstGPT or Mooncake), and say whether end-of-sequence was ignored to force the output length.
  • Cache state. Repeating one prompt measures the prefix cache; random prompts measure no cache at all. Neither is your traffic; multi-turn and agent traffic reuse far more (section 2).
  • Warmup. The first requests pay for lazy initialization; exclude a warmup period or report it separately.
  • A sweep, not a point. Report latency percentiles and goodput (section 1) across arrival rates, up to and past the knee; several runs per point with their spread, the exact versions, flags and hardware. The parent's Engine bench records every run with its command line and the machine's load, with closed- and open-loop sweeps of llama-server, mlx_lm.server and vLLM.
  • A rate, stated. vllm bench serve's --request-rate defaults to infinity, which sends every request at time 0 and measures a burst, not a service; set a rate (--burstiness 1 means Poisson) or --max-concurrency, and sweep.
  • The client is part of the system. A Python client with one thread per request on the same machine can itself become the bottleneck; measure how late each request was sent against its schedule.

Optional: interview rehearsal, four design questions
Design the serving layer for a chat product on 20 GPU replicas. How do you route?
Keep each conversation on the replica that holds its prefix (consistent hashing on a conversation key, or a cache-aware router that tracks prefixes per replica), and fall back to the least-loaded replica, or weigh a queue score, when the preferred one is busy. Watch the prefix hit rate per replica, the busiest replica's share and the TTFT tail; watch for hot spots on popular shared prefixes and for one bad server that sticky routing keeps feeding.
A new replica takes 90 seconds to start. What do you do?
Break it down from the engine's log (node, image pull, weight load, engine initialization, graph capture), then attack the largest part: weights on local NVMe or in host memory, a streaming or loading-optimised format, overlapped loading, a weight daemon or sleep mode instead of a new process, a warm pool sized to the start time, and scaling ahead of known peaks.
You must serve 500 customer fine-tunes of one 8B model. How?
LoRA adapters on shared base weights: an engine that batches different adapters in one pass (Punica or S-LoRA style kernels in vLLM or SGLang), a limit on adapters resident on the GPU with the rest in host memory and least-recently-used swapping, and routing that prefers replicas where the adapter is already loaded (llm-d's LoRA-affinity scorer).
Your benchmark says p99 TTFT is 300 ms but users complain. What could be wrong?
A closed-loop generator (coordinated omission), warm caches from repeated prompts, fixed short prompts, a rate too low to queue, TTFT measured inside the engine rather than at the client, or a p99 read from a coarse histogram. Re-run with open-loop arrivals from real traffic, real lengths and a cold cache, and measure at the client.

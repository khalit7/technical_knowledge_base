6. Observability: the metrics that matter, and alerting on them

The problem. A dashboard full of numbers does not tell you whether users are waiting. For an LLM service a few signals carry nearly everything, and they split into three questions: are users getting tokens fast enough (latency), is the server running out of something (saturation), and is the cache doing its job (efficiency).

Percentiles, not averages, and how a histogram blurs them. An average TTFT hides the users who waited; the p99 is the one in a hundred who gave up. Engines export histograms (counts per bucket), and Prometheus's histogram_quantile interpolates linearly inside a bucket, so a percentile can only be located to its bucket. vLLM's TTFT buckets are 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 640 and 2,560 seconds ({{buckets.py L56|https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/v0.31.0/vllm/v1/metrics/buckets.py#L56}}): a p99 between 1 and 2.5 s is a guess within that range. On this page's round-robin routing run (541 requests), the exact p99 TTFT was 4.10 s; the same samples counted into vLLM's buckets give a histogram_quantile(0.99, ...) of 4.75 s, because the 99th percentile falls in the 2.5 to 5 s bucket and is placed by interpolation. derived from measured samples

Alert on the SLO, not on every metric. Write the target the way users feel it, for example "99% of requests get their first token within 2 seconds, over 30 days". The 1% allowed to miss is the error budget; the burn rate is how fast you spend it (a miss rate of 1% is burn rate 1, 14.4% is 14.4). Google's SRE workbook recommends paging when the burn rate exceeds 14.4 over both the last hour and the last 5 minutes (2% of a 30-day budget gone in an hour), and 6 over 6 hours and 30 minutes, with a ticket at 1 over 3 days and 6 hours ({{SRE workbook, Table 5-8|https://sre.google/workbook/alerting-on-slos/}}); the short window stops the alert as soon as the problem ends. The general method, with its arithmetic, is on {{Observability|n:3ef5c17b0d0d811e8490c54dd42c2b9b}}. For LLMs, keep two SLOs, one on TTFT and one on TPOT, because they fail for different reasons: TTFT when requests queue or prompts are long, TPOT when batches are too large or a long prefill interrupts decoding.

What it costs, when it matters, how to check

Cost: almost nothing for counters and histograms; per-request traces (OpenTelemetry) cost storage and are where {{LLM observability|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fe82c0c6e9360afec4}} takes over. When it matters: always; the router and the autoscaler read the same signals. How to check: measure TTFT at the client too. The server's TTFT starts when the request reaches the engine; queueing in a gateway or router before that is invisible to it.

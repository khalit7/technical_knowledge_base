7. Load testing without fooling yourself

The problem. A load test is meant to predict what users will see. Most published "tokens per second" and "p99 latency" numbers cannot be compared, because the way the load was generated decides the answer as much as the server does. The general theory (queueing, Little's law, load-test types) is on {{Capacity planning and performance|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81458291d55d7443700c}}; here is what is specific to LLM servers, with one experiment.

Closed against open loop. A closed-loop generator simulates N users who each wait for their answer before sending the next request; an open-loop generator sends requests at times fixed in advance (Poisson arrivals at a rate), whether or not earlier ones have finished. Real users of a public service are close to open loop: they do not coordinate with your server. When a closed-loop server slows down, the generator slows down with it and stops sending the requests that would have queued: Gil Tene called this coordinated omission, and wrk2 corrects it by measuring each request from the moment it should have been sent ({{wrk2 README|https://github.com/giltene/wrk2}}).

Predict: the server freezes for 5 seconds in a 40-second test of 120 requests. How many requests will a closed-loop generator with 2 users record as slower than one second?
About 2: each user has one request stuck in the freeze and sends nothing more until it returns. The open-loop generator recorded 94 and 103 of 120 above one second in the two repeats, because the requests that kept arriving during the freeze queued and the backlog took most of the run to clear.

The same 5-second freeze, two load generators

One llama-server, Qwen3-0.6B Q4_K_M, 4 slots; 120 requests of about 150 words, 48-token answers; the server process frozen with SIGSTOP 15 s after the start and resumed 5 s later. Each dot is a request at the time it was sent and its end-to-end latency (log scale); hollow circles are still waiting. First repeat shown; both repeats in the {{Load-test lab|#t-load}}. measured on Apple M1 Pro

The numbers: with the freeze, the closed-loop run's median latency was 0.52 and 0.89 s in the two repeats and only 2 and 4 requests took over a second; the open-loop run's median was 4.76 and 1.81 s. Same server, same freeze. Applying wrk2's correction to the closed-loop samples (each slow sample also stands for the requests its user would have sent meanwhile, at the run's normal pace) raises its p90 from 0.65 and 0.93 s to 1.48 and 2.42 s, closer to what the open loop saw, but it is a model of the missing requests, not a measurement of them. Open-loop arrivals at a stated rate are the honest default for a service; keep closed loops for "N agents in a loop" workloads, where the clients really do wait.

The rest of an honest test, each a mistake seen in published benchmarks:

  • Real lengths. A fixed 1,000-in, 100-out prompt hides prefill interference and the long tail of long prompts; replay a length distribution from your traffic (or a published one such as ShareGPT, BurstGPT or Mooncake). State whether end-of-sequence was ignored to force the output length.
  • Cache state. Repeating one prompt measures the prefix cache; random prompts measure no cache at all. Neither is your traffic; multi-turn and agent traffic reuse far more than single-turn chat (section 1).
  • Warmup. The first requests pay for lazy initialization (llama-server's first token after start was much slower than the second, section 3); exclude a warmup period or report it separately.
  • A sweep, not a point. Report latency percentiles and goodput (requests per second that meet the SLO) across arrival rates, up to and past the knee; several runs per point, with the spread, the exact versions, flags and hardware. The parent page's {{Engine bench|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}} records every run with its command line and the machine's load.
  • The client is part of the system. A Python client with one thread per request, on the same machine, can itself become the bottleneck at high rates; measure the client's own lag (how late each request was sent against its schedule).

Tools (not run here; documentation read 2026-10-10). vllm bench serve replays datasets (ShareGPT, BurstGPT, random, prefix-repetition, a timed trace) against any OpenAI-compatible server and computes goodput with --goodput ttft:... tpot:...; its --request-rate defaults to infinity, which sends every request at time 0, and --burstiness 1 means Poisson ({{docs|https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/cli/bench/serve.html}}; defaults confirmed in {{benchmarks/serve.py|https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/v0.31.0/vllm/benchmarks/serve.py}} L1648 to L1838). GuideLLM (vLLM project) runs synchronous, concurrent, constant, Poisson and sweep profiles with warmup and cooldown and reports full TTFT and ITL distributions ({{GuideLLM|https://github.com/vllm-project/guidellm}}). Whatever the tool, the questions above decide whether its numbers mean anything.

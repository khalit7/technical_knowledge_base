6. Preemption: taking memory back from a running request

vLLM admits a request when its prompt fits in free blocks and reserves nothing for the answer (section 2), whose length nobody knows; so at some step a running request needs a block and there is none, and the engine must take memory from someone: preemption. Fig. 5 runs vLLM's rules in this page's simulator on a deliberately small pool, 30 blocks of 16 tokens, with five requests (lengths illustrative, every admission and preemption simulated); the waiting queue stands on the left.

  1. Admit while the prompt fits. Prompts of 90, 120 and 70 tokens take 20 of the 30 blocks; nothing is set aside for answers of 150, 60 and 130 tokens.
  2. One more, because its prompt fits. R4 (100 tokens) arrives at 0.1 s: 29 of 30 blocks held, every answer still to grow.
  3. No free block: the newest is preempted. vLLM drops R4's cache and puts it at the front of the queue: 24 of 30 held; 6 of R4's freed blocks keep their hashes for now.
  4. Back in, recomputing. R2 finishes at 0.30 s; R4 returns and recomputes 108 tokens (prompt plus the 8 it had generated; its cached blocks were taken meanwhile), and R5 is admitted: 30 of 30.
  5. Thrashing. The next step a block is needed again; the newest, R5, is preempted after one step.
  6. Preempted twice. R4 goes again; R4 and R5 wait while R1 and R3 grow: 22 of 30 held.
  7. A cached prefix softens the second recompute. R3 finishes at 0.64 s; R4 finds 32 of its tokens still cached (2 blocks) and recomputes 98, R5 recomputes its 81: 3 preemptions for 5 requests.

The pool is full and R1 needs a block: vLLM evicts the newest request, SGLang the one with the fewest output tokens, TensorRT-LLM never admitted them

illustrative: 12 blocks of 4 token slots. R1 (oldest) has a 9-token prompt and 7 output tokens, R2 a 13-token prompt and 1 output token, R3 (newest) a 10-token prompt and 3 output tokens; all ask for max_tokens 16. The rules are the engines' own, read in source (table below).

The engines differ in whom they pick; one avoids the question by reserving at admission:

  1. The pool is full. Three requests hold all 12 blocks; R1 (oldest) has just filled its fourth.
  2. R1 needs a fifth block, and none is free. Nothing was reserved for answers.
  3. vLLM preempts the newest request, R3. Its 4 blocks are freed, its cache dropped, and it goes to the front of the queue; R1 takes block 2.
  4. R1 ends; R3 comes back and recomputes. Its 10 prompt and 3 generated tokens, as a prompt chunk in the budget, or loaded from a host tier if one is on (section 4).
  1. The pool is full. Output tokens: R1 7, R2 1, R3 3.
  2. R1 needs a fifth block. SGLang's estimate of future tokens was low.
  3. SGLang retracts the fewest output tokens, R2. The least work is lost (ties: longest prompt); R1 takes block 1.
  4. R1 ends; R2 recomputes 14 tokens, and SGLang raises its estimate.
  1. Admission reserved the worst case. R1 holds prompt + max_tokens = 25 slots, 7 blocks; R2 (8 blocks) and R3 (7) wait, with 5 free.
  2. R1 needs a fifth block: it already has it.
  3. No preemption, ever. One request runs where the others ran three; 7 of 12 blocks hold nothing.
  4. R1 ends; R2 is admitted. Simulated below: 1,785 tokens per second against 2,364 for optimistic admission.

Recompute or swap. A preempted request's cache is either dropped and recomputed later as a prompt, or copied to host memory and back. A swap costs 2 × KV bytes / link bandwidth per token: with 320 KiB over PCIe Gen5 at 64 GB/s each way, about 10 µs against about 0.21 ms of prefill for the running example, twenty times cheaper. Yet vLLM keeps only recompute, for three reasons: Kwon et al. measured the two "comparable end-to-end" for blocks of 16 to 64 tokens, "recomputation overhead is never higher than 20% of swapping's latency" ({{PagedAttention section 7.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180}}, on A100s), because small blocks made swapping many tiny PCIe copies; recompute rides in the token budget on arithmetic a memory-bound decode step leaves idle; and swap needs a host block allocator and copies on the critical path. With an offload tier on (section 4), blocks are written to host memory as they are computed, so a preempted prefix is loaded rather than recomputed: a write-through swap (read in source, not run here).

Admission matters more than the mode. On the simulated H100 with a small cache (2,000 blocks of 16 tokens, Llama 3.1 8B in BF16, 6 requests per second), recompute and swap at 25 GB/s differ by 3% in throughput (2,364 against 2,430 tokens per second) with the same 146 preemptions; a slow 6 GB/s link makes swap the worse one (2,287). Reserving the maximum length, TensorRT-LLM's default, removes all preemptions but serves 1,785 tokens per second with a p99 TTFT of 31.25 s, against 12.61 s for optimistic admission (derived). Measured here, vLLM's CPU backend with a 9,344-token cache hit 100% cache use and 8 preemptions at 16 users, its output falling from 26.5 to 15.6 tokens per second between 8 and 16 users ({{Engine bench|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}}): more sequences give more throughput only while the cache has room.

Check: vllm:num_preemptions ({{loggers.py L677|https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/v0.31.0/vllm/v1/metrics/loggers.py#L677}}) rising while vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc sits at 1.0 means admission is too optimistic. The fixes, in order: a smaller max_num_seqs, a lower max_tokens, a quantized cache (FP8 halves it), more GPUs.

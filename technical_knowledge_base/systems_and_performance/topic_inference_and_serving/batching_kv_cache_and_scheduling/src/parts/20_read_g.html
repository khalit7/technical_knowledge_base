6. Preemption: taking memory back from a running request

Nobody knows an answer's length in advance. vLLM admits a request when its prompt fits in free blocks and reserves nothing for the answer (section 2), so at some step a running request needs one more block and there is none. The engine must take memory from someone: preemption.

Fig. 5 runs vLLM's rules in this page's simulator on a deliberately small pool, 30 blocks of 16 tokens, with five requests (lengths illustrative, every admission and preemption simulated). The waiting queue stands on the left.

  1. Admit while the prompt fits. R1, R2 and R3 arrive with prompts of 90, 120 and 70 tokens and are admitted at once: 20 of the 30 blocks. Nothing is set aside for their answers (150, 60 and 130 tokens).
  2. One more, because its prompt fits. R4 arrives at 0.1 s with a 100-token prompt; it fits, so it is admitted: 29 of 30 blocks are held, and every answer still has to grow.
  3. No free block: the newest is preempted. A running request needs a new block and none is free. vLLM takes the newest request, R4, drops its cache and puts it at the front of the queue: 24 of 30 blocks held, and 6 of R4's freed blocks keep their hashes for now.
  4. Back in, recomputing. R2 finishes at 0.30 s. R4 is admitted again and recomputes 108 tokens, its 100-token prompt and the 8 it had generated (its cached blocks were taken in the meantime); R5 arrives and is admitted too: 30 of 30.
  5. Thrashing. The very next step a running request needs a block again; this time the newest is R5, preempted after one step.
  6. Preempted twice. R4 is preempted a second time; R4 and R5 wait while R1 and R3 grow: 22 of 30 held.
  7. A cached prefix softens the second recompute. R3 finishes at 0.64 s. R4 returns and finds 32 of its tokens still cached (2 blocks), so it recomputes 98; R5 recomputes its 81. In all, 3 preemptions for 5 requests.

The pool is full and R1 needs a block: vLLM evicts the newest request, SGLang the one with the fewest output tokens, TensorRT-LLM never admitted them

illustrative: 12 blocks of 4 token slots. R1 (oldest) has a 9-token prompt and 7 output tokens, R2 a 13-token prompt and 1 output token, R3 (newest) a 10-token prompt and 3 output tokens; all ask for max_tokens 16. The rules are the engines' own, read in source (table below).

The engines differ in whom they pick, and one avoids the question by reserving at admission. Same pool, same three requests, three rules:

  1. The pool is full. Three requests hold all 12 blocks. R1 (the oldest) has just filled its fourth block; R3 arrived last.
  2. R1 needs a fifth block, and none is free. Admission reserved nothing for answers, so the engine must take memory from someone.
  3. vLLM preempts the newest request, R3. R3's 4 blocks are freed and its cache dropped; R3 goes to the front of the waiting queue. R1 takes a freed block (2) and continues.
  4. R1 ends; R3 comes back and recomputes. R3's 10 prompt and 3 generated tokens are computed again as a prompt chunk in the token budget, or loaded from a host tier if one is on (section 4).
  1. The pool is full. The same three requests: R1 has 7 output tokens, R2 1, R3 3.
  2. R1 needs a fifth block, and none is free. SGLang admitted with an estimate of each request's future tokens, and the estimate was low.
  3. SGLang retracts the request with the fewest output tokens, R2. Least work generated is lost: R2 had 1 output token (ties go to the longest prompt). Its 4 blocks are freed and R1 takes block 1.
  4. R1 ends; R2 comes back and recomputes. R2's 13 prompt tokens and 1 output token are computed again. SGLang then raises its estimate of future tokens, admitting less optimistically.
  1. Admission reserved the worst case. R1 was admitted with prompt + max_tokens = 25 slots, 7 blocks. R2 needs 29 slots (8 blocks) and R3 26 (7): only 5 blocks are free, so both wait.
  2. R1 needs a fifth block: it already has it. Its reservation covers any answer up to max_tokens, so no allocation can fail.
  3. No preemption, ever. The price is concurrency: one request runs where vLLM and SGLang ran three, and 7 of the 12 blocks hold nothing.
  4. R1 ends; R2 is admitted with its full reservation. R3 still waits for 7 free blocks. In the simulation below, this rule served 1,785 tokens per second against 2,364 for optimistic admission.

Recompute or swap. A preempted request's cache is either dropped and recomputed later as a prompt, or copied to host memory and back. Per token, swapping costs 2 × KV bytes / link bandwidth and recomputing a prefill: with 320 KiB per token over PCIe Gen5 at 64 GB/s each way, a swap costs about 10 µs per token against about 0.21 ms of prefill for the running example, twenty times cheaper in time. Yet vLLM keeps only recompute, for three reasons: Kwon et al. measured the two "comparable end-to-end" for blocks of 16 to 64 tokens, "recomputation overhead is never higher than 20% of swapping's latency" ({{PagedAttention section 7.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180}}, on A100s), because small blocks made swapping many tiny PCIe copies; recompute rides in the token budget on arithmetic a memory-bound decode step leaves idle; and swap needs a host block allocator and copies on the critical path. With an offload tier on (section 4), blocks are already written to host memory as they are computed, so a preempted request's prefix is found there and loaded rather than recomputed: recompute plus a CPU tier behaves like a write-through swap (read in source, not run here).

Admission matters more than the mode. On the simulated H100 with a deliberately small cache (2,000 blocks of 16 tokens, Llama 3.1 8B in BF16, 6 requests per second), recompute and swap at 25 GB/s differ by 3% in throughput (2,364 against 2,430 tokens per second) with the same 146 preemptions; a slow 6 GB/s link makes swap the worse one (2,287). Reserving every request's maximum length, TensorRT-LLM's default rule, removes all preemptions but serves 1,785 tokens per second with a p99 TTFT of 31.25 s, against 12.61 s for optimistic admission (derived). Measured here, vLLM's CPU backend with a 9,344-token cache reached 100% cache use and 8 preemptions at 16 users, and its output fell from 26.5 to 15.6 tokens per second between 8 and 16 users ({{Engine bench|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}}): more concurrent sequences give more throughput only while the cache has room.

Check: vllm:num_preemptions ({{loggers.py L677|https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/v0.31.0/vllm/v1/metrics/loggers.py#L677}}) rising while vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc sits at 1.0 means admission is too optimistic for the answers you get. The fixes, in order: a smaller max_num_seqs, a lower max_tokens, a quantized cache (FP8 halves it), more GPUs.

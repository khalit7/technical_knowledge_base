8. Compressed caches in a paged engine: what can actually be freed

Long contexts fill the cache however well it is paged, cached and offloaded; what remains is to store fewer bytes per entry or fewer entries. The methods (KV quantisation and its outlier channels; eviction by StreamingLLM's sinks plus a window, H2O's accumulated attention, SnapKV's observation window, with nine policies compared inline) are owned by {{Long context|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5}}, sections 3 and 4. The serving question is different: of what a method saves, how much can a paged engine take back?

  • Fewer bytes per entry is realised in full. Every block shrinks by the same factor and nothing in the layout changes: vLLM and SGLang offer an FP8 cache, llama.cpp q8_0 (8.5 bits per number) and q4_0 (4.5), 0.53 and 0.28 of the F16 size. Measured here on Qwen3-1.7B (WikiText-2, 30 windows of 2,048 tokens): perplexity 15.18 with an F16 cache, 15.18 with q8_0, and 19.42 with q4_0, 28% worse.
  • Fewer entries is realised only if whole blocks empty. A paged engine stores all heads of all layers for 16 tokens in one block, so it can free a block only when every head of every layer has dropped all 16 of its tokens. A method that keeps different tokens per head, as SnapKV and H2O do, shows that quality survives a smaller cache (the algorithmic saving) without letting the engine reclaim memory (the realisable saving). And eviction after a full prefill never lowers the peak: the whole prompt's cache existed first.

Measured here on Qwen3-0.6B with seven policies keeping a quarter of an 800-token prompt (the full experiment is in the closed block below). Predict before you look: which selections leave whole 16-token blocks empty?

Per-head selections scatter what they keep over every block, so a paged engine frees nothing

"Freeable in place": blocks of 16 tokens in which no layer and no head keeps any token (vLLM's layout). A selection shared by every layer and head can also be compacted, the kept tokens rewritten into fewer blocks (their positions are already baked into the keys by RoPE), which realises the full saving; a per-head selection cannot without a per-head cache layout.

Per-head selections (SnapKV, H2O, random per head) left no 16-token block free: 0% of the memory comes back in a paged engine without a per-head layout. Shared random selection freed 1.3% of blocks in place at 25% kept, because kept tokens are scattered over almost every block, although compaction could reclaim all 75%. The shared SnapKV selection, the best on retrieval here, kept its tokens in clusters: 51% of blocks freeable in place, 75% with compaction. So ask of any eviction paper: is its memory figure algorithmic or realisable, and does it beat the cheap control, uniform random eviction that keeps the first tokens?

The experiment in full: protocol, quality against keep ratio for seven policies, the cheap control, newer scorers

measured here: Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B (safetensors @ c1899de2, BF16 weights run in float32), PyTorch 2.14.1 and transformers 5.19.0 on the M1 Pro's CPU with 2 threads, eager attention so the attention weights can be read, greedy decoding; script src/kvc/kvc.py. Protocol, identical for every policy: prefill the prompt except its last token with the full cache, keep a share of the "compressible" positions (Task A: the 60 lines of context, about 800 tokens; Task B: the first 992 of 1,024 context tokens), always keep the rest (the question; the last 32 tokens), then continue. Task A hides one "secret code" line among 60 and asks for it; correct if the first four digits generated match; with the full cache the model answers 98.3% of 60 questions. Task B scores the next 128 tokens of WikiText-2 after a 1,024-token context; full-cache perplexity 17.04 over 24 windows. Random policies were run with three seeds (mean shown, range in brackets). Policies: random over all positions; the first 4 tokens plus random (shared, or per layer and head); the first 4 plus the most recent (StreamingLLM); accumulated attention per head (H2O); attention from the observation window per head, max-pooled over 7 positions (SnapKV); the same score summed over all heads and layers, one shared selection. Wall time 27 min for Task A, 6 min for Task B.

Task A: find one line among 60 (accuracy)
Task B: next 128 tokens of Wikipedia text (perplexity, log scale; random without sinks, in the hundreds, is drawn at the top edge)
  • Random eviction must keep the first tokens. Uniform random choice over all positions sends perplexity from 17.04 to 927 on average at 25% kept, because it can drop the first token, the attention sink; the one seed that kept it scored 23. Keeping the first 4 tokens and choosing the rest at random gives 23.71.
  • On text continuation, random is a respectable control but loses. At 25% kept: H2O 18.08, SnapKV 19.83, random per head 22.16, random shared 23.71. At 10%: 19.97, 22.97, 26.84 and 27.80.
  • On retrieval, random fails outright. At 25% kept, random with sinks answers 5.6% of questions, random per head 13.9%, keeping the most recent tokens 26.7%, H2O 26.7%, SnapKV per head 63.3%, and SnapKV with one selection shared by all heads and layers 91.7%. Retrieval needs one specific line, which only a score informed by the question keeps.
  • The cheap control in the literature. On reasoning traces, Random Attention matched the strongest learned policy on four models and six tasks, at 32% to 43% higher throughput in vLLM ({{arXiv 2609.03430|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03430}}, authors' measurement; their explanation is that reasoning traces repeat what they still need). It does not transfer to finding one fact, as Task A shows.
  • Newer scorers (authors' claims, not run here): KVzip scores by how well the model can reconstruct the context and reports 3 to 4 times smaller caches ({{Kim et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23416}}); Expected Attention estimates future queries' attention and ships KVPress, a library of more than 20 methods ({{Devoto et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00636}}); SnapKV's authors report 3.6 times faster generation and 8.2 times better memory efficiency at 16K tokens ({{Li et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14469}}). Fewer tokens before the decoder (a small encoder turns the context into fewer latent vectors) leaves the cache layout standard, so its saving is realisable ({{Latent Context Language Models|n:3d45c17b0d0d81eb90a8d70a5ce84d19}}).

What transfers: the ranking of methods and the two questions. What does not: a 0.6B model on 800 to 1,024-token contexts is not a 70B model on 100K tokens (eviction matters most where contexts are long), and nothing about speed (eager attention on a CPU).

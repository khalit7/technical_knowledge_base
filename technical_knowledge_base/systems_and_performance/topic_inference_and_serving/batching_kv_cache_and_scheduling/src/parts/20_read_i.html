8. KV compression: algorithmic against realisable savings

The problem. Long contexts fill the cache no matter how well it is paged, cached and offloaded. The remaining option is to store less per token or fewer tokens. Four families, from safest to riskiest:

  • A model with a smaller cache. Grouped-query attention, multi-head latent attention, sliding-window layers: chosen at training time (the parent's section 2).
  • Fewer bytes per entry: KV quantization. vLLM and SGLang offer an FP8 cache; llama.cpp offers q8_0 (8.5 bits per number) and q4_0 (4.5) for keys and values, 0.53 and 0.28 of the F16 size (the parent's {{Engine bench|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}} measured the bytes). Measured here on Qwen3-1.7B (Q8_0 weights, WikiText-2 test, 2,048-token windows, 30 of them): perplexity 15.18 with an F16 cache, 15.18 with q8_0, and 19.42 with q4_0, 28% worse. An 8-bit cache was free on this model; a 4-bit one was not.
  • Fewer entries: eviction. Prefill the prompt, score every cached token by an importance signal, keep the best: StreamingLLM keeps the first few tokens, which soak up attention ("attention sinks"), plus a recent window ({{Xiao et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17453}}); H2O keeps tokens with the most accumulated attention ({{Zhang et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14048}}); SnapKV scores tokens by the attention they get from an "observation window" at the end of the prompt, per head ({{Li et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14469}}, whose authors report 3.6 times faster generation and 8.2 times better memory efficiency at 16K tokens); KVzip scores by how well the model can reconstruct the context and reports 3 to 4 times smaller caches ({{Kim et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23416}}); Expected Attention estimates future queries' attention and ships KVPress, a library of more than 20 methods ({{Devoto et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00636}}). All authors' claims, not run here.
  • Fewer tokens: compress the input before the decoder. A small encoder turns the context into fewer latent vectors; the cache layout stays standard ({{Latent Context Language Models|n:3d45c17b0d0d81eb90a8d70a5ce84d19}}).

Two questions to ask of any eviction paper

Is the saving realisable? An eviction method that prefills the whole prompt first never lowers the peak: the full cache existed. And a paged engine stores all heads of all layers for 16 tokens in one block, so it can free a block only when every head of every layer has dropped all 16 of its tokens. A method that keeps different tokens per head, as SnapKV and H2O do, shows that quality survives a smaller cache (the algorithmic saving) without letting the engine reclaim the memory (the realisable saving) unless the cache layout changes. A selection shared by all heads and layers can be compacted into fewer blocks.

Does it beat the cheap control? Keep the prompt and evict uniformly at random: on reasoning traces, Random Attention matched the strongest learned policy on four models and six tasks, at 32% to 43% higher throughput in vLLM ({{arXiv 2609.03430|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03430}}, authors' measurement; their explanation is that reasoning traces repeat what they still need). Random eviction draws no score, so it costs nothing to compute.

Both questions, measured

Measured here on Qwen3-0.6B (PyTorch on the CPU; the {{KV compression experiment|#t-kvc}} tab has every number). Task A hides one "secret code" line among 60 (about 800 tokens) and asks for it; with the full cache the model answers 98.3% of 60 questions. Task B scores the next 128 tokens of WikiText-2 after a 1,024-token context; full-cache perplexity 17.04 over 24 windows. Each policy keeps 50%, 25% or 10% of the context, after a full prefill.

  • Random eviction must keep the first tokens. Uniform random choice over all positions sends perplexity from 17.04 to 927 on average at 25% kept, because it can drop the first token, the attention sink; the one seed that kept it scored 23. Keeping the first 4 tokens and choosing the rest at random gives 23.71. The cheap control has to be done right.
  • On text continuation, random is a respectable control but loses. At 25% kept: H2O 18.08, SnapKV 19.83, random per head 22.16, random shared 23.71. At 10%: 19.97, 22.97, 26.84 and 27.80.
  • On retrieval, random fails outright. At 25% kept, random with sinks answers 5.6% of questions, random per head 13.9%, keeping the most recent tokens 26.7%, H2O 26.7%, SnapKV per head 63.3%, and SnapKV with one selection shared by all heads and layers 91.7%. Retrieval needs one specific line, which only a score informed by the question keeps. The Random Attention result is about reasoning traces, which repeat themselves; it does not transfer to finding one fact.
  • The realisable saving. Per-head selections (SnapKV, H2O, random per head) left no 16-token block free: 0% of the memory comes back in a paged engine without a per-head layout. Shared random selection freed 1.3% of blocks in place at 25% kept (kept tokens are scattered over almost every block), although compaction could reclaim all 75%. The shared SnapKV selection, the best on retrieval here, also kept its tokens in clusters: 51% of blocks freeable in place, 75% with compaction.

What transfers: the ranking of methods and the two questions. What does not: a 0.6B model on 800 to 1,024-token contexts is not a 70B model on 100K tokens, and eviction matters most where contexts are long. Treat these as a demonstration of the method of checking, not as the verdict on any paper.

7. Who goes next: priority, fairness, shortest first, SLOs

When the server is full, the order of the waiting queue decides who waits. First come, first served (FCFS, every engine's default) is fair per request, not per user: one client that floods the server puts everyone else behind it (head-of-line blocking), and one very long job holds up many short ones. Reordering only matters when a queue forms, that is near or beyond capacity.

Priority classes. Each request carries a number and the scheduler serves the best first. In vLLM (--scheduling-policy priority) lower values go first, ties by arrival time and then by request id; when memory runs out the victim is the running request with the largest value, then the latest arrival. A waiting high-priority request does not evict running ones to get in; in SGLang it can, past a threshold. Priorities need someone to assign them and can starve the low class.

Fairness between clients. Sheng et al. count service in tokens, with output tokens weighted more because they cost more (w_p = 1 per prompt token, w_q = 2 per output token, following OpenAI's prices at the time), and propose the Virtual Token Counter (VTC): keep one counter per client; when there is room, admit the earliest request of the client with the smallest counter; add wp × prompt tokens at admission and wq per generated token; when a client (re)joins the queue, lift its counter to the smallest among waiting clients, so idle time cannot be banked. They prove that two clients who both have work waiting never differ in service by more than 2 × max(wp × longest prompt, wq × largest batch of output tokens) ({{Fairness in Serving Large Language Models, OSDI 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00588}}, Algorithm 2 and Theorem 4.4). A requests-per-minute limit per client, the usual alternative, caps a client even when the server is idle.

When both are heavy they part ways: VTC gives the two clients equal service per second while both have work waiting (26,800 weighted tokens per second each between 5 s and 30 s, against 36,700 for A and 17,200 for B under FCFS), so B, which asks for less than A, still gets prompt service (median TTFT 6.6 s against A's 27 s), while strict priority serves B almost instantly (0.13 s) and leaves A only what B does not use (median 28 s).

Shortest job first minimises mean waiting time, a classic result; the catch is that an answer's length is unknown until it ends, so systems predict it: learning to rank lengths (its authors report 2.8 times lower latency for chatbot serving, {{Fu et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15792}}) or a multi-level feedback queue that demotes long-running jobs (FastServe, {{Wu et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05920}}). SLOs and goodput. Every policy above guesses at what users want; an SLO (service level objective) states it, for example p99 TTFT under 2 s and TPOT under 100 ms, MLPerf's targets for Llama 3.1 8B. DistServe defines goodput as "the maximum request rate that can be served adhering to the SLO attainment goal (say, 90%) for each GPU provisioned" ({{DistServe, section 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.09670}}); simulators, the parent's included, often report the simpler per-second count of requests that met both targets. SLO-aware schedulers order and admit by deadline, and under overload reject early rather than serve everyone late: Mooncake's scheduler predicts load and rejects requests it cannot serve in time ({{Mooncake|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00079}}). Check: TTFT percentiles per tenant and per priority class (tag requests and split your metrics), and vllm:request_queue_time_seconds for time spent waiting.

The simulator. src/sched/bksched.py copies the parent's {{Serving simulator|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}} (a step-by-step copy of vLLM v0.31.0's scheduler on a roofline step time, the larger of FLOPs over peak and bytes over bandwidth plus fixed costs, fitted to NVIDIA's published NIM measurements of Llama 3.1 8B on one H100, held-out rows within 7% median for inter-token latency, or to llama.cpp runs on the M1 Pro; model shapes from each model's config.json) and adds vLLM's priority policy, VTC, oracle shortest-job-first and multi-client traffic. VTC and SJF are the papers' algorithms on vLLM's step structure, not code from a released engine. It leaves out what the parent's leaves out: CUDA graph padding, asynchronous scheduling, tokenization and network time, tensor parallelism ({{Distributed and disaggregated serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d8170bd7cdf47af0ec07b}}), speculative decoding ({{Speculative decoding|n:3f35c17b0d0d81e086fde85940ebac1c}}).

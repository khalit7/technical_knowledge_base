7. Collectives on the wire: algorithms and what they cost

Training code calls a handful of collectives, operations every GPU in a group joins: all-reduce (everyone ends with the sum), reduce-scatter (everyone ends with the sum of one slice), all-gather (everyone ends with every slice), all-to-all (everyone sends a different piece to everyone). {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}} explains which parallelism calls which. A library (NCCL on NVIDIA, RCCL on AMD, XLA on TPU) turns each call into a schedule of point-to-point messages, and the schedule decides how many latencies and how many bytes the operation pays. Sources and caveats of the cost table (1 min)

Formulas: {{Thakur, Rabenseifner and Gropp, 2005|https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1094342005051521}} for recursive doubling and halving-doubling; the ring line is their ring reduce-scatter plus ring all-gather, composed here derived. Their γ term for the additions is dropped: on GPUs the adds are far cheaper than the transfers. The tree row is the usual α-β estimate derived; NVIDIA: rings' "latency scales linearly with the number of GPUs", trees give "full bandwidth and a logarithmic latency", "up to 180x improvement at 24k GPUs" in latency ({{NCCL 2.4 blog, 2019|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/massively-scale-deep-learning-training-nccl-2-4/}}). The in-switch line is idealised (the switch adds as fast as data arrives).

On a torus: one axis, two axes, or a tree

On a TPU slice the ring runs along a torus axis in both directions at once, and a collective over two mesh axes uses both, halving the bandwidth term; a tree trades the ring's n/2 latencies for 2 log2 n. The formulas are in the closed block.

For the 8B's 16.06 GB along one 16-chip v5p axis the ring form gives about 2 × 16.06 GB / 180 GB/s = 0.178 s, 0.059 s over all three axes derived, against 62.5 ms over 8 H100s' NVLink. The book's own statements, and a worked example checked against a profile, are in the closed block.

A bidirectional ring all-gather takes "Ttotal = V / Wici", which "doesn't depend on X!", gathering over several axes "increases the available bandwidth by a factor of Naxes", "an AllReduce is twice as expensive as an AllGather", an all-to-all on a 1-D ring is "1/4 the cost of an AllGather", and an axis without the wraparound "typically doubles the time of most communication" ({{scaling book, sharding|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/sharding/}}). The book's worked example, an all-gather of 34 MB over a 4-chip v5e axis, predicts 378 µs with a wraparound and 560 µs without one; the profile it shows measured 680 µs measured by Google.

What real clusters measure

Against published nccl-tests runs, rings reach 76 to 81% of NVLink's per-direction rate, and in-switch reduction is real but well short of its ideal: 1.29 times the ring on H200 and 1.23 times on B200, not 1.75 times. The table, its model and what nccl-tests' busbw means are in the closed block.

busbw, and published nccl-tests against the model (3 min)

NVIDIA's nccl-tests, the standard benchmark, prints two bandwidths: algbw = S / t, and busbw = algbw × 2(n−1)/n for all-reduce ((n−1)/n for all-gather, reduce-scatter and all-to-all), defined so that it can be compared "with the hardware peak bandwidth, independently of the number of ranks used" ({{nccl-tests PERFORMANCE.md|https://github.com/NVIDIA/nccl-tests/blob/master/doc/PERFORMANCE.md}}). For a ring, busbw is the bandwidth on each wire. In-switch and hierarchical algorithms break that meaning; NCCL's author: "with SHARP enabled ... BusBW may not represent any 'Bus' bandwidth" ({{nccl-tests issue 153|https://github.com/NVIDIA/nccl-tests/issues/153}}). So a busbw above the link's rate is not a measurement error: it is NVLS or a hierarchy at work.

Model: ring busbw equals the link's bandwidth each way; in-switch busbw = 1.75 × link (S/β time, rescaled by 2(n−1)/n); across servers a two-level model (ring inside over NVLink, each GPU's 1/8 share all-reduced over its own NIC, the slower phase setting the time) derived. Measurements: {{Bekman, ML Engineering, network benchmarks|https://github.com/stas00/ml-engineering/blob/master/network/benchmarks/README.md}} independent; {{AWS awsome-distributed-training|https://github.com/aws-samples/awsome-distributed-training/tree/main/micro-benchmarks/nccl-tests}} sample outputs vendor. Together AI's acceptance target for H100 InfiniBand clusters is "around 92% of the theoretical maximum of the fabric: so around 370 GB/s on a 400GB/s fabric" ({{Together AI, Aug 2024|https://www.together.ai/blog/a-practitioners-guide-to-testing-and-running-large-gpu-clusters-for-training-generative-ai-models}}).

Rings reach 76 to 81% of NVLink's per-direction rate, and two-level all-reduces across servers 73 to 101% of the model. In-switch reduction is real but well short of the ideal: 1.29 times the ring on H200 and 1.23 times on B200, not the 1.75 times that halving the bytes would give, because the switch's adders and protocol overheads, not the wires, become the limit. The 2 × p5 case reaches 101% of a model that assumes a ring inside each server, which fits NCCL using NVLS there (the README does not say).

How NCCL picks. NCCL chooses algorithm (ring, tree, NVLS, CollNet, PAT) and protocol by message size and topology from a cost model: logarithmic-latency schemes for small messages, bandwidth-optimal ones for large. NVIDIA's tuning post prints a real choice on 4 servers, 32 GPUs: tree with LL up to 96 KiB, tree with LL128 up to 12 MiB, ring with LL128 up to 96 MiB, ring with Simple above ({{NVIDIA, Jul 2025|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/understanding-nccl-tuning-to-accelerate-gpu-to-gpu-communication/}}).

NCCL's three protocols: LL, LL128, Simple (under 1 min)

The protocols trade latency against bandwidth: LL sends 4 bytes of data with a 4-byte flag (about 1 µs per hop, 25 to 50% of peak bandwidth), LL128 120 bytes of data with an 8-byte flag (about 2 µs, about 95%), Simple large chunks with memory fences (about 6 µs, near peak) ({{Hu et al., Demystifying NCCL, Table I|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04786}}) independent.

Corrections to the old page's sanity numbers
The old page put the 16 GB all-reduce at "~2 x 16/50 = 0.64 s" over 400G InfiniBand and "~70 ms" over NVLink 4. 2S/β is the large-n limit; for 8 GPUs the ring factor is 2 × 7/8 = 1.75, giving 0.56 s and 62 ms () derived. It also said SHARP "roughly halv[es] traffic": the bytes per GPU do fall from 2(n−1)/n · S to S, but measured speed-ups are 1.23 to 1.29 times inside a server (table above).

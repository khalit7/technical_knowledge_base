5. Topology: fat trees, rails, oversubscription and dragonflies

A switch has a fixed number of ports (64 on a Quantum-2), so thousands of NICs need layers of switches, each costing money, power and a hop of latency. How they are wired decides which GPUs can talk at full speed at once. Three shapes cover almost every machine:

Three ways to join many chips: count the hops between the two highlighted endpoints

Fat trees. The standard GPU-cluster answer is a folded Clos network, usually called a fat tree: leaf switches connect to NICs below and to every spine switch above; a third layer joins groups of spines when two are not enough. With k-port switches, two layers reach k²/2 endpoints and three reach k³/4 ({{Al-Fares et al., SIGCOMM 2008|https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1402958.1402967}}); with 64 ports that is 2,048 and 65,536 derived. If each leaf has as many uplinks as downlinks, the network has full bisection bandwidth: cut it into any two halves and each half can send to the other at the full rate of its NICs. Fewer uplinks than downlinks is oversubscription: at 1:7 (Meta) or 15:1 (Alibaba), traffic crossing that layer gets a seventh or a fifteenth of the NIC rate if everyone sends at once.

Rails. In a rail-optimised fabric, GPU k of every server connects to leaf switch k: rail k (rung 4 of the hero figure). NCCL's collectives across servers mostly pair same-index GPUs (the NVLink inside the server does the rest), so their traffic stays on one leaf, one hop, never touching the spine. NVIDIA's DGX SuperPOD reference: "Traffic per rail of the DGX B200 systems is always one hop away from the other 31 nodes in a SU. Traffic between nodes, or between rails, traverses the spine layer" ({{network fabrics|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx-superpod/reference-architecture-scalable-infrastructure-b200/latest/network-fabrics.html}}). Real clusters (Meta, Alibaba, DeepSeek, NVIDIA, Frontier) are in the closed block below.

Predict first. 16 servers of 8 GPUs on a rail-optimised fabric. Every GPU sends 1 GB in an all-to-all, spread evenly over all 128 GPUs. What fraction of each GPU's bytes must cross the spine (go to a different rail on a different server)?
Of the 127 other GPUs, 15 are on the same rail (other servers) and 7 in the same server; the other 105 are on another rail and another server: 105/127 = 83% derived. A ring all-reduce sends none of its bytes over the spine. This is why all-to-all, not all-reduce, is what a network's spine and oversubscription are for. Check it in the explorer below.

Why oversubscribe at all? Alibaba reports that "about 96.3% of in-production LLM training jobs take less than 1K GPUs", so a full-bisection core would mostly carry nothing; HPN places pipeline traffic, the lightest, across pods. DeepSeek's cost table prices a two-layer fat tree at $4.39k per endpoint against $7.5k for three layers, which is why it built eight two-layer planes instead of one three-layer tree ({{Zhao et al., Table 3|https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09343}}). The rail-only proposal goes further and removes the spine, forwarding cross-rail traffic over NVLink, claiming "the same training performance while reducing the network cost by 38% to 77%" with "8.2% to 11.2% completion time overhead for all-to-all traffic" ({{Wang et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.12169}}).

Dragonflies. "Because cables dominate network cost, the number of cables, and particularly the number of long, global cables should be minimized". The dragonfly answer "uses a group of high-radix routers as a virtual router to increase the effective radix of the network", so that "each minimally routed packet traverses at most one global channel"; the authors claim it "reduces cost by 20% compared to a flattened butterfly and by 52% compared to a folded Clos network in configurations with ≥ 16K nodes" ({{Kim, Dally, Scott and Abts, ISCA 2008|https://research.google/pubs/technology-driven-highly-scalable-dragonfly-topology/}}). Inside a group the switches are wired all-to-all with short cables; between groups there is at least one long global link per pair (the Dragonfly view above). The price is that heavy traffic between two groups shares few global links, so the paper adds adaptive routing that sends some packets through a third group. HPC machines such as Frontier use it; the AI training clusters in the table use rail-optimised or multi-plane fat trees, whose rails suit NCCL's same-index rings.

6. TPU pods: a torus of chips, rewired by optical switches

Each TPU has its own inter-chip interconnect (ICI) ports wired straight to neighbouring chips: no NIC, no switch. Chips sit on a grid; each talks to 4 neighbours (a 2-D grid: v2, v3, v5e, v6e) or 6 (a 3-D grid: v4, v5p, Ironwood). Wiring the last chip of every row back to the first makes a torus (the Torus view in section 5's figure), which roughly halves the longest path and lets a ring of chips send both ways at once. A torus is excellent for ring collectives between neighbours and slower for arbitrary point-to-point traffic, which is why TPU programs are written as shardings over a device mesh ({{Sharding and scale|n:3c65c17b0d0d8125b084e6720cc66b3d}}).

Optical circuit switches. A torus wired once is fragile: one dead chip breaks a ring, and the whole machine must be cabled before any of it can be used. TPU v4 builds the pod from 4 × 4 × 4 cubes of 64 chips (one rack, 16 hosts), electrically wired inside, and connects the cubes' faces through optical circuit switches (OCS): MEMS mirrors that steer light from one fibre to another and switch in milliseconds. Each cube face has 16 links, 96 per cube; opposite faces go to the same switch, so 6 × 16 / 2 = 48 switches of 136 ports join 64 cubes into 4,096 chips. The mirrors are not a packet network: they only set which cube's face is plugged into which, like a plugboard: slices of any shape, routing around failed cubes, twisted tori, for under 5% of system cost.

What the optical switches buy, in detail (1 min)

All from the {{TPU v4 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01433}}:

  • Slices of any shape: a job asks for, say, 4 × 8 × 16 chips, and the switches wire the cubes it gets into exactly that torus, wherever they sit in the hall.
  • Routing around failures: a cube with a dead host is swapped out by re-pointing mirrors; with 1,024 hosts each up 99.0% to 99.9% of the time, a large slice is otherwise rarely all healthy.
  • Twisted tori: some links are re-pointed to shorten the longest paths, which raised all-to-all throughput; 40% of slices of 64 chips or more chose a twisted topology in Google's sample.
  • Cost: the switches and optics are "<5% of system cost and <3% of system power".

Slices smaller than a cube get no optical wraparound, so a 2 × 2 × 4 slice is slower per byte than a 4 × 4 × 4. Ironwood keeps the scheme at 9,216 chips: 144 cubes of 64, 1.77 PB of HBM in one pod, ICI at 9.6 Tb/s (1.2 TB/s) per chip ({{Google Cloud, Nov 6 2025|https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/compute/ironwood-tpus-and-new-axion-based-vms-for-your-ai-workloads}}). Per link the scaling book uses 45 GB/s one way on v4 and v5e and 90 GB/s on v5p, v6e and TPU7x ({{scaling book|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/tpus/}}). vendor spec

Beyond a pod. Pods (or slices) join over the ordinary data-centre network, DCN, at about 6.25 GB/s per chip (12.5 on v6e and TPU7x), more than 10 times slower than one ICI link ({{scaling book|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/tpus/}}). So, as on GPUs, only data parallelism (the gradient all-reduce, once per step) crosses that boundary ("multislice"), and everything chatty stays on ICI: the same ladder, with ICI as the top network rung.

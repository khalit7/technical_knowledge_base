5. Leaving the server: network cards, RDMA, InfiniBand and RoCE

The problem. Past the NVLink domain, GPUs in different servers must talk through a network. The ordinary way, a program calling the operating system's TCP stack, copies every byte through kernel memory, takes interrupts and backs off on loss; at 400 Gb/s per GPU that costs CPU time and microseconds the GPUs spend idle. The page {{Networking foundations|n:3c65c17b0d0d8117b21dcb9041879564}} explains TCP and its section 14 why clusters avoid it for heavy traffic.

RDMA from zero. Remote direct memory access moves the whole transport into the network card. The steps, in the vocabulary NCCL's network plugins use:

  1. Register memory. The program tells the NIC which buffer it may touch (on a GPU server, a buffer in GPU memory); the NIC pins it and gets a key. With GPUDirect RDMA the NIC reaches GPU memory across PCIe without the CPU copying it.
  2. Create a queue pair. Each connection is a pair of queues (send and receive) that live in the NIC; a reliable connection keeps ordering and retransmission in hardware.
  3. Post work. The program writes a small descriptor ("send these bytes", or "write them into the remote buffer at this address with this key") into the queue and rings a doorbell. No system call, no copy.
  4. The NICs move the data. A one-sided RDMA write lands in the remote buffer without the remote CPU doing anything; completion shows up in a completion queue, which the GPU or CPU polls.

The same RDMA operations run on three kinds of fabric in practice: InfiniBand, RoCE (RDMA over Converged Ethernet: the InfiniBand transport inside UDP packets, port 4791, on ordinary routed Ethernet) and AWS's EFA (its own SRD protocol); {{Networking foundations|n:3c65c17b0d0d8117b21dcb9041879564}} has the details of each and how NCCL picks between them. What separates InfiniBand from RoCE is how each avoids dropping packets, because RDMA's hardware transport recovers from a lost packet slowly.

InfiniBand: credits

A network built for RDMA, with its own switches and adapters. A sender may not transmit until the receiver has granted buffer space: "Each receiving end of a link supplies credits to the sending device ... Data is not transmitted unless the receiver advertises credits indicating receive buffer space is available" ({{Mellanox, Introduction to InfiniBand|https://network.nvidia.com/pdf/whitepapers/IB_Intro_WP_190.pdf}}). So the fabric never drops for lack of buffer. Current NVIDIA switches add adaptive routing and in-network reduction (SHARP). vendor

RoCE: lossless Ethernet by configuration

Ethernet drops packets when buffers fill; RoCE needs Priority Flow Control (PFC), pause frames sent hop by hop, plus ECN marking and a congestion-control algorithm, usually DCQCN. The Ultra Ethernet Consortium's overview names the costs: DCQCN "requires careful manual tuning", a lost packet triggers "Go-Back-N" retransmission, and PFC backpressure "can produce 'congestion trees' and head-of-line blocking" ({{UEC overview, 2023|https://ultraethernet.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2023/10/23.07.12-UEC-1.0-Overview-FINAL-WITH-LOGO.pdf}}). In exchange it is multi-vendor and runs on the Ethernet a data centre already has.

RoCE at scale, in a real run. Meta trained Llama 3 405B over RoCE at 400 Gb/s per GPU (smaller models on InfiniBand). Two problems and their fixes are documented: "LLM training produces fat network flows that are hard to balance across all available network paths using traditional methods such as Equal-Cost Multi-Path (ECMP) routing", so "our collective library creates 16 network flows between two GPUs, instead of just one" and an enhanced ECMP hashes on more header fields; with that balancing and "Deep-buffer switches in the spine", they ran "without traditional congestion control methods such as ... (DCQCN)" ({{Llama 3 report, section 3.3.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}). The idea: ECMP sends every packet of one flow down one path to keep order, so a few huge flows collide on some links while others sit idle; more flows per connection spread them.

Where Ethernet is going. The Ultra Ethernet Consortium released its Specification 1.0 on June 11, 2025 ({{UEC press release|https://ultraethernet.org/ultra-ethernet-consortium-uec-launches-specification-1-0-transforming-ethernet-for-ai-and-hpc-at-scale/}}). Its design goals, from the overview: "packet spraying" (every flow uses all paths at once), delivery out of order into the application buffer, and a transport that "does not depend on a lossless fabric". NVIDIA's Spectrum-X sells adaptive routing and telemetry-based congestion control on Ethernet ({{Spectrum-X|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/networking/spectrumx/}}, vendor claims). AWS's SRD already sprays and tolerates reordering (its user guide: "built-in OS-bypass and congestion control through the Scalable Reliable Datagram (SRD) protocol", {{EFA|https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/efa.html}}).

6. Topology: fat trees, rails and oversubscription

The problem. A switch has a fixed number of ports (64 on a Quantum-2). Thousands of NICs need several layers of switches, and every layer costs money, power and a hop of latency. How the layers are wired decides which GPUs can talk at full speed at the same time.

Fat trees. The standard answer is a folded Clos network, usually called a fat tree: leaf switches connect to NICs below and to every spine switch above; a third layer joins groups of spines when two are not enough. With k-port switches, two layers reach k²/2 endpoints and three reach k³/4 ({{Al-Fares et al., SIGCOMM 2008|https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1402958.1402967}}); with 64 ports that is 2,048 and 65,536 derived. If each leaf has as many uplinks as downlinks, the network has full bisection bandwidth: cut it into any two halves and each half can send to the other at the full rate of its NICs. Fewer uplinks than downlinks is oversubscription, written as the ratio: at 1:7 (Meta) or 15:1 (Alibaba), traffic crossing that layer gets a seventh or a fifteenth of the NIC rate if everyone sends at once.

Rails. In a rail-optimised fabric, GPU k of every server connects to leaf switch k: rail k. NCCL's collectives across servers mostly pair same-index GPUs (the NVLink inside the server does the rest), so their traffic stays on one leaf, one hop, never touching the spine. NVIDIA's DGX SuperPOD reference: "A modular architecture based on SUs of 32 DGX B200 systems each", "rail-optimized, balanced, full-fat tree", and "Traffic per rail of the DGX B200 systems is always one hop away from the other 31 nodes in a SU. Traffic between nodes, or between rails, traverses the spine layer" ({{components|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx-superpod/reference-architecture-scalable-infrastructure-b200/latest/dgx-superpod-components.html}}, {{network fabrics|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx-superpod/reference-architecture-scalable-infrastructure-b200/latest/network-fabrics.html}}). Four such units, 127 servers, use 32 leaf and 16 spine switches.

Why oversubscribe at all? Alibaba reports that "about 96.3% of in-production LLM training jobs take less than 1K GPUs", so a full-bisection core would mostly carry nothing; HPN places pipeline traffic, the lightest, across pods. DeepSeek's cost table prices a two-layer fat tree at $4.39k per endpoint against $7.5k for three layers, which is why it built eight two-layer planes instead of one three-layer tree ({{Zhao et al., Table 3|https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09343}}). The rail-only proposal goes further and removes the spine, forwarding cross-rail traffic over NVLink, claiming "the same training performance while reducing the network cost by 38% to 77%" with "8.2% to 11.2% completion time overhead for all-to-all traffic" ({{Wang et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.12169}}).

Predict first. 16 servers of 8 GPUs on a rail-optimised fabric. Every GPU sends 1 GB in an all-to-all, spread evenly over all 128 GPUs. What fraction of each GPU's bytes must cross the spine (go to a different rail on a different server)?
Of the 127 other GPUs, 15 are on the same rail (other servers) and 7 in the same server; the other 105 are on another rail and another server: 105/127 = 83% derived. A ring all-reduce sends none of its bytes over the spine. This is why all-to-all, not all-reduce, is what a network's spine and oversubscription are for.

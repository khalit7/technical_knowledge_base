7. Offload and tiering: using the slower rungs on purpose

The problem. When state does not fit in HBM, it can live one rung down (CPU memory, then NVMe) and be brought in when needed. That works only if moving it costs less than the time it saves, or can be hidden behind compute. The arithmetic is the same each time: bytes / bandwidth of the slower link.

All derived: bytes / bandwidth. C2C's 900 GB/s is split evenly between directions here (an assumption); PCIe Gen5 x16 is 128 GB/s both directions on the {{H100 page|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h100/}}; the 50% utilisation is illustrative, and the prefill count ignores attention FLOPs, which only make recomputing slower. The SSD figure is the median of three sequential reads of a 4 GiB file with the page cache bypassed measured here.

Training: ZeRO-Offload and ZeRO-Infinity. The optimizer step touches each parameter's state once per step, so keeping the state in CPU memory (or NVMe) and running the update there costs one transfer per step. ZeRO-Infinity puts GPU, CPU and NVMe memory behind one interface; its paper reports over 25 petaflops on 512 V100s (40% of peak) for models far beyond GPU memory ({{Rajbhandari et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07857}}). At the 1.5 s per direction above, offloading the 8B model's optimizer only pays when a step already takes seconds (large batches), or when the alternative is not training at all. On a GH200-style coherent link the same move takes a fifth of a second. Sharding across GPUs (ZeRO, FSDP) is usually cheaper than offload when you have the GPUs: {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}}.

Inference: weights and KV offload. FlexGen ran OPT-175B on a single 16 GB GPU by streaming weights and cache from CPU memory and disk, reaching 1 token per second with an effective batch of 144: throughput, not latency ({{Sheng et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.06865}}). For the KV cache the table shows why offload wins: bringing a 32K conversation's cache back over PCIe takes 168 ms, recomputing it about 9 s. This is the case for keeping paused conversations' caches in CPU memory or on SSD and reloading them when the user returns, rather than recomputing them.

The cost
Every offloaded byte crosses a link 26× or more slower than HBM, and the CPU or SSD must keep up. Offload buys capacity with time; it is the right trade only when the transfer can overlap with compute or replaces something slower (recomputation, a second GPU you do not have).

How to check: measure the link, not the label. nvidia-smi topo -m shows how GPUs and NICs are attached; a pinned-memory tensor.to('cuda', non_blocking=True) of 1 GB timed with CUDA events gives the achieved host-to-device rate; storage numbers must bypass the page cache (a first attempt here, reading a file that had just been written, reported 9 to 11 GB/s: the operating system was serving it from memory, not the SSD; {{Storage and file systems|n:3f05c17b0d0d8141be7acc1313570cbf}} explains the page cache).

8. Supply: HBM is the constrained part

Only a few companies make HBM, and every datacenter accelerator in the parent's Chip atlas carries it. Verified, dated points:

  • Sep 12, 2025: SK hynix announced it had completed HBM4 development and prepared mass production: 2,048 I/O terminals, "over 10Gbps" per pin against JEDEC's 8 Gb/s, power efficiency improved by more than 40% ({{SK hynix newsroom|https://news.skhynix.com/sk-hynix-completes-worlds-first-hbm4-development-and-readies-mass-production/}}) vendor claim.
  • Jul 21, 2026: NVIDIA described Rubin with up to 288 GB of HBM4 in 12-high stacks at up to 22 TB/s; Rubin was announced as in full production on May 31, 2026, shipping from the fall ({{Rubin blog|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-rubin-gpu-architecture-powering-the-era-of-agentic-ai/}}, {{NVIDIA newsroom|https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/vera-rubin-full-production-agentic-ai-factory}}). NVIDIA's own pages disagree on Rubin's bandwidth: the Vera Rubin NVL72 page lists 19.2 TB/s per GPU; the parent's Chip atlas shows both.
  • AMD MI455X (announced, volume 2H 2026): 432 GB of HBM4 at 23.3 TB/s per GPU ({{AMD MI400|https://www.amd.com/en/products/accelerators/instinct/mi400.html}}), more capacity than Rubin's 288 GB.

The old root page also reported DRAM prices up about 500% in a year, Samsung doubling HBM4 output in 2027, and "high-bandwidth flash" at Hot Chips 2026. None could be confirmed from a primary source here, so they are not stated as fact.

Why it matters to an engineer. HBM capacity per GPU is the scarcest resource in serving and the reason a 288 GB part can be worth more than a 180 GB one with the same 8 TB/s (B300 against B200): it decides how many conversations fit (section 6) and how many GPUs a model needs at all.

2. A DRAM cell, a row, a bank: why order matters and bandwidth costs power

The cell. DRAM (dynamic random-access memory) stores one bit as charge on a microscopic capacitor, reached through one transistor ({{Wikipedia, DRAM|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_random-access_memory}}). One transistor and one capacitor per bit is what makes DRAM dense and cheap. The price: the charge leaks, so every row must be refreshed (read and rewritten) within a fixed interval, 64 ms for DDR2 and half that above 85 °C case temperature ({{Wikipedia, Memory refresh|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memory_refresh}}).

The array. Cells sit in a grid. A row (wordline) is opened by an activate command, which connects the whole row's capacitors to sense amplifiers; these amplify the tiny voltages to full 0s and 1s and hold them. That latch is the row buffer. Reading drains the capacitors, so the sense amplifiers write the values back while the row is open. A read command then picks a few columns from the row buffer and sends them out in a burst. To open a different row the bank must first precharge (close the open row). A chip has several independent banks, each with its own row buffer, so one bank can activate while another sends data. In HBM2 a row is 1 KB ({{O'Connor et al., MICRO 2017|https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2017-10_fine-grained-dram-energy-efficient-dram-extreme-bandwidth-systems}}).

Sequential reads pay for one activate and then stream from the open row. Random reads into one bank pay precharge plus activate every time and open 16 KB of rows to use 1 KB. Spreading random reads over many banks hides most of that, which is why memory controllers interleave consecutive addresses across banks and channels, and why a GPU wants many independent requests in flight.

Measured on this laptop. The {{Memory lab|#t-lab}} read 512 MB of the M1 Pro GPU's memory in random blocks. Blocks of 16 bytes delivered 5.5 GB/s of useful data, 128-byte blocks 40.7 GB/s, 4 KB blocks 142.9 GB/s, close to the sequential rate measured here. Below 128 bytes the useful rate falls in proportion to the block size: the GPU fetches whole cache lines, so a 16-byte read costs as much as a 128-byte one. This is the hardware reason behind "coalesced" access on the {{CUDA root's GPU simulator|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c39f34d5e070d783c1}}, and behind the KV cache being stored in blocks of many tokens rather than token by token (section 6).

Why bandwidth costs power

The arithmetic is cheap; the wire is not. In stacked HBM2 the energy to read a bit was about 3.92 pJ: 1.21 pJ to activate rows, 2.24 pJ to move the bits across the DRAM die and down the through-silicon vias, about 0.3 pJ for the short wires on the interposer, the rest error correction; off-board GDDR5 cost about 14.0 pJ per bit ({{O'Connor et al. 2017|https://research.nvidia.com/publication/2017-10_fine-grained-dram-energy-efficient-dram-extreme-bandwidth-systems}}, Figure 1 and section 2) published measurement model. At that HBM2 energy per bit:

  • an H100 reading at its full 3.35 TB/s would spend 3.35 × 1012 × 8 × 3.92 pJ = 105 W on memory alone, and Rubin's 22 TB/s would spend 690 W derived, illustrative. HBM3 and HBM4 must cost less per bit than HBM2 for Rubin to exist; SK hynix claims more than 40% better power efficiency for HBM4 than the previous generation (section 8).
  • generating one token of Llama 3.1 8B at batch 1 reads 16.06 GB of weights: about 0.50 J per token just to move them derived, illustrative. Batching divides that over many sequences.
The cost
Each activate opens a 1 KB row (HBM2) even if 32 bytes are wanted; O'Connor et al. note most GPU workloads use only a small fraction of each activated row, which is why activation energy is a large share. Access patterns that touch whole rows and whole cache lines save both time and energy.

When it matters for training. Element-wise kernels (normalisation, activations, optimizer updates) stream every byte once; their speed is the memory's sequential bandwidth. Gathers (embedding lookups, MoE token routing, paged KV reads) touch scattered blocks; their speed depends on the block size. How to check: in Nsight Compute, compare a kernel's DRAM throughput against the device's peak, and its sectors per request (32-byte sectors): many sectors per request means scattered access.

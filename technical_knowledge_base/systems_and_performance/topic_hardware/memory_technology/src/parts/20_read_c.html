3. SRAM and the cache ladder

The cell. SRAM (static RAM) stores a bit in a loop of transistors, typically six (a "6T" cell), that holds its value as long as power is on: no capacitor, no refresh, and it can be built on the processor die itself. It is faster than DRAM but needs far more silicon area per bit ({{Wikipedia, SRAM|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_random-access_memory}}). Every register, L1 cache, shared memory and L2 cache on a GPU is SRAM.

How much an H100 has. 132 SMs, each with a 256 KB register file and 256 KB of combined L1 and shared memory, plus a 50 MB L2: 34.6 + 34.6 + 50 = 119 MB, 0.149% of its 80 GB of HBM derived (per-SM sizes: {{CUDA Programming Guide, compute capabilities|https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-programming-guide/05-appendices/compute-capabilities.html}} and the {{Hopper architecture blog|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/}}). A matmul kernel's whole job is to keep a tile of each operand in that small, fast memory and reuse it many times before going back to HBM; how the programmer does that is the {{CUDA root|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c39f34d5e070d783c1}}'s subject.

The ladder, measured on this laptop's GPU

Two experiments on the M1 Pro GPU, each repeated in three runs: a kernel that reads a buffer of a given size over and over with all threads (how much data per second), and a single thread that follows a chain of pointers scattered at random through the buffer, one 128-byte line per hop, so each read must wait for the previous one (how long one read takes).

Up to 128 KB1,239 GB/s; about 61 ns per dependent read (up to 16 KB). The per-core 8 KB L1 and the GPU's caches.
256 KB to 2 MB579 GB/s at 1 MB; about 95 ns per read up to 1 MB. The GPU's 256 KB L2 and beyond.
4 MB to 32 MB318 GB/s at 32 MB; about 300 ns per read. The 24 MB system level cache, shared with the CPU.
128 MB and up127.5 GB/s; about 467 ns per read (433 to 571 across runs). LPDDR5 DRAM.

Cache sizes are independent measurements by {{Philip Turner's metal-benchmarks|https://github.com/philipturner/metal-benchmarks}} (L1 8 KB per core, L2 256 KB, SLC 24 MB for the M1 Pro); Apple publishes none. The measured steps sit near those sizes but not exactly on them: the latency plateau runs past 8 KB to 16 KB and past 256 KB to 1 MB, and random pointer chasing also pays for address translation (16 KB pages here), so the labels are a reading of the curve, not a proof. measured here MLX 0.32.3, load average 6.1 to 7.5 (the laptop was shared).

Little's law ties the two curves together. Bytes in flight = bandwidth × latency. To pull 127.5 GB/s from DRAM when each read takes about 467 ns, the GPU must keep about 127.5 × 467 = 60 KB of reads outstanding at all times derived, hundreds of cache lines. One thread gets one line per 467 ns, 0.27 GB/s; that is why GPUs run thousands of threads. The {{CUDA root's GPU simulator|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c39f34d5e070d783c1}} measured the other side of this on the same GPU (bandwidth against threads in flight, plateau about 145 GB/s) and inferred about 370 ns of DRAM latency from it; the direct pointer chase here finds about 467 ns, a little higher because a random chase also misses in the address-translation caches.

NVIDIA's ladder has the same shape. Luo et al. measured, in clock cycles, about 29 for shared memory, 38 to 43 for L1, 262 to 273 for L2 and 466 to 542 for global memory on A100, H800 and RTX 4090 ({{Benchmarking and Dissecting the Nvidia Hopper GPU Architecture, Table IV|https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13499v1}}) independent measurement. Each step down costs several times more latency; each step up gives several times more bandwidth.

SRAM-only machines
Some accelerators keep the whole model in SRAM spread over many chips or one wafer: Cerebras WSE-3 has 44 GB of on-wafer SRAM at 21 PB/s; the announced Groq 3 LPX rack has 128 GB of SRAM across 256 chips at 40 PB/s. Huge bandwidth, small capacity per chip: Llama 3.1 8B's 16 GB would occupy about a third of a wafer or 33 of the 0.5 GB Groq chips derived. Their bets are compared on the sibling {{TPUs and other accelerators|n:3c65c17b0d0d8151bbc2e0ceea196187}}.
The cost
Six transistors per bit instead of one transistor and a capacitor: SRAM takes much more die area per bit, so every megabyte of cache displaces arithmetic units. Chips trade cache for compute differently: the RTX 5090 has a 96 MB L2 (RTX Blackwell whitepaper), the H100 50 MB.

HBM supply: the constrained part (dated, volatile)

Only a few companies make HBM, and every datacenter accelerator in the parent's Chip atlas carries it. Verified, dated points:

  • Sep 12, 2025: SK hynix announced it had completed HBM4 development and prepared mass production: 2,048 I/O terminals, "over 10Gbps" per pin against JEDEC's 8 Gb/s, power efficiency improved by more than 40% ({{SK hynix newsroom|https://news.skhynix.com/sk-hynix-completes-worlds-first-hbm4-development-and-readies-mass-production/}}) vendor claim.
  • Jul 21, 2026: NVIDIA described Rubin with up to 288 GB of HBM4 in 12-high stacks at up to 22 TB/s; Rubin was announced as in full production on May 31, 2026, shipping from the fall ({{Rubin blog|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-rubin-gpu-architecture-powering-the-era-of-agentic-ai/}}, {{NVIDIA newsroom|https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/vera-rubin-full-production-agentic-ai-factory}}). NVIDIA's own pages disagree on Rubin's bandwidth: the Vera Rubin NVL72 page lists 19.2 TB/s per GPU; the parent's Chip atlas shows both.
  • AMD MI455X (production shipments from September 2026, ramping through the first half of 2027): 432 GB of HBM4 at 23.3 TB/s per GPU ({{AMD MI400|https://www.amd.com/en/products/accelerators/instinct/mi400.html}}), more capacity than Rubin's 288 GB; shipment date from Lisa Su at the OCP Global Summit, Oct 12 2026 ({{Converge Digest|https://convergedigest.com/amd-lisa-su-ocp-helios-shipping-verano-192-core/}}).
  • Sep 30, 2026: Micron's CEO Sanjay Mehrotra said supply will be tighter in 2027 and 2028 than in 2026, with demand above supply in both years: "We do not have line of sight to when supply and demand will return to balance." About 75% of Micron's 2027 output was already committed, much of next year's HBM sold out at "much higher" prices, and new clean-room capacity arrives from 2028 and ramps gradually ({{The Next Web, Oct 2 2026|https://thenextweb.com/news/micron-record-quarter-memory-shortage-2028}}) vendor statement.

Figures that circulate without a primary source (DRAM prices up about 500% in a year, Samsung doubling HBM4 output in 2027, "high-bandwidth flash" at Hot Chips 2026) are not stated as fact here; the supplier's own outlook above is the sourced version of the squeeze.

Common mistakes (4)
Reading "80 GB" as "an 80 GB model fits"
The weights share HBM with activations, the CUDA context, allocator fragmentation and the KV cache; vLLM by default claims only 92% of memory and still needs room for the cache. A model that exactly fills the memory serves a batch of one at a very short context.
Quoting peak bandwidth as achievable
Measured streams land below the datasheet: the M1 Pro reaches 127.5 to 165 GB/s of its 200 depending on the access pattern, and Chips and Cheese measured an H100 PCIe pulling "just short of 2 TB/s" of its 2 TB/s (the parent's Chip atlas). Scattered access falls much further (section 2).
Forgetting that the KV cache grows with batch × context
The cache is per sequence. Batch 64 at 32K tokens of Llama 3.1 8B is 64 × 4.29 = 275 GB of cache, 17 times the weights.
Mixing directions and units
Link bandwidths are often quoted for both directions together (PCIe Gen5 x16: 128 GB/s total, 64 each way; NVLink 5: 1.8 TB/s total); network links in bits (400 Gb/s = 50 GB/s); memory sizes in GB or GiB (TPU7x: 192 GiB = 206 GB). Convert before dividing.
Interview questions, short answers (6)
Why is HBM so much faster than GDDR, and why don't consumer GPUs use it?
HBM uses a 1,024-bit (HBM4: 2,048-bit) interface per stack over very short wires on a silicon interposer, so it gets bandwidth from width at a modest pin rate and low energy per bit. It needs stacked dies with TSVs and an interposer, which cost more and are supply-limited; GDDR gets bandwidth from fast pins on ordinary board wiring, cheaper but narrower and capped in capacity.
Estimate the KV cache of Llama 3.1 70B at 32K context.
2 × 80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 dims × 2 bytes = 320 KiB per token; × 32,768 = 10.7 GB per sequence. On an 8-GPU H100 node (640 GB) the 141 GB of weights leave about 499 GB: room for at most 46 such sequences, before activations and allocator overhead.
What is a row buffer hit, and why does it matter for a GPU kernel?
DRAM opens a whole row (1 KB in HBM2) into sense amplifiers before reading any column; further reads in the same row are fast and cheap, a different row needs precharge and activate. Contiguous, coalesced accesses hit open rows and use whole cache lines; scattered accesses waste both, which is why block sizes of a few KB (paged KV caches, embedding rows) read near full bandwidth and 16-byte gathers do not.
Why does a GPU need tens of thousands of threads to use its memory bandwidth?
Little's law: bytes in flight = bandwidth × latency. At about 467 ns of DRAM latency and 127.5 GB/s, the M1 Pro needs about 60 KB outstanding; an H100 at 3.35 TB/s and similar latency needs megabytes in flight. Each thread has only a few loads outstanding, so it takes many threads.
When is offloading the KV cache to CPU memory better than recomputing it?
Almost always for long contexts: reloading 10.7 GB over PCIe Gen5 takes about 0.17 s, recomputing a 32K-token prefill of a 70B model takes seconds on one H100. Offload loses only when the link is busy or the context is very short.
MLA vs GQA: what does each save and what does each cost?
GQA shares K/V heads among query heads (Llama 3.1: 8 KV heads), cutting the cache 4× against MHA at some quality cost. MLA caches a low-rank latent (512 + 64 values per layer in DeepSeek-V3, 68.6 KiB per token for a 671B model) and reconstructs keys and values from it, saving more but adding projections and complicating RoPE.

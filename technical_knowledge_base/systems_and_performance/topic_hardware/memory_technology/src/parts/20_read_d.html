3. SRAM and the cache ladder

The cell. SRAM (static RAM) stores a bit in a loop of transistors, typically six (a "6T" cell), that holds its value while power is on: no capacitor, no refresh, built on the processor die itself. Faster than DRAM, but far more silicon per bit ({{Wikipedia, SRAM|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_random-access_memory}}), so every megabyte of cache displaces arithmetic: the RTX 5090 carries a 96 MB L2 (RTX Blackwell whitepaper), the H100 50 MB. Every register, L1, shared memory and L2 on a GPU is SRAM.

How much an H100 has. 132 SMs, each with a 256 KB register file and 256 KB of combined L1 and shared memory, plus a 50 MB L2: 34.6 + 34.6 + 50 = 119 MB, 0.149% of its 80 GB of HBM derived (per-SM sizes: {{CUDA Programming Guide, compute capabilities|https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cuda-programming-guide/05-appendices/compute-capabilities.html}} and the {{Hopper architecture blog|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/}}). A matmul kernel's whole job is to keep a tile of each operand in that small memory and reuse it many times before going back to HBM ({{GPU memory hierarchy|n:3c65c17b0d0d814a8edef7bd21c303d3}} shows how).

The ladder, measured on this laptop's GPU, in three runs: all threads reading a buffer of a given size over and over (bytes per second), and one thread chasing random pointers through it, one 128-byte line per hop, so each read waits for the previous one (time per read).

Bandwidth falls and latency rises in steps as the data outgrows each cache

Up to 128 KB1,239 GB/s; about 61 ns per dependent read (up to 16 KB). The per-core 8 KB L1 and the GPU's caches.
256 KB to 2 MB579 GB/s at 1 MB; about 95 ns per read up to 1 MB. The GPU's 256 KB L2 and beyond.
4 MB to 32 MB318 GB/s at 32 MB; about 300 ns per read. The 24 MB system level cache, shared with the CPU.
128 MB and up127.5 GB/s; about 467 ns per read (433 to 571 across runs). LPDDR5 DRAM.

Cache sizes are independent measurements by {{Philip Turner's metal-benchmarks|https://github.com/philipturner/metal-benchmarks}} (L1 8 KB per core, L2 256 KB, SLC 24 MB for the M1 Pro); Apple publishes none. The steps sit near those sizes, not exactly on them (the latency plateau runs to 16 KB and to 1 MB, and random chasing also pays for address translation with 16 KB pages), so the labels are a reading of the curve, not a proof. measured here MLX 0.32.3, load average 6.1 to 7.5 (the laptop was shared).

Little's law ties the two curves together. Bytes in flight = bandwidth × latency. To pull 127.5 GB/s from DRAM when each read takes about 467 ns, the GPU must keep 127.5 × 467 = 60 KB of reads outstanding at all times derived, hundreds of cache lines. One thread gets one line per 467 ns, 0.27 GB/s; that is why GPUs run thousands of threads, and why an H100 at 3.35 TB/s and similar latency needs megabytes in flight. The {{CUDA root's GPU simulator|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c39f34d5e070d783c1}} measured the other side on the same GPU: 138 to 151 GB/s once 8,192 or more threads are in flight.

NVIDIA's ladder has the same shape. Luo et al. measured about 29 cycles for shared memory, 38 to 43 for L1, 262 to 273 for L2 and 466 to 542 for global memory on A100, H800 and RTX 4090 ({{Benchmarking and Dissecting the Nvidia Hopper GPU Architecture, Table IV|https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13499v1}}) independent measurement.

SRAM-only machines
Some accelerators keep the whole model in SRAM across many chips or one wafer: Cerebras WSE-3 has 44 GB of on-wafer SRAM at 21 PB/s; the announced Groq 3 LPX rack has 128 GB of SRAM across 256 chips at 40 PB/s. Huge bandwidth, small capacity per chip: Llama 3.1 8B's 16 GB would occupy about a third of a wafer or 33 of the 0.5 GB Groq chips derived. At the frontier the count is in wafers: Cerebras lays out Kimi K3 (about 3T parameters, 4-bit weights) across 59 of them (vendor, October 2026). Their bets are compared on {{TPUs and other accelerators|n:3c65c17b0d0d8151bbc2e0ceea196187}}.
How the laptop measurements were made, and what transfers to an NVIDIA GPU

Everything marked measured here ran on this laptop: the Apple M1 Pro's 16-core GPU through custom Metal kernels in MLX, and its internal SSD. Each GPU experiment ran 7 timed trials after 2 warm-ups, in three separate runs; lines are the median of the three run medians and bars their spread. Every kernel was checked against NumPy before it was timed. Bandwidth: 65,536 threads, coalesced 16-byte loads. Latency: one thread chasing a random pointer cycle, launch overhead removed by timing two chain lengths and taking the difference; measured up to 512 MB (a chain over 1 GB would not fit beside the bandwidth buffer).

TransfersThe shape: bandwidth falls in steps as the working set outgrows each cache; latency rises in steps; Little's law links them; scattered small reads waste most of the bandwidth; decode attention time follows cache bytes when the kernel is good. Luo et al. measured the same staircase in cycles on A100, H800 and RTX 4090.
Does not transferThe numbers. An H100 has 50 MB of L2 instead of 256 KB plus a 24 MB SLC, and 3.35 TB/s of HBM instead of 200 GB/s of LPDDR5. MLX's attention kernel is not FlashAttention or vLLM's; its MHA and MQA results describe this kernel, not the hardware's limit.
Not measuredNo GPU performance counters (Instruments) were read, so cache levels are inferred from the curves; HBM, GDDR and NVLink were not run here (no NVIDIA GPU was available).

3. Cooling: why dense racks need liquid

Every watt a rack draws becomes a watt of heat inside it; if it is not carried away as fast as it is made, chips throttle (section 1) and then shut down. How much coolant that takes is one line of physics:

Q = ṁ × cp × ΔT
Q
heat removed per second (watts), equal to the rack's power at steady state
ṁ
mass flow of coolant, kg per second
cp
specific heat: joules to warm 1 kg by 1 °C (air about 1,005, water about 4,186)
ΔT
how much warmer the coolant leaves than it arrived

Per litre, water carries about 3,464 times as much heat as air for the same temperature rise ({{standard properties near 20 °C|https://webbook.nist.gov/chemistry/fluid/}}: air 1.2 kg/m³, water 998 kg/m³) derived. That ratio is the whole argument. It checks on a real server: a DGX H100 at 10.2 kW with its rated 1,105 cubic feet per minute (CFM) of airflow at 80% fan speed ({{user guide|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx/dgxh100-user-guide/introduction-to-dgxh100.html}}) would warm that air by 16.2 °C derived, a sensible design rise. Predict the airflow a 120 kW rack needs, then try the presets.

Same heat, two coolants derived: air flow = Q / (1.2 × 1,005 × ΔT) m³/s; water flow = Q / (4,186 × ΔT) kg/s. At the defaults a 120 kW rack needs about 6.6 m³/s of air (14,000 CFM) or about 170 L/min of water. Real racks also hit limits this ignores: fan power grows steeply with airflow, and a rack's front face is only so large.

Three ways to carry the heat. Air: fans push room air through the servers, and the hall separates cold intake aisles from hot exhaust. Direct-to-chip liquid, the common choice for dense AI racks: in a GB200 NVL72, cold plates sit on the CPUs and GPUs of every compute tray and liquid runs through them, while "the rest of the components like networking and storage devices are air cooled" ({{DGX GB200 user guide|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx/dgxgb200-user-guide/hardware.html}}); the warm liquid goes to a coolant distribution unit that passes the heat to the building's water loop. Google moved its TPUs to liquid at v3 in 2018 ({{Jouppi et al. 2020|https://doi.org/10.1145/3360307}}), and Ironwood pods are liquid cooled. Immersion: whole servers sit in a dielectric fluid. In Microsoft's two-phase version the fluid is "engineered to boil at 122 degrees Fahrenheit" (50 °C), the vapour condenses on a cooled lid and rains back, and the company reported it "reduced power consumption for any given server by 5% to 15%" ({{Microsoft, Apr 6 2021|https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/innovation/datacenter-liquid-cooling/}}) vendor.

Water. Many sites reject heat by evaporating water in cooling towers, so cooling has a water cost too: Google counts 0.26 mL for the median Gemini Apps text prompt, data-centre water consumption included ({{Google Cloud, Aug 21 2025|https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference}}) published.

4. Power swings: tens of thousands of GPUs in lockstep

In synchronous data-parallel training every GPU computes its part of a step, then all wait for the gradient all-reduce, then all start the next step together. Computing draws close to full power; communicating draws close to idle. One GPU swinging by a few hundred watts is nothing; sixteen thousand at the same instant is a load the grid can see. Meta reported exactly that on Llama 3: "tens of thousands of GPUs may increase or decrease power consumption at the same time", for instance all waiting for a checkpoint or a collective, giving "instant fluctuations of power consumption across the data center on the order of tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid" ({{Llama 3 §3.3.4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}) published. The order of magnitude checks: 16,384 H100s at 700 W are 11.5 MW of GPU power alone, and 2,048 servers at a DGX H100's 10.2 kW maximum would be 20.9 MW derived.

A 57-author team led by Microsoft measured swings of "tens or hundreds of megawatts" within one data centre, concentrated around 0.2 to 3 Hz, a range that can resonate with parts of the grid's own equipment and damage it ({{Choukse et al., arXiv 2508.14318|https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14318}}, Aug 2025) published. Utilities therefore limit how fast a site may ramp, in megawatts per second. Step through six training steps, then switch on the fix and step again.

Only the 90% floor and the idea of ramp limits come from the paper; step times, idle level and ramp rate are chosen for teaching (recomputed in src/recompute.py).

Three fixes, each with a cost (from that paper and NVIDIA):

  • Software fill: run filler work (the paper's "Firefly" ran scaled matrix multiplications) when a GPU would otherwise idle. Measured overhead was under 5% of performance, and the energy is spent on nothing.
  • GPU power smoothing: GB200 GPUs accept programmable ramp-up and ramp-down rates and a "Minimum Power Floor" of up to 90% of TDP; at a 90% floor the paper measured "a total energy overhead of 10.5%" on real training. GB300 NVL72 adds a power cap that rises gradually at the start of a job and a "power burner" mode at the end ({{NVIDIA, Jul 28 2025|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/how-new-gb300-nvl72-features-provide-steady-power-for-ai/}}) vendor.
  • Energy storage: batteries or capacitors absorb the ripple. GB300 NVL72 power supplies hold "65 joules/GPU" in capacitors, and NVIDIA reports the grid's peak demand fell by 30% training a Megatron LLM vendor. 65 J covers 46 ms of a 1,400 W GPU derived: enough for the sub-second ripple, nowhere near a checkpoint pause, which is why the paper pairs rack-level storage with the GPU-level floor.

For you: a power floor or filler work means paying for energy that trains nothing, and a cluster under a site power cap may run its GPUs below rated clocks. Both show up as lower MFU for reasons no profiler of your code will find.

1. In one screen

An accelerator turns electricity into arithmetic and heat, nothing else. Every watt it draws has to be delivered through a chain of transformers and converters, and every watt comes back out as heat that has to be carried away. Multiply one chip by tens of thousands, all running the same step at the same moment, and three new problems appear that a single GPU never shows you: the site's power connection caps how many chips you can plug in, the cluster's power draw swings by megawatts in time with the training step, and something breaks every few hours, which stops the whole job.

Log scale: equal lengths are equal ratios, from a single GPU to a site a million times larger. Power decides how many chips

Chips draw 700 to 1,400 W each and keep rising. A gigawatt of facility power holds roughly half a million Blackwell GPUs (section 3).

Dense racks need liquid

A 120 kW rack would need about 14,000 cubic feet of air a minute; water carries the same heat in about 170 litres a minute (section 4).

Lockstep makes swings

Synchronous training draws near full power while computing and near idle while communicating, so the whole site swings by tens of megawatts each step (section 5).

Failures scale with chip count

Llama 3 405B stopped 419 times in 54 days on 16,384 GPUs, once every 3.1 hours. At the same rate 100,000 GPUs stop every 30 minutes (section 6).

Checkpoint at the square root

The best interval is about the square root of twice the checkpoint cost times the mean time between failures. Get it wrong and a day of training loses hours (section 7, {{Checkpoint simulator|#t-ckpt}}).

Some failures are silent

A defective core can return wrong numbers without any error. At Gemini's scale Google expected one such event every week or two (section 8).

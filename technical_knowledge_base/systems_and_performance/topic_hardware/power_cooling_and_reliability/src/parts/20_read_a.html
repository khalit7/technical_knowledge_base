The idea

Every watt a chip draws has to be delivered and then carried away as heat, and tens of thousands of chips running one job in lockstep multiply all of it: the power a site must connect, the swing of its load every step, and how often some part breaks and stops the job.

Middle row: every one of those watts leaves as heat, through a cold plate on the chip, a coolant distribution unit and the building's water loop. Bottom row: Llama 3's 16,384 GPUs swung the site by tens of megawatts and stopped once every 3.1 hours published. TDP, TBP
thermal design power, total board power: the sustained watts a chip is allowed and its cooling must remove
DVFS
dynamic voltage and frequency scaling: the chip lowers clock and voltage when it nears its power or temperature limit
PUE
power usage effectiveness: facility power divided by the power reaching IT equipment
CDU
coolant distribution unit: passes heat from the rack's liquid loop to the building's water
MTBF
mean time between failures, here of a whole job
SDC
silent data corruption: a wrong result that raises no error
Power decides how many chips

Chips draw 700 to 1,400 W each and keep rising. A gigawatt of facility power holds roughly half a million Blackwell GPUs (section 2).

Dense racks need liquid

A 120 kW rack would need about 14,000 cubic feet of air a minute; water carries the same heat in about 170 litres a minute (section 3).

Lockstep makes swings

Compute near full power, all-reduce near idle: the whole site swings by tens of megawatts each step (section 4).

Failures scale with chip count

Llama 3 405B stopped 419 times in 54 days on 16,384 GPUs; at the same rate 100,000 GPUs stop every 30 minutes (section 5).

Checkpoint at the square root

The best interval is about √(2 × checkpoint cost × MTBF); get it wrong and a day of training loses hours (section 6).

Some failures are silent

A defective core can return wrong numbers with no error. At Gemini's scale Google expected one such event every week or two (section 7).

1. Watts per chip rise; energy per FLOP falls

What a watt does inside a chip. Each transistor switch charges or drains a tiny capacitance, so switching power is roughly activity × capacitance × voltage² × frequency, plus leakage that flows even when nothing switches ({{processor power dissipation|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processor_power_dissipation}}). Higher clocks need higher voltage, so the last few hundred megahertz cost far more power than the first. Moving data costs more than computing on it: reading 32 bits from DRAM costs about 640 pJ against 3.7 pJ for a float multiply in Horowitz's 45 nm figures ({{Han et al. 2015, Fig. 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02626}}), which is why {{Memory technology|n:3f05c17b0d0d818ab063e327e3d7009f}} spends a section on energy per bit.

The ceiling. NVIDIA quotes "up to 700 W" for H100 SXM ({{H100|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h100/}}); AMD quotes total board power, 750 W for MI300X ({{data sheet|https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/instinct-tech-docs/data-sheets/amd-instinct-mi300x-data-sheet.pdf}}). Firmware enforces it continuously with DVFS, and you can set a lower cap yourself (nvidia-smi -pl <watts>). Meta saw DVFS in a real run: Llama 3 405B's throughput varied by 1 to 2% with the time of day, because higher mid-day temperatures changed the GPUs' clocks ({{Llama 3 §3.3.4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}) published.

Each generation draws more power and does much more work per joule. Toggle the chart: watts climb from 280 W to 2,500 W while picojoules per dense BF16 FLOP at peak (a ceiling, not what a run achieves) fall more than tenfold.

pJ/FLOP derived = max power (W) / dense BF16 peak (FLOP/s) × 1012. Predict. A B300 (Blackwell Ultra) is allowed 1,400 W, twice an H100's 700 W. Per dense BF16 FLOP at peak, does it use more or less energy than an H100?
0.56 pJ against 0.71 pJ, about 21% less derived. Its headline format is FP4, 15 PF dense per GPU: 0.093 pJ per FP4 operation. Watts per chip rise because the chip does far more per second; what rises with them is heat per square centimetre, which forces liquid cooling (section 3).

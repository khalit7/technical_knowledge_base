6. What fails, how often, and how the hardware tells you

The problem. Synchronous training needs every GPU in the job: if one dies, the collective it was part of never completes and the whole job stops. So the job fails whenever any part fails, and with N parts that is N times as often as one part.

The best public record is Meta's Llama 3 405B pre-training: in a 54-day snapshot on up to 16,384 H100s there were 466 interruptions, 47 planned and 419 unexpected, about 78% of them confirmed or suspected hardware; only three needed significant manual intervention, and the run kept more than 90% effective training time ({{Llama 3 §3.3.4, Table 5|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}) published. Table 5 prints "Faulty GPU: 148, 30.1%", but 148 of 419 is 35.3%; every other row's percentage matches its count. By count, the GPU rows add to 268 of 419, 64.0% derived, not the 58.7% the paper's text gives (the sum of the printed percentages). The parent page's section 8 repeats the printed 30.1%.

Read it as a hardware engineer. The GPU itself (die, HBM, on-chip SRAM, its management processor, thermal interface and sensors) caused 64% of stops. Memory on the GPU alone, HBM3 plus SRAM, caused 91, 21.7% derived. Yet each GPU is very reliable: 72 HBM interruptions over 16,384 GPUs for 54 days is one per 33.6 GPU-years derived. Nothing here is a bad part; it is the multiplication.

MTBFjob = 1 / (Nservers × r)
r
failures per server per day of running (one server = 8 GPUs)
Nservers
servers in the job; MTBF is the mean time between failures

Llama 3's 419 in 54 days on 2,048 servers is 3.79 failures per thousand server-days derived: one 8-GPU server fails about once every 264 days, one GPU-share about every 5.8 years, yet the 16,384-GPU job stops every 3.1 hours, and at the same rate a 100,000-GPU job would stop every 30 minutes. Meta's research clusters measured 6.50 and 2.34 per thousand node-days, which project to 1.8 hours for 16,384 GPUs and 0.23 hours for 131,072 ({{Kokolis et al., Feb 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680}}; reproduced here) published. Google's Gemini report says the same in words: scaling up the number of accelerators "results in a proportionate decrease in the mean time between failure" ({{Gemini report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11805}}). The {{Training Infrastructure|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c9b2cac997583bf572}} page has this law with more clusters (OPT, MegaScale, ByteRobust) and its Goodput calculator.

How the hardware reports a fault

  • ECC on memory. HBM and on-chip SRAM store extra check bits with every word: a single flipped bit is corrected on the fly, two flipped bits are detected and reported as uncorrectable. Correctable errors are logged and counted; an uncorrectable one in data the job is using kills the process.
  • Row remapping. From the Ampere generation on, "every bank in DRAM is equipped with spare rows"; a row that keeps producing errors is remapped to a spare, which "requires a GPU reset to take effect and will remain persistent throughout the life of the GPU" ({{NVIDIA memory error management|https://docs.nvidia.com/deploy/a100-gpu-mem-error-mgmt/row-remapping.html}}) vendor. When a bank runs out of spares, the GPU is due for replacement.
  • Xid errors. The NVIDIA driver writes numbered "Xid" messages to the kernel log for GPU faults (memory errors, a GPU that stops answering on PCIe, hangs); NVIDIA keeps a catalogue of what each number means and what to do ({{Xid errors|https://docs.nvidia.com/deploy/xid-errors/index.html}}, updated Sep 2026). Cluster health checks watch these.
  • Lemon nodes. Some servers fail again and again. Meta's research clusters traced their repeat-offender ("lemon") nodes to GPUs in 28.2% of cases and PCIe errors in 15.4%; the leading failure sources overall were InfiniBand links, filesystem mounts, GPU memory errors and PCIe errors ({{Kokolis et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680}}). Finding and removing them is a large part of cluster reliability work.

7. Checkpoint math from zero: Young, Daly and when they break

The problem. A checkpoint saves the model and optimizer state so a failed job can restart from it instead of from the beginning. Checkpointing often wastes time writing; checkpointing rarely wastes the work done since the last save each time something fails. There is a best interval in between, and it follows from four numbers.

waste = C/T + (1 − C/T) × (D + R + T/2) / μ
T
the period: T − C of training, then a checkpoint
C
time training is paused per checkpoint
D, R
downtime after a failure (detect, replace) and recovery (restart, reload the checkpoint)
μ
MTBF of the whole job, from section 6

The first term is the time spent checkpointing. The second is the failures: on average one every μ hours, each costing the downtime, the recovery, and on average half a period of lost work, because a failure is equally likely anywhere in the period. Minimising over T gives the classic answers ({{Aupy et al. 2013, eq. 12 and 13|https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.3752}}):

TYoung = √(2 μ C) + C
TDaly = √(2 (μ + D + R) C) + C
Young
1974, the first-order optimum ({{Young 1974|https://doi.org/10.1145/361147.361115}})
Daly
2006, adds recovery time ({{Daly 2006|https://doi.org/10.1016/j.future.2004.11.016}}); Aupy et al.'s refined version is √(2(μ − D − R)C)

The square root is the intuition: double the failure rate and the best interval shrinks only by √2. For Llama 3's rate (μ = 3.09 h) and a 5-minute synchronous checkpoint, Young gives 48.1 minutes; with 10 minutes of downtime and 5 of recovery the exact optimum is 44.8 minutes and the job keeps 72.5% of its time derived. A 3-hour interval on the same run keeps 53.2%.

The exact answer. If failures arrive at random (a Poisson process, the standard model), the expected time is known exactly: Timefinal = (μ + D) eR/μ (eT/μ − 1) Timebase / (T − C) (quoted by Aupy et al. from earlier work). This page's simulator uses it and checks it two ways: it reproduces all forty periods in Aupy et al.'s Table 2 to within 0.12% (independently), and a Monte Carlo of 2,000 MTBFs per run lands within 0.7 percentage points of it at every interval tried (src/recompute.py).

Predict. At 131,072 GPUs (Llama 3's failure rate, μ = 23 minutes) with a 5-minute checkpoint, the first-order waste formula predicts a negative useful fraction. Is Young's interval still a good choice?
Yes. The exact formula gives 26.6% useful time at Young's interval against 27.0% at the true optimum; the first-order formula's −6.2% is the formula breaking, not the job derived. The interval rule is robust; the efficiency estimate is not, once μ is only a few times C + D + R. The fix at that scale is not a cleverer interval but a smaller C, D and R: in the table below, a 10-second pause roughly doubles the useful fraction.

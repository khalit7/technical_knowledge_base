5. What fails, how often, and how the hardware tells you

Synchronous training needs every GPU in the job: if one dies, the collective it was part of never completes and the whole job stops. So the job fails whenever any part fails, and with N parts that is N times as often as one part.

MTBFjob = 1 / (Nservers × r)
r
failures per server per day of running (one server = 8 GPUs)
Nservers
servers in the job

Double the GPUs, halve the time between stops

Job MTBF against GPUs, both axes log derived, at three published failure rates. Llama 3's 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days on 2,048 servers is 3.79 failures per thousand server-days: one 8-GPU server fails about once every 264 days, one GPU-share about every 5.8 years, yet the 16,384-GPU job stops once every 3.1 hours, and at the same rate 100,000 GPUs stop every 30 minutes. Meta's research clusters measured 6.50 and 2.34 per thousand node-days, which project to 1.8 hours for 16,384 GPUs and 0.23 hours for 131,072 ({{Kokolis et al., Feb 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680}}; reproduced here) published. The dashed line is 20 minutes, a 5-minute checkpoint plus 15 minutes to recover: where MTBF approaches it, section 6's first-order formula breaks. Google's Gemini report says the same in words: more accelerators "results in a proportionate decrease in the mean time between failure" ({{Gemini report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11805}}).

What actually fails. Meta's Llama 3 405B pre-training is the best public record: in a 54-day snapshot on up to 16,384 H100s there were 466 interruptions, 47 planned and 419 unexpected, about 78% of them confirmed or suspected hardware; only three needed significant manual intervention, and the run kept more than 90% effective training time ({{Llama 3 §3.3.4, Table 5|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}) published. Describing Prometheus, its GB200 and GB300 cluster (section 2), Meta reports "more than 85% goodput for pre-training runs" of Muse Wonder ({{Meta engineering, Oct 19 2026|https://engineering.fb.com/2026/10/19/data-center-engineering/inside-prometheus-meta-gigawatt-scale-supercluster/}}) vendor; goodput, the share of time that produces kept progress, is defined on {{Fault tolerance at scale|n:3fc5c17b0d0d815786d1c1dddb311a9b}}. The GPU itself (die, HBM, on-chip SRAM, its management processor, thermal interface and sensors) caused 64.0% of stops, 268 of 419 by count derived. Correction: Table 5's "Faulty GPU" percentage
Table 5 prints "Faulty GPU: 148, 30.1%", but 148 of 419 is 35.3%; every other row's percentage matches its count. By count the GPU rows add to 268 of 419, 64.0%, not the 58.7% the paper's text gives (the sum of the printed percentages). The parent page's section 8 quotes 35.3% and links here.

How the hardware reports a fault, from a single flipped bit to a replaced GPU:

  • ECC on memory. HBM and on-chip SRAM store extra check bits with every word: a single flipped bit is corrected on the fly and counted; two flipped bits are detected and reported as uncorrectable, and if the job was using that data the process dies.
  • Row remapping. From Ampere on, "every bank in DRAM is equipped with spare rows"; a row that keeps producing errors is remapped to a spare, which "requires a GPU reset to take effect and will remain persistent throughout the life of the GPU" ({{NVIDIA memory error management|https://docs.nvidia.com/deploy/a100-gpu-mem-error-mgmt/row-remapping.html}}) vendor. When a bank runs out of spares, the GPU is due for replacement.
  • Xid errors. The NVIDIA driver writes numbered "Xid" messages to the kernel log for GPU faults (memory errors, a GPU that stops answering on PCIe, hangs); NVIDIA keeps a catalogue of what each number means and what to do ({{Xid errors|https://docs.nvidia.com/deploy/xid-errors/index.html}}, updated Sep 2026). Cluster health checks watch these.
  • Lemon nodes. Some servers fail again and again. Meta's research clusters traced repeat-offender ("lemon") nodes to GPUs in 28.2% of cases and PCIe errors in 15.4%; the leading failure sources overall were InfiniBand links, filesystem mounts, GPU memory errors and PCIe errors ({{Kokolis et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21680}}). Finding and removing them is a large part of cluster reliability work.

6. The checkpoint interval: Young, Daly and when they break

A checkpoint saves model and optimiser state so a failed job restarts from it. Checkpointing often wastes time writing; checkpointing rarely wastes the work done since the last save at every failure. The best interval in between follows from four numbers:

waste = C/T + (1 − C/T) × (D + R + T/2) / μ
TYoung = √(2 μ C) + C    TDaly = √(2 (μ + D + R) C) + C
T
the period: T − C of training, then a checkpoint
C
time training is paused per checkpoint
D, R
downtime after a failure (detect, replace) and recovery (restart, reload)
μ
MTBF of the whole job, from section 5

The first term is time spent checkpointing; the second is failures, one every μ hours, each costing the downtime, the recovery and on average half a period of lost work, because a failure is equally likely anywhere in the period. Minimising over T gives Young's interval ({{Young 1974|https://doi.org/10.1145/361147.361115}}) and Daly's, which adds recovery ({{Daly 2006|https://doi.org/10.1016/j.future.2004.11.016}}; both as eq. 12 and 13 of {{Aupy et al. 2013|https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.3752}}). The square root is the intuition: double the failure rate and the best interval shrinks only by √2. For Llama 3's rate (μ = 3.09 h) and a 5-minute synchronous checkpoint, Young gives 48.1 minutes; with 10 minutes of downtime and 5 of recovery the exact optimum is 44.8 minutes and the job keeps 72.5% of its time derived. A 3-hour interval on the same run keeps 53.2%.

The exact answer. If failures arrive at random (a Poisson process), the expected time is known exactly: Timefinal = (μ + D) eR/μ (eT/μ − 1) Timebase / (T − C) (quoted by Aupy et al. from earlier work). The curve above uses it; the first-order line falls below it as the interval grows and can go negative at large scale.

Predict. At 131,072 GPUs (Llama 3's failure rate, μ = 23 minutes) with a 5-minute checkpoint, the first-order waste formula predicts a negative useful fraction. Is Young's interval still a good choice?
Yes. The exact formula gives 26.6% useful time at Young's interval against 27.0% at the true optimum; the first-order formula's −6.2% is the formula breaking, not the job derived. The interval rule is robust; the efficiency estimate is not, once μ is only a few times C + D + R. The fix at that scale is a smaller C, D and R: in the table below, a 10-second pause roughly doubles the useful fraction.

What reproduces what, and the simulator's inputs
  • The model is Aupy et al. 2013, section 3: a period is T − C of work plus a checkpoint C; a failure loses all work since the last completed checkpoint, then downtime D (no failures counted) and recovery R (failures can strike). Aupy et al.'s refined first-order period is √(2(μ − D − R)C).
  • Aupy et al.'s Table 2 (C = R = 600 s, D = 60 s, ten platform sizes): Young, Daly, refined first-order and exact-optimum periods, all forty reproduced within 0.12% independently (src/recompute.py; the largest gaps are on the two smallest platforms, where the paper fixed a two-hour job).
  • A Monte Carlo of 8 runs of 2,000 MTBFs at nine intervals lands within 0.7 percentage points of the exact formula; the curve's live Monte Carlo runs the same engine as the day animation.
  • Kokolis et al.'s projected 1.8 h and 0.23 h job MTBF at 6.50 per 1,000 node-days for 16,384 and 131,072 GPUs: the presets give them by construction (same formula). Llama 3's rate, 3.79 per 1,000 server-days, is 419 failures / (54 days × 2,048 servers).

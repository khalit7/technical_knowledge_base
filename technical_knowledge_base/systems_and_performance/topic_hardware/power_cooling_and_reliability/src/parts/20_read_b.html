2. Watts per chip: where the power goes, and why it keeps rising

The problem. Every number you see on a GPU spec sheet, FLOPS and bandwidth alike, is bought with power, and the power has a ceiling set by how much heat the package can shed. To read hardware you need to know what that ceiling is called, what fills it, and what happens when you hit it.

What a watt does inside a chip. A chip is billions of transistors switching on and off. Each switch charges or drains a tiny capacitance, so the switching ("dynamic") power is roughly activity × capacitance × voltage² × frequency, plus a leakage term that flows even when nothing switches ({{processor power dissipation|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Processor_power_dissipation}}, the textbook model). Two things follow. Higher clocks need higher voltage, so the last few hundred megahertz cost far more power than the first. And moving data costs more than computing on it: reading 32 bits from DRAM costs about 640 pJ against 3.7 pJ for a float multiply in Horowitz's 45 nm figures ({{Han et al. 2015, Fig. 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02626}}), which is why {{Memory technology|n:3f05c17b0d0d818ab063e327e3d7009f}} spends a section on energy per bit.

TDP, TBP and "max power". Thermal design power (TDP) is the sustained heat the cooling system must be able to remove; NVIDIA quotes "up to 700 W" for H100 SXM ({{H100|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h100/}}), AMD quotes total board power (TBP), 750 W for MI300X ({{data sheet|https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/instinct-tech-docs/data-sheets/amd-instinct-mi300x-data-sheet.pdf}}). The chip's firmware enforces the limit continuously: when the board approaches its power or temperature limit, it lowers clock and voltage (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, DVFS). You can set a lower cap yourself (nvidia-smi -pl <watts>). Meta saw DVFS in a real run: Llama 3 405B's throughput varied by 1 to 2% with the time of day, because higher mid-day temperatures changed the GPUs' clocks ({{Llama 3 §3.3.4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS3.SSS4}}) published.

Watts per chip rise; energy per FLOP falls

Each generation draws more power and does much more work per joule. The chart divides each chip's maximum power by its dense BF16 peak: picojoules per FLOP at peak, a ceiling, not what a real run achieves.

pJ/FLOP derived = max power (W) / dense BF16 peak (FLOP/s) × 1012. Power and peaks: A100, H100 (NVIDIA product pages), Blackwell 1,200 W and Blackwell Ultra 1,400 W ({{NVIDIA, Aug 22 2025|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-blackwell-ultra-the-chip-powering-the-ai-factory-era/}}, with the GB200/GB300 per-GPU dense BF16 of 2,500 TFLOPS from the {{GB200 NVL72|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/gb200-nvl72/}} page), MI300X and MI355X (AMD data sheets), MI455X announced 2,500 W max TBP and 5 PF BF16 (AMD's page does not say dense), TPU v2 and v3 TDP and TPU v4 measured maximum ({{Jouppi et al. 2020|https://doi.org/10.1145/3360307}}, {{TPU v4 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01433}}). Every figure is in the {{Chip atlas|n:3c65c17b0d0d8118beeefaed56da6f8e}} tab of the parent page.

Predict. A B300 (Blackwell Ultra) is allowed 1,400 W, twice an H100's 700 W. Per dense BF16 FLOP at peak, does it use more or less energy than an H100?
0.56 pJ against 0.71 pJ, about 21% less derived. And Blackwell Ultra's headline format is FP4, 15 PF dense per GPU, which is 0.093 pJ per FP4 operation. Watts per chip rise because the chip does far more per second, not because it got less efficient; what rises with them is the heat per square centimetre, which is what forces liquid cooling (section 4).

3. From rack to site: power delivery, PUE and the per-gigawatt view

Server. An 8-GPU DGX H100 is rated 10.2 kW maximum, fed by six 3.3 kW power supplies in 4+2 redundancy (four carry the load, two are spares) ({{DGX H100 user guide|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx/dgxh100-user-guide/introduction-to-dgxh100.html}}) vendor. Eight GPUs at 700 W are 5.6 kW, about 55% of it derived; the rest is two CPUs, memory, NVSwitches, network cards, storage and fans. So "GPU watts" understate what a GPU costs a data centre by nearly a factor of two. A DGX B200 is about 14.3 kW ({{DGX B200|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/dgx-b200/}}).

Rack. A GB200 NVL72 rack (72 GPUs, 36 Grace CPUs) draws "approximately 120 kW"; eight power shelves, each with six 5.5 kW supplies and 33 kW of input, give N+N redundancy ({{DGX GB200 user guide|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx/dgxgb200-user-guide/hardware.html}}) vendor. Four DGX H100s in one rack would already be 40.8 kW derived.

Delivery. Power arrives from the grid at high voltage, is stepped down by transformers at the site's substation, passes switchgear and uninterruptible power supplies (UPS: batteries that bridge short outages), reaches the rack, where power shelves convert it to 54 V DC on a copper busbar, and is converted again on each board down to the low voltage the silicon runs at. Every conversion loses a few percent as heat. Current is power divided by voltage, so at 54 V a 1 MW rack would need 18,519 A derived: NVIDIA writes that this "requires up to 200 kg of copper busbar" per rack, and plans 800 V DC distribution (1,250 A for the same megawatt) with "up to 5% improvement in end-to-end power efficiency", "starting in 2027" with its Kyber racks ({{NVIDIA, May 20 2025|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-800-v-hvdc-architecture-will-power-the-next-generation-of-ai-factories/}}) vendor. The same post puts today's 54 V racks at up to 200 kW.

PUE = total facility power / IT equipment power
PUE
power usage effectiveness: 1.0 would mean every watt reaches a server; the excess is cooling, power conversion, lighting

Google's fleet ran at a trailing-twelve-month PUE of 1.09 in 2025; the Uptime Institute's 2025 survey average was 1.54 ({{Google|https://datacenters.google/efficiency/}}) published. The difference matters at scale: at 1.54 a site spends 54 watts on overhead for every 100 that compute, at 1.09 only 9.

The per-gigawatt view

Sites are now planned in megawatts and gigawatts of grid connection, so the question "how many accelerators?" becomes a division:

accelerators = site power / (kW per accelerator × PUE)
kW per accelerator
a system's power divided by the accelerators in it (servers, CPUs and fans included)

At PUE 1.2 (the parent page's assumption) derived; system powers vendor as above, the Ironwood pod "spanning nearly 10 MW" for 9,216 chips ({{Google, Apr 2025|https://blog.google/products/google-cloud/ironwood-tpu-age-of-inference/}}; whether that figure includes cooling is not stated unconfirmed). The DGX B200 row reproduces the parent page's "about 466,000" by construction. Systems at full rated power: real sites provision below nameplate and add switches and storage, so this is an order of magnitude, not a plan. Change every input in the {{Gigawatt planner|#t-gw}}.

Power is cheap per hour and scarce per site. A DGX H100's share of 10.2 kW per GPU at PUE 1.2 is 1.53 kW; at the US industrial average of 9.77 cents per kWh (July 2026, {{EIA|https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_6_a}}) that is about $0.15 per GPU-hour, 3.7% of Lambda's on-demand $3.99 for an H100 derived. A gigawatt for a year is 8.77 TWh, about $0.86 billion at that price. Electricity is a small line in the cost of a GPU-hour; the binding constraint is how many megawatts a site can get connected, which is why recent hardware comparisons are quoted in tokens per megawatt (section 8 of the parent page).

2. From rack to site: power delivery, PUE and the per-gigawatt view

Server. An 8-GPU DGX H100 is rated 10.2 kW maximum, fed by six 3.3 kW power supplies in 4+2 redundancy ({{DGX H100 user guide|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx/dgxh100-user-guide/introduction-to-dgxh100.html}}) vendor. Eight GPUs at 700 W are 5.6 kW, about 55% of it derived; the rest is two CPUs, memory, NVSwitches, network cards, storage and fans. So "GPU watts" understate what a GPU costs a data centre by nearly a factor of two. A DGX B200 is about 14.3 kW ({{DGX B200|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/dgx-b200/}}).

Rack. A GB200 NVL72 rack (72 GPUs, 36 Grace CPUs) draws "approximately 120 kW"; eight power shelves, each with six 5.5 kW supplies and 33 kW of input, give N+N redundancy ({{DGX GB200 user guide|https://docs.nvidia.com/dgx/dgxgb200-user-guide/hardware.html}}) vendor. Four DGX H100s in one rack would already be 40.8 kW derived.

Delivery (the hero's top row). Grid power at high voltage is stepped down at the site's substation, passes switchgear and uninterruptible power supplies (UPS: batteries that bridge short outages), reaches the rack's power shelves, which convert it to 54 V DC on a copper busbar, and is converted again on each board to the silicon's voltage. Every conversion loses a few percent as heat.

Why 54 V runs out. Current is power divided by voltage, so a 1 MW rack at 54 V would need 18,519 A derived: NVIDIA writes that this "requires up to 200 kg of copper busbar" per rack, and plans 800 V DC distribution (1,250 A for the same megawatt) with "up to 5% improvement in end-to-end power efficiency", "starting in 2027" with its Kyber racks; it puts today's 54 V racks at up to 200 kW ({{NVIDIA, May 20 2025|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-800-v-hvdc-architecture-will-power-the-next-generation-of-ai-factories/}}) vendor.

PUE = total facility power / IT equipment power
accelerators = site power / (kW per accelerator × PUE)
PUE
1.0 would mean every watt reaches a server; the excess is cooling, power conversion, lighting
kW per accelerator
a system's power divided by the accelerators in it (servers, CPUs and fans included)

Google's fleet ran at a trailing-twelve-month PUE of 1.09 in 2025; the Uptime Institute's 2025 survey average was 1.54 ({{Google|https://datacenters.google/efficiency/}}) published: 54 watts of overhead for every 100 that compute, against 9. Sites are now planned in megawatts of grid connection, so "how many accelerators?" is the division above. Predict how many B200s a gigawatt holds at PUE 1.2, then move the site power and PUE.

Systems at the vendor's maximum vendor: DGX H100 10.2 kW, DGX B200 about 14.3 kW, DGX B300 about 14 kW, GB200 NVL72 about 120 kW, Ironwood pod "spanning nearly 10 MW" for 9,216 chips ({{Google, Apr 2025|https://blog.google/products/google-cloud/ironwood-tpu-age-of-inference/}}; whether that includes cooling is not stated unconfirmed). Peaks are dense BF16 per accelerator (H100 989.5, HGX B200 and B300 2,250, GB200 2,500, TPU7x 2,307 TFLOPS). Rents on demand, fetched 2026-10-05: Lambda H100 $3.99 and B200 $6.69 per GPU-hour, Google Cloud Ironwood $12.00 per chip-hour. Average draw changes only energy and cost: the site is still sized for the maximum. Site power slider is log scale, 1 MW to 5 GW. Everything else derived; the DGX B200 row reproduces the parent page's "about 466,000" per gigawatt by construction. Real sites provision below nameplate and add switches and storage: an order of magnitude, not a plan.

Power is cheap per hour and scarce per site. A DGX H100's share per GPU at PUE 1.2 is 1.53 kW; at 9.77 cents per kWh that is about $0.15 per GPU-hour, 3.7% of Lambda's on-demand $3.99 for an H100 derived. A gigawatt for a year is 8.77 TWh, about $0.86 billion at that price. Electricity is a small line in a GPU-hour; the binding constraint is how many megawatts a site can get connected, which is why recent hardware comparisons are quoted in tokens per megawatt (section 8 of the parent page).

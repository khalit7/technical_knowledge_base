8. Silent data corruption: the failure that raises no error

The problem. Everything in section 6 announces itself: a crash, an Xid, a timeout. A silent data corruption (SDC) is a wrong answer with no error. ECC protects bits sitting in memory, not the arithmetic units that compute on them, so a marginal transistor in a multiplier can return a wrong product and nothing notices.

  • It is real and systemic. Meta's fleet testing found "hundreds of CPUs" with SDCs across hundreds of thousands of machines over more than 18 months, and notes SDCs "are not captured by error reporting mechanisms" ({{Dixit et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.11245}}) published. Google observed "on the order of a few mercurial cores per several thousand machines", with frequency, voltage, temperature and age all affecting whether a defective core misbehaves ({{Hochschild et al., HotOS 2021|https://sigops.org/s/conferences/hotos/2021/papers/hotos21-s01-hochschild.pdf}}) published. Both studies are of CPUs; the mechanisms (manufacturing defects that show up only under some conditions) apply to any chip.
  • At training scale it is a weekly event. For Gemini, Google wrote that "we can expect SDC events to impact training every week or two", and found the faulty hardware with "deterministic replay to isolate incorrect computations, combined with proactive SDC scanners on idle machines and hot standbys" ({{Gemini report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11805}}) published. Llama 3's Table 5 attributes 6 of its 419 interruptions to SDC: the ones that were caught.

What it does to a run. In data parallelism a corrupted gradient on one GPU is averaged into everyone's all-reduce, so every replica applies the same wrong update: the loss may spike, turn into not-a-number values, or drift without any visible sign. Two detection ideas follow from how training works (this page's reasoning, not from a single source): replicas that should hold identical weights can compare checksums after a step, which catches corruption that happens after the all-reduce on one replica; and a step can be replayed deterministically on other hardware and compared, which catches corruption before it, the method Google describes. Treat an unexplained loss spike as a possible hardware event, not only as an optimiser problem.

9. Energy per token

Everything above reduces to one number a reader can compare across systems: joules per token.

J per token = system power (W) / tokens per second

Training, worked from published figures. Llama 3.1 405B used 30.84 million H100 GPU-hours ({{model card|https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}}) on 15.6 trillion tokens ({{Llama 3 paper|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}). At the 700 W board limit that is at most 21.6 GWh of GPU energy and 4.98 J per training token derived. From first principles: one token costs 6 × 405 × 109 = 2.43 × 1015 FLOPs; at an H100's 0.71 pJ per BF16 FLOP that is 1.72 J at 100% of peak and 4.19 J at the 41% MFU Meta reports. The GPU-hour figure sits about a fifth above the MFU figure, as it should: it includes restarts, evaluation and slower phases. Add the rest of the server (a DGX H100 is 1.82 times its GPUs' power) and a PUE of 1.2, and the facility spends roughly 10.9 J per training token derived, an upper-bound estimate because GPUs rarely sit at their limit.

Inference. Google measured the median Gemini Apps text prompt at 0.24 Wh (864 J), counting idle provisioned machines, host CPU and memory, and data-centre overhead; counting only the active accelerators gives 0.10 Wh; and the median prompt's energy fell 33 times over twelve months ({{Google Cloud, Aug 21 2025|https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference}}) published. Two lessons: the boundary you draw changes the answer by more than two times, and software (batching, quantisation, better kernels) moves energy per token faster than hardware does. 10. Common mistakes and interview questions

Sizing a site from GPU TDP
A DGX H100 draws 10.2 kW for 5.6 kW of GPUs, and the facility adds its PUE on top: 700 W of GPU becomes about 1.5 kW at the meter at PUE 1.2.
Treating TDP as average draw
TDP is the ceiling the cooling must handle. Average draw depends on the workload (communication phases run near idle, section 5) and on power caps.
Quoting MFU between failures as the run's throughput
Multiply by the useful fraction of wall-clock time. At Llama 3's failure rate with a 5-minute checkpoint and 15 minutes to recover, that fraction is about 73%; Meta reached over 90% by making all of these faster.
Keeping the checkpoint interval when the job grows
The best interval scales with the square root of MTBF, and MTBF falls with the number of GPUs. Eight times the GPUs means checkpointing about 2.8 times as often.
Trusting the first-order efficiency formula at very large scale
Once MTBF is only a few times the checkpoint and recovery time, the first-order waste formula goes badly wrong (even negative). Young's interval stays near optimal; use the exact formula (section 7) for the efficiency.
Assuming every loss spike is the optimiser
Silent data corruption produces loss spikes and not-a-number values too. Large runs replay suspicious steps on other hardware.

Interview questions

How many H100s can a 100 MW site hold?
At a DGX H100's 10.2 kW per 8 GPUs and PUE 1.2: 100,000 kW / (1.275 kW × 1.2) = about 65,000 GPUs. Say what you assumed: server maximum power, no network or storage, the PUE.
Why is a GB200 NVL72 rack liquid cooled?
About 120 kW in one rack. Air at a 15 °C rise would need about 6.6 m³/s (14,000 CFM) through it; water at a 10 °C rise needs about 170 L/min, because water holds about 3,500 times more heat per litre.
Derive the optimal checkpoint interval.
Waste per unit time is C/T for checkpointing plus (T/2 + D + R)/μ for failures. Differentiate in T: −C/T² + 1/(2μ) = 0, so T = √(2μC) (Young adds C back for the checkpoint itself).
A 32,768-GPU job fails at Llama 3's rate and a checkpoint costs 2 minutes. How often should it checkpoint?
MTBF halves from 185.6 to 92.8 minutes. Young: √(2 × 92.8 × 2) + 2 = about 21.3 minutes.
Why does synchronous training stress the power grid, and what are the fixes?
All GPUs compute and communicate in lockstep, so the site swings between near full and near idle power every step, by tens of megawatts. Fixes: filler work during idle phases, GPU power floors and ramp limits (about 10% energy overhead measured at a 90% floor), energy storage at the rack.
What is silent data corruption, and how would you look for it in a training run?
A wrong computation with no error raised. Compare checksums of weights across replicas that should be identical, replay suspicious steps deterministically on other hardware, and scan idle machines with known-answer tests.

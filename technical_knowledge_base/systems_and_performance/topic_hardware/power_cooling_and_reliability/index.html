1. In one screen

An accelerator turns electricity into arithmetic and heat, nothing else. Every watt it draws has to be delivered through a chain of transformers and converters, and every watt comes back out as heat that has to be carried away. Multiply one chip by tens of thousands, all running the same step at the same moment, and three new problems appear that a single GPU never shows you: the site's power connection caps how many chips you can plug in, the cluster's power draw swings by megawatts in time with the training step, and something breaks every few hours, which stops the whole job.

Log scale: equal lengths are equal ratios, from a single GPU to a site a million times larger. Labels: vendor a manufacturer's figure, published a figure a paper or operator reported, derived computed here (formula shown), illustrative an input chosen for teaching. Sources are in the sections below.

Power decides how many chips

Chips draw 700 to 1,400 W each and keep rising. A gigawatt of facility power holds roughly half a million Blackwell GPUs (section 3).

Dense racks need liquid

A 120 kW rack would need about 14,000 cubic feet of air a minute; water carries the same heat in about 170 litres a minute (section 4).

Lockstep makes swings

Synchronous training draws near full power while computing and near idle while communicating, so the whole site swings by tens of megawatts each step (section 5).

Failures scale with chip count

Llama 3 405B stopped 419 times in 54 days on 16,384 GPUs, once every 3.1 hours. At the same rate 100,000 GPUs stop every 30 minutes (section 6).

Checkpoint at the square root

The best interval is about the square root of twice the checkpoint cost times the mean time between failures. Get it wrong and a day of training loses hours (section 7, Checkpoint simulator).

Some failures are silent

A defective core can return wrong numbers without any error. At Gemini's scale Google expected one such event every week or two (section 8).

2. Watts per chip: where the power goes, and why it keeps rising

The problem. Every number you see on a GPU spec sheet, FLOPS and bandwidth alike, is bought with power, and the power has a ceiling set by how much heat the package can shed. To read hardware you need to know what that ceiling is called, what fills it, and what happens when you hit it.

What a watt does inside a chip. A chip is billions of transistors switching on and off. Each switch charges or drains a tiny capacitance, so the switching ("dynamic") power is roughly activity × capacitance × voltage² × frequency, plus a leakage term that flows even when nothing switches (processor power dissipation, the textbook model). Two things follow. Higher clocks need higher voltage, so the last few hundred megahertz cost far more power than the first. And moving data costs more than computing on it: reading 32 bits from DRAM costs about 640 pJ against 3.7 pJ for a float multiply in Horowitz's 45 nm figures (Han et al. 2015, Fig. 1), which is why Memory technology spends a section on energy per bit.

TDP, TBP and "max power". Thermal design power (TDP) is the sustained heat the cooling system must be able to remove; NVIDIA quotes "up to 700 W" for H100 SXM (H100), AMD quotes total board power (TBP), 750 W for MI300X (data sheet). The chip's firmware enforces the limit continuously: when the board approaches its power or temperature limit, it lowers clock and voltage (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, DVFS). You can set a lower cap yourself (nvidia-smi -pl <watts>). Meta saw DVFS in a real run: Llama 3 405B's throughput varied by 1 to 2% with the time of day, because higher mid-day temperatures changed the GPUs' clocks (Llama 3 §3.3.4) published.

Watts per chip rise; energy per FLOP falls

Each generation draws more power and does much more work per joule. The chart divides each chip's maximum power by its dense BF16 peak: picojoules per FLOP at peak, a ceiling, not what a real run achieves.

pJ/FLOP derived = max power (W) / dense BF16 peak (FLOP/s) × 1012. Power and peaks: A100, H100 (NVIDIA product pages), Blackwell 1,200 W and Blackwell Ultra 1,400 W (NVIDIA, Aug 22 2025, with the GB200/GB300 per-GPU dense BF16 of 2,500 TFLOPS from the GB200 NVL72 page), MI300X and MI355X (AMD data sheets), MI455X announced 2,500 W max TBP and 5 PF BF16 (AMD's page does not say dense), TPU v2 and v3 TDP and TPU v4 measured maximum (Jouppi et al. 2020, TPU v4 paper). Every figure is in the Chip atlas tab of the parent page.

Predict. A B300 (Blackwell Ultra) is allowed 1,400 W, twice an H100's 700 W. Per dense BF16 FLOP at peak, does it use more or less energy than an H100?
0.56 pJ against 0.71 pJ, about 21% less derived. And Blackwell Ultra's headline format is FP4, 15 PF dense per GPU, which is 0.093 pJ per FP4 operation. Watts per chip rise because the chip does far more per second, not because it got less efficient; what rises with them is the heat per square centimetre, which is what forces liquid cooling (section 4).

3. From rack to site: power delivery, PUE and the per-gigawatt view

Server. An 8-GPU DGX H100 is rated 10.2 kW maximum, fed by six 3.3 kW power supplies in 4+2 redundancy (four carry the load, two are spares) (DGX H100 user guide) vendor. Eight GPUs at 700 W are 5.6 kW, about 55% of it derived; the rest is two CPUs, memory, NVSwitches, network cards, storage and fans. So "GPU watts" understate what a GPU costs a data centre by nearly a factor of two. A DGX B200 is about 14.3 kW (DGX B200).

Rack. A GB200 NVL72 rack (72 GPUs, 36 Grace CPUs) draws "approximately 120 kW"; eight power shelves, each with six 5.5 kW supplies and 33 kW of input, give N+N redundancy (DGX GB200 user guide) vendor. Four DGX H100s in one rack would already be 40.8 kW derived.

Delivery. Power arrives from the grid at high voltage, is stepped down by transformers at the site's substation, passes switchgear and uninterruptible power supplies (UPS: batteries that bridge short outages), reaches the rack, where power shelves convert it to 54 V DC on a copper busbar, and is converted again on each board down to the low voltage the silicon runs at. Every conversion loses a few percent as heat. Current is power divided by voltage, so at 54 V a 1 MW rack would need 18,519 A derived: NVIDIA writes that this "requires up to 200 kg of copper busbar" per rack, and plans 800 V DC distribution (1,250 A for the same megawatt) with "up to 5% improvement in end-to-end power efficiency", "starting in 2027" with its Kyber racks (NVIDIA, May 20 2025) vendor. The same post puts today's 54 V racks at up to 200 kW.

PUE = total facility power / IT equipment power
PUE
power usage effectiveness: 1.0 would mean every watt reaches a server; the excess is cooling, power conversion, lighting

Google's fleet ran at a trailing-twelve-month PUE of 1.09 in 2025; the Uptime Institute's 2025 survey average was 1.54 (Google) published. The difference matters at scale: at 1.54 a site spends 54 watts on overhead for every 100 that compute, at 1.09 only 9.

The per-gigawatt view

Sites are now planned in megawatts and gigawatts of grid connection, so the question "how many accelerators?" becomes a division:

accelerators = site power / (kW per accelerator × PUE)
kW per accelerator
a system's power divided by the accelerators in it (servers, CPUs and fans included)

At PUE 1.2 (the parent page's assumption) derived; system powers vendor as above, the Ironwood pod "spanning nearly 10 MW" for 9,216 chips (Google, Apr 2025; whether that figure includes cooling is not stated unconfirmed). The DGX B200 row reproduces the parent page's "about 466,000" by construction. Systems at full rated power: real sites provision below nameplate and add switches and storage, so this is an order of magnitude, not a plan. Change every input in the Gigawatt planner.

Power is cheap per hour and scarce per site. A DGX H100's share of 10.2 kW per GPU at PUE 1.2 is 1.53 kW; at the US industrial average of 9.77 cents per kWh (July 2026, EIA) that is about $0.15 per GPU-hour, 3.7% of Lambda's on-demand $3.99 for an H100 derived. A gigawatt for a year is 8.77 TWh, about $0.86 billion at that price. Electricity is a small line in the cost of a GPU-hour; the binding constraint is how many megawatts a site can get connected, which is why recent hardware comparisons are quoted in tokens per megawatt (section 8 of the parent page). Costing a whole run is on Performance math.

4. Cooling: why dense racks need liquid

The problem. Every watt a rack draws becomes a watt of heat inside it. If the heat is not carried away as fast as it is made, chips throttle (section 2) and then shut down. How much coolant you must push through the rack follows from one line of physics:

Q = ṁ × cp × ΔT
ṁ
mass flow of coolant, kg per second
Q
heat removed per second (watts), equal to the rack's power at steady state
cp
specific heat: joules to warm 1 kg by 1 °C (air about 1,005, water about 4,186)
ΔT
how much warmer the coolant leaves than it arrived

Per litre, water carries about 3,464 times as much heat as air for the same temperature rise (standard properties near 20 °C: air 1.2 kg/m³, water 998 kg/m³) derived. That single ratio is the whole argument.

Check it on a real server. NVIDIA rates a DGX H100 at 10.2 kW maximum with 1,105 cubic feet per minute (CFM) of front-to-back airflow at 80% fan speed (user guide). If all 10.2 kW left in that air, it would warm by 16.2 °C derived, a sensible design rise for an air-cooled server, so the formula and the spec sheet agree.

Same heat, two coolants derived: air flow = Q / (1.2 × 1,005 × ΔT) m³/s; water flow = Q / (4,186 × ΔT) kg/s. Rack powers vendor (sections 3 and 4). A real rack also has limits this ignores: fan power grows steeply with airflow, and a rack's front face is only so large.

What liquid cooling looks like. In a GB200 NVL72, cold plates sit directly on the CPUs and GPUs of every compute tray and liquid runs through them; "the rest of the components like networking and storage devices are air cooled" by fans (DGX GB200 user guide). This direct-to-chip design is the common one; the warm liquid goes to a coolant distribution unit that passes the heat to the building's water loop. Google moved its TPUs to liquid at v3 in 2018 (Jouppi et al. 2020), and Ironwood pods are liquid cooled.

Water. Many sites reject heat by evaporating water in cooling towers, so cooling has a water cost as well as an energy cost. Google counts 0.26 mL of water for the median Gemini Apps text prompt, data-centre water consumption included (Google Cloud, Aug 21 2025) published.

5. Power swings: tens of thousands of GPUs in lockstep

The problem. In synchronous data-parallel training every GPU computes its part of a step, then all of them wait for the gradient all-reduce, then all start the next step together. Computing draws close to full power; communicating draws close to idle. One GPU swinging by a few hundred watts is nothing. Sixteen thousand of them swinging at the same instant is a load the grid can see.

Meta reported exactly that on Llama 3: "tens of thousands of GPUs may increase or decrease power consumption at the same time", for instance all waiting for a checkpoint or a collective, giving "instant fluctuations of power consumption across the data center on the order of tens of megawatts, stretching the limits of the power grid" (Llama 3 §3.3.4) published. The order of magnitude checks out: 16,384 H100s at 700 W are 11.5 MW of GPU power alone, and 2,048 servers at a DGX H100's 10.2 kW maximum would be 20.9 MW derived.

A 57-author team led by Microsoft described the problem in detail in August 2025: swings of "tens or hundreds of megawatts" within one data centre, concentrated around 0.2 to 3 Hz, a range that can resonate with parts of the grid's own equipment and damage it (Choukse et al., arXiv 2508.14318) published. Utilities therefore set limits on how fast a site may ramp up and down, in megawatts per second.

An illustrative trace illustrative: 16,384 GPUs at 1,200 W, steps of 6 s compute and 2 s all-reduce at 15% of full power, one 12 s checkpoint pause after the third step. Only the 90% floor and the idea of ramp limits come from the paper; the step times, idle level and ramp rate are chosen for teaching. The same trace is recomputed in src/recompute.py.

Three fixes, each with a cost (from that paper and NVIDIA):

  • Software fill: run filler work (the paper's "Firefly" ran scaled matrix multiplications) when a GPU would otherwise idle, keeping power up. Measured overhead was under 5% of performance, and the energy is spent on nothing.
  • GPU power smoothing: GB200 GPUs accept programmable ramp-up and ramp-down rates and a "Minimum Power Floor" of up to 90% of TDP. With the floor at 90%, the paper measured "a total energy overhead of 10.5%" on real training. GB300 NVL72 adds a power cap that rises gradually at the start of a job and a "power burner" mode at the end (NVIDIA, Jul 28 2025) vendor.
  • Energy storage: batteries or capacitors absorb the ripple so the grid sees a flatter load. GB300 NVL72 power supplies hold "65 joules/GPU" in capacitors, and NVIDIA reports the grid's peak demand fell by 30% training a Megatron LLM vendor. 65 J covers 46 ms of a 1,400 W GPU derived: enough for the sub-second ripple, nowhere near a checkpoint pause, which is why the paper pairs rack-level storage with the GPU-level floor.

Why it matters for you: a power floor or filler work means paying for energy that trains nothing, and a cluster running under a site power cap may run its GPUs below their rated clocks. Both show up as lower MFU for reasons no profiler of your code will find.

6. What fails, how often, and how the hardware tells you

The problem. Synchronous training needs every GPU in the job: if one dies, the collective it was part of never completes and the whole job stops. So the job fails whenever any part fails, and with N parts that is N times as often as one part.

The best public record is Meta's Llama 3 405B pre-training: in a 54-day snapshot on up to 16,384 H100s there were 466 interruptions, 47 planned and 419 unexpected, about 78% of them confirmed or suspected hardware; only three needed significant manual intervention, and the run kept more than 90% effective training time (Llama 3 §3.3.4, Table 5) published. Correction carried from the Llama 3 and Training Infrastructure pages
Table 5 prints "Faulty GPU: 148, 30.1%", but 148 of 419 is 35.3%; every other row's percentage matches its count. By count, the GPU rows add to 268 of 419, 64.0% derived, not the 58.7% the paper's text gives (the sum of the printed percentages). The parent page's section 8 repeats the printed 30.1%.

Read it as a hardware engineer. The GPU itself (die, HBM, on-chip SRAM, its management processor, thermal interface and sensors) caused 64% of stops. Memory on the GPU alone, HBM3 plus SRAM, caused 91, 21.7% derived. Yet each GPU is very reliable: 72 HBM interruptions over 16,384 GPUs for 54 days is one per 33.6 GPU-years derived. Nothing here is a bad part; it is the multiplication.

MTBFjob = 1 / (Nservers × r)
r
failures per server per day of running (one server = 8 GPUs)
Nservers
servers in the job; MTBF is the mean time between failures

Llama 3's 419 in 54 days on 2,048 servers is 3.79 failures per thousand server-days derived: one 8-GPU server fails about once every 264 days, one GPU-share about every 5.8 years, yet the 16,384-GPU job stops every 3.1 hours, and at the same rate a 100,000-GPU job would stop every 30 minutes. Meta's research clusters measured 6.50 and 2.34 per thousand node-days, which project to 1.8 hours for 16,384 GPUs and 0.23 hours for 131,072 (Kokolis et al., Feb 2025; reproduced here) published. Google's Gemini report says the same in words: scaling up the number of accelerators "results in a proportionate decrease in the mean time between failure" (Gemini report). The Training Infrastructure page has this law with more clusters (OPT, MegaScale, ByteRobust) and its Goodput calculator.

How the hardware reports a fault

  • ECC on memory. HBM and on-chip SRAM store extra check bits with every word: a single flipped bit is corrected on the fly, two flipped bits are detected and reported as uncorrectable. Correctable errors are logged and counted; an uncorrectable one in data the job is using kills the process.
  • Row remapping. From the Ampere generation on, "every bank in DRAM is equipped with spare rows"; a row that keeps producing errors is remapped to a spare, which "requires a GPU reset to take effect and will remain persistent throughout the life of the GPU" (NVIDIA memory error management) vendor. When a bank runs out of spares, the GPU is due for replacement.
  • Xid errors. The NVIDIA driver writes numbered "Xid" messages to the kernel log for GPU faults (memory errors, a GPU that stops answering on PCIe, hangs); NVIDIA keeps a catalogue of what each number means and what to do (Xid errors, updated Sep 2026). Cluster health checks watch these.
  • Lemon nodes. Some servers fail again and again. Meta's research clusters traced their repeat-offender ("lemon") nodes to GPUs in 28.2% of cases and PCIe errors in 15.4%; the leading failure sources overall were InfiniBand links, filesystem mounts, GPU memory errors and PCIe errors (Kokolis et al.). Finding and removing them is a large part of cluster reliability work.

7. Checkpoint math from zero: Young, Daly and when they break

The problem. A checkpoint saves the model and optimizer state so a failed job can restart from it instead of from the beginning. Checkpointing often wastes time writing; checkpointing rarely wastes the work done since the last save each time something fails. There is a best interval in between, and it follows from four numbers.

waste = C/T + (1 − C/T) × (D + R + T/2) / μ
T
the period: T − C of training, then a checkpoint
C
time training is paused per checkpoint
D, R
downtime after a failure (detect, replace) and recovery (restart, reload the checkpoint)
μ
MTBF of the whole job, from section 6

The first term is the time spent checkpointing. The second is the failures: on average one every μ hours, each costing the downtime, the recovery, and on average half a period of lost work, because a failure is equally likely anywhere in the period. Minimising over T gives the classic answers (Aupy et al. 2013, eq. 12 and 13):

TYoung = √(2 μ C) + C
TDaly = √(2 (μ + D + R) C) + C
Young
1974, the first-order optimum (Young 1974)
Daly
2006, adds recovery time (Daly 2006); Aupy et al.'s refined version is √(2(μ − D − R)C)

The square root is the intuition: double the failure rate and the best interval shrinks only by √2. For Llama 3's rate (μ = 3.09 h) and a 5-minute synchronous checkpoint, Young gives 48.1 minutes; with 10 minutes of downtime and 5 of recovery the exact optimum is 44.8 minutes and the job keeps 72.5% of its time derived. A 3-hour interval on the same run keeps 53.2%.

The exact answer. If failures arrive at random (a Poisson process, the standard model), the expected time is known exactly: Timefinal = (μ + D) eR/μ (eT/μ − 1) Timebase / (T − C) (quoted by Aupy et al. from earlier work). This page's simulator uses it and checks it two ways: it reproduces all forty periods in Aupy et al.'s Table 2 to within 0.12% (independently), and a Monte Carlo of 2,000 MTBFs per run lands within 0.7 percentage points of it at every interval tried (src/recompute.py).

Predict. At 131,072 GPUs (Llama 3's failure rate, μ = 23 minutes) with a 5-minute checkpoint, the first-order waste formula predicts a negative useful fraction. Is Young's interval still a good choice?
Yes. The exact formula gives 26.6% useful time at Young's interval against 27.0% at the true optimum; the first-order formula's −6.2% is the formula breaking, not the job derived. The interval rule is robust; the efficiency estimate is not, once μ is only a few times C + D + R. The fix at that scale is not a cleverer interval but a smaller C, D and R: in the table below, a 10-second pause roughly doubles the useful fraction.

Useful fraction of wall-clock time at the exact optimum interval, Llama 3's failure rate, D = 10 min, R = 5 min illustrative, against the first-order formula's prediction at Young's interval derived. 8. Silent data corruption: the failure that raises no error

The problem. Everything in section 6 announces itself: a crash, an Xid, a timeout. A silent data corruption (SDC) is a wrong answer with no error. ECC protects bits sitting in memory, not the arithmetic units that compute on them, so a marginal transistor in a multiplier can return a wrong product and nothing notices.

  • It is real and systemic. Meta's fleet testing found "hundreds of CPUs" with SDCs across hundreds of thousands of machines over more than 18 months, and notes SDCs "are not captured by error reporting mechanisms" (Dixit et al. 2021) published. Google observed "on the order of a few mercurial cores per several thousand machines", with frequency, voltage, temperature and age all affecting whether a defective core misbehaves (Hochschild et al., HotOS 2021) published. Both studies are of CPUs; the mechanisms (manufacturing defects that show up only under some conditions) apply to any chip.
  • At training scale it is a weekly event. For Gemini, Google wrote that "we can expect SDC events to impact training every week or two", and found the faulty hardware with "deterministic replay to isolate incorrect computations, combined with proactive SDC scanners on idle machines and hot standbys" (Gemini report) published. Llama 3's Table 5 attributes 6 of its 419 interruptions to SDC: the ones that were caught.

What it does to a run. In data parallelism a corrupted gradient on one GPU is averaged into everyone's all-reduce, so every replica applies the same wrong update: the loss may spike, turn into not-a-number values, or drift without any visible sign. Two detection ideas follow from how training works (this page's reasoning, not from a single source): replicas that should hold identical weights can compare checksums after a step, which catches corruption that happens after the all-reduce on one replica; and a step can be replayed deterministically on other hardware and compared, which catches corruption before it, the method Google describes. Treat an unexplained loss spike as a possible hardware event, not only as an optimiser problem.

9. Energy per token

Everything above reduces to one number a reader can compare across systems: joules per token.

J per token = system power (W) / tokens per second

Training, worked from published figures. Llama 3.1 405B used 30.84 million H100 GPU-hours (model card) on 15.6 trillion tokens (Llama 3 paper). At the 700 W board limit that is at most 21.6 GWh of GPU energy and 4.98 J per training token derived. From first principles: one token costs 6 × 405 × 109 = 2.43 × 1015 FLOPs; at an H100's 0.71 pJ per BF16 FLOP that is 1.72 J at 100% of peak and 4.19 J at the 41% MFU Meta reports. The GPU-hour figure sits about a fifth above the MFU figure, as it should: it includes restarts, evaluation and slower phases. Add the rest of the server (a DGX H100 is 1.82 times its GPUs' power) and a PUE of 1.2, and the facility spends roughly 10.9 J per training token derived, an upper-bound estimate because GPUs rarely sit at their limit.

Inference. Google measured the median Gemini Apps text prompt at 0.24 Wh (864 J), counting idle provisioned machines, host CPU and memory, and data-centre overhead; counting only the active accelerators gives 0.10 Wh; and the median prompt's energy fell 33 times over twelve months (Google Cloud, Aug 21 2025) published. Two lessons: the boundary you draw changes the answer by more than two times, and software (batching, quantisation, better kernels) moves energy per token faster than hardware does. Tokens per second for a given chip and model come from the Performance calculator on the parent page and Performance math.

10. Common mistakes and interview questions

Sizing a site from GPU TDP
A DGX H100 draws 10.2 kW for 5.6 kW of GPUs, and the facility adds its PUE on top: 700 W of GPU becomes about 1.5 kW at the meter at PUE 1.2.
Treating TDP as average draw
TDP is the ceiling the cooling must handle. Average draw depends on the workload (communication phases run near idle, section 5) and on power caps.
Quoting MFU between failures as the run's throughput
Multiply by the useful fraction of wall-clock time. At Llama 3's failure rate with a 5-minute checkpoint and 15 minutes to recover, that fraction is about 73%; Meta reached over 90% by making all of these faster.
Keeping the checkpoint interval when the job grows
The best interval scales with the square root of MTBF, and MTBF falls with the number of GPUs. Eight times the GPUs means checkpointing about 2.8 times as often.
Trusting the first-order efficiency formula at very large scale
Once MTBF is only a few times the checkpoint and recovery time, the first-order waste formula goes badly wrong (even negative). Young's interval stays near optimal; use the exact formula (section 7) for the efficiency.
Assuming every loss spike is the optimiser
Silent data corruption produces loss spikes and not-a-number values too. Large runs replay suspicious steps on other hardware.

Interview questions

How many H100s can a 100 MW site hold?
At a DGX H100's 10.2 kW per 8 GPUs and PUE 1.2: 100,000 kW / (1.275 kW × 1.2) = about 65,000 GPUs. Say what you assumed: server maximum power, no network or storage, the PUE.
Why is a GB200 NVL72 rack liquid cooled?
About 120 kW in one rack. Air at a 15 °C rise would need about 6.6 m³/s (14,000 CFM) through it; water at a 10 °C rise needs about 170 L/min, because water holds about 3,500 times more heat per litre.
Derive the optimal checkpoint interval.
Waste per unit time is C/T for checkpointing plus (T/2 + D + R)/μ for failures. Differentiate in T: −C/T² + 1/(2μ) = 0, so T = √(2μC) (Young adds C back for the checkpoint itself).
A 32,768-GPU job fails at Llama 3's rate and a checkpoint costs 2 minutes. How often should it checkpoint?
MTBF halves from 185.6 to 92.8 minutes. Young: √(2 × 92.8 × 2) + 2 = about 21.3 minutes.
Why does synchronous training stress the power grid, and what are the fixes?
All GPUs compute and communicate in lockstep, so the site swings between near full and near idle power every step, by tens of megawatts. Fixes: filler work during idle phases, GPU power floors and ramp limits (about 10% energy overhead measured at a 90% floor), energy storage at the rack.
What is silent data corruption, and how would you look for it in a training run?
A wrong computation with no error raised. Compare checksums of weights across replicas that should be identical, replay suspicious steps deterministically on other hardware, and scan idle machines with known-answer tests.

Gigawatt planner

Start from the power a site can draw from the grid and work inward: how much of it reaches the servers (PUE), how many accelerators that powers, what peak compute they add up to, and what the electricity costs per year and per accelerator-hour. Average draw only changes energy and cost: the site must still be sized for the maximum. Default price: US industrial average, July 2026, 9.77 cents per kWh (EIA, released Sep 24 2026). Site power uses a log scale from 1 MW to 5 GW.

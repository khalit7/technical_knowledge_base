2. Inside a GPU: the parts every other page names

A GPU must keep tens of thousands of lanes fed while memory takes hundreds of cycles to answer; its layout follows from that. The H100 is the reference because most published training numbers were measured on it. Six parts, each defined once here and taught on {{GPU architecture|n:3c65c17b0d0d810682aae39bd614ec64}}:

SMThe building block: an H100 SXM enables 132 of the 144 on its die (the spares absorb defects). Four sub-partitions, each with a scheduler, 32 fp32 lanes and one tensor core.
Warp32 threads issued one instruction together (SIMT). Up to 64 warps stay resident per SM; while one waits for memory, another computes. The share of slots filled is occupancy.
Register file256 KB per SM, the largest and fastest memory on the chip: 33.0 MiB across 132 SMs derived. It is why switching warps is free.
Shared memory and L1256 KB per SM, up to 228 KB of it a scratchpad the program fills by hand to reuse a tile.
L2 cache50 MB shared by all SMs, between them and HBM.
HBM80 GB in 5 stacks beside the die, 3.35 TB/s (section 3).

What a root must say about GPUs. Peak FLOPS are tensor-core FLOPS, reached only when the matmuls are big enough to fill every SM with tiles and the data arrives in time; everything else (normalisation, softmax, the optimizer) runs on the ordinary lanes, a sixteenth of the tensor-core rate in fp32, and is usually memory-bound anyway (section 5). Each generation adds a format and machinery to keep the tensor cores fed: Hopper's TMA copy engine and FP8, Blackwell's tensor memory, FP4 and two dies presented as one GPU. nvidia-smi "utilization" only says some kernel was running; the profiler's tensor-pipe utilisation is the real number.

Chip atlas

Every accelerator an ML engineer is likely to meet in October 2026, on one set of axes: how fast it multiplies, how much memory it has, how fast that memory is, how chips are joined, how much power it draws and what it costs to rent. Each number links to the vendor page or datasheet it came from; independent measurements sit beside them, never mixed in.

Four questions to ask of any spec sheet
  1. Dense or sparse? NVIDIA and AMD often print tensor-core figures "with sparsity": the throughput if half of every group of four weights is zero (2:4 structured sparsity, a pattern almost no training run uses). Dense is half. This atlas shows dense everywhere and the printed sparse figure beside it.
  2. Which number format, and which accumulator? A chip does 2x the operations per second in FP8 as in BF16 and often 2x again in FP4. GeForce cards also halve their BF16 rate when the running sum is kept in FP32, which is what training does. Compare at the same format.
  3. Per chip, per server or per rack? "1.44 exaFLOPS" is a rack of 72 GPUs. The toggle below puts everything on the same footing.
  4. Vendor peak or measured? A peak is a ceiling computed from clock speed and unit count. Real large matrix multiplications reach roughly half to three quarters of it (measured below).
Words used here. FLOPS: floating-point operations per second (one multiply-add counts as two). TFLOPS = 1012, PFLOPS = 1015. HBM (high-bandwidth memory): DRAM stacked next to the chip, wide and fast; GDDR: the cheaper memory on gaming cards; SRAM: memory on the chip itself, fastest and smallest. Bandwidth: bytes per second between memory and chip. Scale-up domain: the set of chips joined by the chip's own fast link (NVLink, Infinity Fabric, TPU ICI, NeuronLink), where they can share memory traffic as if they were one big chip; beyond it, traffic goes over a slower network (scale-out: InfiniBand or Ethernet).

Read the headline: the same normalisation on two chips

Why chips get more compute-bound: compute grows faster than bandwidth

A matrix multiply needs both arithmetic and bytes from memory. The ridge point, peak FLOP/s divided by bandwidth, is how many operations a kernel must do per byte it reads before arithmetic, not memory, is the limit (the {{Roofline lab|#t-roof}} measures it on a real GPU). Step through six NVIDIA generations, all scaled to the A100.

The dashed lines are the arithmetic intensity of real work, from the formula in the {{Roofline lab|#t-roof}}: a linear layer reading BF16 weights does about one FLOP per byte per token in the batch, so decoding one sequence sits near 1, a batch of 64 near 64, and a 4,096 x 4,096 x 4,096 training matmul near 1,400 (each element read once; FP8 doubles these, FP4 quadruples them). Anything left of a chip's bar is memory-bound on that chip.

Interview questions

Why can two chips with the same TFLOPS train at very different speeds?
Because a training step is not one big matmul. Attention, normalisation, optimizer updates and every elementwise op are limited by memory bandwidth; the gradient all-reduce is limited by the link between chips. H100 and H200 have identical compute, yet H200's 43% more bandwidth and 76% more memory make it faster on anything memory-bound and let it fit larger batches. Ask for bandwidth, memory and link bandwidth, not only FLOPS.
What does "1,979 TFLOPS BF16" on the H100 page mean, and what will you get?
It is the 2:4 structured-sparsity figure. Dense BF16 peak is 989.5. SemiAnalysis measured about 720 TFLOP/s on large BF16 GEMMs (73% of dense peak), and a whole training step reaches less, because attention, normalisation and communication run below the matmul rate. The share of dense peak a whole step achieves is MFU (model FLOPs utilisation); the {{Roofline lab|#t-roof}} measures it on a real GPU and the {{Performance calculator|#t-calc}} uses it.
Why do NVL72 racks matter for training and inference?
Because they make 72 GPUs one scale-up domain: every GPU reaches every other at 1.8 TB/s (900 GB/s each way) through NVLink switches. Tensor and expert parallelism, which exchange activations several times per layer, need that bandwidth; on an 8-GPU server they must stop at 8 GPUs, and anything larger crosses the scale-out network at 400 Gb/s (DGX B200) or 800 Gb/s (DGX B300) per GPU, that is 50 to 100 GB/s each way: 9 to 18 times slower. The TPU answer is the same idea at pod scale: 9,216 TPU7x chips on one ICI network.
Which chip would you pick to serve a 70B model at low latency, and why not the one with the most FLOPS?
Decoding at small batch reads every weight once per token, so time per token is about weight bytes divided by bandwidth: 70 GB in FP8 on one H200 (4.8 TB/s) is at least about 15 ms per token, on one B200 (8 TB/s) about 9 ms. FLOPS barely matter at batch 1; bandwidth and capacity (the weights plus the KV cache must fit) decide. SRAM-only designs (Groq, Cerebras) attack exactly this, at the cost of spreading a large model over many chips.
A vendor quotes FP4 PFLOPS. When does that number apply to you?
Only when your matmuls run in FP4 with block scaling (NVFP4 or MXFP4), which today means mostly inference of quantized models and some experimental training recipes. BF16 training uses the BF16 rate, which grew far less (B200 to B300: no change in BF16, 1.5x in FP4). Read the BF16 and FP8 rows for training.

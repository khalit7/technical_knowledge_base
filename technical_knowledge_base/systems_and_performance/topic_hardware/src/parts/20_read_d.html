4. Numbers: formats and what the silicon accelerates

The problem. Every byte saved per number is saved three times: in memory capacity, in bandwidth, and (when the tensor cores support the format) in arithmetic, because a multiplier for 8-bit numbers is much smaller than one for 16-bit numbers, so more of them fit in the same area. Halving the bits roughly doubles the peak FLOPS. The catch is accuracy: fewer bits mean less range (how large and small a number can be) and less precision (how finely values are spaced).

Block scaling. An 8-, 6- or 4-bit number cannot cover the spread of values in a whole tensor, so the newest formats give each small block of values its own scale factor. The OCP MX formats (MXFP8, MXFP6, MXFP4, MXINT8) share one 8-bit power-of-two scale (E8M0) per block of 32 values ({{OCP MX spec v1.0|https://www.opencompute.org/documents/ocp-microscaling-formats-mx-v1-0-spec-final-pdf}}, Sep 2023). NVIDIA's NVFP4 uses blocks of 16 with an FP8 (E4M3) scale, plus one FP32 scale per tensor ({{NVIDIA, Jun 24 2025|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/introducing-nvfp4-for-efficient-and-accurate-low-precision-inference/}}). The scales cost bits: MXFP4 stores 4 + 8/32 = 4.25 bits per value, NVFP4 4 + 8/16 = 4.5 derived. Blackwell's tensor cores apply the scales in hardware, inside the multiply.

What it shows. A chip only gets faster at a format it has silicon for: the A100 has no FP8 path, the H100 no FP4, TPU v6e lists no FP8 figure, and on those chips the smaller format saves memory but not arithmetic. Within a chip each halving doubles the peak (H100: 989.5 BF16, 1,979 FP8; B200: 2,250, 4,500, 9,000). And consumer cards are half-rate where training lives: the RTX 5090 does 419 TFLOPS of FP16 with FP16 accumulation but 209.5 with the FP32 accumulation that training needs ({{RTX Blackwell whitepaper|https://images.nvidia.com/aem-dam/Solutions/geforce/blackwell/nvidia-rtx-blackwell-gpu-architecture.pdf}}, Appendix A).

The cost
Lower precision is not free accuracy-wise: FP8 training needs careful scaling (per tensor or per block) and keeps the master weights, the optimizer and the accumulation in higher precision. Published large runs show both choices: Llama 3 405B reported its throughput in BF16 ({{Llama 3, Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}), DeepSeek-V3 trained with an FP8 mixed-precision framework ({{DeepSeek-V3 report|https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19437v2}}).

How to check. The format of a matmul is decided by its input dtypes (torch.autocast, or the FP8 recipe of a library such as Transformer Engine); a run that is unexpectedly slow on a new GPU is often doing its matmuls in a format the tensor cores do not accelerate, or in fp32.

6. Joining chips: why the network picks your parallelism

The problem. The step's state (128.5 GB) does not fit one GPU and its arithmetic would take 64 s on one, so it is split across many. Whatever way it is split, the GPUs must exchange data: at least the gradients, 16.06 GB per step. The links between chips are a hierarchy, each level roughly an order of magnitude slower than the one above, and which kind of parallelism you can afford depends on which level its traffic crosses.

Memory: one chipHBM3 / HBM3e: 3.35 / 8 TB/s.
Scale-up: one server or rackNVLink 4 (H100) / NVLink 5 (Blackwell) through NVSwitch chips: 450 / 900 GB/s per GPU each way. 8 GPUs in an HGX server; 72 in a GB200 or GB300 NVL72 rack.
Host link: one serverPCIe 5.0 x16, about 64 GB/s each way: CPU to GPU, NIC to GPU, and between consumer cards.
Scale-out: thousands of serversInfiniBand NDR (400 Gb/s) or RoCE Ethernet, one NIC per GPU: 50 GB/s each way; XDR is 800 Gb/s.

Vendor figures are usually both directions added: NVLink 4 is "900 GB/s total" ({{Hopper in depth|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/}}), NVLink 5 "1.8 TB/s" per GPU and 130 TB/s across an NVL72 ({{Blackwell|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/technologies/blackwell-architecture/}}); halved here to one direction. InfiniBand: {{Quantum-X800|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/networking/products/infiniband/quantum-x800/}} (800 Gb/s ports); an AWS p5 server has 3,200 Gb/s of network for 8 H100s, 400 Gb/s per GPU ({{AWS p5|https://aws.amazon.com/ec2/instance-types/p5/}}). PCIe 5.0 runs 32 GT/s per lane, about 63 GB/s per direction on 16 lanes after encoding derived. vendor spec

The two kinds of fabric. Scale-up links (NVLink with NVSwitch, AMD Infinity Fabric, Google's ICI) join a small group of chips so closely that they can read each other's memory at hundreds of GB/s; the group is called the NVLink domain. Scale-out links (InfiniBand, or RoCE: RDMA over Ethernet) join servers through network switches; slower, but they reach tens of thousands of GPUs. Both use RDMA: the network card writes straight into another machine's GPU memory without either CPU copying it. InfiniBand has lossless, credit-based flow control built in; RoCE needs lossless Ethernet configured (PFC, ECN) and is cheaper and multi-vendor; Llama 3 trained on RoCE at 400 Gb/s per GPU, in a three-layer network of 24K GPUs ({{Llama 3 report|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}).

The same all-reduce, inside and outside the NVLink domain

An all-reduce leaves every GPU with the sum of everyone's data: data-parallel training does one on the gradients every step. The usual ring algorithm splits the data into N chunks and passes them around a ring of the N GPUs: N−1 steps where chunks are added (a reduce-scatter), then N−1 steps where the finished sums are passed on (an all-gather). Each GPU sends 2(N−1)/N times the data, whatever N is, so the time is that volume divided by one link's bandwidth.

The 8B's bf16 gradients, 16.06 GB, all-reduced over 8 H100s: 2 × 7/8 × 16.06 GB = 28.1 GB sent per GPU, at 450 GB/s (NVLink 4, one direction) or 50 GB/s (one 400 Gb/s NIC per GPU) derived, ignoring latency and assuming the link is the only limit. Real collectives reach somewhat less; NCCL picks rings, trees or in-switch reduction (SHARP) by size and topology.

Why the network picks your parallelism. With 8 GPUs at 40% MFU the step's arithmetic takes 8.0 s, so even the InfiniBand all-reduce (0.56 s) is small, and it can overlap with the backward pass: data parallelism tolerates the slow network. Tensor parallelism is different. It splits each layer's matrices across GPUs, so the GPUs must all-reduce activations twice per layer going forward and twice going back. That traffic sits on the critical path between layers, and it scales with the activations, not the parameters. The {{Performance calculator|#t-calc}} works it for Llama 3.1 70B with 8-way tensor parallelism at 8,192 tokens: the all-reduces cost 16% of the layer's compute time over NVLink, but 141% over a 400 Gb/s NIC, so the GPUs would spend more time waiting than computing. So: tensor (and expert) parallelism stays inside the NVLink domain; pipeline and data parallelism cross servers.

Two real runs that show the rule
  • Llama 3 405B used tensor parallelism of 8, exactly one 8-GPU server, in every configuration, with pipeline (16), context and data parallelism across servers ({{Llama 3, Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}).
  • DeepSeek-V3 trained on 2,048 H800s with NVLink at 160 GB/s and InfiniBand at 50 GB/s (as the report gives them), and avoided "costly Tensor Parallelism" entirely, using 16-way pipeline, 64-way expert parallelism across 8 nodes and ZeRO-1 data parallelism; its all-to-all kernels needed only 20 SMs to saturate both links ({{DeepSeek-V3 report|https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19437v2}}).

Racks and TPU pods. The NVL72 rack exists to make the NVLink domain 72 GPUs instead of 8, so tensor and expert parallelism (and giant mixture-of-experts models) can span a rack. Google's TPUs take another route: each chip links directly to its neighbours over ICI in a 2D or 3D torus (a grid whose edges wrap around), with no switches between chips, and optical circuit switches (mirrors that redirect light) reconfigure how 64-chip cubes connect; in TPU v4 they were under 5% of system cost and 3% of its power ({{TPU v4 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01433}}). An Ironwood pod joins 9,216 chips, each with 1.2 TB/s of ICI (both directions) ({{Google, Apr 2025|https://blog.google/products/google-cloud/ironwood-tpu-age-of-inference/}}). A torus is excellent for ring collectives between neighbours and slower for arbitrary point-to-point traffic, which is why TPU programs are written as shardings over a device mesh.

Predict: two RTX 5090s in one workstation have no NVLink, only PCIe 5.0 (about 64 GB/s each way). How long does the ring all-reduce of the 8B's 16.06 GB of gradients take between them?
With N = 2 each GPU sends 2 × 1/2 × 16.06 = 16.06 GB; at 64 GB/s that is 251 ms derived.

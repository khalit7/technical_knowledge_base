7. The landscape: what each vendor is betting on

Every accelerator answers the same three columns (arithmetic, memory, links) with a different bet about which one matters most. One line each, with the numbers that show the bet; every spec, dense and sparse, is in the {{Chip atlas|#t-chips}}.

NVIDIA (A100, H100, H200, B200, B300; Rubin next)

General-purpose GPUs plus the software everyone targets first (CUDA, cuBLAS, NCCL), and a scale-up domain grown from 8 GPUs to 72 per rack (NVL72). Each generation adds a format and roughly doubles dense BF16: 312, 989.5, 2,250 TFLOPS (A100, H100, HGX B200). Vera Rubin announced: 4 PF BF16 dense and 288 GB of HBM4 per GPU, production shipments "starting this fall" ({{NVIDIA, May 31 2026|https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/vera-rubin-full-production-agentic-ai-factory}}).

AMD Instinct (MI300X, MI325X, MI355X; MI455X next)

More memory per GPU at similar compute: MI300X had 192 GB when H100 had 80; MI355X has 288 GB at 8 TB/s and 2,516.6 TFLOPS BF16 dense ({{AMD MI355X|https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/instinct-tech-docs/product-briefs/amd-instinct-mi355x-gpu-brochure.pdf}}). Software (ROCm) is the gap. An independent test found BF16 matmuls reaching about 620 TFLOP/s on MI300X against 1,307 peak, and about 720 on H100 against 989.5 ({{SemiAnalysis, Dec 22 2024|https://semianalysis.com/2024/12/22/mi300x-vs-h100-vs-h200-benchmark-part-1-training/}}): spec sheets do not rank chips.

Google TPU (v5e, v5p, v6e Trillium, TPU7x Ironwood)

Systolic arrays and a compiler instead of threads and caches, and switchless torus pods: 9,216 Ironwood chips, each 2,307 TFLOPS BF16 with 192 GiB at 7.38 TB/s ({{TPU7x docs|https://docs.cloud.google.com/tpu/docs/tpu7x}}). Rented by the chip-hour on Google Cloud ($12.00 on demand for Ironwood, fetched 2026-10-05). Programmed through JAX and XLA, or PyTorch/XLA.

AWS Trainium (Trainium2, Trainium3)

Vertical integration: AWS owns the chip, the NeuronLink fabric, the Neuron SDK and the data centre, and prices against NVIDIA's margin. Trainium2: 667 TFLOPS BF16, 96 GiB at 2.9 TB/s ({{Neuron docs|https://awsdocs-neuron.readthedocs-hosted.com/en/latest/about-neuron/arch/neuron-hardware/trainium2.html}}); Trainium3 UltraServers join up to 144 chips. The cost is porting to Neuron.

Groq LPU (now NVIDIA Groq 3 LPX)

No DRAM for the model: weights live in on-chip SRAM across many chips, and the compiler schedules every transfer in advance so timing is deterministic. Its current page lists the NVIDIA Groq 3 LPX rack announced: 256 LPUs, 128 GB SRAM, 40 PB/s, "1,000 tokens/sec/user" ({{Groq|https://groq.com/lpu-architecture}}). Bet: batch-1 latency, the regime where a GPU waits on HBM (section 3).

Cerebras (WSE-3, CS-3)

Do not cut the wafer: one 46,225 mm2 chip with 900,000 cores and 44 GB of SRAM at 21 PB/s, so a model that fits never crosses a chip boundary ({{Cerebras, Mar 13 2024|https://www.cerebras.ai/press-release/cerebras-announces-third-generation-wafer-scale-engine}}; the 21 PB/s and 27 kW are from the CS-3 datasheet, in the {{Chip atlas|#t-chips}}). The 125 PF headline is a sparse figure. Bought as whole systems (CS-3, up to 27 kW each).

The reading instruction: at what scale was that number measured?

Hardware numbers are quoted per chip, per server, per rack, per pod and now per gigawatt of power, and they are not interchangeable. Per chip decides what fits in memory; per scale-up domain decides which parallelism is affordable (section 6); per megawatt decides how much a fixed power connection can host. 8. Power, cooling, reliability

Watts per chip keep rising. A100 400 W, H100 700 W, Blackwell up to 1,200 W and Blackwell Ultra 1,400 W per GPU ({{NVIDIA, Aug 22 2025|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-blackwell-ultra-the-chip-powering-the-ai-factory-era/}}), MI355X 1,400 W, and AMD's announced MI455X up to 2,500 W. Every watt becomes heat in a few square centimetres, so above about a kilowatt per chip air cooling gives way to liquid: the GB200 NVL72 rack and Ironwood pods are liquid-cooled ({{GB200 NVL72|https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/gb200-nvl72/}}, {{Google, Apr 2025|https://blog.google/products/google-cloud/ironwood-tpu-age-of-inference/}}).

Megawatts per cluster. A full 9,216-chip Ironwood pod spans "nearly 10 MW" (same Google post). Llama 3's training cluster saw its power draw swing by "tens of megawatts" across the data centre as all GPUs paused and resumed together, a load the grid itself notices ({{Llama 3 report|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}). Worldwide, data centres used about 415 TWh in 2024, around 1.5% of electricity, projected to more than double to about 945 TWh by 2030 ({{IEA, Energy and AI, 2025|https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary}}). That is why recent comparisons are quoted per megawatt: SemiAnalysis measured a Vera Rubin NVL72 at up to 7 times a GB300 rack's tokens per megawatt on early pre-release software, and 1.4 to 3 times the throughput per total cost of ownership at 60 to 100 tokens per second per user ({{SemiAnalysis, Sep 14 2026|https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/vera-rubin-nvl72-agentic-inference}}, paywalled) independent.

Reliability is a hardware number too. In a 54-day snapshot of Llama 3 405B training on up to 16K H100s there were 419 unexpected interruptions, about 78% attributed to confirmed hardware issues; faulty GPUs caused 148 (30.1%) and HBM3 memory 72 (17.2%) ({{Llama 3 report|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}). At that scale something fails every few hours, so checkpoint speed and automatic restart are part of the hardware budget, and MFU measured between failures overstates the run's real throughput.

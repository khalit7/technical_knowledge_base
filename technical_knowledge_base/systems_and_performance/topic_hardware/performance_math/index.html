Every performance question about a model is three counts divided by three numbers of the chip: FLOPs over peak FLOP/s gives the time when arithmetic is the limit, bytes moved over memory bandwidth gives the time when memory is the limit, and bytes held against memory capacity says whether it fits. MFU is the share of the peak a real run reaches, and the price per hour turns the time into money.

The running example in four divisions: a training step, what it holds, a decoded token, a whole run

Llama 3.1 8B (8.03 billion parameters, 32 layers, hidden size 4,096) on one H100 SXM (989.5 TFLOP/s dense BF16, 3.35 TB/s, 80 GB; vendor specifications from the parent's Chip atlas). Notice which chip number each row divides by: the training step is limited by arithmetic, the decoded token by bandwidth, and the step's memory only fits once the model state is sharded over 8 GPUs. Each row is derived in the section named on it.

1. FLOPs: 6ND, and what it leaves out

One matrix multiply. Llama 3.1 8B's query projection is a 4,096 × 4,096 weight. Applied to 8,192 tokens it makes 8,192 × 4,096 outputs, each a sum of 4,096 products: 4,096 multiplies and 4,096 adds, so 2 × 8,192 × 4,096 × 4,096 = 275 GFLOP. In general an (m × k) by (k × n) multiply is 2mkn FLOPs, and almost all of a transformer's work is of this kind: tokens (m) times a weight matrix (k × n). Each weight therefore meets each token once going forward (2 FLOPs) and twice going back (4: the gradient of the input, to keep backpropagating, and the gradient of the weight).

Four things move the count away from 6N:

  • The input embedding is not a multiply, it looks a row up. Its 525M parameters are 6.5% of N, so 6N over-counts by that much at short sequences. The output head is a multiply and counts.
  • Attention over the context: each query meets every earlier key, 4 × T × h forward FLOPs per layer per token, growing with the sequence while 6N does not. At 2,048 tokens the two effects nearly cancel (+1.3% over 6N); at 8,192 attention adds 24.7%, at 32,768 118%, at 131,072 it is five times 6N.
  • Causal masking: a causal kernel skips the blocks above the diagonal, about half the score square. Counting full squares (PaLM's 12LHQT, PyTorch's counter) gives the upper figure; the work done is nearer half the attention term. Say which convention you use.
  • FlashAttention's backward recomputes the scores instead of reading them back, so its attention backward is 2.5 times its forward, not 2 times (counted: 10.74 against 4.29 GFLOP per token).

Mixture of experts. Each token passes through only k of E experts, so FLOPs follow the active parameters and memory follows the total: DeepSeek-V3 is 671.0B total and 37.55B active, 17.9 times fewer FLOPs per token than a dense model of the same size (parameter counts recounted from its config by the parent's calculator). The router's own FLOPs are tiny; the cost moves to the network (section 8).

2. Arithmetic intensity and the roofline: compute-bound or memory-bound

The same weight, two ways. The 4,096 × 4,096 BF16 query weight is 33.6 MB. Used by one token (decoding at batch 1) it does 33.6 MFLOP for 33.6 MB moved, about 1 FLOP per byte: an H100 finishes the arithmetic in 0.034 µs and then waits 10.0 µs for the bytes. Used by 8,192 tokens (a training sequence or a long prompt) it does 275 GFLOP for 167.8 MB moved, 1,638 FLOPs per byte: 277.8 µs of arithmetic against 50.1 µs of reading. Same weight, same chip; the first is limited by memory, the second by arithmetic.

A weight matrix needs about 345 tokens per read before an H100 stops waiting on memory

Predict, then drag: how many tokens must share one read of this weight before the H100 reaches its peak? 10, 100, 1,000?

One 4,096 × 4,096 BF16 matmul: FLOPs 2mkn, bytes 2(mk + kn + mn) for reading both inputs and writing the output. Roofs: H100 SXM, 989.5 TFLOP/s dense BF16 and 3.35 TB/s (vendor), ridge 295 FLOPs per byte; Apple M1 Pro, 5.06 TFLOP/s and 165 GB/s measured in the parent's Roofline lab, ridge 31. The fixed point is a BF16 elementwise add (1 FLOP per 6 bytes) derived. A roofline is a ceiling: real kernels sit below it (section 5).

Three consequences run through the rest of the page. Decode at small batch sits near 1 FLOP per byte, so its speed is bytes over bandwidth (section 6), and batching is how serving climbs the slope. Training and prefill run thousands of tokens per weight read, so they are compute-bound and their time is FLOPs over peak (sections 4 and 7). Elementwise operators (norms, activations, residual adds, casts) do about one FLOP per several bytes whatever the batch, so they are always memory-bound: on an H100 a BF16 add reaches 0.56 TFLOP/s, and the only cure is to fuse it into a neighbouring kernel (section 5). The parent's Roofline lab measures these ceilings on a real GPU; the CUDA and serving pages apply the model to kernels and to batching.

3. Memory: what one training step holds

The model state. Mixed-precision training with Adam keeps, for every parameter, the BF16 weight used in the matmuls (2 bytes), its BF16 gradient (2), and the optimizer's state in FP32: a master copy of the weight (4, because tiny updates vanish when added in BF16) and Adam's two moments m and v (4 + 4). That is 2 + 2 + 12 = 16 bytes per parameter, the ZeRO paper's 2Ψ + 2Ψ + KΨ with K = 12 (ZeRO, section 3 "Where did all the memory go?"; paper page): 128.5 GB for Llama 3.1 8B. That does not fit one 80 GB H100, nor two 32 GB RTX 5090s; sharded over an 8-GPU server (ZeRO stage 3, FSDP) the 128.5 GB is 16.1 GB per GPU. 70B's 1.13 TB does not fit an 8-GPU H100 server (640 GB) even sharded: it needs more servers or the optimizer offloaded to CPU memory.

Activations. Every backward rule needs something from the forward pass (a linear layer's weight gradient needs its input, SiLU needs its input, softmax its output), so those tensors stay alive from the moment they are made until backward uses them; they grow with tokens per GPU. Counting every tensor autograd saves in the real model gives 49 bytes per token per hidden unit per layer: 52.7 GB for the 32 layers at 8,192 tokens and micro-batch 1, plus 4.47 GB for the output, because the loss needs the logits in FP32 (128,256 × 4 bytes per token, more than two whole layers). Total 57.1 GB, against the 36.5 GB that the GPT-3 count (34sbh) predicts counted. With plain ("eager") attention each layer also keeps its scores, 32 heads × 8,192 × 8,192 in FP32 and again in BF16: 13.0 GB per layer, 473 GB in all. With full recomputation only each layer's 2h input is kept: 6.6 GB, most of it the logits.

Correction to the old page, and to the parent's calculator
The old notes wrote activations as "sbh × L × c (batch s × seq b ...)": in Korthikanti et al. s is the sequence length and b the micro-batch, and the per-layer constant is 34 + 5as/h (about 34 with FlashAttention, 2 with full recomputation). The parent calculator's "store all, FlashAttention" option used 34sbh, about 1.4 times low for Llama-style models in the Transformers code (49h counted) and without the FP32 logits; since 2026-10-24 it uses this page's count.

Real runs choose per layer. Megatron's selective recomputation recomputes only the attention-score part, cheap in FLOPs and expensive in memory (Korthikanti et al., section 5); with FlashAttention there is nothing quadratic left, so the choice is between keeping the 49h and recomputing layers. Tensor parallelism with sequence parallelism divides the per-layer figure by the TP degree. How checkpointing and sharding work is Distributed Training's subject; activation memory for any model, length and choice is the FLOP and memory ledger tab.

Three differences from GPT-3's 34 explain the 49:

  • SwiGLU. Llama's MLP has three matrices and an intermediate width of 14,336 = 3.5h, and four 3.5h-wide tensors are saved (gate output, up output, SiLU output, their product): 28h against GPT's 18h.
  • RMSNorm in FP32. The reference code upcasts to FP32 before normalising, so each norm saves a 4h FP32 copy plus 2h BF16 copies: 16h for two norms against 4h. A fused norm kernel saves only its input and the per-token scale.
  • Grouped-query attention saves K and V at 8 heads instead of 32 (0.5h each), and there is no dropout.

Fused RMSNorm and SwiGLU kernels bring the real figure down; the counted number is for the reference code.

4. Utilisation: MFU, HFU, and what real runs reach

A real run. Llama 3 405B trained at 430 TFLOP/s per GPU on 8,192 H100s (Llama 3, Table 4). The H100's dense BF16 peak is 989.5 TFLOP/s, so the run kept 430 / 989.5 = 43.5% of the arithmetic the chips could do: its MFU. The other big runs that published enough to check land in the same band:

Large runs keep 30 to 46% of peak; one GPU on big matmuls reaches 75%; the hours on a model card imply far less

Bars: MFU against each chip's dense peak in the format the matmuls ran in; the hatched extension is PaLM's HFU; the dashed line is the best large BF16 GEMM on an H100 (about 720 of 989.5 TFLOP/s, 73%, in SemiAnalysis's independent measurement, Dec 2024). DeepSeek-V3 is shown against the BF16 peak; against the FP8 peak it trained in, 17.6%. Each bar's arithmetic is in the closed block below.

Rearranged, the same formula answers every planning question: time = total FLOPs / (chips × peak × MFU). The parent's calculator checks it against 27 published figures (its "Checks against published figures" table).

Where the other 55 to 60% goes.

  • Matmuls below peak. Even a large BF16 GEMM reaches about 73% of an H100's peak; that ceiling is reached before anything else goes wrong.
  • Memory-bound operators (norms, activations, residual adds, the loss): no matmul, so no MFU, and on an H100 each byte costs as much time as 295 FLOPs (section 2; section 5 counts them).
  • Communication that does not overlap (section 8), pipeline bubbles (idle stages at the start and end of each step), stragglers, data loading.
  • Failures and restarts, which MFU measured in steady state leaves out: Llama 3.1 8B's model card lists 1.46M H100-hours for about 15T tokens, an implied 13.9% if every hour were training.

Recomputation, and why HFU flatters

Section 3's full recomputation holds 23 GB instead of 73 GB by running each layer's forward again during backward. The textbook cost is 8N instead of 6N, 1.33 times the work; the counted cost for Llama 3.1 8B at 8,192 tokens is 1.24 times counted, for three reasons: the output head is not recomputed (only the transformer layers are checkpointed); PyTorch's non-reentrant checkpoint stops recomputing as soon as every saved tensor exists again, so each layer's last matmul (the MLP's down projection, which produces nothing backward needs) is not rerun (PyTorch 2.14 docs: "Non-reentrant checkpoint stops recomputation as soon as all needed intermediate activations have been recomputed"); and FlashAttention's backward already costs 2.5 times its forward, so the recompute is a smaller share. The recompute is 118.7 TFLOP, not the layers' full 149.5 TFLOP forward (30.8 TFLOP of down projections skipped).

MFU counts model FLOPs     HFU counts hardware FLOPs
model FLOPs

hardware FLOPs

Korthikanti et al. define them this way: "model FLOPs are hardware and implementation independent and only depend on the underlying model", while "if an implementation requires activation recomputation ... the hardware FLOPs are going to be larger" (section 6.3). A run that recomputes everything can show high HFU while training no faster: PaLM reported 46.2% MFU and 57.8% HFU. Compare runs by MFU. Recomputation can still make training faster: it frees memory for a larger micro-batch or less model parallelism, which is why PaLM chose it "because this results in higher training throughput at larger batch sizes".

5. Measuring MFU yourself, and predicting it operator by operator

How to measure. Warm up (the first iterations compile kernels and fill allocator caches), then time many steps; synchronise before reading the clock, because GPU calls return before the work finishes (torch.cuda.synchronize(), here torch.mps.synchronize()); take the median, repeat the whole run, and report the spread and what else was running. MFU = model FLOPs of the step / time / dense peak, with the FLOPs from torch.utils.flop_counter.FlopCounterMode or from section 1's formulas; they agree.

Measured here: two decoder layers with the real 8B shapes (436M parameters; the 128K-vocabulary embedding and head left out to fit the shared laptop's memory), FP16, forward and backward, batch 1, PyTorch 2.14.1 on the M1 Pro GPU (MPS backend) measured.

The M1 Pro runs 72 to 75% MFU, and the step takes 14 to 22% longer than its roofline sum

Peak 5.06 TFLOP/s (FP16 fused multiply-add) and bandwidth 165 GB/s are the parent's Roofline lab measurements on this GPU. Script: src/code/m1_step.py.

72 to 75% MFU on one GPU. With large matrices, no communication and no tensor cores (the M1's FP16 runs at the same rate as FP32 through ordinary ALUs, so its ridge point is only about 31 FLOPs per byte), almost every operator is compute-bound and the GPU runs near its own matmul speed. A single GPU on big matmuls reaches high MFU; the losses of section 4 come from elsewhere.

Predicting the step from its operators. The same step was run once more with a TorchDispatchMode recording every operator PyTorch launched (447 per step, 315 of which compute or move data): its FLOPs and the bytes it reads and writes. Each operator gets a roofline time, max(FLOPs / peak, bytes / bandwidth), and the step's prediction is the sum. The measurement takes 14 to 22% longer than the prediction: the overhead of launching each kernel and of matmuls that do not quite reach the FMA peak.

The same operators on an H100: why fusion matters more on faster chips

That projection is a roofline estimate, not a run. torch.compile and hand-written fused kernels (FlashAttention, fused norms, fused cross-entropy) exist to remove exactly this traffic, and it matters wherever the ridge is high: 295 FLOPs per byte for an H100 at BF16, twice that at FP8. Writing such kernels is the Topic: cuda-and-gpu-programming root's subject; timing them honestly is its Profiling, benchmarking and correctness child's.

The M1 runs, step by step (1 min)

Each run is the median of 8 timed steps after 3 warm-up steps; 3 runs, 20 s apart, with the 1-minute load average of the shared machine recorded. At 2,048 tokens the first run hit memory swapping (41.7 s) and is excluded by taking the median of the three runs.

6. Serving: prefill, decode, and tokens per second

A served request has two phases on opposite sides of section 2's ridge. Prefill runs the whole prompt through the model at once: thousands of tokens per weight read, compute-bound. Decode then produces one token per step, and each step must read every weight it uses plus the conversation's KV cache (the keys and values of every earlier token, which attention needs): one token per weight read, memory-bound.

Batching is the whole economics of serving. Each step reads the weights once whatever the batch, so 64 users share the 16 GB read and cost 21 times less per token, at the price of each user waiting 3 times longer per token. The cache does not share: at long contexts each conversation's KV reads dominate and batching stops helping, the reason grouped-query attention, latent attention and FP8 caches exist. Cache sizing is Long context's subject; the step at batch 1 and 64 is animated in the parent's Performance calculator, and tokens per second against cost for any model, chip and batch is the Run and serving planner tab.

Mixture of experts. At batch 1 a decode step reads only the routed experts: DeepSeek-V3 in FP8 reads 37.6 GB of its 671 GB. But 64 tokens with 8 of 256 experts each touch about 222 experts per layer (uniform routing, 256 × (1 − (1 − 8/256)64)), so the step reads 585 GB: an MoE's bandwidth advantage disappears at the batch sizes that make serving cheap, while its FLOP advantage stays derived.

Long prompts: prefill grows faster than the prompt (under a minute)

Llama 3.1 70B with a 32,768-token prompt on 8 H100s at 50% of peak: 1.52 s before the first token, and attention is 23% of those FLOPs (causal count). Prefill time grows faster than linearly with the prompt. Scheduling, paged caches and speculative decoding are Topic: inference-and-serving's.

8. Communication: the same division, for bytes on the wire

On many GPUs every step also moves data, and the arithmetic is section 2's again: count the bytes each GPU sends, divide by the bandwidth of the link it crosses, and ask whether it overlaps with compute. For Llama 3.1 8B on 8 GPUs, data parallelism all-reduces 28.1 GB of BF16 gradients per step (62.5 ms over NVLink 4), once per step and overlapped with backward, so it can cross the slower network between servers; tensor parallelism moves 0.47 GB per layer on the critical path, so it stays inside the NVLink domain. The layouts are Distributed Training's subject and the links and collectives Interconnects and scaling's; the parent calculator's "Why tensor parallelism stays inside the NVLink domain" chart puts numbers on the placement.

Rehearsal (optional)

Mistakes worth knowing and questions to answer before revealing; every answer is computed in the sections above. The parent's Performance calculator has ten more drills.

Common mistakes the sections do not already show (1 min)
Dividing by the sparse peak
Datasheets print 2:4 structured-sparsity figures, twice the dense ones (H100 BF16: 1,979 sparse, 989.5 dense). Training never uses them; MFU against a sparse peak is half what it should be. The parent's Chip atlas marks which is which.
Using 6N at long context
At 32,768 tokens the attention core more than doubles the per-token cost of Llama 3.1 8B (105 against 48 GFLOP, full-square count). State the convention (full or causal) whenever you add it.
Counting the input embedding as compute
It is a lookup: 6.5% of Llama 3.1 8B's parameters do no FLOPs. Labs differ on whether "N" includes it (gpt-oss-120b's card leaves it out of the active count).
Forgetting the logits
With a 128K vocabulary the FP32 logits kept for the loss cost 4.47 GB per 8,192-token sequence. Chunked or fused cross-entropy kernels exist for this reason.
Leaving out goodput and price terms
A cost estimate without failures, restarts and the price's date and terms is a lower bound. Say which.
Drills: commit to an answer, then reveal (4 min)
1. One training step of Llama 3.1 8B on one 8,192-token sequence. How many FLOPs, counting attention?
6N × 8,192 = 395 TFLOP; counted, 492 TFLOP (60.06 GFLOP per token × 8,192). At 40% MFU on one H100 that is 1.24 s.
2. With FlashAttention and no recomputation, how much activation memory does that step keep?
49 bytes × 4,096 × 8,192 tokens per layer = 1.65 GB, × 32 layers = 52.7 GB, plus 4.47 GB of FP32 logits: 57.1 GB. 470 GB is the same step with plain attention.
3. Why can plain (non-flash) attention not train this model at 8,192 tokens on an H100?
32 heads × 8,192 × 8,192 scores, kept in FP32 (softmax output) and BF16 (for the next matmul): 6 bytes each, 12.9 GB per layer, 13.0 GB counted with the repeated K and V. The FLOPs are the same as with FlashAttention's forward.
4. Full activation checkpointing in PyTorch: by how much does the hardware's work exceed the model's?
Counted 1.24 times, not 8/6 = 1.33: the head is not checkpointed, FlashAttention's backward is already 2.5 times its forward, and the non-reentrant checkpoint skips each layer's final down projection.
5. Llama 3 405B ran at 430 TFLOP/s per GPU on 8,192 H100s with 16.8M tokens per step. How long was a step?
16,777,216 × 6 × 405.85e9 / (8,192 × 430e12) = 11.6 s (12.6 s if Meta's FLOP count includes attention). 177 tokens per second per GPU.
6. How many H100s pretrain Llama 3.1 8B on 15T tokens in 30 days at 40% MFU and 90% goodput?
7.2e23 / (989.5e12 × 0.4) / 3,600 = 507K GPU-hours; / 0.9 / 720 hours = 783 GPUs, about $2.25M at $3.99 per hour.
7. Serving Llama 3.1 8B on one H100 at 4,096 tokens of context: how much cheaper per token is batch 64 than batch 1, at the ceilings?
$5.49 against $0.26 per million tokens: 21 times, not 64, because each of the 64 conversations still reads its own 0.54 GB cache every step.
8. DeepSeek-V3 in FP8 decoding a batch of 64: what share of its 671 GB of weights does one step read?
64 tokens touch about 222 of 256 experts per layer: 585 GB, 87% of the weights, against 37.6 GB (5.6%) at batch 1.
10. Your 8B run reaches 25% MFU on 64 H100s. Where do you look, in order?
Single-GPU MFU first (matmul efficiency, memory-bound ops, fusion); then communication that does not overlap (FSDP across servers, TP outside NVLink); then data loading and stragglers. Each has a number from this page to compare with the profiler.

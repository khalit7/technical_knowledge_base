7. Serving: prefill, decode, and tokens per second

The problem. A served request has two phases with opposite limits. Prefill runs the whole prompt through the model at once; decode then produces one token per step, and each step must read every weight it uses plus the conversation's KV cache (the keys and values of every earlier token, which attention needs). The first is limited by arithmetic, the second by memory bandwidth.

time to first token ≈ prefill FLOPs / (peak × efficiency)     time per output token ≈ bytes read per step / bandwidth
prefill FLOPs
2N per prompt token, plus attention over the prompt (causal: about half the score square)
bytes per step
weights used (all of them for a dense model) plus every sequence's KV cache

The lesson in the table: batching is the whole economics of serving. Each step reads the weights once whatever the batch, so 64 users share the 16 GB read and cost 21 times less per token, at the price of each user waiting 3 times longer per token. The cache does not share: at long contexts each conversation's KV reads dominate and batching stops helping, the reason grouped-query attention, latent attention and FP8 caches exist. The physical side of the KV cache (sizes for 13 real configs, paging) is the sibling {{Memory technology|n:3f05c17b0d0d818ab063e327e3d7009f}} page; the step at batch 1 and 64 is animated in the parent's {{Performance calculator|n:3c65c17b0d0d8118beeefaed56da6f8e}}.

Long prompts. Llama 3.1 70B with a 32,768-token prompt on 8 H100s at 50% of peak: 1.52 s before the first token, and attention is 23% of those FLOPs (causal count). Prefill time grows faster than linearly with the prompt.

Mixture of experts. At batch 1 a decode step reads only the routed experts: DeepSeek-V3 in FP8 reads 37.6 GB of its 671 GB. But 64 tokens with 8 of 256 experts each touch about 222 experts per layer (uniform routing, 256 × (1 − (1 − 8/256)64)), so the step reads 585 GB: an MoE's bandwidth advantage disappears at the batch sizes that make serving cheap, while its FLOP advantage stays derived.

8. Communication: bytes per step for each kind of parallelism

The problem. On many GPUs every step also moves data. The arithmetic is the same each time: count the bytes each GPU sends, divide by the bandwidth of the link it crosses, and ask whether it overlaps with compute.

Ring collectives follow NCCL's bus-bandwidth model ({{nccl-tests PERFORMANCE.md|https://github.com/NVIDIA/nccl-tests/blob/master/doc/PERFORMANCE.md}}). The MoE row is an upper bound: tokens whose experts sit on the same GPU do not move, and DeepSeek-V3 limits each token to 4 nodes.

The pattern decides placement. Data-parallel traffic is per step and overlaps with backward, so it can cross the slower network between servers. Tensor-parallel traffic is per layer and blocks compute, so it stays inside the NVLink domain; expert parallelism's all-to-all is per layer too. The parent calculator's "Why tensor parallelism stays inside the NVLink domain" chart puts numbers on it; the sibling {{Interconnects and scaling|n:3c65c17b0d0d81808563fd41fb163af0}} page covers the links and collectives, and {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}} the layouts.

Headline GPU-hours are a budget, not a measurement of efficiency. DeepSeek-V3's $5.576M is 2.788M H800-hours at the $2 per hour DeepSeek assumes, and excludes "prior research and ablation experiments" by its own statement (the {{DeepSeek-V3 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}} checks the bill).

Smaller jobs. Fine-tuning the 8B on 1 billion tokens with FSDP on one 8-GPU H100 server takes 4.2 hours at 40% MFU: about $135 at list price (the parent's worked example).

Serving capacity. To produce one million output tokens per minute (16,667 per second) from Llama 3.1 8B at 4,096 tokens of context: about 4 H100s at batch 64, or 83 at batch 1, at the bandwidth ceiling. Real deployments reach a fraction of the ceiling and must also run prefill, so plan from measured throughput once you have it.

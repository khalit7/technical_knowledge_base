1. FLOPs: where 6ND comes from, and when it is wrong

The problem. To know how long training takes you need the number of floating-point operations (FLOPs: one add or one multiply each). Counting them operator by operator is tedious; one rule gets within a few percent for most runs, and you need to know when it does not.

A matrix multiply. Multiplying an (m × k) matrix by a (k × n) matrix makes m × n outputs, each a sum of k products: k multiplies and k adds, so 2mkn FLOPs. In a transformer almost all the work is of this kind: tokens (m) times a weight matrix (k × n).

the backward pass multiplies twice for every forward multiply: once for the gradient of the input (to keep backpropagating), once for the gradient of the weight

Each weight meets each token once going forward (2 FLOPs) and twice going back (4). The math topic's {{Matrix calculus and backprop|n:3f05c17b0d0d817e9892e01088179c93}} page derives the factor 2 and measures it on a small model (exactly 2.00 for the matrix products). The rule is Kaplan et al.'s ({{Scaling Laws, section 2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.08361}}), and PaLM's Appendix B made it the basis of MFU ({{PaLM|https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311}}).

Training FLOPs per token by part, against 6N (dashed). The bars are PyTorch's count on the real model at each sequence length. The FLOP and memory ledger tab draws it for any length and for 70B and 405B.

Four things move it away from 6N:

  • The input embedding is not a multiply. It looks a row up. Its 525M parameters are 6.5% of N, so 6N over-counts by that much at short sequences. The output head is a multiply and counts.
  • Attention over the context. Each token's query meets every earlier key: forward 4 × T × h FLOPs per layer, growing with the sequence while 6N does not. At 2,048 tokens the two effects nearly cancel (+1.3% over 6N); at 8,192 attention adds 24.7%, at 32,768 118%, at 131,072 it is five times 6N.
  • Causal masking. A causal kernel skips blocks above the diagonal, about half the score square. Counting full squares (PaLM's 12LHQT, PyTorch's counter) gives the upper figure; the work done is nearer half the attention term. At 8,192 tokens the attention term is 26.7% of 6N counted in full, 14.3% of the matmul-only 6N counted causally: both conventions are in use, so say which you mean.
  • FlashAttention's backward recomputes the scores instead of reading them back, so its attention backward is 2.5 times its forward, not 2 times (counted: 10.74 against 4.29 GFLOP per token).
Conventions differ, even on the parent page
The parent's calculator adds attention as PaLM's 12LHQT in full (15% for 70B at 8,192); its Reading tab quotes 14% for 8B at 8,192, which is the causal half over matmul-only 6N. Both are right under their own convention. Counted in full with FlashAttention's backward, the whole step is 24.7% above 6N for 8B and 16.3% for 70B.

Mixture of experts. Each token passes through only k of E experts, so FLOPs follow the active parameters and memory follows the total: DeepSeek-V3 is 671.0B total and 37.55B active, 17.9 times fewer FLOPs per token than a dense model of the same size (parameter counts recounted from its config by the parent's calculator). The router's own FLOPs are tiny; the cost moves to the network (section 8).

Predict: training Llama 3.1 8B at 131,072-token sequences. How many times 6N does one token cost?
285.5 GFLOP per token counted, against 48.2 for 6N: 5.9 times. The attention core alone is 241 GFLOP per token (counted in full; a causal kernel does about half). This is why long-context training runs as a short final stage (Llama 3 used 131,072 tokens only at the end, at 38% MFU) and why context parallelism exists.

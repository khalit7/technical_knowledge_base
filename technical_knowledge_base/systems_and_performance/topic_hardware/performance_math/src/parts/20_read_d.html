4. Utilisation: MFU, HFU, and what real runs reach

A real run. Llama 3 405B trained at 430 TFLOP/s per GPU on 8,192 H100s ({{Llama 3, Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3}}). The H100's dense BF16 peak is 989.5 TFLOP/s, so the run kept 430 / 989.5 = 43.5% of the arithmetic the chips could do: its MFU. The other big runs that published enough to check land in the same band:

Rearranged, the same formula answers every planning question: time = total FLOPs / (chips × peak × MFU). The parent's calculator checks it against 27 published figures (its "Checks against published figures" table).

Where the other 55 to 60% goes.

  • Matmuls below peak. Even a large BF16 GEMM reaches about 73% of an H100's peak; that ceiling is reached before anything else goes wrong.
  • Memory-bound operators (norms, activations, residual adds, the loss): no matmul, so no MFU, and on an H100 each byte costs as much time as 295 FLOPs (section 2; section 5 counts them).
  • Communication that does not overlap (section 8), pipeline bubbles (idle stages at the start and end of each step), stragglers, data loading.
  • Failures and restarts, which MFU measured in steady state leaves out: Llama 3.1 8B's model card lists 1.46M H100-hours for about 15T tokens, an implied 13.9% if every hour were training.

Recomputation, and why HFU flatters

Section 3's full recomputation holds 23 GB instead of 73 GB by running each layer's forward again during backward. The textbook cost is 8N instead of 6N, 1.33 times the work; the counted cost for Llama 3.1 8B at 8,192 tokens is 1.24 times counted, for three reasons: the output head is not recomputed (only the transformer layers are checkpointed); PyTorch's non-reentrant checkpoint stops recomputing as soon as every saved tensor exists again, so each layer's last matmul (the MLP's down projection, which produces nothing backward needs) is not rerun ({{PyTorch 2.14 docs|https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/checkpoint.html}}: "Non-reentrant checkpoint stops recomputation as soon as all needed intermediate activations have been recomputed"); and FlashAttention's backward already costs 2.5 times its forward, so the recompute is a smaller share. The recompute is 118.7 TFLOP, not the layers' full 149.5 TFLOP forward (30.8 TFLOP of down projections skipped).

MFU counts model FLOPs     HFU counts hardware FLOPs
model FLOPs
what the model needs for one forward and one backward pass, however it is implemented (6N plus attention)
hardware FLOPs
what the chips actually executed, including recomputation

Korthikanti et al. define them this way: "model FLOPs are hardware and implementation independent and only depend on the underlying model", while "if an implementation requires activation recomputation ... the hardware FLOPs are going to be larger" ({{section 6.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05198}}). A run that recomputes everything can show high HFU while training no faster: PaLM reported 46.2% MFU and 57.8% HFU. Compare runs by MFU. Recomputation can still make training faster: it frees memory for a larger micro-batch or less model parallelism, which is why PaLM chose it "because this results in higher training throughput at larger batch sizes".

Selective recomputation's ratio. 5. Measuring MFU yourself, and predicting it operator by operator

How to measure. Warm up (the first iterations compile kernels and fill allocator caches), then time many steps; synchronise before reading the clock, because GPU calls return before the work finishes (torch.cuda.synchronize(), here torch.mps.synchronize()); take the median, repeat the whole run, and report the spread and what else was running. MFU = model FLOPs of the step / time / dense peak, with the FLOPs from torch.utils.flop_counter.FlopCounterMode or from section 1's formulas; they agree.

Measured here: two decoder layers with the real 8B shapes (436M parameters; the 128K-vocabulary embedding and head left out to fit the shared laptop's memory), FP16, forward and backward, batch 1, PyTorch 2.14.1 on the M1 Pro GPU (MPS backend) measured.

The M1 Pro runs 72 to 75% MFU, and the step takes 14 to 22% longer than its roofline sum

Peak 5.06 TFLOP/s (FP16 fused multiply-add) and bandwidth 165 GB/s are the parent's {{Roofline lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d8118beeefaed56da6f8e}} measurements on this GPU. Script: src/code/m1_step.py.

72 to 75% MFU on one GPU. With large matrices, no communication and no tensor cores (the M1's FP16 runs at the same rate as FP32 through ordinary ALUs, so its ridge point is only about 31 FLOPs per byte), almost every operator is compute-bound and the GPU runs near its own matmul speed. A single GPU on big matmuls reaches high MFU; the losses of section 4 come from elsewhere.

Predicting the step from its operators. The same step was run once more with a TorchDispatchMode recording every operator PyTorch launched (447 per step, 315 of which compute or move data): its FLOPs and the bytes it reads and writes. Each operator gets a roofline time, max(FLOPs / peak, bytes / bandwidth), and the step's prediction is the sum. The measurement takes 14 to 22% longer than the prediction: the overhead of launching each kernel and of matmuls that do not quite reach the FMA peak.

The same operators on an H100: why fusion matters more on faster chips

That projection is a roofline estimate, not a run. torch.compile and hand-written fused kernels (FlashAttention, fused norms, fused cross-entropy) exist to remove exactly this traffic, and it matters wherever the ridge is high: 295 FLOPs per byte for an H100 at BF16, twice that at FP8. Writing such kernels is the {{Topic: cuda-and-gpu-programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c39f34d5e070d783c1}} root's subject; timing them honestly is its {{Profiling, benchmarking and correctness|n:3c65c17b0d0d813da895ffee55b0457c}} child's.

4. Pods: a torus of chips, rewired with mirrors

The problem. One chip holds neither a big model nor its training state (the root's 8B example needs 128.5 GB of weights, gradients and Adam state). Chips must exchange gradients and activations every step, and the faster that exchange, the more of the work can be split across them. A GPU cluster joins 8 or 72 GPUs through NVLink switches and everything else through network cards and switches (the root's {{"Joining chips" section|n:3c65c17b0d0d8118beeefaed56da6f8e}}). Google took another route.

How ICI works. Each TPU has its own inter-chip interconnect (ICI) ports wired straight to neighbouring chips: no network card, no switch in between. Chips sit on a grid; each talks to 4 neighbours (a 2D grid: v2, v3, v5e, v6e) or 6 (a 3D grid: v4, v5p, Ironwood). If the last chip of every row is also wired back to the first, the grid becomes a torus (a doughnut: no edges), which roughly halves the longest path and lets a ring of chips send data both ways at once. What it changes for collectives. How To Scale Your Model gives a simple rule for a TPU axis with a wraparound: gathering an array of V bytes along one axis takes about V / Wbidi, where Wbidi is one link's two-way bandwidth (1.8 × 1011 B/s on v5p, v6e and TPU7x), independent of how many chips are on the axis; an all-reduce costs twice that; using 3 axes at once divides the time by 3; and an axis without the wraparound "typically doubles the time of most communication" ({{scaling book, sharding|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/sharding/}}; {{TPUs|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/tpus/}}). For the root's 8B model, all-reducing its 16.06 GB of bf16 gradients along one 16-chip v5p axis takes about 2 × 16.06 GB / 180 GB/s = 0.178 s, 0.059 s over all three axes, and 0.357 s without wraparound derived, against 62.5 ms over 8 H100s' NVLink in the root.

The rule against a measurement (the scaling book's worked example)

The book's own worked example, an all-gather of 34 MB over a 4-chip v5e axis, predicts 378 μs (it prints 377) with a wraparound and 560 μs without one; v5e has no wraparound on a 4-chip axis, and the profile it shows measured 680 μs measured by Google.

Optical circuit switches. A torus wired once is fragile: one dead chip breaks a ring, and the whole machine must be cabled before any of it can be used. TPU v4 builds the pod from 4 × 4 × 4 cubes of 64 chips (one rack, 16 hosts), electrically wired inside, and connects the cubes' faces through optical circuit switches (OCS): MEMS mirrors that steer light from one fibre to another and switch in milliseconds. Each cube face has 16 links, 96 per cube; opposite faces go to the same switch to close the torus, so 6 × 16 / 2 = 48 switches of 136 ports join 64 cubes into 4,096 chips. The mirrors are not a packet network: they only set which cube's face is plugged into which, like a plugboard. That buys four things, all from the {{TPU v4 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01433}}:

  • Slices of any shape: a job asks for, say, 4 × 8 × 16 chips, and the switches wire the cubes it gets into exactly that torus, wherever they sit in the hall.
  • Routing around failures: a cube with a dead host is swapped out by re-pointing mirrors; with 1,024 hosts each up 99.0% to 99.9% of the time, a large slice is otherwise rarely all healthy.
  • Twisted tori: some links are re-pointed to shorten the longest paths, which raised all-to-all throughput; 40% of slices of 64 chips or more chose a twisted topology in Google's sample.
  • Cost: the switches and optics are "<5% of system cost and <3% of system power".

Slices smaller than a cube get no optical wraparound, which is why the scaling book warns that a 2 × 2 × 4 slice is slower per byte than a 4 × 4 × 4. Ironwood keeps the scheme at 9,216 chips: 144 cubes of 64, 1.77 PB of HBM in one pod, ICI at 9.6 Tb/s (1.2 TB/s) per chip ({{Google Cloud, Nov 6 2025|https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/compute/ironwood-tpus-and-new-axion-based-vms-for-your-ai-workloads}}).

Rules and sources for the slice builder

Wraparound: "Any multiple of a full cube (e.g. 4x4x4 or 4x4x8) will have wraparounds provided by the optical switches"; "a 2x2x4 won't have any wraparounds"; without one "typically doubles the time of most communication" ({{How To Scale Your Model, TPUs|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/tpus/}}). Here an axis wraps when every dimension is a multiple of 4. Per-link bandwidth one way / both ways: v4 45 / 90 GB/s, v5p and TPU7x 90 / 180 GB/s (scaling book; the TPU v4 paper gives "6 links @ 50 GB/s"). All-reduce time = 2V / Σ W over the chosen axes, W = both-way bandwidth with wraparound and one-way without, and at least 1 μs per hop along the axes (the book's Tmin) derived; real collectives reach about 95% of the link figure (the book's measurement on v5e). Hosts hold 4 chips (2 × 2 × 1). Optical links: 96 per cube (16 per face, TPU v4 paper). Largest pods: v4 4,096 chips (16 × 16 × 16), v5p 8,960, TPU7x 9,216.

Beyond a pod. Pods (or slices) join over the ordinary data-centre network, DCN, at about 6.25 GB/s per chip (12.5 on v6e and TPU7x), more than 10 times slower than one ICI link ({{scaling book|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/tpus/}}). So, as on GPUs, only data parallelism (the gradient all-reduce, once per step) crosses that boundary ("multislice"), and everything chatty stays on ICI.

The mesh is the machine
Because the pod is a grid, TPU programs describe parallelism as a grid too: a JAX Mesh with named axes ('data', 'model') laid onto the physical torus, and every array annotated with which axes split it. Choosing which model axis goes on which physical axis is choosing which links carry which traffic.

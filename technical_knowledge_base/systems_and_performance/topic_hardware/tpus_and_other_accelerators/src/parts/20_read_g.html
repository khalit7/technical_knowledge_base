7. Groq: no DRAM, and a compiler that knows every cycle

The bet. When a model generates one token at a time for one user, every weight is read from memory for every token, and the chip waits on memory bandwidth (the root's decode section). Groq's answer is to keep the weights in on-chip SRAM, spread across hundreds of chips, and to remove everything in the chip whose timing is unpredictable, so a compiler can schedule the whole computation, chip-to-chip transfers included, cycle by cycle.

The first chip (TSP). From Groq's ISCA 2020 paper ({{Abts et al., "Think Fast"|https://web.archive.org/web/2021/https://groq.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/ISCA-TSP.pdf}}, archived copy): a 25 × 29 mm chip in 14 nm at a nominal 900 MHz, 26.8 billion transistors, "220 MiBytes" of SRAM (which is the "230 MB" of Groq's marketing: 220 MiB = 230.7 MB), and a peak of "820 TeraOps/sec" in int8 at 1 GHz. Its matrix units are "four (4) MXM arrays of 320×320 MACC" (large matrix arrays again), beside vector, switching and memory "slices" through which data streams east and west. It achieves determinism "by eliminating all reactive elements in the hardware (e.g. arbiters, and caches)": no cache misses, no arbitration, so the compiler knows exactly when every byte arrives. Chips connect directly, 16 links per chip, and a 2022 paper describes systems of up to 10,440 chips in which the compiler also schedules the network, avoiding "the latency variation introduced by dynamic contention for network links" ({{Abts et al., ISCA 2022|https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://groq.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/GroqISCAPaper2022_ASoftwareDefinedTensorStreamingMultiprocessorForLargeScaleMachineLearning-1.pdf}}).

Now part of NVIDIA's rack. On December 24, 2025 Groq announced "a non-exclusive licensing agreement with Nvidia for Groq's inference technology", with its founder and president joining NVIDIA and Groq continuing independently ({{Groq newsroom|https://groq.com/newsroom/groq-and-nvidia-enter-non-exclusive-inference-technology-licensing-agreement-to-accelerate-ai-inference-at-global-scale}}; the reported price is unconfirmed). NVIDIA's "Groq 3 LPX" rack, "now in full production" (August 24, 2026), holds 256 LPUs with 128 GB of on-chip SRAM, 0.5 GB per chip derived, and 640 TB/s of scale-up bandwidth; it sits beside Vera Rubin NVL72 racks so that "Rubin GPUs handle large-scale context processing while LPX accelerates latency-sensitive decode" ({{NVIDIA, Mar 16 2026|https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/nvidia-vera-rubin-platform}}; {{NVIDIA blog, Aug 24 2026|https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/vera-rubin-lpx-spectrum-x-nvlink-fusion/}}) vendor. That split (prefill on HBM GPUs, decode on SRAM chips) is the clearest statement of what an SRAM machine is for.

The cost: chip count. SRAM is fast but small per chip. Counting only the bf16 weights, how many chips does each design need just to hold the model?

Chips = ceil(parameters × bytes / memory per chip) derived, weights only: the KV cache, activations and (for training) gradients and optimizer state come on top, and real deployments add chips for speed. Parameters from the models' safetensors index on Hugging Face (8,030,261,248; 70,553,706,496; 405,853,388,800). Memory per chip: Groq TSP 220 MiB (ISCA 2020), Groq 3 LPU 128 GB / 256 (NVIDIA), Cerebras WSE-3 44 GB SRAM, H100 80 GB, B200 180 GB (HGX), MI355X 288 GB, TPU7x 192 GiB (the root's Chip atlas). What it means for you. Groq is an inference service (GroqCloud) and a rack you buy from NVIDIA, not something you train on: its papers say the chip supports "model training with floating point", but Groq sells inference ("Groq makes inference work at scale"). For a training engineer it matters as the design point that answers the question "what if decode never touched DRAM?"

8. Cerebras: do not cut the wafer

The bet. Chips are cut from a 300 mm silicon wafer, and everything slow in a cluster happens when data leaves a chip. Cerebras keeps the whole wafer as one chip. The WSE-3 is 46,225 mm2 (an H100 die is 814 mm2, {{NVIDIA|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/}}) with 900,000 cores and 44 GB of SRAM at 21 PB/s, joined by a 2D mesh on the wafer itself ({{Cerebras, Mar 13 2024|https://www.cerebras.ai/press-release/cerebras-announces-third-generation-wafer-scale-engine}}; the 125 PF fp16 headline is a sparse figure, see the root's Chip atlas).

How a wafer survives defects. Every wafer has defects, which is why normal chips are small. Cerebras makes each core tiny, "approximately 0.05mm2" against about 6 mm2 for an H100 SM, builds 970,000 and uses 900,000 (93%), and routes the mesh around dead cores, so a defect costs one tiny core instead of the chip ({{Cerebras blog, Jan 13 2025|https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/100x-defect-tolerance-how-cerebras-solved-the-yield-problem}}). On the previous generation each core had 48 KB of its own SRAM and four fp16 multiply-add units, and computation is "triggered by the data": a core runs when a 32-bit message ("wavelet") arrives from a neighbour ({{Hot Chips 2022 talk, Aug 29 2022|https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/cerebras-architecture-deep-dive-first-look-inside-the-hw-sw-co-design-for-deep-learning}}). Per-core SRAM on WSE-3 is not published; 44 GB / 900,000 = 48.9 KB derived.

Training: weights streamed in, never stored. A frontier model does not fit in 44 GB, so for training Cerebras does the opposite of a GPU cluster. The weights and optimizer state live off the wafer in a memory appliance (MemoryX); the model runs "one layer at a time": a broadcast fabric (SwarmX) sends a layer's weights to every wafer, each wafer runs its share of the batch (data parallelism), and on the way back SwarmX "aggregates (sums together) the gradients from multiple WSEs" and MemoryX updates the weights ({{Cerebras weight streaming|https://training-docs.cerebras.ai/rel-2.5.0/concepts/weight-streaming-execution.md}}). Activations never leave the wafer; only weights and gradients cross the network. There is no tensor or pipeline parallelism to choose: model size is a property of MemoryX (Cerebras claims models "up to 24 trillion parameters" for CS-3, in the same Mar 13 2024 press release) vendor.

Inference: the model on the wafers. For serving, the weights sit in SRAM across several wafers, split at layer boundaries: Cerebras said "70B models fit on as few as four systems" in 16-bit ({{Cerebras, Aug 27 2024|https://www.cerebras.ai/blog/introducing-cerebras-inference-ai-at-instant-speed}}), which the chart above reproduces independently: 141.1 GB / 44 GB rounds up to 4 derived. The payoff is speed per user: on independent measurement, Artificial Analysis listed Cerebras at 1,740.8 output tokens per second on gpt-oss-120B, the fastest provider shown, against 472.6 for Groq ({{Artificial Analysis|https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/gpt-oss-120b/providers}}, past 72 hours, fetched 2026-10-05).

What it means for your training code. PyTorch through Cerebras's own library: import cerebras.pytorch as cstorch, cstorch.compile(model, backend) (compiled after the first iteration, like torch.compile) and Cerebras-compliant optimizers from cstorch.optim, mostly on models from its Model Zoo; custom kernels in CSL, its own language for programming individual cores ({{Cerebras SDK|https://sdk.cerebras.ai/}}). A CS-3 system draws up to 27 kW, is bought or rented whole, and clusters reach 2,048 of them.

2. Inside a TPU chip

A GPU has about a hundred small cores (SMs) each running thousands of threads. A TPU chip has one or two big cores, called TensorCores, and no threads at all. Each TensorCore holds:

MXUThe matrix multiply unit: a 128 × 128 systolic array (256 × 256 on v6e), 2 to 4 per TensorCore. Inputs bf16 (int8 and, on Ironwood, fp8), accumulation in fp32. It "performs one bf16[8,128] @ bf16[128,128] -> f32[8,128] matrix multiply every 8 cycles", about 5 × 1013 FLOP/s per MXU at 1.5 GHz on v5e.
VPUThe vector unit: a 2D grid of 8 × 128 lanes (8 "sublanes" by 128 "lanes") for everything elementwise: activations, normalisation, the exponentials and sums inside softmax, reductions. Much slower than the MXU, so a model that is not mostly matmuls is slow on a TPU.
VMEMA software-managed on-chip scratchpad (128 MiB on v5e; 16 MiB per core plus a 128 MiB shared CMEM on v4). Every tensor is copied from HBM into VMEM before the core can touch it; VMEM bandwidth is about 22 times HBM's.
Scalar unit, SMEMA small scalar core that runs the control flow and issues the wide instructions (the TPU is a VLIW machine: one long instruction, 322 bits on TPU v2, drives many units at once, per {{CACM 2020|https://doi.org/10.1145/3360307}}).
HBMThe chip's main memory: 16 GB (v5e) to 192 GB (Ironwood). Streamed through VMEM into the MXU, pipelined so the copies overlap the arithmetic.
SparseCoreSeparate dataflow processors for embedding lookups (scatter and gather over huge tables), which a systolic array is bad at. In TPU v4 they sped up embedding-heavy models 5x to 7x "yet use only 5% of die area and power"; part of every TPU since v2 per the same paper; 4 per chip on v4, v5p and Ironwood.

Sources: {{How To Scale Your Model, TPUs|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/tpus/}} (MXU, VPU, VMEM, 22x); {{TPU v4 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01433}} (v4 VMEM and CMEM, SparseCore, 4 per chip); {{v5p|https://cloud.google.com/tpu/docs/v5p}} and {{TPU7x|https://docs.cloud.google.com/tpu/docs/tpu7x}} docs (SparseCores). Chips cited by their Cloud names: v5e, v5p, v6e (Trillium), TPU7x (Ironwood).

No caches, no warps, no occupancy. The biggest difference from a GPU is who decides what is on chip. A GPU has hardware caches and hides memory latency by switching between thousands of threads; a TPU has a scratchpad and no threads, so the compiler (XLA) decides, ahead of time, every copy from HBM into VMEM and every instruction, and pipelines them so the MXU never waits. Performance on a TPU is a compiler problem, not an occupancy problem. That is also why shapes must be static: the schedule is computed for one shape, and a new shape means a new compilation. How you write kernels when the compiler is not enough (Pallas) is on {{Topic: jax-and-tpu|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ecbc04eea6aa45ea1a}}.

Two TensorCores, one chip. On v4, v5p and v6e the chip's cores share memory and appear to software as one accelerator with twice the FLOPs ("megacore"). Ironwood is built from two chiplets (two dies in one package), each with its own TensorCore, joined by a high-bandwidth die-to-die link instead ({{scaling book|https://jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book/tpus/}}; {{TPU7x docs|https://docs.cloud.google.com/tpu/docs/tpu7x}}).

Does not reconcile: TPU v6e
The v6e docs say "Each v6e chip contains one TensorCore. Each TensorCore has 2 matrix-multiply units (MXU)", and the scaling book says the MXU is 256 × 256. Two such arrays need a 3.5 GHz clock to reach the published 918 TFLOPS, far above any TPU clock Google has published (v5e: 1.5 GHz). Google's launch post says Trillium got its 4.7 times over v5e "by expanding the size of matrix multiply units and increasing the clock speed" ({{Google Cloud, May 14 2024|https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/compute/introducing-trillium-6th-gen-tpus}}) without numbers. One of the published pieces (the MXU count, the array size, or what an MXU does per cycle) is incomplete; the page shows the gap rather than picking one. Ironwood's MXU size and clock are not published either.

3. Eight generations, two tracks

Google has built a TPU roughly every year or two since 2015, and since v5 in two tracks: an "e" chip (efficiency: one TensorCore, a 2D torus, 256-chip pods, cheaper per hour) and a "p" chip (performance: two TensorCores, a 3D torus, pods of thousands). Ironwood folds both into one chip.

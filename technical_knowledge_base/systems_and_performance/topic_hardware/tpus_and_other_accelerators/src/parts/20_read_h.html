9. Other bets: Gaudi, SambaNova, Etched and in-house chips

Four more answers to the same two questions, each with its bet, its cost and who can get it. Each is a variation on a mechanism above, so a sentence or two places it.

Intel Gaudi 3: systolic arrays, and Ethernet for everything. Two compute dies with 8 matrix engines (MMEs), each "a large output stationary systolic array: 256x256 MAC structure w/ FP32 accumulators" doing "64k MACs/cycle for BF16 and FP8"; 64 programmable VLIW cores (TPCs) for everything that is not a matmul; 96 MB of on-die SRAM; 128 GB of HBM2e at 3.67 TB/s; 1,835 dense BF16 TFLOPS, which implies a clock of 1,835 × 1012 / (8 × 65,536 × 2) = 1.75 GHz derived; the clock is not on the slides. Output stationary is the other layout from section 1: each cell keeps its partial sum while both operands flow past. The bet is the network: each chip carries 24 × 200 GbE RDMA ports, and "Gaudi NICs are used both for scale-up and for scale-out", so an 8-card node needs no switch and no proprietary link: 67.2 Tb/s scale-up and 9.6 Tb/s scale-out per node, which works out to 21 ports per card to its 7 peers (3 each) and 3 to the network (8 × 21 × 200 Gb/s × 2 directions = 67.2) derived ({{Kaplan, Intel, Hot Chips 2024|https://www.hc2024.hotchips.org/assets/program/conference/day1/60_HC2024.Intel.RomanKaplan.Gaudi3-0826.pdf}}) vendor. The cost is software and a short road ahead: Intel said on January 30 2025 that Falcon Shores, the planned successor, would only be an internal test chip ({{TechCrunch|https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/30/intel-wont-bring-its-falcon-shores-ai-chip-to-market}}). Gaudi 3 is bought as cards or servers; its software suite plugs into the main frameworks, and a graph compiler schedules the MMEs and TPCs in parallel (same slides).

SambaNova: a dataflow chip with three tiers of memory. The SN40L RDU (reconfigurable dataflow unit) maps a whole chain of operators onto the chip at once: "streaming dataflow executes operators as a coarse-grained pipeline", fusing "hundreds of complex operations with arbitrary access patterns into a single kernel call", so intermediate tensors pass from unit to unit in SRAM instead of going back to HBM between kernels. Its compute units are each "configurable as an output stationary systolic array or as a pipelined SIMD core". The memory bet: "520 MiB of on-chip PMU SRAM, 64 GiB of co-packaged HBM, and up to 1.5 TiB of DDR DRAM" per socket, 638 BF16 TFLOPS, 8 sockets per node, so many models (Samba-CoE: 150 experts, a trillion parameters) wait in DDR and are swapped into HBM when called; SambaNova reports 3.7 times a DGX H100 on that workload and up to 19 times smaller machine footprint ({{Prabhakar et al. 2024, arXiv:2405.07518|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07518}}) vendor. It is sold as an inference cloud (SambaCloud) and as racks.

SambaNova's current page: SN50 (dated, fetched 2026-10-23)

SambaNova's {{RDU page|https://sambanova.ai/rdu}} lists a fifth-generation SN50: 1,600 BF16 and 3,200 FP8 TFLOPS, 432 MB of on-chip SRAM, 64 GB of HBM2E, up to 512 GB of DDR5, 8 chips per node and scale-up to 256 RDUs; no launch date. The same table gives its predecessor (SN40) 16 chips per node and up to 512 GB of DDR5, against the paper's 8 sockets per node and up to 1.5 TiB per socket; the page does not say whether its DDR figure is per chip or per node, so the two are not reconciled here. vendor unconfirmed

Etched Sohu: one model architecture in silicon. The furthest bet from a GPU: an inference chip that runs only transformers. Etched's June 2024 announcement (now offline; quoted on {{LessWrong, Jun 26 2024|https://lesswrong.com/posts/qhpB9NjcCHjdNDsMG/new-fast-transformer-inference-asic-sohu-by-etched}}) claimed 500,000 tokens per second from an 8-chip server on Llama 3 70B in fp8 with 2,048 input and 128 output tokens. That is a vendor claim with no independent measurement; memory, clock and availability are unconfirmed. The cost of the bet is plain: a model family that is not a transformer cannot run at all.

In-house chips you cannot rent. Trainium is the in-house chip you can rent; Microsoft's Maia 300 and OpenAI's Jalapeño (with Broadcom) are built for their owners' own serving, with vendor figures only.

Maia 300 and Jalapeño in numbers (vendor claims, dated)

Microsoft's Maia 300 (shown Oct 12 2026): 288 GB of HBM4 at 18 TB/s, 96 accelerators per liquid-cooled rack, and, as Microsoft's claim, "40% more tokens per megawatt" than leading GPU systems, with deployments starting in 2027 and no rental to outside customers ({{StorageReview|https://www.storagereview.com/news/microsoft-maia-300-hbm4-n2p-chiplet-96-accelerator-rack}}). OpenAI's Jalapeño, designed with Broadcom and in qualification at its B0 stepping: an inference chip of one compute die plus an I/O chiplet, 216 GiB of HBM4 at 15.4 TB/s, 700 W peak, 128 per local domain and 2,048 per system; OpenAI claims 1.5 to 1.9 times the performance per watt of GB200 and GB300 at peak throughput ({{interview with OpenAI's Richard Ho, Sep 30 2026|https://morethanmoore.substack.com/p/interview-with-richard-ho-openai}}). Both sets of figures are the vendors' own; neither chip has an independent measurement, and neither changes your training code unless you work for its owner.

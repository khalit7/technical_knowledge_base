The root page, {{Topic: hardware|n:3c65c17b0d0d8118beeefaed56da6f8e}}, follows one Llama 3.1 8B training step down to NVIDIA GPUs. This page asks how the other machines run the same work. Its running example is one matrix multiply from that step: the gate projection of one layer for one 8,192-token sequence, activations (8,192 × 4,096) times weights (4,096 × 14,336), 9.62 × 1011 FLOPs. Every design below is an answer to the same three questions: where is the multiply done, where do the weights wait, and how do chips talk.

How to read this page. Section 1 builds the systolic array from zero, since the TPU and Trainium are built around one and every other design answers the same question differently. Sections 2 to 4 are the TPU in depth: the chip, its generations, and pods. Sections 5 to 8 take AMD, Trainium, Groq and Cerebras in turn, each with its bet, its cost, and what it changes in your code. Section 9 puts those side by side for a training job, and section 10 lists mistakes and interview questions. The software for TPUs (JAX, XLA, Pallas, sharding) is on {{Topic: jax-and-tpu|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ecbc04eea6aa45ea1a}}; for GPUs, including AMD's ROCm, on {{Topic: cuda-and-gpu-programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c39f34d5e070d783c1}}.

Predict before you read: the running example's weight matrix has 59 million numbers and the multiply performs 481 billion multiply-adds. If every multiply-add fetched its weight from memory, how many times would each weight be fetched?
8,192 times, once per token (and each activation 14,336 times, once per output column). Every design on this page is a different way to avoid most of those fetches; the systolic array's way is in section 1.

1. Systolic arrays from zero

The problem. Take the running example: the gate projection of one Llama 3.1 8B layer for one 8,192-token sequence, an 8,192 × 4,096 matrix of activations X times a 4,096 × 14,336 weight matrix W. That is 2 × 8,192 × 4,096 × 14,336 = 9.62 × 1011 FLOPs derived, but only 34 million activations and 59 million weights. Every weight is used 8,192 times (once per token) and every activation 14,336 times (once per output column). The arithmetic is cheap; what costs energy and time is fetching the operands each time they are used.

How cheap? Mark Horowitz's table of rough energy costs at 45 nm (Horowitz, ISSCC 2014, Figure 1.1.9; old process, so read the ratios, not the absolute numbers): a 16-bit floating-point multiply costs 1.1 pJ and a 32-bit add 0.9 pJ, so one bf16 multiply with an fp32 accumulate is about 2 pJ derived. A register-file access costs about 6 pJ, a 64-bit read from an 8 KB SRAM 10 pJ, from a 1 MB SRAM 100 pJ, and from DRAM 1.3 to 2.6 nJ. A programmable core also pays about 70 pJ per instruction for fetching and decoding it. So a machine that fetches both operands from a register file for every multiply-add spends most of its energy on the fetches. The question for an accelerator designer is: how many times is each number fetched from storage, per multiply-add?

How a systolic array answers it. A systolic array is a grid of identical multiply-accumulate cells, each wired only to its neighbours. In the weight-stationary layout the TPU uses, each cell is loaded with one weight, which then stays put. A row of activations enters from the left edge and moves one cell to the right per clock cycle; at each cell it is multiplied by that cell's weight, and the product is added to a partial sum that moves one cell down per cycle. A finished dot product leaves the bottom of each column. Because row k must meet the partial sum coming from row k − 1, each row of input starts one cycle later than the row above (the skew), so the work moves through the grid as a diagonal wave. The name comes from the heart: data is pumped through in rhythm. The TPU v1 paper says it directly: "As reading a large SRAM uses much more power than arithmetic, the matrix unit uses systolic execution to save energy by reducing reads and writes of the Unified Buffer ... data flows in from the left, and the weights are loaded from the top. A given 256-element multiply-accumulate operation moves through the matrix as a diagonal wavefront" (Jouppi et al. 2017, section 2).

What it shows. On this tiny problem the array is slower: 15 cycles against 8, because the wave needs time to fill the array and drain out of it, and two thirds of the cell-cycles are idle. But it read storage 36 times against 160. The fill and drain cost a fixed number of cycles per tile, and the reads saved grow with the matrix, so the trade only pays at scale, which is exactly where matrix multiplies in training live.

A big matrix multiply is cut into tiles the size of the array. While one tile streams, the next tile's weights shift in behind it (double buffering), so in steady state each tile costs max(M, R) cycles on an R × R array, and the fill and drain are paid once. For the running example on a 128 × 128 array that is 32 × 112 = 3,584 tiles, each streaming 8,192 rows; reads per multiply-add fall to 1/128 + 1/8,192 = 0.0079 (each activation read once per 128 output columns, each weight once), about 250 times fewer than the scalar lanes' 2 derived.

The cost: the array only likes big, aligned matrices
  • Padding. A dimension that is not a multiple of the array's side wastes a whole tile. K = N = 130 on a 128 × 128 array needs 4 tiles for the work of about 1.03: 26% utilisation. How To Scale Your Model's rule: weight matrices "need to be padded to at least size 128 (256 on TPU v6e) in both dimensions to fill up the MXU" (scaling book, TPUs).
  • Small batches. With fewer rows of X than the array's side, the array waits for the next tile's weights to shift in: 8 tokens per chip on a 128-wide array reach at most 8/128 = 6% of peak in this model, before HBM bandwidth (which limits decode anyway, see the root's memory section) is even counted.
  • Bigger is not always better. Google measured that four 128 × 128 arrays reached 37% to 48% utilisation on its production models, 1.6 times one 256 × 256 array (22% to 30%) in about the same area, because many real matrices are smaller than 256 (Jouppi et al., CACM July 2020). TPU v6e moved to 256 × 256 anyway, betting that today's matrices are big enough.
  • Everything else. Softmax, normalisation, activation functions and anything with data-dependent control flow run on a much smaller vector unit beside the array (section 2).

Is a GPU tensor core a systolic array? It solves the same problem at a smaller scale: one tensor-core instruction multiplies a small matrix tile (for example m16n8k16, a 16 × 16 by 16 × 8 product, in the PTX ISA) with operands fetched once into registers, so it also divides fetches per multiply-add by the tile size. NVIDIA does not publish the circuit inside a tensor core, so whether it is "systolic" is not stated; what matters is the reuse factor, which is the tile size. The instructions and tile shapes (mma, wgmma, tcgen05) belong to Topic: cuda-and-gpu-programming; the tensor core generations to the sibling GPU architecture page. AWS Trainium's tensor engine (section 6) is a 128 × 128 systolic array too, and Groq's 320 × 320 matrix arrays (section 7) attack the same problem.

2. Inside a TPU chip

A GPU has about a hundred small cores (SMs) each running thousands of threads. A TPU chip has one or two big cores, called TensorCores, and no threads at all. Each TensorCore holds:

MXUThe matrix multiply unit: a 128 × 128 systolic array (256 × 256 on v6e), 2 to 4 per TensorCore. Inputs bf16 (int8 and, on Ironwood, fp8), accumulation in fp32. It "performs one bf16[8,128] @ bf16[128,128] -> f32[8,128] matrix multiply every 8 cycles", about 5 × 1013 FLOP/s per MXU at 1.5 GHz on v5e.
VPUThe vector unit: a 2D grid of 8 × 128 lanes (8 "sublanes" by 128 "lanes") for everything elementwise: activations, normalisation, the exponentials and sums inside softmax, reductions. Much slower than the MXU, so a model that is not mostly matmuls is slow on a TPU.
VMEMA software-managed on-chip scratchpad (128 MiB on v5e; 16 MiB per core plus a 128 MiB shared CMEM on v4). Every tensor is copied from HBM into VMEM before the core can touch it; VMEM bandwidth is about 22 times HBM's.
Scalar unit, SMEMA small scalar core that runs the control flow and issues the wide instructions (the TPU is a VLIW machine: one long instruction, 322 bits on TPU v2, drives many units at once, per CACM 2020).
HBMThe chip's main memory: 16 GB (v5e) to 192 GB (Ironwood). Streamed through VMEM into the MXU, pipelined so the copies overlap the arithmetic.
SparseCoreSeparate dataflow processors for embedding lookups (scatter and gather over huge tables), which a systolic array is bad at. In TPU v4 they sped up embedding-heavy models 5x to 7x "yet use only 5% of die area and power"; part of every TPU since v2 per the same paper; 4 per chip on v4, v5p and Ironwood.

Sources: How To Scale Your Model, TPUs (MXU, VPU, VMEM, 22x); TPU v4 paper (v4 VMEM and CMEM, SparseCore, 4 per chip); v5p and TPU7x docs (SparseCores). Chips cited by their Cloud names: v5e, v5p, v6e (Trillium), TPU7x (Ironwood).

No caches, no warps, no occupancy. The biggest difference from a GPU is who decides what is on chip. A GPU has hardware caches and hides memory latency by switching between thousands of threads; a TPU has a scratchpad and no threads, so the compiler (XLA) decides, ahead of time, every copy from HBM into VMEM and every instruction, and pipelines them so the MXU never waits. Performance on a TPU is a compiler problem, not an occupancy problem. That is also why shapes must be static: the schedule is computed for one shape, and a new shape means a new compilation. How you write kernels when the compiler is not enough (Pallas) is on Topic: jax-and-tpu.

Two TensorCores, one chip. On v4, v5p and v6e the chip's cores share memory and appear to software as one accelerator with twice the FLOPs ("megacore"). Ironwood is built from two chiplets (two dies in one package), each with its own TensorCore, joined by a high-bandwidth die-to-die link instead (scaling book; TPU7x docs).

4. Pods: a torus of chips, rewired with mirrors

The problem. One chip holds neither a big model nor its training state (the root's 8B example needs 128.5 GB of weights, gradients and Adam state). Chips must exchange gradients and activations every step, and the faster that exchange, the more of the work can be split across them. A GPU cluster joins 8 or 72 GPUs through NVLink switches and everything else through network cards and switches (the root's "Joining chips" section). Google took another route.

How ICI works. Each TPU has its own inter-chip interconnect (ICI) ports wired straight to neighbouring chips: no network card, no switch in between. Chips sit on a grid; each talks to 4 neighbours (a 2D grid: v2, v3, v5e, v6e) or 6 (a 3D grid: v4, v5p, Ironwood). If the last chip of every row is also wired back to the first, the grid becomes a torus (a doughnut: no edges), which roughly halves the longest path and lets a ring of chips send data both ways at once. Click a chip to see how many hops every other chip is from it.

An 8 × 8 slice of a 2D-torus chip such as v5e or v6e (an illustrative size; 3D slices in the Pod builder). Numbers are hops from the selected chip by the shortest path; the counters give the farthest chip and the average.

What it changes for collectives. How To Scale Your Model gives a simple rule for a TPU axis with a wraparound: gathering an array of V bytes along one axis takes about V / Wbidi, where Wbidi is one link's two-way bandwidth (1.8 × 1011 B/s on v5p, v6e and TPU7x), independent of how many chips are on the axis; an all-reduce costs twice that; using 3 axes at once divides the time by 3; and an axis without the wraparound "typically doubles the time of most communication" (scaling book, sharding; TPUs). For the root's 8B model, all-reducing its 16.06 GB of bf16 gradients along one 16-chip v5p axis takes about 2 × 16.06 GB / 180 GB/s = 0.178 s, 0.059 s over all three axes, and 0.357 s without wraparound derived, against 62.5 ms over 8 H100s' NVLink in the root. The book's own worked example, an all-gather of 34 MB over a 4-chip v5e axis, predicts 378 μs (it prints 377) with a wraparound and 560 μs without one; v5e has no wraparound on a 4-chip axis, and the profile it shows measured 680 μs measured by Google.

Optical circuit switches. A torus wired once is fragile: one dead chip breaks a ring, and the whole machine must be cabled before any of it can be used. TPU v4 builds the pod from 4 × 4 × 4 cubes of 64 chips (one rack, 16 hosts), electrically wired inside, and connects the cubes' faces through optical circuit switches (OCS): MEMS mirrors that steer light from one fibre to another and switch in milliseconds. Each cube face has 16 links, 96 per cube; opposite faces go to the same switch to close the torus, so 6 × 16 / 2 = 48 switches of 136 ports join 64 cubes into 4,096 chips. The mirrors are not a packet network: they only set which cube's face is plugged into which, like a plugboard. That buys four things, all from the TPU v4 paper:

  • Slices of any shape: a job asks for, say, 4 × 8 × 16 chips, and the switches wire the cubes it gets into exactly that torus, wherever they sit in the hall.
  • Routing around failures: a cube with a dead host is swapped out by re-pointing mirrors; with 1,024 hosts each up 99.0% to 99.9% of the time, a large slice is otherwise rarely all healthy.
  • Twisted tori: some links are re-pointed to shorten the longest paths, which raised all-to-all throughput; 40% of slices of 64 chips or more chose a twisted topology in Google's sample.
  • Cost: the switches and optics are "<5% of system cost and <3% of system power".

Slices smaller than a cube get no optical wraparound, which is why the scaling book warns that a 2 × 2 × 4 slice is slower per byte than a 4 × 4 × 4. Ironwood keeps the scheme at 9,216 chips: 144 cubes of 64, 1.77 PB of HBM in one pod, ICI at 9.6 Tb/s (1.2 TB/s) per chip (Google Cloud, Nov 6 2025).

Beyond a pod. Pods (or slices) join over the ordinary data-centre network, DCN, at about 6.25 GB/s per chip (12.5 on v6e and TPU7x), more than 10 times slower than one ICI link (scaling book). So, as on GPUs, only data parallelism (the gradient all-reduce, once per step) crosses that boundary ("multislice"), and everything chatty stays on ICI.

The mesh is the machine
Because the pod is a grid, TPU programs describe parallelism as a grid too: a JAX Mesh with named axes ('data', 'model') laid onto the physical torus, and every array annotated with which axes split it. Choosing which model axis goes on which physical axis is choosing which links carry which traffic. That software is on Sharding and scale: GSPMD, Mesh, shard_map (Topic: jax-and-tpu); the same idea for GPU clusters is Distributed Training.

5. AMD Instinct: a GPU built from chiplets, with more memory

The bet. AMD builds the same kind of machine as NVIDIA (a GPU: many cores, thousands of threads, caches) and competes on memory per GPU and on price, while its software, ROCm, catches up with CUDA. The design is the interesting part, because it shows how a big chip is made from small ones.

MI300X, die by die. Instead of one huge die, the package stacks 8 compute dies (XCDs, TSMC 5 nm) on top of 4 I/O dies (TSMC 6 nm), surrounded by 8 HBM3 stacks. Each XCD has 40 compute units (CUs, AMD's SM) of which 38 are switched on, "with 2 disabled for yield management", so 8 × 38 = 304; each XCD has its own 4 MB L2 cache. The I/O dies hold a 256 MB "Infinity Cache" in front of memory (17.2 TB/s peak) and the HBM controllers, two stacks per I/O die, 24 GB per stack, so 192 GB at 5.3 TB/s. Each CU does 2,048 dense bf16 FLOPs per clock in its matrix cores, so 304 × 2,048 × 2.1 GHz = 1,307.4 TFLOPS, exactly AMD's figure (AMD CDNA 3 white paper) derived, reproduces.

What chiplets buy and cost. Small dies yield better (a defect kills one small die, not a giant one; the two spare CUs per XCD absorb more) and let each part use the process that suits it. The cost is distance: the XCDs' L2 caches are only "coherent within an XCD", so data shared across the chip goes through the Infinity Cache, and MI300X can even be split into "up to eight partitions, one per XCD". NVIDIA's Blackwell made the same move with two dies (sibling GPU architecture page).

MI355X (CDNA 4). 8 XCDs on 3 nm with 32 of 36 CUs on (256 CUs), only 2 I/O dies, 8 stacks of 12-high HBM3E (288 GB, 8 TB/s), 4,096 bf16 FLOPs per CU per clock at 2.4 GHz (256 × 4,096 × 2.4 GHz = 2,516.6 TFLOPS, AMD's figure) plus MXFP6 and MXFP4 at 16,384. Local shared memory (LDS, AMD's name for shared memory) grew from 64 KB to 160 KB per CU, and TF32 left the hardware: it is "supported through software emulation utilizing the BF16 datatype" (AMD CDNA 4 white paper).

Eight GPUs without a switch. An NVIDIA HGX server joins its 8 GPUs through NVSwitch chips, so any GPU can send to any other at full NVLink speed. AMD's 8-GPU board instead gives every GPU 7 Infinity Fabric links, one straight to each other GPU, "to form a fully connected 8-GPU system": 7 × 128 GB/s = 896 GB/s aggregate on MI300X (64 GB/s each way per link), 7 × 153.6 = 1,075.2 GB/s on MI355X. A collective that uses all 7 links at once (an all-reduce, an all-to-all) sees the aggregate; a copy between two GPUs sees one link, a seventh of it. The scale-up domain stops at 8; AMD's answer for 72 is the announced Helios rack (72 MI455X joined by UALink over Ethernet, 3.6 TB/s per GPU; AMD calls it "a reference design, not a product for sale", AMD MI400) announced.

The trap when porting kernels: 64-thread wavefronts
AMD's equivalent of a warp, the wavefront, is 64 threads on CDNA 3 and 4 (MI300X, MI355X), not 32 (ROCm hardware specifications). Code that assumes 32 (a 32-bit lane mask, a 32-way shuffle reduction, a block of 32 threads per warp) silently computes the wrong thing or wastes half the machine. AMD's MI455X page advertises "Wave32 execution" for its next architecture, so the answer is changing by generation announced.

What it means for your training code. Mostly nothing, by design: "PyTorch for HIP intentionally reuses the existing torch.cuda interfaces", so .to('cuda'), torch.cuda.synchronize() and the rest run unchanged on a ROCm build (check torch.version.hip) (PyTorch HIP notes); collectives go through RCCL, AMD's NCCL; Triton supports "AMD GPUs (ROCm 6.2+)" (Triton README); CUDA C++ is translated by HIPIFY. The cost is everything below the framework: custom CUDA kernels, libraries that ship only CUDA builds, and tuning. An independent test in December 2024 found BF16 matmuls on MI300X reaching about 620 of its 1,307 peak TFLOPS against about 720 of 989.5 on H100, and wrote that "MI300X performance is held back by AMD software" (SemiAnalysis, Dec 22 2024); ROCm has had two major versions since, so re-measure before you decide.

6. AWS Trainium: systolic arrays you can program, on AWS only

The bet. Vertical integration: AWS designs the chip (through its Annapurna Labs team), the links, the SDK and the data centre, and rents it below the price of GPUs with NVIDIA's margin. You can only get it as an AWS instance.

Inside a NeuronCore. A Trainium2 chip has 8 NeuronCores; each is "a fully-independent heterogenous compute unit" with four engines and its own scratchpad (NeuronCore-v3):

Tensor engineA systolic array: "a grid of 128x128 processing elements" at 2.4 GHz. You load a stationary tile, then stream a moving tile across it ("LoadStationary", "MultiplyMoving"): the same weight-stationary idea as section 1. 79 bf16 TFLOPS per core; for fp8 each cell does two multiplies, which looks like a 256 × 128 array.
Vector engineOperations where each output depends on many inputs (layer norm, pooling).
Scalar engineElementwise work: activations, exponentials.
GpSimd engine"Eight fully-programmable 512-bit wide vector processors" that run C code for custom operators.
SBUF and PSUM28 MiB of software-managed SRAM per core, laid out as 128 partitions (one per row of the array), plus a 2 MiB buffer for partial sums. Like a TPU, no cache: the compiler or your kernel moves data.

Sources: Trainium2 architecture guide for NKI; Trainium2 and Trainium3 pages, all fetched 2026-10-05.

Check the peak. 128 × 128 cells × 2 FLOPs × 2.4 GHz = 78.6 TFLOPS per core, matching the docs' 79; × 8 cores = 629 TFLOPS, but the chip page says 667 derived. The 6% gap is not explained in the docs (perhaps a different clock); the page shows it rather than hiding it.

Chips into machines. A Trn2 instance has 16 Trainium2 chips "connected using a 4x4, 2D Torus topology" over NeuronLink (1,024 GB/s per chip inside the instance), and a Trn2 UltraServer joins 4 instances, 64 chips, with chips at the same position linked in rings (256 GB/s more per chip): a small TPU-like torus (Trn2 architecture). Trainium3 changes course: its UltraServers (64 or 144 chips) use switches, "replacing the previous 2D-torus architecture" with all-to-all connectivity (Trn3 architecture). The docs give three different per-chip link figures for Trainium3 (2.56 TB/s, 2,048 GiB/s, and 704 GB/s from the listed PCIe links), so no single number is quoted here. At scale: Project Rainier, "nearly half a million Trainium2 chips" across several US data centres, built for Anthropic (Amazon, Oct 29 2025).

What it means for your training code. Three ways in, in flux. The newest is TorchNeuron, a native PyTorch device: "Change .to('cuda') to .to('neuron')" and compile with torch.compile(backend="neuron"), with FSDP, DTensor, DDP and tensor parallelism; the docs call it "recommended" on one page and "only available as part of a closed Beta program" on another (PyTorch on Neuron). The older torch-neuronx path is built on PyTorch/XLA (lazy tensors, whole graphs compiled ahead of time, so static shapes matter, as on a TPU) and JAX runs through a plugin in beta. Custom kernels are written in NKI, a Python kernel language "similar to numpy and Triton" whose tiles are bounded by the hardware: the first dimension is the partition dimension (at most 128), and a matmul's stationary tile is at most 128 × 128 and its moving tile 128 × 512 (NKI). Anthropic says it writes "low-level kernels that allow us to directly interface with the Trainium silicon" (Anthropic, Nov 22 2024). Inferentia is the inference-only sibling on the same SDK.

7. Groq: no DRAM, and a compiler that knows every cycle

The bet. When a model generates one token at a time for one user, every weight is read from memory for every token, and the chip waits on memory bandwidth (the root's decode section). Groq's answer is to keep the weights in on-chip SRAM, spread across hundreds of chips, and to remove everything in the chip whose timing is unpredictable, so a compiler can schedule the whole computation, chip-to-chip transfers included, cycle by cycle.

The first chip (TSP). From Groq's ISCA 2020 paper (Abts et al., "Think Fast", archived copy): a 25 × 29 mm chip in 14 nm at a nominal 900 MHz, 26.8 billion transistors, "220 MiBytes" of SRAM (which is the "230 MB" of Groq's marketing: 220 MiB = 230.7 MB), and a peak of "820 TeraOps/sec" in int8 at 1 GHz. Its matrix units are "four (4) MXM arrays of 320×320 MACC" (large matrix arrays again), beside vector, switching and memory "slices" through which data streams east and west. It achieves determinism "by eliminating all reactive elements in the hardware (e.g. arbiters, and caches)": no cache misses, no arbitration, so the compiler knows exactly when every byte arrives. Chips connect directly, 16 links per chip, and a 2022 paper describes systems of up to 10,440 chips in which the compiler also schedules the network, avoiding "the latency variation introduced by dynamic contention for network links" (Abts et al., ISCA 2022).

Now part of NVIDIA's rack. On December 24, 2025 Groq announced "a non-exclusive licensing agreement with Nvidia for Groq's inference technology", with its founder and president joining NVIDIA and Groq continuing independently (Groq newsroom; the reported price is unconfirmed). NVIDIA's "Groq 3 LPX" rack, "now in full production" (August 24, 2026), holds 256 LPUs with 128 GB of on-chip SRAM, 0.5 GB per chip derived, and 640 TB/s of scale-up bandwidth; it sits beside Vera Rubin NVL72 racks so that "Rubin GPUs handle large-scale context processing while LPX accelerates latency-sensitive decode" (NVIDIA, Mar 16 2026; NVIDIA blog, Aug 24 2026) vendor. That split (prefill on HBM GPUs, decode on SRAM chips) is the clearest statement of what an SRAM machine is for.

The cost: chip count. SRAM is fast but small per chip. Counting only the bf16 weights, how many chips does each design need just to hold the model?

Chips = ceil(parameters × bytes / memory per chip) derived, weights only: the KV cache, activations and (for training) gradients and optimizer state come on top, and real deployments add chips for speed. Parameters from the models' safetensors index on Hugging Face (8,030,261,248; 70,553,706,496; 405,853,388,800). Memory per chip: Groq TSP 220 MiB (ISCA 2020), Groq 3 LPU 128 GB / 256 (NVIDIA), Cerebras WSE-3 44 GB SRAM, H100 80 GB, B200 180 GB (HGX), MI355X 288 GB, TPU7x 192 GiB (the root's Chip atlas). Log scale.

What it means for you. Groq is an inference service (GroqCloud) and a rack you buy from NVIDIA, not something you train on: its papers say the chip supports "model training with floating point", but Groq sells inference ("Groq makes inference work at scale"). For a training engineer it matters as the design point that answers the question "what if decode never touched DRAM?"

8. Cerebras: do not cut the wafer

The bet. Chips are cut from a 300 mm silicon wafer, and everything slow in a cluster happens when data leaves a chip. Cerebras keeps the whole wafer as one chip. The WSE-3 is 46,225 mm2 (an H100 die is 814 mm2, NVIDIA) with 900,000 cores and 44 GB of SRAM at 21 PB/s, joined by a 2D mesh on the wafer itself (Cerebras, Mar 13 2024; the 125 PF fp16 headline is a sparse figure, see the root's Chip atlas).

How a wafer survives defects. Every wafer has defects, which is why normal chips are small. Cerebras makes each core tiny, "approximately 0.05mm2" against about 6 mm2 for an H100 SM, builds 970,000 and uses 900,000 (93%), and routes the mesh around dead cores, so a defect costs one tiny core instead of the chip (Cerebras blog, Jan 13 2025). On the previous generation each core had 48 KB of its own SRAM and four fp16 multiply-add units, and computation is "triggered by the data": a core runs when a 32-bit message ("wavelet") arrives from a neighbour (Hot Chips 2022 talk, Aug 29 2022). Per-core SRAM on WSE-3 is not published; 44 GB / 900,000 = 48.9 KB derived.

Training: weights streamed in, never stored. A frontier model does not fit in 44 GB, so for training Cerebras does the opposite of a GPU cluster. The weights and optimizer state live off the wafer in a memory appliance (MemoryX); the model runs "one layer at a time": a broadcast fabric (SwarmX) sends a layer's weights to every wafer, each wafer runs its share of the batch (data parallelism), and on the way back SwarmX "aggregates (sums together) the gradients from multiple WSEs" and MemoryX updates the weights (Cerebras weight streaming). Activations never leave the wafer; only weights and gradients cross the network. There is no tensor or pipeline parallelism to choose: model size is a property of MemoryX (Cerebras claims models "up to 24 trillion parameters" for CS-3, in the same Mar 13 2024 press release) vendor.

Inference: the model on the wafers. For serving, the weights sit in SRAM across several wafers, split at layer boundaries: Cerebras said "70B models fit on as few as four systems" in 16-bit (Cerebras, Aug 27 2024), which the chart above reproduces independently: 141.1 GB / 44 GB rounds up to 4 derived. The payoff is speed per user: on independent measurement, Artificial Analysis listed Cerebras at 1,740.8 output tokens per second on gpt-oss-120B, the fastest provider shown, against 472.6 for Groq (Artificial Analysis, past 72 hours, fetched 2026-10-05).

What it means for your training code. PyTorch through Cerebras's own library: import cerebras.pytorch as cstorch, cstorch.compile(model, backend) (compiled after the first iteration, like torch.compile) and Cerebras-compliant optimizers from cstorch.optim, mostly on models from its Model Zoo; custom kernels in CSL, its own language for programming individual cores (Cerebras SDK). A CS-3 system draws up to 27 kW, is bought or rented whole, and clusters reach 2,048 of them.

The checklist before you port a training job
  1. Does your model's arithmetic land on the matrix units? Matmul dimensions multiples of 128 (or 256), batch per chip well above the array's side; elementwise-heavy or custom ops run on the slow vector units.
  2. Are your shapes static? Variable sequence lengths mean padding or bucketing on TPU and Trainium, or recompiling every step.
  3. Which custom ops do you rely on? FlashAttention variants, fused optimizers, MoE routing kernels: each must exist for the target (Pallas, NKI, HIP or Triton on AMD) or be written.
  4. How big is the scale-up domain, and what shape? 8 GPUs on an AMD board, 72 on an NVL72 rack, 16 to 144 Trainium chips, thousands of TPUs on one torus: this decides how far tensor and expert parallelism can stretch (the root's "Joining chips" section).
  5. Measure, then cost. Spec sheets do not rank chips: compare achieved throughput on your model at the same precision, then price per hour (the root's Performance calculator and Chip atlas).

10. Common mistakes and interview questions

"A TPU is just a GPU from Google."
It has no threads, no warps and no caches: a few big cores with systolic arrays, a vector unit and a scratchpad, all scheduled ahead of time by a compiler. It is closer to a very wide VLIW processor than to a GPU (section 2).
"The systolic array is faster because it does more arithmetic."
Per cycle it does one multiply-add per cell, like any other multiply-add unit. It wins by fetching each operand once per tile instead of once per use, which saves energy and register ports; on tiny problems it is slower (section 1's animation: 15 cycles against 8).
Comparing an fp8 pod against an fp64 supercomputer.
Google's "24x El Capitan" divides fp8 exaflops by fp64 Linpack exaflops. Always compare at the same precision, dense on both sides (section 3).
Assuming every slice gets the torus.
Slices smaller than a 4 × 4 × 4 cube have no optical wraparound, which "typically doubles the time of most communication"; v5e only wraps an axis of 16 (section 4).
Porting a CUDA kernel to AMD with a warp size of 32.
CDNA 3 and 4 run 64-thread wavefronts. Lane masks, shuffles and reductions written for 32 break silently (section 5).
"Groq and Cerebras are faster chips."
They are faster at one thing, generating tokens for one user, because the weights sit in SRAM. Per chip they hold far less: 70B in bf16 needs hundreds of Groq chips or four wafers (section 7).

Interview questions, short answers

Why does a systolic array save energy?
Each weight is loaded into a cell once and reused for every row of input; each input is read once at the edge and passed cell to cell; partial sums move to the neighbour. Operand fetches from storage per multiply-add fall from about 2 to about 1/side, and a storage access costs several times the multiply-add itself (Horowitz: ~6 pJ register file against ~2 pJ for a bf16 multiply with fp32 add, at 45 nm).
How long does one tile take on a weight-stationary array?
M + K + N − 2 cycles after the weights are loaded: the skew and the diagonal wave add K + N − 2 to the M rows. With double-buffered weights a big matmul costs about tiles × max(M, side).
Why must TPU matrix dimensions be multiples of 128?
The MXU is a 128 × 128 array (256 on v6e); a dimension of 130 needs two tiles, the second almost empty, so utilisation can fall to about a quarter.
What does an optical circuit switch do in a TPU pod?
MEMS mirrors connect the faces of 64-chip cubes, like a plugboard: they build each job's slice as a torus of the requested shape, route around failed cubes and allow twisted tori, for under 5% of system cost and 3% of power (TPU v4).
All-reduce on a TPU axis: how does time scale with the number of chips?
With a wraparound (a ring both ways), it is about 2V / Wbidi, independent of the axis length; without the wraparound it roughly doubles; spreading over 3 axes divides it by 3.
MI300X vs H100 in one sentence each.
MI300X: 8 compute chiplets, 304 CUs, 192 GB at 5.3 TB/s, 1,307 dense bf16 TFLOPS, 8 GPUs fully connected without a switch, ROCm. H100: one die, 132 SMs, 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s, 989.5 dense bf16, NVSwitch, CUDA. Achieved throughput, not the peak, decides.
How does Cerebras train a model that does not fit on the wafer?
Weight streaming: weights and optimizer state live in MemoryX; each layer's weights are broadcast to all wafers, which run data-parallel shards of the batch; gradients are summed on the way back and MemoryX updates the weights. Activations stay on the wafer.
When would you not use a TPU?
When the model needs dynamic shapes or control flow, custom kernels that exist only in CUDA, or ops that do not map to big matmuls, or when the team cannot move off PyTorch's GPU ecosystem.

Reduce over axes:

Rules and sources. Wraparound: "Any multiple of a full cube (e.g. 4x4x4 or 4x4x8) will have wraparounds provided by the optical switches"; "a 2x2x4 won't have any wraparounds"; without one "typically doubles the time of most communication" (How To Scale Your Model, TPUs). Here an axis wraps when every dimension is a multiple of 4 (or the axis has length 1 or 2 and the slice is otherwise whole cubes, which this tab does not allow). Per-link bandwidth one way / both ways: v4 45 / 90 GB/s, v5p and TPU7x 90 / 180 GB/s (scaling book; the TPU v4 paper gives "6 links @ 50 GB/s"). All-reduce time = 2V / Σ W over the chosen axes, W = both-way bandwidth with wraparound and one-way without, and at least 1 μs per hop along the axes (the book's Tmin) derived; real collectives reach about 95% of the link figure (the book's measurement on v5e). Hosts hold 4 chips (2 × 2 × 1). Optical links: 96 per cube (16 per face, TPU v4 paper). Largest pods: v4 4,096 chips (16 × 16 × 16), v5p 8,960, TPU7x 9,216.

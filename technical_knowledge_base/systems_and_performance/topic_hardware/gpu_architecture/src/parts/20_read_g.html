8. Feeding the tensor cores: async copy, TMA, clusters, tensor memory

The problem. Each generation doubled the tensor cores' appetite, but the threads that had to fetch their data stayed the same. On an A100 a thread copies 16 bytes per instruction, so filling our tile's 32 KB of inputs takes 2,048 copy instructions plus the address arithmetic for each 32,768 / 16, every k-step, competing for the same issue slots as the math. The hardware added since then moves that work off the threads.

Ampere: asynchronous copy

A copy from global memory straight into shared memory that skips the registers and does not stall the thread that issued it ("eliminating the need for intermediate register file usage", {{A100 in depth|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-ampere-architecture-in-depth/}}). Threads still issue one per 16 bytes, but can issue the next tile's copies and then compute on the current one: double buffering.

Hopper: TMA

A copy engine in each SM. One thread hands it a descriptor (base address, shape and strides of a 1D to 5D tensor, the box to fetch) and the TMA moves the whole tile between global and shared memory, address arithmetic included ({{Hopper in depth|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/}}). Our tile's inputs: 2 instructions instead of 2,048. Completion is tracked by an asynchronous transaction barrier that counts arriving bytes.

Hopper: clusters and DSMEM

A cluster is a group of up to 16 thread blocks that "run concurrently across SMs within a GPC" (Hopper in depth); each can read and write the others' shared memory (distributed shared memory), about 7 times faster than exchanging through global memory per NVIDIA, 180 cycles and up to 3.27 TB/s per Luo et al. One TMA load can also be multicast to several SMs of the cluster that need the same tile (.multicast::cluster in the PTX ISA).

Blackwell: tensor memory

256 KB per SM, organised as 128 lanes × 512 columns of 32-bit cells, allocated by columns and read only by the tensor cores and by explicit copies to registers ({{PTX ISA|https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/parallel-thread-execution/index.html}}, tcgen05). Our tile's FP32 accumulators take 128 of the 512 columns, so two tiles can be accumulating while a third is being written out.

What this changed in kernels. Once copies and multiplies are both asynchronous, the fastest kernels split warps by role: one producer warp issues TMA copies into a ring of shared-memory buffers, consumer warps issue the matrix multiplies and the epilogue, and barriers pass buffers between them. {{FlashAttention-3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.08608}} names this warp specialisation and the overlap of TMA and tensor cores as two of its three techniques, reaching up to 740 TFLOPS in FP16 on an H100 (75% of peak). Reading CUTLASS or FlashAttention code for Hopper or Blackwell means reading this pipeline.

The RTX 5090 is in between
Its sm_120 target compiles TMA copies and clusters (the CUDA topic's compiles) but has no wgmma, no tcgen05 and no tensor memory, so its kernels look like Ampere's mma.sync kernels fed by Hopper's copy engine.

9. The whole chip: GPCs, L2, memory, dies

How SMs are grouped. Two SMs form a TPC (texture processing cluster); TPCs form a GPC (graphics processing cluster), the unit that also bounds a thread-block cluster. The full GA100 has 8 GPCs of 8 TPCs (128 SMs); the A100 enables 108. The full GH100 has 8 GPCs of 9 TPCs (144 SMs); the H100 SXM enables 132. The RTX 5090's GB202 has 12 GPCs (192 SMs) and enables 170 in 11 GPCs. Shipping fewer SMs than the die has lets a die with a few defective SMs still be sold; NVIDIA's documents give the counts, not the yields.

L2 and memory. Memory controllers sit at the die edge, each driving one channel: on the A100 and H100, 10 of the die's 12 512-bit controllers are enabled, feeding 5 of its 6 HBM stack sites; the RTX 5090 has sixteen 32-bit GDDR7 controllers (a 512-bit bus). The L2 is split into partitions near the controllers (two on the A100, "divided into two partitions to enable higher bandwidth and lower latency"; four on the B200 per Jarmusch and Chandrasekaran).

Two dies as one GPU. A die cannot be larger than the area a lithography scanner exposes at once (the reticle); the A100 (826 mm²) and H100 (814 mm²) are near it. Blackwell's answer is two "reticle-sized" dies, 208 billion transistors in total, joined by a 10 TB/s link (NV-HBI) and presented to software as one GPU with one memory space. Blackwell Ultra keeps the two-die design with 160 SMs in 8 GPCs and eight 12-high HBM3e stacks on 16 512-bit controllers; Rubin is again two dies, 336 billion transistors and 224 SMs ({{Inside Blackwell Ultra|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-blackwell-ultra-the-chip-powering-the-ai-factory-era/}}, {{Inside Rubin|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-rubin-gpu-architecture-powering-the-era-of-agentic-ai/}}).

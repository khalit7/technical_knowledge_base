6. The memory ladder inside the chip

The problem. Our tile's 32 KB of inputs (a 128 × 64 and a 64 × 128 slice of BF16, 2 bytes per number) are used 128 times each. Fetching them from HBM every time would need 128 times the bytes; keeping them close to the tensor cores turns a memory-bound tile into a compute-bound one. Each rung of the ladder below is bigger, farther and slower than the one before.

What it shows. Shared memory answers in about 29 cycles on all three GPUs; L2 in about 260 to 270, nine times longer; HBM in 470 to 540, almost twice L2 again. A likely reason, not measured by the paper: L2 is one structure shared by every SM on a die of about 800 mm², so a request may cross the chip and back. Luo et al. also measured H800's L2 throughput at 4,472 bytes per clock, 2.2 times the A100's, and about 3.27 TB/s for DSMEM traffic between SMs of a 2-SM cluster, falling to 2.65 TB/s with 4.

Measured on the M1 Pro GPU. One thread chasing pointers through buffers from 4 KB to 256 MB shows the same staircase: about 41 ns up to 16 KB, 74 ns from 32 KB to 1 MB, about 340 ns from 4 to 32 MB, and 485 ns at 256 MB (DRAM, with address-translation misses included). Apple does not publish its GPU cache sizes, so the rungs are inferred from where the steps fall. Chart and spread: {{M1 GPU lab|#t-lab}}. measured here

Two things the ladder decides for training. First, the L2 sizes matter more than they look: 40 MB (A100), 50 MB (H100) and 96 MB (RTX 5090) are large enough to hold a whole layer's activations for small batches, so a kernel that re-reads data soon after it was written may never touch HBM. Second, the split between L1 and shared memory is chosen per kernel: matmul and attention kernels take nearly all of it as shared memory, because they know exactly which tile they will reuse; elementwise kernels leave it as cache.

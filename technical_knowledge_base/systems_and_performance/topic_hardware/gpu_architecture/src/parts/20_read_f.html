7. Tensor cores, generation by generation

The problem. Ordinary lanes do one multiply-add per thread per instruction. An H100 SM has 128 of them, so our tile's million FMAs would take 8,192 cycles of perfect issue. But in a matrix multiply every number of A meets a whole row of B, and a tensor core exploits that structure: a small fixed-function array that takes a tile of A, a tile of B and an accumulator tile and returns A·B + C in one instruction.

Throughput per SM. On the A100 each of the 4 tensor cores per SM does 256 FP16 multiply-adds per clock, 1,024 per SM, sixteen times the A100 SM's 64 FP32 lanes ({{A100 in depth|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-ampere-architecture-in-depth/}}). The H100's do twice that per SM on the same types and four times with FP8 ({{Hopper in depth|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/}}): 2,048 FP16 FMAs per SM per clock, a rate that reproduces the H100's 989.5 TFLOPS at a 1.83 GHz clock 989.5e12 / (132 × 2,048 × 2), a clock NVIDIA's blog leaves "not finalized". NVIDIA publishes no per-SM rate for Blackwell; Rubin "doubles the Tensor Core throughput per clock by doubling the amount of data it can process along the K dimension" ({{Inside Rubin|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-rubin-gpu-architecture-powering-the-era-of-agentic-ai/}}).

The bigger change across generations is not the rate but who issues the multiply, where its operands come from, and where its results go: that is what decides whether threads, registers and shared memory can keep up. Predict before you step: on which generation does the accumulator leave the register file?

The same tile on three generations: fewer instructions, issued by fewer threads, and the accumulators move out of the registers

Tile 128 × 128 × 64, BF16 inputs, FP32 accumulators, one thread block on one SM. Instruction counts from the shapes in the {{PTX ISA|https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/parallel-thread-execution/index.html}} (9.4): mma.sync m16n8k16 per warp, wgmma m64nNk16 (N up to 256) per warpgroup of 4 warps, tcgen05.mma 128 × N × 16 issued by one thread derived: (128/m) × (128/n) × (64/k). Clocks at the published per-SM rates; none for Blackwell (not published). Layout schematic, not to silicon area.

What it shows. Ampere needs 512 warp instructions and every operand passes through registers; Hopper needs 8 warpgroup instructions reading straight from shared memory, but its accumulators still fill a quarter of the register file; Blackwell needs 4, issued by a single thread, and the accumulators move to tensor memory. Each step frees threads for addresses, epilogues or softmax. On an H800, Luo et al. found the Ampere-style mma reaching on average only 62.9% of the tensor cores' peak, while wgmma reaches 729.3 of 756.5 TFLOPS (96.4%) in FP16 ({{Luo et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13499v1}}, Table VIII) independent: a kernel written for the A100 runs on an H100 but leaves a third of its tensor-core peak unused.

Read the compile target, not the generation name
NVIDIA calls the RTX 5090's tensor cores "5th Gen", like the B200's, but they do not share an instruction set: tcgen05 and tensor memory exist for sm_100a, sm_103a and sm_110a, not for sm_120, so the 5090 multiplies with mma.sync like an Ada card.

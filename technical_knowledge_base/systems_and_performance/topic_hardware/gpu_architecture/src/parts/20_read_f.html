7. Tensor cores, generation by generation

The problem. Ordinary lanes do one multiply-add per thread per instruction. An H100 SM has 128 of them, so our tile's million FMAs would take 8,192 cycles of perfect issue. But in a matrix multiply every number of A meets a whole row of B, and a tensor core exploits that structure: a small fixed-function array that takes a tile of A, a tile of B and an accumulator tile and returns A·B + C in one instruction.

Throughput per SM. On the A100 each of the 4 tensor cores per SM does 256 FP16 multiply-adds per clock, 1,024 per SM, sixteen times the A100 SM's 64 FP32 lanes ({{A100 in depth|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-ampere-architecture-in-depth/}}). The H100's do twice that per SM on the same types and four times with FP8 ({{Hopper in depth|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-hopper-architecture-in-depth/}}): 2,048 FP16 FMAs per SM per clock, a rate that reproduces the H100's 989.5 TFLOPS at a 1.83 GHz clock 989.5e12 / (132 × 2,048 × 2), a clock NVIDIA's blog leaves "not finalized". NVIDIA publishes no per-SM rate for Blackwell; Rubin "doubles the Tensor Core throughput per clock by doubling the amount of data it can process along the K dimension" ({{Inside Rubin|https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/inside-nvidia-rubin-gpu-architecture-powering-the-era-of-agentic-ai/}}).

The bigger change across generations is not the rate but who issues the multiply, where its operands come from, and where its results go: that is what decides whether threads, registers and shared memory can keep up. The same tile goes through each generation on the right; predict before it plays: on which generation does the accumulator leave the register file?

Ampere: mma.sync

  1. The inputs wait in HBM. A 128 × 64 slice of A and a 64 × 128 slice of B, 32,768 bytes in BF16, in HBM or L2.
  2. 2,048 asynchronous copies. The 128 threads issue 2,048 copies of 16 bytes each (16 per thread), global to shared memory, skipping the registers. Every one costs an issue slot and address arithmetic.
  3. Fragments into registers. Each of the 4 warps copies its 64-row strip of A and 64-column strip of B from shared memory into registers (ldmatrix): 65,536 bytes of shared-memory reads, twice the tile.
  4. mma.sync, one warp at a time. One m16n8k16 per warp multiplies a 16 × 16 piece of A by a 16 × 8 piece of B (2,048 FMAs); the first 16-wide k-step takes 128 of them. The 64 KB of FP32 accumulators sit in registers, 128 per thread.
  5. 512 instructions in all. The other 3 k-steps: 512 mma.sync, each issued by a warp scheduler. At the A100's 1,024 FMAs per SM per clock the math takes 1,024 clocks.
  6. Epilogue from registers. The results are already in registers: convert to BF16 and store. Operands and results both passed through the register file.
  1. The same tile, on Hopper. The same 32,768 bytes of inputs in HBM or L2.
  2. TMA: 2 instructions. One thread issues 2 copies, one per input, each with a tensor descriptor; the copy engine moves all 32,768 bytes and an asynchronous transaction barrier counts them in.
  3. wgmma reads shared memory. wgmma m64n128k16: the warpgroup (4 warps) issues one instruction per 64 rows, reading A and B straight from shared memory: no ldmatrix, no operand registers. The first k-step takes 2.
  4. Asynchronous, but the sums stay in registers. The warps can issue other work while the tensor cores run, and wait on a fence later; the 64 KB of accumulators are still in registers, 128 per thread.
  5. 8 instructions in all. 4 k-steps: 8 wgmma, 64 times fewer than Ampere. At the H100's 2,048 FMAs per SM per clock: 512 clocks.
  6. Epilogue from registers, as on Ampere.
  1. The same tile, on a Blackwell datacenter GPU (sm_100).
  2. TMA, as on Hopper: 2 instructions from one thread.
  3. tcgen05.mma from a single thread. 128 × 128 × 16 per instruction; operands come from shared memory, and the accumulator is allocated in tensor memory, 128 of its 512 columns.
  4. 4 instructions in all. The register file holds no accumulators, so the warps are free for other work, such as writing out the previous tile.
  5. Epilogue through tcgen05.ld. It copies the results from tensor memory into registers, a slice at a time, then they are converted and stored.
  6. Two SMs, one instruction. With cta_group::2 a pair of SMs cooperates on one instruction covering a 256-row tile ("paired SMs cooperate on a single MMA operation", Inside Blackwell Ultra). NVIDIA publishes no per-SM clock rate for Blackwell, so no clock count is shown.

What it shows. From 512 warp instructions to 8 to 4, the operands stop passing through registers and then the accumulators move to tensor memory. Each step frees threads for addresses, epilogues or softmax. On an H800, Luo et al. found the Ampere-style mma reaching on average only 62.9% of the tensor cores' peak, while wgmma reaches 729.3 of 756.5 TFLOPS (96.4%) in FP16 ({{Luo et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13499v1}}, Table VIII) independent: a kernel written for the A100 runs on an H100 but leaves a third of its tensor-core peak unused.

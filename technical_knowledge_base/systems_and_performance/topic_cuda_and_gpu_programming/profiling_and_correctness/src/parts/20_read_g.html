6. The workflow, common mistakes, and interview questions

One loop, written down

  1. Timeline first (Nsight Systems or torch.profiler with CUDA activity, a few steps after warm-up, NVTX ranges on the phases). Is the GPU busy? If not, the problem is the CPU, launches, data or communication, and no kernel work will help.
  2. Rank the kernels by total GPU time (cuda_gpu_kern_sum, or key_averages() sorted by device time). Work on the top one or two.
  3. Place each on the roofline: bytes and FLOPs it must move and do ({{Performance math|n:3c65c17b0d0d81faad1ef390b0e54d08}}), against its measured time. Far below both limits means latency or occupancy; near one limit means that limit.
  4. Nsight Compute on that kernel, top-down: Speed of Light, then the section it points to, then source counters. Treat the rule estimates as hypotheses (section 4).
  5. Change one thing, check the output against the reference (section 5), and re-measure with the honest harness (section 2): warm, cold-cache, many runs, median and spread, same clocks.
  6. Log the change, the numbers, the environment. Simon Boehm's {{matmul worklog|https://siboehm.com/articles/22/CUDA-MMM}} is the model: each kernel, its GFLOP/s, and what the profiler blamed.

Common mistakes

Timing an asynchronous launch with time.time()
Measures the queueing (0.18 ms against 4.15 ms in section 1). Synchronise before both clock reads, or use events.
Including the first call
Compilation, autotuning, algorithm search and allocator growth land in it (a first softmax about 13 times slower than the fifteenth call here, median of 9 processes). Warm up every shape.
A hot cache
A tensor smaller than L2 timed in a loop reports cache bandwidth (1.7× too fast at 16 MB here). Flush or rotate buffers, as do_bench does.
Reporting the minimum, or the mean of a noisy run
Report the median and the spread over several runs, and the machine's load. Three runs on this laptop disagreed by a factor of 2.
Comparing an ncu duration with a training-step time
Nsight Compute locks clocks, flushes caches and serialises kernels; its durations are for comparing kernels under the same conditions, not for predicting the step.
Profiling the whole run with Nsight Compute
Dozens of replay passes per kernel (42 for the full set in NVIDIA's samples). Filter with -k, -c and NVTX ranges.
Believing the guided analysis's estimated speedup
64.67% estimated, 0.14% measured for NVIDIA's own uncoalesced sample. Measure.
Requiring bit-equality with PyTorch
Different summation order gives different bits. Compare with a tolerance built from the format and K; require bit-equality only from the same kernel on the same GPU with deterministic settings.
A tolerance so loose it hides a bug
bfloat16's default rtol of 1.6% would hide an off-by-one tile that changes a few per cent of one row. Look at the worst element and its location, and test float32 versions of the kernel with tight tolerances.
Testing only sizes that are multiples of the tile
Boundary handling is where kernels break: test 1, 7, 127, 4,097 and a size larger than any you expect.

Interview questions

Nsight Systems or Nsight Compute first? Why?
Systems: one cheap pass shows where wall time goes (gaps, launches, copies, communication). Compute replays one kernel dozens of times to explain it; use it only on kernels the timeline convicted.
How do you time a CUDA kernel from PyTorch?
Warm up; torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True) pairs recorded around the call, synchronise, elapsed_time; flush L2 or rotate inputs; many runs; report the median and spread; or just triton.testing.do_bench (mean by default; ask for the median).
Speed of Light shows Compute 20%, Memory 25%. What now?
Latency-bound: neither unit is busy. Check achieved occupancy and its limiter, waves per SM (tail), warp stall reasons (long scoreboard: memory latency, not enough loads in flight), and whether the kernel is simply tiny (then fuse it or use CUDA graphs).
How do you detect shared-memory bank conflicts?
l1tex__data_bank_conflicts_pipe_lsu_mem_shared_op_ld.sum against ..._wavefronts_mem_shared_op_ld.sum in Memory Workload Analysis Tables; the Source Counters section points to the line. Typical fix: pad ([32][33]) or swizzle.
Why does my fused kernel not match PyTorch exactly?
Floating-point addition is not associative and the kernel sums in a different order (tiles, split-K, warp shuffles, atomics). Compare with a tolerance built from the output format and the reduction length, against a float64 reference of the same rounded inputs.
Why accumulate in float32 with bfloat16 inputs?
bfloat16 has 8 significant bits; a running sum in it loses each small term against a large total, with error growing about linearly in K (14.6% of the scale at K = 65,536 here, against about 10−6 in float32).
Your training loss differs between two identical runs. Where do you look?
Atomics-based ops (index_add_, scatter_add_, some backward passes), cuDNN autotuning (cudnn.benchmark), split-K choices that depend on the GPU, multiple streams sharing a cuBLAS handle, data-loader order and seeds. torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True) plus CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG=:4096:8 narrows it.
What does compute-sanitizer's racecheck catch that tests miss?
Shared-memory hazards between threads of a block (a missing __syncthreads()), which may only corrupt results at large sizes or on another GPU.

The tooling is starting to run this loop itself

AMD's Hyperloom, released with ROCm 10.0 on August 27, 2026, is an "agentic system that automates end-to-end inference workload optimization" by profiling, analysing, planning, applying changes and validating correctness in a loop, and "AMD reports that Hyperloom cuts what used to be weeks of manual optimization work down to hours" (a vendor claim; {{ROCm blog|https://rocm.blogs.amd.com/ecosystems-and-partners/rocm-x-blog/README.html}}). Dream-RSI ({{arXiv 2609.14858|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14858}}, September 2026) reports, among other domains, GPU kernel engineering with "competitive or improved discovery quality while substantially reducing discovery cost". Either way, an agent's reported speedup is only as good as the method in sections 2 and 5: no warm-up, a hot cache or an unchecked output make it worthless. The old page also cited a "Phi-Bench" result (Hardware and Edge category at 5.4%); it could not be found and is left out as unconfirmed.

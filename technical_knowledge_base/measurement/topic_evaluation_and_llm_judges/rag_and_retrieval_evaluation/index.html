RAG and retrieval evaluation: faithfulness, groundedness, retrieval metrics

How to tell whether a retrieval-augmented system failed at finding the evidence or at using it, with every metric worked on released human labels. A child of Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges. Sources linked inline; data as of October 2026.

A retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) system answers in two steps: a retriever puts a few passages in the prompt, and a generator writes an answer from them. Each step fails in its own way, and a single end-to-end score ("62% of answers were correct") cannot say which one did, so it cannot say what to fix. Evaluation therefore splits the system: classical retrieval metrics over labelled passages for the first step, faithfulness and relevance checks for the second, and an end-to-end check that ties them together. This page works every metric on released human labels: a BM25, dense and hybrid run on three BEIR test sets scored against their qrels, 1,584 RAGTruth responses whose hallucinated spans were marked by annotators, and MS MARCO's own marks of which passage holds the answer. About 30 minutes; the two tabs are optional.

The parent topic owns judges in general (who grades, bias, calibration, statistics); its Judge bias lab and Judge atlas tabs are linked, not rebuilt. How a RAG system is built (chunking, BM25, dense search, fusion, reranking, agent loops) is Topic: rag-and-retrieval and its deep dives; this page is only about measuring one.

The one habit
For every wrong answer, first ask whether a passage that holds the answer was in the prompt. That one bit gives the failure to the retriever (not in the prompt) or to the generator (in the prompt, and still wrong) before anybody touches a component.

Two halves, and why one score hides which failed

With one gold passage per question, end-to-end accuracy is a product: the share of questions whose gold passage the first stage returns among the K candidates, times the share the reranker keeps in the k that reach the prompt, times the share the generator then answers correctly, A = rK · pk|K · g. Any factor caps the whole, and the same 67.5% can come from a weak retriever or from a weak generator. Topic: rag-and-retrieval works the product through a 200-question example in its "where an answer gets lost" section; here is the same split done on real items.

Retrieval metrics

For one query q, the labels give a set of relevant passages Rel(q), each with a grade (binary: relevant or not; graded: for example 3 = answers the question on its own, 2 = holds a needed part, 1 = related background, 0 = irrelevant). The retriever returns a ranked list, and a metric "at k" looks only at the top k, because only those reach the prompt. Each metric is computed per query and averaged over the queries; TREC's trec_eval and its Python binding pytrec_eval are the reference implementations, and BEIR and MTEB use them.

recall@k = |Rel(q) ∩ Topk(q)| / |Rel(q)|    precision@k = |Rel(q) ∩ Topk(q)| / k
hit@k = 1 if any relevant passage is in Topk(q), else 0    RR = 1 / rank of the first relevant passage
DCG@k = Σi=1..k gain(reli) / log2(i+1)    nDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k
Topk(q)
the first k retrieved passages
reli
the grade of the passage at rank i (0 if unjudged or irrelevant)
gain
linear, gain = rel (what trec_eval, BEIR and MTEB use), or exponential, gain = 2rel − 1 (Burges et al. 2005, used by some learning-to-rank libraries); the two give different numbers on graded labels
IDCG@k
the DCG of the ideal order, all relevant passages sorted by grade, so nDCG lies in [0, 1]
MRR
the mean of RR over queries, often cut at 10 (RR = 0 when the first hit is below rank 10)
  • recall@k is the number RAG lives on: if the evidence is not in the prompt nothing downstream can recover it. Measure it twice, at the k that reaches the prompt and at the depth the reranker reads (often 50 to 150), or a retrieval miss and a ranking miss look the same. It has no value for a query with no relevant passage (an unanswerable question): leave those out of the average and score abstention on them instead.
  • hit@k (also "success@k", or "recall@k" in some dense-retrieval papers, which use the name for this per-query 0 or 1) equals recall@k when every query has exactly one relevant passage, and is more lenient otherwise. Check which one a paper means before comparing.
  • precision@k penalises padding the prompt: raising k can only raise recall and usually lowers precision. It is capped by the labels: with one relevant passage per query, precision@10 cannot exceed 0.1.
  • MRR fits single-answer lookups and ignores everything after the first hit.
  • nDCG@10 rewards putting the most relevant passage first; it is the standard for comparing rerankers, whose whole job is moving items up, and BEIR's and MTEB's headline retrieval number.

The calculator below holds one ranked list. Click a slot to cycle its grade (0 to 3); the starting list is the worked example from the old RAG evaluation page, a query with three relevant passages A (grade 3), B (2) and C (1), before and after a cross-encoder reranker. First stage: X, B, Y, C, Z in the top 5 and A at rank 8, so recall@5 = 2/3, precision@5 = 0.4, RR = 1/2, and nDCG@5 = (2/1.585 + 1/2.322) / (3 + 2/1.585 + 1/2) = 1.693 / 4.762 = 0.355. Reranked: A, B, X, C, Y, so recall@5 = 1, precision@5 = 0.6, RR = 1 and nDCG@5 = 4.693 / 4.762 = 0.985. The reranker could rescue A only because the first stage had it within the 20 it was given.

Relevant passages not in this list count toward recall and IDCG.

Two recalls are easy to confuse. ANN recall measures an approximate nearest-neighbour index against exact search over the same embeddings; retrieval recall measures the whole system against human labels. A perfect index cannot fix an embedding model that puts the right passage far from the query; Embeddings and vector search owns the first.

A real run, scored and reproduced

To see what the metrics say on real labels, we ran five retrievers over three BEIR test sets (Thakur et al., NeurIPS 2021 Datasets and Benchmarks) and scored them against the released qrels: SciFact (5,183 abstracts, 300 scientific claims), NFCorpus (3,633 medical documents, 323 queries, graded labels 1 and 2) and FiQA-2018 (57,638 financial forum posts, 648 questions). The systems: BM25 reimplemented the way Anserini's "flat" regression runs it (title and text as one field, Lucene's English stopwords, Porter stemming, k1 = 0.9, b = 0.4), the same BM25 with k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75, the rank_bm25 package as installed, a small dense retriever (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 22M parameters, cosine), and a hybrid fusing BM25 and dense with reciprocal rank fusion (k = 60). All runs took under 15 minutes on a laptop CPU, two threads (src/beir_run.py).

Defaults reproduce published numbers, independently

What the run teaches:

  • The winner depends on the corpus. BM25 beats the dense model on SciFact (nDCG@10 0.676 against 0.645), where claims reuse the abstracts' technical terms, and loses badly on FiQA (0.239 against 0.369), where forum questions paraphrase. A leaderboard average hides both; test on your corpus.
  • Fusion wins everywhere here. The RRF hybrid is best on all three sets (0.719, 0.356, 0.373) and lifts SciFact recall@100 from 0.925 to 0.962: the two retrievers miss different passages.
  • "BM25" is not one system. rank_bm25 as installed (no stemming, no stopwords, k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75) scores 0.652 on SciFact and 0.217 on FiQA, against 0.676 and 0.239 for the Lucene-style run; moving k1 and b alone takes FiQA from 0.239 to 0.253. Quote the implementation and parameters with the score.
  • Recall and hit rate tell different stories when labels are many. NFCorpus has 38 labelled passages per query on average, so BM25's recall@10 is 0.152 while 68% of queries have at least one relevant passage in the top 10 (hit@10 0.684), and precision@10 can never approach 1 on SciFact (1.1 labels per query).
  • Measure at the reranker's depth. On SciFact, BM25 recall@10 is 0.800 and recall@100 0.925: a reranker reading the top 100 has 12.5 more points of recall to work with than the prompt ever sees, and none beyond 0.925.

Generation metrics

The generation half is graded on what the generator did with the passages it was given. Five checks cover it; the names vary by framework (next section), the ideas do not. All but citation checks are usually produced by prompting a judge model, so everything on LLM-as-judge applies to them.

Faithfulness (groundedness)

Split the answer into atomic claims and ask, claim by claim, whether the retrieved passages support it:

faithfulness = claims in the answer supported by the passages / claims in the answer

RAGAS's own example: the passage says Einstein was born on 14 March 1879 in Germany; the answer says "Einstein was born in Germany on 20th March 1879". The judge splits it into "born in Germany" (supported) and "born on 20th March 1879" (not supported), so faithfulness = 1/2 = 0.5 (RAGAS docs). Low faithfulness is hallucination despite retrieval. Two things it is not:

  • Not correctness. It checks the answer against the passages, not the passages against the world. In the animation's first question, GPT-4's answer is perfectly faithful and four of its six "still alive" are now wrong, because the passage dates from about 2014 (June Whitfield died in 2018, Barbara Windsor in 2020, Leslie Phillips in 2022 and Julian Holloway in 2025, per Wikipedia as read on 4 October 2026). Freshness and source checks are a separate test.
  • Not the same as "true". RAGTruth's annotators flagged whether each hallucinated span was nevertheless true in the world (implicit_true): 846 of the 2,927 question-answering spans were (28.9%; the model filled the gap from memory, correctly). A faithfulness metric counts them as failures, which is right for a system that must answer from its sources and wrong if you only care about accuracy. Decide which you are measuring.

Answer relevance

Does the answer address the question? It says nothing about truth: Llama-2-13B's answer in the animation is about whether Kenneth Williams is alive, true, and irrelevant. RAGAS measures it backwards: an LLM writes N questions the answer would answer (N = 3 by default), and the score is their mean cosine similarity to the real question in embedding space. In RAGAS's code, not its docs, the score is set to 0 when the judge flags every generated question as noncommittal ("I don't know"; _answer_relevance.py, February 2026). DeepEval measures it forwards, as the share of the answer's statements that are relevant to the question. Same name, different instruments: the next section sets them side by side.

Context relevance and context precision

Graded on the retrieved passages, but by a judge rather than by labels, so they work without qrels. Context relevance (TruLens) asks whether each retrieved passage is relevant to the question. Context precision (RAGAS) asks whether the relevant passages were ranked above the irrelevant ones:

context precision@K = Σk=1..K (precision@k × vk) / (relevant passages in the top K)
vk
the judge's 0 or 1 verdict on the passage at rank k (against the reference answer, or against the response when there is none)

It is the average precision of the judged list. Irrelevant passages ranked above relevant ones lower it; irrelevant passages ranked below every relevant one do not, so one relevant passage at rank 1 followed by nine misses scores 1.0 while precision@10 is 0.1 (the third preset in the calculator above). On the worked example with binary verdicts, the first-stage list (relevant at ranks 2 and 4) scores (1/2 + 2/4) / 2 = 0.5 and the reranked list (ranks 1, 2 and 4) scores (1 + 1 + 3/4) / 3 = 0.917.

Context recall

The share of the reference answer's claims that the retrieved passages could support: the judged cousin of retrieval recall, usable when you have reference answers but no passage labels. A reference answer with 4 claims of which the passages support 3 scores 0.75: a quarter of what a correct answer needs never reached the generator.

Citation accuracy

When the system cites, check the citations themselves. ALCE (Gao et al., EMNLP 2023) splits the answer into statements, each citing a set of passages Ci, and uses an NLI model (TRUE, a T5-11B fine-tuned for entailment) as the checker:

  • citation recall of a statement is 1 if it cites at least one passage and the cited passages, concatenated, entail it; averaged over statements;
  • citation precision of a citation is 0 if it is "irrelevant": it does not entail the statement on its own, and the other citations entail the statement without it; averaged over citations. Redundant but supporting citations are not penalised.

ALCE's headline: on ELI5, even the best models of 2023 lacked complete citation support about half the time. Citation checks are the cheapest groundedness test in production, because they need no reference answer and the NLI check is a small model; their blind spot is a claim left uncited.

Frameworks, their definitions, and where they disagree

Every framework implements "faithfulness" and "answer relevance", and they do not mean the same thing. A score is a reading of an instrument; name the instrument, its version and its judge model next to it, as you would name the harness next to a benchmark score (Eval harnesses). Three disagreements change numbers on the same item:

  • Unsupported but uncontradicted claims. RAGAS fails them; DeepEval's default passes them. Llama-2-13B's true but unsourced sentence about Kenneth Williams in the animation is the case: a RAGAS-style faithfulness of 0, a DeepEval-default faithfulness of 1. RAGTruth's annotators side with RAGAS (Subtle Baseless Info).
  • Abstentions. TruLens counts a refusal as grounded; RAGAS has no claims to check; an end-to-end correctness check counts it wrong when the answer was in the passages. In RAGTruth's QA set, 36 bare refusals were made with an answer-bearing passage in context (Reading, the outcome bars).
  • Answer relevance runs in opposite directions. RAGAS asks whether the answer implies the question; DeepEval asks what share of the answer is on topic. A long answer with one relevant sentence can score well on the first and badly on the second.

How well do they track people? RAGAS reported agreement with human annotators on its 50-question WikiEval set of 0.95 for faithfulness, 0.78 for answer relevance and 0.70 for context relevance, against 0.72, 0.52 and 0.63 for a direct GPT score (Table 1). On harder ground, RAGChecker (Ru et al., arXiv v2, 17 Aug 2024) collected 280 human pairwise comparisons (annotators agreeing 90.95% of the time) and measured overall Pearson correlation with them: RAGChecker's own claim-level metric 61.93, RAGAS answer similarity 48.31, TruLens answer relevance 35.15, ARES answer relevance 17.81, against a human-to-human ceiling of 70.09. ARES, in turn, reported Kendall's τ with true system rankings 0.065 higher than RAGAS for context relevance and 0.132 higher for answer relevance on KILT and SuperGLUE tasks. No study we found runs RAGAS, DeepEval and TruLens on the same items and reports their agreement (unconfirmed gap as of October 2026); the nearest is a July 2026 preprint on 96 business questions (arXiv 2607.07302) finding claim recall tracking humans at about 0.7 and the faithfulness metrics only weakly.

TREC's automatic evaluation, read with its topic count
For the 2024 track, run-level Kendall's τ between fully automatic and manual nugget scoring was 0.783 on the 21 topics first judged (Pradeep et al., Nov 2024), and 0.887 (Vital Strict) on 36 topics against post-edited nuggets in the follow-up ("The Great Nugget Recall", SIGIR 2025), 0.727 against fully manual nuggets. Per topic it is about 0.5. Automatic nugget evaluation ranks systems well and individual answers poorly. On support, GPT-4o and assessors agreed exactly on 56% of judgments made from scratch, 72% after post-editing (arXiv 2504.15205).

Judging faithfulness with an LLM, and how it fails

A faithfulness metric is an LLM judge with a fixed prompt, so it inherits the judge's position, verbosity and self-preference biases and its drift across versions; LLM-as-judge owns the catalogue and the calibration method (a few hundred hand-labelled items, Cohen's kappa, the pass rate corrected for the judge's measured error rates). What is specific to faithfulness:

  • Decomposition decides the score. Claim-splitting is itself an LLM step, and claim-level factuality scores are "sensitive to the decomposition method used" (Wanner et al., 2024): a split that drops a qualifier turns a faithful sentence unfaithful and charges the generator for the judge's error.
  • Leniency is a setting, not a bug. DeepEval's default and TruLens's abstention rule (previous section) raise scores by design. Read the prompt before reading the number.
  • Prompted judges over-flag. On RAGTruth's QA responses, prompted GPT-4-turbo found 90.6% of hallucinated answers but only 33.2% of the answers it flagged were hallucinated (F1 45.6); at span level its F1 was 32.6. A 13B model fine-tuned on the labels reached 68.2 and 58.2 (Hallucination spans).
  • Judges grade their own family up. FACTS Grounding uses three judges from three labs because, measured, each rated its own family's responses 3.23% higher on average (Jacovi et al., Jan 2025).
  • Long contexts. Claims must be checked against everything retrieved; with 20 passages the judge's prompt is long, and judges degrade with length as generators do. We found no clean measurement of faithfulness-judge accuracy against context length (unconfirmed); measure it on your own lengths before trusting a single judge there.
  • Stale or wrong sources pass. Faithfulness never asks whether the passage is true or current (the animation's last step).

The practical answer in 2026 is a small trained detector for the bulk (cheap, deterministic, span-level) and a calibrated frontier judge, or a person, for the disputed slice. The next section has the evidence for the detectors.

Long context against RAG: what to evaluate

When a corpus fits in a million-token window, "do we need retrieval at all?" is an evaluation question, answered on your own questions, not by a leaderboard. Three findings frame it:

  • Position matters inside the window: accuracy is highest when the relevant passage sits at the start or end and drops in the middle (Lost in the Middle). Long-context and multimodal benchmarks owns this and the effective-length benchmarks (its "Position" and "Beyond retrieval" sections, and HELMET's RAG category, which predicts downstream behaviour better than needle tests).
  • More retrieved passages is not monotone: quality rises, then falls as hard negatives pile in (Jin et al., Oct 2024). So evaluate recall@k and end-to-end accuracy together across k, not recall alone.
  • Neither wins everywhere: long context beat RAG on average when budget allowed, and routing each query to RAG or long context by the model's own judgement kept quality at 65% lower cost on Gemini-1.5-Pro (Self-Route, EMNLP 2024 Industry); LaRA's 2,326 cases over 11 models found the winner depends on model, window, task and chunking (Mar 2025).

Evaluate both arms on the same golden set, with the same correctness check, at your corpus size, and report cost and latency beside accuracy. Retrieval metrics apply only to the RAG arm; the long-context arm is scored end to end plus faithfulness against the whole window. Advanced and agentic RAG prices the two.

Building a RAG test set from your own corpus

A few hundred well-made items beat ten thousand synthetic ones.

  1. Mine real questions from logs, support tickets and subject-matter experts. Synthetic questions come out longer and more literal: one September 2026 comparison on a production corpus found real queries averaged 6.8 words against 15.7 for generated ones, and that tuning on the synthetic set picked a retriever with up to 8 times the latency of the fastest (Kucia and Gawlik, arXiv 2609.14579, preprint).
  2. Label each question twice: the relevant passage ids with grades (for the retrieval half) and a reference answer, or a list of nuggets (for the generation half). Expert time is the bottleneck; spend it here. Labelling only the top results of one system biases the labels toward that system; pool the top 10 of several systems, as TREC does.
  3. Bootstrap coverage with generation, then filter by hand. RAGAS's generator builds a knowledge graph over your chunks (entities, keyphrases, similarity links) and by default draws a third each of single-hop specific, multi-hop abstract and multi-hop specific questions (docs). Unfiltered, generated questions quote the chunk's own phrasing and are easy for lexical retrieval by construction.
  4. Include the hard cases on purpose: unanswerable questions (the system should abstain; score abstention, not recall), multi-hop questions, ambiguous ones, near-duplicate documents, and questions whose answer changed over time.
  5. Version it. Re-chunking the index invalidates passage-id labels; re-label or map them. Never tune on the held-out slice.

How many questions a comparison needs is on Eval statistics; label quality and agreement on Human evaluation and annotation; turning the set into a release gate on Production eval engineering.

Bucket every failure

  • Retrieval miss: the answer is not in the corpus (a corpus gap, not a retriever bug), or it is but was not retrieved. Fix chunking, hybrid search, query rewriting.
  • Ranking miss: retrieved at rank 40, the prompt takes 10. Fix the reranker or k.
  • Generation miss: the passage was in the prompt and the answer is wrong, unfaithful or incomplete. Fix the prompt, the model, the context formatting; check for distraction by irrelevant passages.
  • Grounding-refusal miss: the context sufficed and the model refused or hedged; or, the inverse, the context did not suffice and it answered anyway. Fix the instructions and abstention.
  • Source miss: both halves passed and the source itself was stale or wrong. Fix the corpus.

Agentic RAG

When the model calls retrieval as a tool in a loop, the per-call metrics still apply to each search, and the trajectory needs its own checks: did it search when it should (and not when it should not), how good were its reformulated queries, how many calls until it had enough, and are the final citations verified against what it actually read. Agent and trajectory evaluation owns grading trajectories; public agentic benchmarks are on Agentic benchmarks.

Common mistakes

Reporting one end-to-end score
It cannot say which half failed. Split retrieval from generation, and ask the one-bit question on every failure.
Measuring recall only at the prompt's k
Measure the first stage at the reranker's input depth too, or a retrieval miss and a ranking miss look the same.
Reading high faithfulness as correctness
Faithfulness is answer against passages. A stale passage gives a faithful wrong answer (four of six "still alive" in the animation), and a true answer from memory scores as unfaithful (846 of RAGTruth's 2,927 QA spans).
Comparing "faithfulness" across frameworks
RAGAS fails unsupported claims; DeepEval's default passes them unless they contradict the context; TruLens counts refusals as grounded. Name the framework, version and judge with every number.
Reading high context precision as a clean prompt
Irrelevant passages below the relevant ones do not lower it; precision@k catches the padding.
Comparing nDCG across libraries, or "recall" across papers
Check the gain (linear in trec_eval, exponential in some libraries), the cutoff, and whether "recall@k" means the share of relevant passages or a per-query hit.
Calling two BM25 runs the same system
On SciFact our Lucene-style BM25 scores nDCG@10 0.676 and rank_bm25 as installed 0.652: tokenisation, stemming and k1, b move the third decimal and sometimes the second (Score a real retrieval run).
Unpinned judges
Upgrading the judge model moves every generation metric; treat it as a new instrument and recalibrate on the gold slice.
Synthetic-only test sets and stale labels
Generated questions are easy by construction; re-chunking invalidates passage-id labels.
Averaging recall over unanswerable questions
It has no value there (zero relevant passages to divide by); score abstention separately.

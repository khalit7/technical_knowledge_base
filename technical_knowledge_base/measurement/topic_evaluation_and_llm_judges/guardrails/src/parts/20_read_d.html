The rule of thumb the evidence supports: encoder classifiers cost milliseconds on a GPU and a few hundred on a CPU; an 8B guard costs a few hundred milliseconds per call; reasoning guards (gpt-oss-safeguard, AlignmentCheck, reasoning monitors) cost more and are used behind a cheap first stage. Six patterns hide the cost, all in production frameworks: run guards in parallel with each other (NeMo's rails.input.parallel, so latency is the slowest guard, not the sum); run the input rail in parallel with generation (OpenAI's cookbook and Agents SDK default, NeMo's speculative generation), wasting tokens when it fires and unsafe when tools have side effects; stream the output rail in chunks; score every token with a classifier head; cascade a cheap high-recall screen into an expensive classifier; and pick the smallest guard that holds accuracy (ONNX took Protect AI's scanner from 81 ms to 7.65 ms).

OpenAI states the same trade for gpt-oss-safeguard: it "can be time and compute-intensive", so OpenAI uses "smaller and faster classifiers to determine which content to assess" and sometimes runs the reasoner "asynchronously" ({{technical report|https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/08b7dee4-8bc6-4955-a219-7793fb69090c/Technical_report__Research_Preview_of_gpt_oss_safeguard.pdf}}).

Evaluating guardrails

Guardrails are classifiers, so they get the full measurement treatment, with three twists: attack traffic is rare and adversarial, the cost of each error is asymmetric and policy-specific, and the system's behaviour is the whole pipeline, not any one rail.

Measure both errors, at a fixed operating point

Report per-category recall on a labelled attack set and the over-block rate on a benign set drawn from real traffic, at the threshold you will deploy. Comparing guards at their own default thresholds compares thresholds, not guards: the STACK authors instead set every classifier to the same 15% refusal rate on benign queries before comparing attack success ({{McKenzie et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.24068}}). Threshold-free summaries (AUC, AUPRC) help compare models but hide the one number production depends on; IBM's table of true-positive rate at fixed false-positive rates shows why: at 1% false positives, Llama Guard 3 8B catches 32.0% of harmful items in IBM's aggregate and Granite Guardian 3.0 8B 29.0%, although Granite's AUC is higher (0.871 against 0.826; {{Granite Guardian paper, Table 11|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07724}}).

Over-refusal is the cost users feel

Over-blocking is silent: a benign request refused looks like a product that does not work, and without a benign regression set nobody notices until users leave. Benign-but-scary prompt sets measure it: XSTest's 250 safe prompts, OR-Bench's 80,000 "seemingly toxic" prompts (across 32 chat models, refusal of safe and of toxic prompts correlates at Spearman 0.89: models that refuse more harm also refuse more safe requests; OR-Bench does not test standalone guard models: {{arXiv 2405.20947|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20947}}). Guard cards that print false-positive rates beside F1 are the exception: Llama Guard 3's XSTest false-positive rate is 4.4% at 8B, 6.8% at 1B and 15.2% for the 1B int4 build (Meta, {{1B card|https://github.com/meta-llama/PurpleLlama/blob/main/Llama-Guard3/1B/MODEL_CARD.md}}): quantising the 1B guard to 4 bits more than doubled its over-blocking. The refusal benchmarks themselves are owned by {{Safety and honesty benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d818da9d5efda1a443166}}.

Measure the pipeline, not the parts

Attackers need one path through. The quantity that matters is the end-to-end leak rate: the share of attack attempts whose harmful output or action reaches the user or the world after every rail has had its chance. If rails failed independently it would be the product of their miss rates, but they fail together on the hard cases, so the product is an optimistic lower bound. Against an attacker who targets the stack the gap is far larger: STACK's staged attack, which defeats each layer separately and then composes, reached 71% attack success on a pipeline where an off-the-shelf attack got 0% (33% with transfer only), at a cost of 507 H100 GPU-hours ({{STACK|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.24068}}). Monitor numbers measured without adaptation say so: OpenAI's o3 bio monitor recall of 98.7% (4 misses in 309 red-team conversations) "does not simulate adaptive attacks" ({{o3 system card|https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/2221c875-02dc-4789-800b-e7758f3722c1/o3-and-o4-mini-system-card.pdf}}).

The grader is a classifier too

Labelling attack outcomes at scale needs an automated judge, and judges miss. In JailbreakBench's study of 300 human-labelled outputs, Llama Guard 1 as a judge agreed with the experts 72.0% of the time with a 60.9% false-negative rate, Llama Guard 2 87.7%, GPT-4 90.3%, and the chosen Llama-3-70B 90.7% with 5.5% false negatives ({{Chao et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01318}}). MLCommons' AILuminate keeps its evaluator ensemble secret "to prevent the ensemble from being used as a guard model" and says it "has a high false-unsafe rate" by design ({{AILuminate paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05731}}). A groundedness rail inherits the same requirement: a human-agreement number before its blocks are trusted ({{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}). This page found a smaller instance of the same thing: Qwen3Guard's refusal flag and XSTest's human annotators disagree on most answers that refuse a question's premise (Pipeline lab).

Choosing the threshold with a guarantee

A precision-recall curve does not say where to put the threshold, and the threshold fixes the block rate in production. Conformal risk control picks it from held-out data so that the expected value of a chosen loss stays below a target α, with a finite-sample, distribution-free guarantee, provided deployment data are exchangeable with the calibration data ({{Angelopoulos et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02814}}). The loss can be "a harmful item got through" (control the leak rate) or "a benign item got blocked" (control the over-block rate). Schirmer, Jazbec and colleagues apply it to online monitoring of generations: a verifier scores each step, an alarm fires when any score falls below λ, and λ is calibrated on good generations so the false-alarm risk is controlled, either in expectation (their CRC variant) or with high probability (UCB) ({{"Online Safety Monitoring for LLMs", arXiv 2607.02510|https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02510}}). The guarantee is a property of the calibration, not of the classifier, and the catch is exchangeability: attack distributions drift faster than any other input these rails see, and an adaptive attacker breaks exchangeability by construction, so recalibration frequency becomes the real operating parameter. The {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}} calibrates a threshold this way on half of XSTest's safe prompts and tests it on the other half.

Correction: the "CRC Monitor" paper

The previous version cited "the CRC Monitor work (Schirmer, Jazbec et al., 2026)" as reducing a monitor "to a single score". The paper is titled "Online Safety Monitoring for LLMs" (arXiv 2607.02510, 2 July 2026; Schirmer, Jazbec, Timans, Naesseth, Waldron, Nalisnick); CRC is one of its two monitor variants, the phrase "single-score" does not appear in it, and the risk it controls is false alarms on good generations; detection power is measured, not guaranteed, and depends strongly on whether the verifier was trained to score partial outputs.

Keep the attack side fresh

Red-teaming tools generate the attack corpus (promptfoo's scanner, NVIDIA's garak, Microsoft's PyRIT); public sets anchor it (AILuminate's 12 hazards, HarmBench and JailbreakBench for jailbreaks, AgentDojo with 629 security cases and InjecAgent with 1,054 for indirect injection: in InjecAgent, ReAct-prompted GPT-4 was "vulnerable to attacks 24% of the time", {{Zhan et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.02691}}). Anthropic's evidence for its classifiers came from people, not sets: 183 active red-teamers, an estimated 4,720 hours (90% interval 3,242 to 7,417; the blog's ">3,000 hours" is the lower bound), with automated jailbreak success falling from 86% to 4.4% at a 0.38% rise in refusals; the 2026 system was red-teamed for "over 1,700 cumulative hours" across 198,000 attempts. Wire guard metrics into the same regression gates as quality metrics: a guard-model version bump is a promotion event like any model swap ({{Production eval engineering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b39ad9e96193d21adc}}).

Failure modes

Reading the response string in an agent
A post-call rail on an agent's final message measures the wrong variable; the harm is in the tool calls and the state they changed. Classify actions during the call, and audit state after it (MOLE's best monitor still missed 21 of 45 completed harms; correction above).
The guard is a model too
Adversarial suffixes, multilingual and obfuscated payloads that fool the policy model can fool the guard. Ensemble diversity and input normalisation mitigate, but only where the payload is decodable at the gate: an encrypted payload decrypted by the agent's own interpreter (the Grok case above) is out of reach of any input rail. Character tricks alone defeat most detectors tested (emoji smuggling 100%, Unicode tags 90%).
Over-blocking that nobody measures
Refusal cascades on benign traffic destroy product quality silently. In this page's run, going from loose to strict on both rails cut harmful answers from 5 to 1 and raised blocked safe prompts from 30 to 64 of 250. Without a benign regression set you will not see it until users churn.
Latency stacking
Three serial stages can add half a second or more (NVIDIA: 0.91 s to 1.44 s). Run guards in parallel, run the input rail alongside prefill where tools have no side effects, stream the output check, and keep heavy checks for sampled or asynchronous scoring with a kill switch.
Taxonomy and definition mismatch
A guard's categories rarely match your policy, and even its definitions may not match your labellers' (an injection detector passes every harmful request; a refusal flag ignores refusals of a premise). Measure against your policy labels, not the model card's benchmark. Policy-adaptive guards (gpt-oss-safeguard, Shieldstral, Granite Guardian 4.1, Nemotron 3.5's custom mode) take your policy as text, which moves the problem from retraining to writing and testing that text.
Undefined behaviour when the guard fails
A guard that times out must do something. Decide fail-open or fail-closed per stage: a pre-call timeout on a general assistant should not take the product down; a groundedness check on a medical surface should fail closed. NeMo Guardrails made streaming rails "fail closed when rail actions fail" in v0.24.0 (Aug 2026).
Trusting a vendor's leaderboard
PINT is run by Lakera, which tops it; every guard model's card re-runs its rivals under the maker's own thresholds and mappings; Qwen's averages pick the better mode per benchmark. Prefer independent runs (GuardBench), and better, your own.

Deployment checklist

  1. Write the policy first, as your own taxonomy with examples per category; choose guard models against it, not against model-card scores.
  2. Place each check where its risk becomes visible: injection, toxicity and PII before the call; tool-call, action and topical rails during it; groundedness, leakage and policy checks after it. Give each stage a latency budget before choosing models.
  3. Use two guards with different strengths, fast gate first (a cascade); normalise inputs (decode encodings, strip invisible and look-alike characters) before classifying.
  4. Build both evaluation sets on day one: an attack set (red-team generated, refreshed) and a benign set sampled from real traffic for over-block tracking; report both errors at the deployed threshold, and the end-to-end leak rate through all stages.
  5. Choose thresholds on held-out data with a stated target (conformal risk control gives a guarantee while traffic resembles the calibration set) and recalibrate on a schedule, since attack traffic drifts fastest.
  6. Wire guard metrics into the same regression gates as quality metrics; a guard-model or threshold change is a promotion event.
  7. Log every block and flag with stage, category and score; review samples weekly, since blocked-traffic drift is the earliest attack-trend signal.
  8. Define fail-open or fail-closed per stage explicitly.
  9. Assume one path through the rails will be found, and make it worthless: hold credentials outside the agent's runtime, take approvals through a channel the model cannot write into, restrict egress, and withhold capabilities the task does not need.

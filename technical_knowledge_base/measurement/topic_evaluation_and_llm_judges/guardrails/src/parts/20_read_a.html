In one screen

A guardrail is an evaluation that runs at request time and is allowed to act: a classifier or a policy in the request path that can block, rewrite, redact, regenerate or ask a human. Because it acts on live traffic, every guardrail trades two errors against each other (harm let through, and legitimate work blocked) and pays for the trade in latency and compute. One checkpoint cannot strike that balance for every risk, so production systems stage their rails by where a risk becomes visible and what blocking it costs at that point.

What changed since this page was last written (September 2026), each covered below: action rails for agents have become the stage that matters most, with Microsoft's Foundry guardrails now intervening at four points: input, tool call, tool response, output ({{Microsoft Learn, 31 Jul 2026|https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/guardrails/guardrails-overview}}); the cheapest strong design in production is a cascade, a small high-recall screen in front of an expensive classifier, which took Anthropic's classifier overhead from 23.7% of inference compute ({{Sharma et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18837}}) to "roughly 1%" ({{Anthropic, Jan 2026|https://www.anthropic.com/research/next-generation-constitutional-classifiers}}); three of the 2023 to 2025 open frameworks were archived, shut down or replaced after acquisitions (LLM Guard, Guardrails AI's hub, Llama Stack's shields: Frameworks); and new guard models keep arriving (IBM Granite Guardian 4.1, NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Content Safety, Mistral Shieldstral), each with a scorecard run by its own makers (Guard models).

The rule this page keeps returning to

A guardrail number means nothing without its operating point. Every guard has a threshold; every threshold fixes one pair (share of harm missed, share of benign traffic blocked). Report both, at your traffic's base rate, on your own policy, through the whole pipeline. The {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}} lets you move that point on 450 real conversations.

A rail is a classifier with a threshold

Strip away the framework and every rail is the same object: a scoring function over some text or action, a threshold, and an action taken when the score crosses it. That makes a guardrail a binary classifier, and everything known about measuring classifiers applies (the definitions are on the {{Evaluation metrics|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a29acef722e842a29b}} page). Two error rates matter, and they pull against each other:

miss rate = FN / (TP + FN)     over-block rate = FP / (FP + TN)
FN
harmful items the rail let through (a leak)
FP
benign items the rail blocked (an over-block, which users feel as a refusal)
threshold
moving it up lowers FP and raises FN; there is no setting that lowers both

Two consequences decide how rails are designed. First, base rates: attacks are rare in real traffic, so even a small over-block rate produces more false blocks than true ones. Second, severity sets the stage: a risk that is cheap to detect and expensive to let through (a known jailbreak string, a credit card number) is caught early, where blocking costs nothing; a risk that only exists once the answer is written (an ungrounded medical claim) can only be caught late, where catching it costs a second model pass. The old version of this page put it as "severity determines where you pay the cost"; that still holds.

What share of blocks are mistakes?

Try: keep the defaults, then lower the attack share to 1 in 10,000 (an illustrative value: no lab publishes the attack share of its traffic). The defaults are this page's own measurement of Qwen3Guard-Gen-0.6B as an input rail on XSTest (loose mode, Pipeline lab).

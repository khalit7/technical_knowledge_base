Juries and robust aggregation

PoLL ({{Verga et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796}}) replaces one large judge with three smaller ones from disjoint families (Command R, Claude 3 Haiku, GPT-3.5), each judging independently. Binary correctness verdicts are pooled by majority ("max voting"); Arena-style 1 to 5 scores by their average, because three judges often produce no majority. On KILT Natural Questions answers the panel reached kappa 0.763 with human labels against 0.627 for GPT-4 alone (exact string match: 0.651; Claude 3 Haiku alone: 0.749), and its model ranking correlated with Chatbot Arena at Pearson 0.917 against 0.817, "while being over seven times less expensive". The evidence is the authors' own, on question answering with references and Arena-Hard, and the best single small judge came within 0.014 kappa of the panel: much of the gain over GPT-4 is GPT-4 doing worse on this task, not pooling.

Why the mean is fragile. {{RoPoLL (Acharya et al., arXiv 2606.30931, June 2026, preprint)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30931}} models a jury under Huber contamination and proves that mean pooling "incurs unbounded bias under any positive contamination, regardless of jury size, whenever a single judge fails in a biased, LLM-typical way (mode collapse, sycophancy, safety refusal)". It swaps the mean for the geometric median (computed by a modified Weiszfeld iteration), which needs no tuning, keeps the scores' joint structure, and tolerates up to half the jurors failing. Across 13 open-weight judges (4B to 675B), three reward-model benchmarks and corruption rates up to 50%, it beat mean pooling on every biased corruption type, by about 19% on cross-dimensional attacks; a 3-judge committee totalling 38B beat Mistral-Large-3 (675B) by 1.31x on HelpSteer-2 under 30% bimodal corruption. Under benign Gaussian noise the mean remains the statistically optimal choice, so the median buys insurance, not accuracy. Practical form: median or trimmed mean over jurors, plus per-juror agreement tracked over time so a degraded juror is noticed.

Debate and meta-judges (agents argue, or a judge adjudicates juror disagreement) are expensive and not reliably better. On JudgeBench, ChatEval's multi-agent debate with GPT-4o scored 34.0%, against 56.57% for one GPT-4o with the Arena-Hard prompt (atlas, JudgeBench v1 Table 1). Keep them for offline audits where a disagreement needs a written argument.

Escalation spends the judge budget where the signal is: a cheap jury on everything; items where jurors disagree, or scores near the threshold, go to a stronger judge; items where that judge and the jury still disagree go to a person. The escalated items are also the most useful additions to the gold slice.

Judge models: fine-tuned judges, reward models, verifiers, agents

Four instrument families get called "the judge". They differ in what they output and in how far their accuracy transfers off the data they were built on. Every reading below is in the parent's {{Judge atlas|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}} with its source and who ran it.

Fine-tuned judge models

Open models trained on judgments written by a strong model (usually GPT-4), cheap to run and private. Each looks close to GPT-4 on its own test set and drops on independent ones.

Reward models as judges

A scalar head trained on preference pairs (Bradley-Terry): one forward pass per response, no rationale, built for RLHF and best-of-n. They are strong on the tests made for them and transfer unevenly. Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B tops RewardBench 2 at 84.1%, yet scores Kendall tau 0.133 on IF-RewardBench's ranking task against 0.513 for Gemini 3 Flash; Skywork-Reward fell to 46.6% on RM-Bench's style-against-substance pairs, below a coin. On JudgeBench's hard correctness pairs, though, a 27B reward model (Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B, 64.29%) beat every fine-tuned judge and GPT-4o. They inherit their preference data's length and format preferences in concentrated form, which is what a policy trained against them learns to exploit ({{Reward hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}).

Generative verifiers

{{GenRM (Zhang et al. 2024)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15240}} trains the verifier with the ordinary next-token objective to answer "Is the answer correct (Yes/No)?", optionally after writing a verification chain of thought, and reads the probability of "Yes" as the score. Because it generates, it can reason and can be sampled several times and majority-voted. Used for best-of-n on reasoning tasks it lifted GSM8K from 73% to 93.4% (Gemma2-9B verifier on Gemini 1.0 Pro solutions) and algorithmic tasks from 5% to 45.3%, ahead of discriminative reward models and LLM-as-judge prompting. The best JudgeBench figure, 81.4%, is NVIDIA's own submission of a generative reward model (vendor-run); independent rankings on IF-RewardBench put other generative reward models far lower (RRM-32B tau 0.072).

Agent-as-judge

The judge gets tools: it reads the produced files, runs the code, checks claims against retrieval, replays a trajectory step by step. The {{original paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10934}} reports 90.44% alignment with expert consensus against 60.38% for an LLM judge on its 55 development tasks (own runs); {{AJ-Bench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18240}} (April 2026, third party) found the agentic mode added 12.85 points for DeepSeek V3.2 and 13.41 for GPT-5-mini. The cost is more calls and a new failure surface, the judge's own tool use, and MobileJudgeBench adds a caution: a simple screenshot judge matched purpose-built pipelines, the backbone mattering more than the method. Grading actions rather than final messages is the subject of the sibling page {{Agent and trajectory evaluation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81fdbabaf3582bff37f2}}.

Cost. A reasoning judge with a long chain of thought can cost more per item than the system it grades. A jury of small models is usually the better cost-accuracy point for batch scoring, with a reasoning judge kept for the escalated items. Published judge costs come in incompatible units (per 1,000 benchmark runs, per million tokens, hours of human time), so the atlas quotes them as stated rather than converting.

Meta-evaluations: how judges are rated

A meta-evaluation is a labelled test for judges: pairs or sets of responses where the right verdict is known. Each measures something different, and a judge's rank moves between them by up to 14 places (Norman et al.).

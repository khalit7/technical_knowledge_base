Juries and robust aggregation

PoLL ({{Verga et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796}}) replaces one large judge with three smaller ones from disjoint families (Command R, Claude 3 Haiku, GPT-3.5), each judging independently. Binary correctness verdicts are pooled by majority ("max voting"); Arena-style 1 to 5 scores by their average, because three judges often produce no majority. On KILT Natural Questions answers the panel reached kappa 0.763 with human labels against 0.627 for GPT-4 alone (exact string match: 0.651; Claude 3 Haiku alone: 0.749), and its model ranking correlated with Chatbot Arena at Pearson 0.917 against 0.817, "while being over seven times less expensive". The evidence is the authors' own, and the best single small judge came within 0.014 kappa of the panel: much of the gain over GPT-4 is GPT-4 doing worse on this task, not pooling.

One collapsed juror drags the mean; the median holds

Illustrative scores on a 1 to 5 scale, five jurors. When one juror and then two mode-collapse to 1, the mean falls from 4.00 to 3.30 to 2.65; the median from 4.00 to 3.75 to 3.50. For one score per item the geometric median is the ordinary median.

Why the mean is fragile. {{RoPoLL (Acharya et al., arXiv 2606.30931, June 2026, preprint)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30931}} models a jury under Huber contamination and proves that mean pooling "incurs unbounded bias under any positive contamination, regardless of jury size, whenever a single judge fails in a biased, LLM-typical way (mode collapse, sycophancy, safety refusal)". It swaps the mean for the geometric median (a modified Weiszfeld iteration), which needs no tuning, keeps the scores' joint structure, and tolerates up to half the jurors failing. It beat mean pooling on every biased corruption type tested (numbers in the closed block). Under benign Gaussian noise the mean remains optimal, so the median buys insurance, not accuracy. Practical form: median or trimmed mean over jurors, plus per-juror agreement tracked over time so a degraded juror is noticed.

RoPoLL's experiments (depth)

Across 13 open-weight judges (4B to 675B), three reward-model benchmarks and corruption rates up to 50%, the geometric median beat mean pooling on every biased corruption type, by about 19% on cross-dimensional attacks; a 3-judge committee totalling 38B beat Mistral-Large-3 (675B) by 1.31x on HelpSteer-2 under 30% bimodal corruption. Preprint; the authors' own corruption setups.

Debate and meta-judges are expensive and not reliably better: on JudgeBench, ChatEval's multi-agent debate with GPT-4o scored 34.0%, below a single GPT-4o (56.57%, Arena-Hard prompt; JudgeBench v1 Table 1) and below a coin. Keep them for offline audits where a disagreement needs a written argument. Escalation spends the budget where the signal is: a cheap jury on everything; items where jurors disagree, or scores near the threshold, go to a stronger judge; where that judge and the jury still disagree, to a person. The escalated items are also the most useful additions to the gold slice.

Judge models: fine-tuned judges, reward models, verifiers, agents

Four instrument families get called "the judge". They differ in what they output and in how far their accuracy transfers off the data they were built on. One benchmark puts them on one axis.

On hard pairs with a right answer, fine-tuned judges fall below a coin and a reward model beats them all

{{JudgeBench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784}} accuracy (350 hard GPT-4o pairs, one response objectively right; both orders combined). Fine-tuned judges 13.14% (PandaLM-7B) to 40.29% (Prometheus 2 8x7B); reward model Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B 64.29%; o3-mini (high) 80.86% (v2, Apr 2025); NVIDIA's own generative reward model submission 81.4% (vendor-run). Readings from the parent's Judge atlas, each dated there.

Fine-tuned judges (Prometheus 2, JudgeLM, Auto-J, PandaLM, OffsetBias) are open models trained on judgments written by a strong model, usually GPT-4: cheap and private. Each looks close to GPT-4 on its own test set and drops on independent ones (table below). Glider and Selene, named on the old page as part of this family, were not checked here (unconfirmed; not in the atlas). Re-measure on your own gold slice before trusting one on a rubric it was not trained on.

Reward models are a scalar head trained on preference pairs (Bradley-Terry): one forward pass per response, no rationale, built for RLHF and best-of-n. They are strong on tests made for them and transfer unevenly: Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B tops RewardBench 2 at 84.1%, yet scores Kendall tau 0.133 on IF-RewardBench's ranking task against 0.513 for Gemini 3 Flash; Skywork-Reward fell to 46.6% on RM-Bench's style-against-substance pairs, below a coin. They inherit their preference data's length and format preferences in concentrated form, which is what a policy trained against them learns to exploit ({{Reward hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}; how reward models are trained is {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}'s).

Generative verifiers ({{GenRM, Zhang et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15240}}) are trained with the next-token objective to answer "Is the answer correct (Yes/No)?", optionally after a verification chain of thought, and read P("Yes") as the score; because they generate, they can reason and be sampled and majority-voted. Agent-as-judge gives the judge tools: it reads produced files, runs code, checks claims against retrieval, replays a trajectory, at the price of more calls and a new failure surface, its own tool use. Numbers for both are in the closed block; grading trajectories is {{Agent and trajectory evaluation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81fdbabaf3582bff37f2}}'s.

Generative verifiers and agent-as-judge: the evidence (depth)

GenRM for best-of-n lifted GSM8K from 73% to 93.4% (Gemma2-9B verifier on Gemini 1.0 Pro solutions) and algorithmic tasks from 5% to 45.3%, ahead of discriminative reward models and LLM-as-judge prompting. Independent rankings on IF-RewardBench put other generative reward models far lower (RRM-32B tau 0.072). The agent-as-judge {{original paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10934}} reports 90.44% alignment with expert consensus against 60.38% for an LLM judge on its 55 development tasks (own runs); {{AJ-Bench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18240}} (April 2026, third party) found the agentic mode added 12.85 points for DeepSeek V3.2 and 13.41 for GPT-5-mini. MobileJudgeBench adds a caution: a simple screenshot judge matched purpose-built pipelines, the backbone mattering more than the method.

Cost. A reasoning judge with a long chain of thought can cost more per item than the system it grades. A jury of small models is usually the better cost-accuracy point for batch scoring, with a reasoning judge kept for escalated items. Published judge costs come in incompatible units (per 1,000 benchmark runs, per million tokens, hours of human time), so the atlas quotes them as stated rather than converting.

Meta-evaluations: how judges are rated

A meta-evaluation is a labelled test for judges. Each measures something different, and a judge's rank moves between them by up to 14 places (Norman et al.), so use them to shortlist. The ranking that matters is on your own gold slice: a small judge that agrees with your expert at 92% beats a frontier judge at 85% for your task, and the only way to know which is which is to measure both. MT-Bench's human judgments are saturated as a meta-evaluation (all 21 judges in Norman et al. sit inside kappa 0.376 to 0.511) but remain what they are used for here: a real, released human reference.

List-level against instance-level reliability

A judge can rank systems well while being wrong on many single items, because item errors not correlated with the system cancel in the average. The two must be reported, and used, separately.

The judge stretches the scale but keeps the order

Win rate of each MT-Bench model over non-tie verdicts on the same 3,355 votes, humans against GPT-4 (both orders, released). Spearman between the two rankings 0.94 (one swap: Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5-turbo, 1.1 points apart for humans), while the judge agrees with only 66.2% of single votes. It gives GPT-4 a 92.5% win rate where humans give 79.7%, and LLaMA-13B 1.6% where humans give 8.4%. More slices in the {{Protocol lab|#t-proto}}.

RocketEval (ICLR 2025; {{paper page|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ffa8e5f8fa5a5806c8}}) shows the same split with a small grader: with checklists written once by a frontier model, Gemma-2-2B ranks WildBench models at Spearman 0.965 against Chatbot Arena (GPT-4o 0.979, Mistral-Nemo 0.986; one discordant pair of 66 more), yet agrees with humans on single MT-Bench comparisons 57.9% of the time, against 66.6% for GPT-4o and a 64.7% human-to-human level. Its cost: $27.70 for 1,000 WildBench tests with Gemma-2-2B against $3,400 with GPT-4o (123 times less; OpenAI batch prices for GPT-4o). The checklist questions (3 to 25 per query in the released data) never show the grader the other answers, so position and anchoring have no path into the grade, and the score read from the logits as P(Yes)/(P(Yes)+P(No)) keeps the grader's uncertainty.

Use list-level reliability for leaderboards, drift monitoring and distribution-level regression gates ("did the pass rate on 2,000 items drop"); never to adjudicate one item, gate a change on one case, or generate preference labels for training. A checklist needs a frontier model to read every query (never the responses), which weakens the privacy argument for a small local grader.

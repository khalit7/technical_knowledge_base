Calibration against human gold

No judge number means anything until it has been checked against human labels on the same kind of data. Calibration answers three questions in turn: how often does the judge agree with people, corrected for chance; what are its error rates on each class; and what does its pass rate become once those errors are corrected. The labels come from a gold slice: a random subset of the items, labelled by one domain expert with pass or fail and a written critique (Hamel Husain's Critique Shadowing, steps below), about 100 per failure mode.

Building the gold slice: Critique Shadowing, step by step (depth)

Hamel Husain's method ({{Creating an LLM-as-a-Judge That Drives Business Results, 29 Oct 2024|https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/}}), as the post reads in October 2026. How annotators are trained and audited in general is {{Human evaluation and annotation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d819485bcc54a45d49420}}'s.

  1. One principal domain expert (a psychologist for a mental-health assistant, a lawyer for legal analysis) decides what is acceptable.
  2. A dataset spanning the product's features, scenarios and personas, with real system outputs (synthetic user inputs are fine).
  3. The expert gives each output pass or fail with a written critique, detailed enough that a new employee could follow the reasoning. No 1 to 5 scales.
  4. Fix the obvious system failures the review turns up before building any judge.
  5. Build the judge from the expert's critiques as few-shot examples and iterate the prompt until judge and expert agree; in the post's Honeycomb example this reached ">90% agreement" in three iterations.
  6. Error analysis by root cause; about 100 labelled examples per failure mode to validate a judge ("Below 60 examples, the confidence intervals are often too wide").
  7. Specialised judges only where the analysis shows a recurring failure.

The post asks for the judge's true positive rate and true negative rate separately rather than raw agreement, because "agreement can be misleading when failures are rare", and splits the labelled data about 10 to 20% for few-shot examples and 40 to 45% each for development and test. Those two rates are exactly what the correction below needs.

Agreement, corrected for chance

Raw agreement flatters a judge whenever one label dominates. Cohen's kappa subtracts the agreement two raters would reach by chance given how often each uses each label. Here it is on all 3,355 MT-Bench votes against GPT-4's released verdicts.

Two thirds agreement, one third of the way from chance to perfect

Rows: the human vote; columns: GPT-4's verdict (both orders, a disagreement is a tie); "A" is the first model of the pair in a fixed order. The diagonal holds 2,220 of 3,355 votes: po = 0.662. Chance agreement from the row and column totals: pe = Σ row × column / N² = 0.490. κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe) = 0.337.

Across 21 judges in 2026, raw agreement overstated kappa by 33 to 41 points on MT-Bench ({{Norman et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544}}). The human ceiling bounds what a judge can show: two MT-Bench experts agree 63% with ties and 81% without, so a judge near those figures is at the ceiling of the labels, not below it (the parent's Calibration section charts it).

Correcting the pass rate

A judge with errors reports a biased pass rate even when its agreement looks fine. Call the true pass rate θ, the judge's pass rate p, its sensitivity q1 and its specificity q0. Then p = q1θ + (1 − q0)(1 − θ), which inverts to the Rogan-Gladen estimator (1978), used for LLM judges by {{Lee et al. (arXiv 2511.21140, v4 May 2026)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21140}}. Step through it on real data first; the formula follows.

Before: report the judge's rate. After: correct it with a gold slice

Real data: lmsys/mt_bench_human_judgments (3,355 expert and author votes; 6 models; 80 questions, 2 turns) and the GPT-4 verdicts released with it. Human ties are left out, so every item has a true win or loss; judge ties count as "not a win", which is part of why the raw rate is low. Gold slice: seeded random draw (seed 1).

θ̂ = (p̂ + q̂0 − 1) / (q̂0 + q̂1 − 1)
p̂
the judge's pass rate on all n test items
q̂1, q̂0
sensitivity and specificity measured on a gold slice of m1 passing and m0 failing items
interval
the paper's eq. 8: an adjusted Wald interval (add two successes and two failures to each rate) whose variance adds the test-set term p(1−p)/n and the calibration terms (1−θ)²q0(1−q0)/m0 and θ²q1(1−q1)/m1, all divided by (q0+q1−1)², truncated to [0, 1]

The denominator is the whole story. A judge with q0 + q1 near 1 carries almost no information about the truth, and the correction then divides noise by a small number. GPT-3.5-turbo is the clean case: the raw rate is 15 points low mostly because the judge calls many of its wins ties (144 of the 226 human-judged wins it missed), and 100 human labels bring the estimate within 3 points. GPT-4 is the failure case: when humans preferred the other answer, the GPT-4 judge still sided with GPT-4 63% of the time (q̂0 about 0.37), so q0 + q1 is near 1.2 and the interval spans most of [0, 1]. Its raw rate is only 2 points off, by luck of cancelling errors, and nothing in the raw number says so.

Three results from Lee et al. matter in practice. The estimator stays unbiased when the gold slice and the test set have different pass rates, as long as the judge's error rates are the same on both, which is not true of the prediction-powered alternatives. Allocating more gold labels to the class the judge errs on shortens the interval at a fixed budget. And spending labels on calibration beats spending them directly on the eval only when the judge is good enough: on Chatbot Arena win rates none of seven recent judges (Claude Opus 4.6 the closest) reached that region, while on AlpacaEval GPT-4.1-mini did; with 10% of Arena's labelled pairs as the gold slice, intervals covered the true win rate in 95 to 99% of 100 splits, with widths of 0.18 to 0.27. Reliability is not validity

A judge that always prefers slot A is perfectly reproducible

Schematic (dot positions illustrative). Norman et al. found test-retest reliability above 0.95 together with position bias above 0.10 in two production judges: Qwen 3 8B, test-retest 0.992 and position bias 0.192 (JudgeBench kappa 0.289); Gemini 2.5 Flash, 0.988 and 0.125.

Their minimum validation protocol before deploying a judge:

  1. Chance-correct: report Cohen's kappa (or Krippendorff's alpha) as the headline, beside any raw agreement.
  2. Swap positions: run AB and BA and report |P(A wins) − 0.5|.
  3. Replicate: at least 3 runs at temperature 0 with response caching off.
  4. Cross-validate: at least 2 meta-evaluations, one preference-style and one correctness-style.
  5. Audit the paradox: when test-retest is above 0.95, check that position bias is below 0.10 before calling the judge reliable.

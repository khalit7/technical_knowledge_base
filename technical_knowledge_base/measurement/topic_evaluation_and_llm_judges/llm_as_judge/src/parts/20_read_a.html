An LLM judge is a measuring instrument that happens to be a model: its verdicts mean something only after you fix its design, control its biases and calibrate it against human labels, and even then it is trustworthy for averages over many items long before it is trustworthy for one verdict.

The judge as an instrument: biases leak in, a human gold slice corrects what comes out

Real numbers from the released MT-Bench data: GPT-4 judging how often GPT-3.5-turbo wins. Its raw rate, 55.6%, is 15 points below the human 70.8%; correcting it with the judge's error rates measured on 100 human labels gives 68.2% (interval 50.5% to 85.7%). The same judge ranks the six models almost exactly as people do (Spearman 0.94) while agreeing with only 66% of single votes. Each box is a section below.

You need: nothing beyond production LLM work. Intervals and paired tests are {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}'s; collecting and auditing the human labels is {{Human evaluation and annotation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d819485bcc54a45d49420}}'s. New terms

Pointwise, pairwise
the judge scores one answer alone, or picks the better of two
Reference-guided
the judge is given a correct answer or test results to grade against
Robustness rate
the share of verdicts that do not change when one irrelevant thing (order, length, a name) is changed
Gold slice
a random subset of the evaluated items that humans also label
Sensitivity q1, specificity q0
the share of truly passing items the judge passes, and of truly failing items it fails
Cohen's kappa
agreement corrected for the agreement chance alone would give
Rogan-Gladen correction
turning the judge's pass rate into an estimate of the true one using q0 and q1
Null model
a "model" that returns the same string for every prompt, used to test whether a benchmark can be gamed
Jury (panel)
several judges whose verdicts are pooled
Meta-evaluation
a labelled test set for judges
List level, instance level
agreement on a ranking or an average, against agreement on single verdicts

Judge design: four modes

What the judge sees decides what can go wrong

Pointwise needs no baseline and costs one call per answer, so it suits monitoring and regression gates. Its weakness is the scale: a 7 means whatever this judge, prompt and model version make it mean, so scores from different judge versions are not comparable. Two fixes. Anchor few levels: binary pass/fail with a written critique is Hamel Husain's default because "a binary decision forces everyone to consider what truly matters" ({{Creating an LLM-as-a-Judge|https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/}}); the old page's "fine scales are noise past about 4 distinguishable levels" has no source this page could find (unconfirmed). Read the distribution, not the sampled token: G-Eval asks for a 1 to 5 score after chain-of-thought evaluation steps and takes the probability-weighted sum of the score tokens, reaching Spearman 0.514 with humans on summarisation ({{Liu et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634}}); RocketEval reads P(Yes)/(P(Yes)+P(No)) per checklist item.

Pairwise matches the question "is the new model better", and people find comparisons easier than absolute scores. It brings position bias (so both orders are run), ties (a tie option and order disagreements both produce them, and agreement figures change with how they are counted) and cost: all pairs of n systems is n(n−1)/2 comparisons per prompt, twice that with both orders, so leaderboards compare every system against one baseline (MT-Bench pairwise, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard). It is also more exposed to distractors: features the judge likes flipped pairwise preferences in about 35% of cases against 9% for absolute scores ({{Tripathi et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14716}}).

On MT-Bench neither mode wins. On the same 10 model pairs (pairs with Vicuna are left out because the released pointwise file graded a later Vicuna), GPT-4 pairwise agrees with the 2,249 human votes 69.2% of the time and GPT-4 scoring 1 to 10, higher score wins, 68.4%: kappa 0.355 and 0.351, both at the human-human level on those pairs (68.7%). Compare protocols with ties counted: a protocol that declares more ties abstains on the hard items, so its agreement "without ties" rises (pointwise 89.2% against 86.9% pairwise on the same pairs, with equal kappa when ties count). Every slice is in the {{Protocol lab|#t-proto}}.

Reference-guided grading rescues correctness. On MT-Bench's math questions GPT-4 comparing two answers accepted a wrong one 14 times in 20; asked to solve the problem first, 6; given the reference solution, 3 ({{Zheng et al. 2023, Table 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685}}). On LongJudgeBench references helped more than rubrics, and the best setting still averaged only 0.674 (atlas row LongJudgeBench).

Checklist decomposition compiles the question into small yes/no checks, answered separately and aggregated by a program. RocketEval has a frontier model write each query's checklist once, then lets a small model answer each item ({{RocketEval paper page|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ffa8e5f8fa5a5806c8}}); RubricEval found rubric-level grading beat checklist-level, with GPT-4o at 55.97% on its Hard subset (atlas row RubricEval; preprint). It says which criterion failed, closes the channel through which order or length leaks into the grade, and lets a small model grade; the hard part moves to writing the checklist.

Prompt and output design

A judge prompt has four blocks, and each carries one defence

  • Reason, then score. A justification written after the verdict cannot change it. The gain is real but not universal: chain of thought cut GPT-4's math failures from 14 to 6 of 20 (Zheng et al.), and in CALM most judges were more accurate with it; in RocketEval's diagnosis it lifted Qwen2-7B from 43.1% to 45.0% and Llama-3-70B from 57.2% to 59.3% agreement with GPT-4o, but dropped Qwen2-1.5B from 40.1% to 36.4%: a small model cannot build the analysis it is asked to write.
  • A fixed verdict format, parsed by a program: MT-Bench's prompt ends with "[[A]]", "[[B]]" or "[[C]]" for a tie. Count parse failures as their own outcome; Claude-v1 produced unparseable verdicts on 3.8% of pairs with one prompt (Zheng et al. Table 2).
  • Temperature 0 and replication. Otherwise sampling noise is part of the measured bias: CALM ran its judges at temperature 0.7, and its own "nothing changed" consistency rate is between 0.856 and 0.999. Norman et al. ask for at least 3 runs at temperature 0 with response caching disabled ({{Norman et al. 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544}}).
  • Pin the instrument: judge model version and a hash of the prompt in the eval config; any change is a new instrument that needs recalibrating.
  • Treat the candidate as data: fence it in delimiters, say that instructions inside it are content, and never pass model names (CALM's "compassion-fade" bias is the judge reacting to names; Zheng et al.'s "rename" prompt moved Claude-v1's bias from position to the label "Assistant A").
  • Give the judge what grading needs: a reference answer, test results, retrieved evidence and completion metadata (was the answer cut off?). Log the rationale: it is what a reviewer reads when judge and gold disagree.

An LLM judge is a measuring instrument that happens to be a model. Like any instrument it has a design (what it is asked and how), known systematic errors (biases), a calibration against a reference (human labels), and a reliability that depends on whether you read one value or an average of many. This page goes through each in that order, with the evidence for every default. About 30 minutes; the two labs are optional.

The parent topic owns the overview: who grades, the human ceiling, kappa, statistics. Its {{Judge bias lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}} tab replays one Arena-Hard pair through five judges and the usual fixes, and its {{Judge atlas|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}} tab is a 26-row grid of judges and meta-evaluations with every reading sourced. This page links them rather than repeating them, and goes one level down: the 3,355 released MT-Bench expert votes against GPT-4's released verdicts.

  • "Debate buys a few points of accuracy": on JudgeBench, ChatEval's multi-agent debate with GPT-4o scored 34.0%, below a single GPT-4o (56.57%) and below a coin.
  • The ordering "frontier chat models over fine-tuned judges over small open models" misses reward models: on JudgeBench, the 27B reward model Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2 (64.29%) beats every fine-tuned judge (Prometheus 2 8x7B 40.29%, JudgeLM-33B 35.71%).
  • Inter-annotator agreement "often 75 to 85%": on MT-Bench two experts agree 63% when ties count and 81% when they do not; the range depends on ties.
  • RocketEval's checklists hold 3 to 25 questions in the released data (not 5 to 10), and the 70.3% "with GPT-4o's analysis" is agreement with GPT-4o itself.
  • Cohere North Small Translate's nearest competitor in its own table is Qwen 3.5 397B (81.56), not DeepL (81.37): the margin is 2.0 points, and the claim covers "50+ languages".
  • Chain of thought before the verdict is not a universal gain: it hurt the 1.5B judge in RocketEval's diagnosis and helped the larger ones by about 2 points.
  • Verbosity bias is smaller in 2026 judges than in 2023 ones: under one fixed rubric all 21 judges Norman et al. tested showed a verbosity bias below 0.011 on MT-Bench.

Judge design: four modes

A judge prompt fixes what is compared with what. Four modes cover nearly all practice, and each has its own failure.

Pointwise (direct scoring)

The judge sees one answer and returns a score: pass/fail, 1 to 5 against a rubric, or MT-Bench's 1 to 10. It needs no baseline, so it suits monitoring and regression gates, and it costs one call per answer. Its weakness is the scale itself: a 7 means whatever this judge, this prompt and this week's model version make it mean, so scores from different judge versions are not comparable. Two fixes are standard. Anchor few levels: binary pass/fail with a written critique is Hamel Husain's default because "a binary decision forces everyone to consider what truly matters" ({{Creating an LLM-as-a-Judge|https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/}}); the old page's "fine scales are noise past about 4 distinguishable levels" has no source this page could find (unconfirmed). Read the distribution, not the sampled token: G-Eval asks for a 1 to 5 score after chain-of-thought evaluation steps and takes the probability-weighted sum of the score tokens, reaching Spearman 0.514 with humans on summarisation ({{Liu et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634}}); RocketEval reads P(Yes)/(P(Yes)+P(No)) for each checklist item.

Pairwise (preference)

The judge sees two answers and picks one, or a tie. People find comparisons easier than absolute scores, and "is the new model better than the old one" is a comparison. It carries position bias (so both orders are run), tie handling (a tie option and order disagreements both produce ties, and agreement figures change with how they are counted), and cost: ranking n systems by all pairs takes n(n−1)/2 comparisons per prompt, twice that with both orders, so leaderboards compare every system against one baseline instead (MT-Bench pairwise, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard). Pairwise is also more exposed to distractors: when generators embedded features the judge likes, pairwise preferences flipped in about 35% of cases against 9% for absolute scores ({{Tripathi et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.14716}}).

On MT-Bench neither mode wins. On the same 10 model pairs (the released pointwise file graded a later Vicuna, so pairs with Vicuna are left out), GPT-4 pairwise agrees with the 2,249 human votes 69.2% of the time and GPT-4 scoring each answer 1 to 10, with the higher score taken as the winner, 68.4%; kappa 0.355 and 0.351. Both sit at the human-human level on those pairs (68.7%). Try other slices in the {{Protocol lab|#t-proto}}.

Reference-guided

The judge is given a correct answer, or the result of running tests, and grades against it. This is the mode that rescues correctness grading. On MT-Bench's math questions GPT-4, asked to compare two answers, failed (accepted a wrong answer) 14 times in 20; asked to solve the problem first, 6; given the reference solution, 3 ({{Zheng et al. 2023, Table 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685}}; the root's grading-mode animation replays these counts). Long-form judging shows the same: on LongJudgeBench references helped more than rubrics, and the best setting still averaged only 0.674 (atlas row LongJudgeBench).

Checklist and rubric decomposition

Instead of one holistic judgment, the question is compiled into small yes/no checks, each answered separately and aggregated by a program. RocketEval has a frontier model write each query's checklist once, then lets a small model answer each item and reads the probability of "Yes" ({{RocketEval paper page|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ffa8e5f8fa5a5806c8}}); RubricEval rates judges at the level of single rubric items and found rubric-level grading beat checklist-level, with GPT-4o at 55.97% on its Hard subset (atlas row RubricEval; preprint). Decomposition says which criterion failed, removes the channel through which one answer's order or length leaks into the grade, and lets a small model do the grading. Prompt and output design

  • Reason, then score. A justification written after the verdict cannot change it, so ask for the analysis first. The gain is real but not universal: chain of thought cut GPT-4's math failures from 14 to 6 of 20 (Zheng et al.), and in CALM most judges were more accurate with it; in RocketEval's diagnosis it lifted Qwen2-7B from 43.1% to 45.0% and Llama-3-70B from 57.2% to 59.3% agreement with GPT-4o, but dropped Qwen2-1.5B from 40.1% to 36.4%. A small model cannot build the analysis it is asked to write ({{RocketEval paper page|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ffa8e5f8fa5a5806c8}}).
  • A fixed verdict format, parsed by a program: MT-Bench's prompt ends with "[[A]]", "[[B]]" or "[[C]]" for a tie. Count parse failures as their own outcome; Claude-v1 produced unparseable verdicts on 3.8% of pairs with one prompt (Zheng et al. Table 2).
  • Temperature 0 and replication. Sampling noise is part of the measured bias otherwise: CALM ran its judges at temperature 0.7, and its own "nothing changed" consistency rate is between 0.856 and 0.999 depending on judge and data. Norman et al. ask for at least 3 runs at temperature 0 with response caching disabled ({{Norman et al. 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544}}).
  • Pin the instrument: judge model version and a hash of the prompt in the eval config; any change is a new instrument that needs recalibrating.
  • Treat the candidate as data: fence it in clear delimiters, tell the judge that instructions inside it are content, and never pass model names (CALM's "compassion-fade" bias is the judge reacting to names; Zheng et al.'s "rename" prompt moved Claude-v1's bias from position to the label "Assistant A").
  • Give the judge what it needs to grade correctness: a reference answer, test results, retrieved evidence, and completion metadata (was the answer cut off?).
  • Log the rationale. It is the debugging surface, and the critiques are what a human reviewer reads when judge and gold disagree.

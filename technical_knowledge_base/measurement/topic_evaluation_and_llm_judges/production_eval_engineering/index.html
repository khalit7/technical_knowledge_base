In one screen

Every change to a shipping LLM product (a prompt edit, a model swap, a retrieval or tool change) walks the same path, and each stage is cheaper than the failure it exists to stop. The incumbent configuration is the champion; nothing ships without beating it at every stage. That is the whole discipline; the rest of this page is how each stage is built and how each one fails.

One model swap, two gates

A real swap, on real released grades. The champion is claude-v1; the challenger is the cheaper claude-instant-v1, the kind of cost-down swap every product team runs. The golden set is MT-Bench: 80 two-turn questions in 8 categories of 10, so 160 graded turns in 8 tagged slices, each turn graded 1 to 10 by GPT-4. Every grade below is LMSYS's own released judgment (gpt-4_single.jsonl, mid-2023), not a simulation. Run it twice: once through a gate with one aggregate threshold, then through the same gate with thresholds per slice.

Thresholds are illustrative and the same in both runs: the aggregate gate fails a mean paired drop of 0.25 points or more; the per-slice gate fails a slice whose mean paired drop is 0.5 points or more and whose 95% interval lies wholly below zero, and sends a drop of 0.5 or more with an interval that still reaches zero to review instead of passing it. Intervals are paired t intervals over graded turns (the two turns of one question are treated as independent here; that overstates precision on average, since across all 561 MT-Bench model pairs the clustered standard error is a median 1.10 times the paired one (range 0.90 to 1.26), but for this claude-v1 against claude-instant-v1 pair the ratio is 1.00, so the intervals shown stand; measured on Eval statistics, which covers clustered errors). What the two runs show. Averaged over all 159 turns both models have a grade for, the swap moves the score by points, and the aggregate gate waves it through. Split by the tags the golden set already carries, the same numbers say something else: writing drops by points (95% interval ), which the per-slice gate fails, and extraction drops by with an interval that still crosses zero, which goes to review. Math improves by about as much as writing falls (and coding a little), which is exactly why the average hid it. Per-slice thresholds cost nothing extra to compute; they only need the tags.

Golden sets

A golden set is the frozen list of inputs, with expected behaviour, that every change is scored on. It is the instrument the gate reads, so it is built and maintained like code.

What goes in

  • A representative slice of real traffic, deduplicated and scrubbed of personally identifiable information (PII).
  • Regression cases: every production incident becomes a test.
  • Adversarial and edge cases, and safety canaries (items that must always be refused or always be answered).

Each item carries an input, the expected behaviour (a reference answer, a rubric, or programmatic checks) and metadata tags: intent, difficulty, source incident. The tags are what make per-slice gating possible: the animation above could split its 160 turns only because MT-Bench tags every question with a category.

How big

The practical range has been 100 to 500 items per product surface: smaller sets can only gate large effects, larger ones rot because nobody maintains them. Published teams sit on both sides of it, because they mean different things by an item:

Team, dateWhat they gate on
Anthropic, Demystifying evals for AI agents, 9 Jan 2026"20-50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a great start"; regression suites "should have a nearly 100% pass rate"
Uber, Enhanced Agentic-RAG, 29 May 2025A "golden set of 100+ test queries" curated by experts; evaluation time cut "from weeks to minutes"
Rechat (Hamel Husain, Your AI Product Needs Evals, 29 Mar 2024)"hundreds" of assertion-style unit tests, updated from new failures, run in CI on every change
GitHub Copilot (How we evaluate AI models, 17 Jan 2025)"more than 4,000 offline tests, most of them as part of our automated CI pipeline": about 100 containerised repositories with deliberately broken tests, and more than 1,000 chat questions graded by an LLM judge
GitLab Duo (How we validate and test AI models at scale, 9 May 2024)"thousands of prompts tied to dozens of use cases" as a "proxy for production" without customer data; a focused subset during development, the broad set before release, and a daily run against the previous day's baseline

Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's FAQ gives the working numbers for building one from traces: "Start with 100 diverse traces and annotate at least the first 30 yourself", "Label 100 to 200 examples for each failure mode", and expect regression sets to "grow to 100 or more examples"; in CI, "Favor assertions or other deterministic checks over LLM-as-judge" (evals FAQ, September 2026 revision). Anthropic's own documentation leans the other way on volume: "More questions with slightly lower signal automated grading is better than fewer questions with high-quality human hand-graded evals" (Develop tests).

How many items a given threshold needs is a statistics question, answered on Eval statistics. The engineering rule that follows from it: write each gate's minimum detectable effect next to its threshold, so nobody believes a 20-item slice can see a half-point drop.

Promotion paths: how production failures become golden items

The set grows from error analysis: read raw transcripts of failures (Hamel Husain's core discipline, in his evals FAQ), cluster them into named failure modes, then turn each cluster into new tagged items and, where a judge grades it, a new rubric criterion. The last step of the animation is this loop. Teams describe the same path in their own words:

  • DoorDash: "Newly identified issues are systematically added to Promptfoo test suites" (Dasher support automation, Sep 2024).
  • Vercel, for v0: "we add new, failing prompts to the eval set" (Eval-driven development, 17 Oct 2024).
  • Anthropic: capability evals that the system has learned to pass "graduate" to the regression suite, and failures from the bug tracker and support queue become new tasks (9 Jan 2026, above).

Two paths run into a golden set, and they should be kept apart. A regression item guards something that already works and should pass almost always; a capability item measures something the system cannot yet do and is expected to fail often. Mixing them makes the pass rate meaningless. Notion's team, in a Braintrust case study (vendor page, undated), notes that a new model "might pass a regression eval at 100%", which is why they also keep separate frontier evals: a set everyone passes has stopped measuring.

Lifecycle

  • Decay. A golden set drifts away from reality three ways: the product changes, traffic changes, and the set leaks into prompts and fine-tunes. Refresh it on a schedule from recent traffic and error analysis.
  • Criteria drift. Shankar et al. found that "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria" (Who Validates the Validators?, Apr 2024): the expected behaviour itself changes as people read outputs, so rubrics are versioned with the items.
  • Versioning. Any edit to the set is a reviewed change, and the score history is re-baselined when the set changes: a v2 score is not comparable to a v1 score.
  • A held-out split that is never published or sent to a vendor that might train on your traffic.

Regression gates in CI

What trips a gate

  • A paired drop against the champion, per slice. Same items, both configurations, compare per item; a threshold per tagged slice, not one aggregate, for the reason the animation shows. Gating 8 or 20 slices at once means some will fire by chance on every run: use a stricter level per slice, or gate on the aggregate and the slices that matter and treat the rest as diagnostics (Eval statistics has the arithmetic).
  • Hard floors on safety slices, where no trade is allowed: Vercel keeps refusal and safety evals at "a 100% pass rate" while other evals may move.
  • Deterministic assertions: the format parses, the tool call is valid, no forbidden string, the right citation. Cheap, exact, and the first layer of Hamel Husain's three levels (assertion tests on every code change, human and model evaluation on a cadence, A/B tests after significant product changes).
  • Budget limits computed from the same runs (next section).

A gate result is a decision with three outcomes, not two: pass, fail, and "cannot tell", which escalates to more samples or a human. "Re-run until green" turns a gate into a random number generator with a bias toward shipping.

Flaky evals

A gate that fails on an unchanged configuration trains everyone to ignore it. Three sources of flakiness are now well documented:

  • Temperature 0 is not deterministic on a shared server. Thinking Machines sampled the same prompt 1,000 times at temperature 0 and got "80 unique completions", the first divergence at token 103; the cause is that server load changes the batch size, and the kernels are not batch-invariant. With batch-invariant kernels all 1,000 were identical, at a cost of 26 s rising to 55 s for the run (42 s with an improved attention kernel) (Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference, 10 Sep 2025). Through a provider API you cannot fix this, only measure it: score each item K times and gate on the mean. OpenAI's seed is "best effort" and "Determinism is not guaranteed" (API reference); Anthropic's docs say that "Even with temperature set to 0, the results will not be fully deterministic", and the parameter is now deprecated: "Models released after Claude Opus 4.6 do not support setting temperature" (Messages API). A gate that relied on temperature 0 for repeatability has to move to K samples.
  • The judge is a model too. In the animation's data, one of 320 GPT-4 judge calls looped and never emitted a grade; across the whole file 4 of 5,440 did. Parse failures need an explicit rule, and a judge model or prompt change is a new instrument that needs re-baselining (the root page's Calibration section and LLM-as-judge).
  • Agents fail some trials and pass others. Report pass^k (every one of k trials succeeds) beside pass@k: Anthropic's example is a 75% per-trial success rate becoming (0.75)^3, about 42%, over three trials. Agent and trajectory evaluation owns this for agents.

Practice: pin every version, cache champion outputs, re-score K times where decoding is nondeterministic, quarantine items that flip on an unchanged configuration and fix or remove them, and log every gate run with its configuration so a failure can be replayed.

What trips the gate in common tools

The tools differ in what counts as a failure, and the default is often stricter or looser than you think (docs read 2026-10-04; tool depth on Eval harnesses):

DeepEvalpytest-style: "when a metric falls below its threshold the assertion fails, so the build goes red". A flaky=True flag turns a failure into a warning for borderline cases that "flip between passing and failing across runs".

Cost and latency budgets

The product's budget

The second gate reads tokens per request, p95 latency and projected spend from the runs the first gate already made, so it is nearly free. A model swap that wins on quality at 3 times the cost fails promotion unless someone explicitly waives it. Latency belongs in it from the start: Honeycomb's query assistant saw responses take "from two to 15+ seconds" in 2023 (All the Hard Stuff Nobody Talks About, 26 May 2023), and GitHub describes Copilot model choices as a trade between acceptance rate and latency (17 Jan 2025). Discord measures tokens per second during a limited release "to predict the costs of a complete launch" (Developing Rapidly with Generative AI, 12 Apr 2024).

Model choice is a Pareto problem. Plot calibrated quality against cost per request (and latency) for every candidate, and only points on the frontier are candidates. Three practical additions. Cascades and routing: a cheap model with escalation on low confidence or judge-flagged outputs often beats any single point. Quality per dollar as the gate metric on cost-sensitive surfaces. And re-run the frontier on every price or model-version change, because frontiers move month to month: the chart above is a 2023 snapshot, not advice.

The evals' own budget

The gate costs money too, and judge calls dominate it. The animation's run took 320 GPT-4 calls averaging tokens each (counted with GPT-4's tokenizer on the released prompts and judgments): at the 2023 price of $0.03 per 1,000 prompt tokens and $0.06 per 1,000 completion tokens (OpenAI pricing, May 2023 capture). Small per run, but a gate runs on every change, a shadow judge runs on a share of all traffic, and the champion's scores must be recomputed whenever the judge changes. Published reference points:

  • Vercel: "LLM evals still cost 1.5x to 2x more than code-based grading", so deterministic checks go first (17 Oct 2024).
  • LinkedIn "had to set timetables for when it was ok to test the product or not, as it'd consume too many tokens" (Musings on Building a Generative AI Product, 25 Apr 2024).
  • Checklist judges move the economics: grading all of WildBench 1,000 times costs $27.7 with Gemma-2-2B against $3,400 with GPT-4o, at a near-identical model ranking but weaker per-item agreement, so they suit aggregate and drift gates, not single-item verdicts (RocketEval, paper page).

Judge cost is therefore part of the frontier: panels of small judges exist because scoring production traffic with a frontier judge inverts the economics. The Judge atlas tab on the root page compares judges.

Offline against online

Offline evals catch the regressions you anticipated; online metrics catch what your golden set could not imagine. Both are required, and they disagree routinely.

  • Online instruments: sampled judge scoring of live traces (asynchronous, outside the request path); implicit signals (retry and regenerate rate, how much users edit an output, abandonment, escalation to a human); explicit feedback (thumbs, ratings); and the business or task-completion metric itself.
  • Their delays differ by days. LinkedIn's annotators "give more granular feedback but have a ~1 day turnaround", while member metrics "can take 3+ days for a single change"; their linguists rated up to 500 conversations a day (25 Apr 2024).
  • Offline rankings often fail to predict online ones. Copilot Arena's in-editor preference ranking had "low correlation (rs ≤ 0.1) with most static benchmarks" (0.62 with Chatbot Arena's coding ranking). And the online metric is itself a proxy: for Copilot, acceptance rate "correlates best with aggregate productivity" among usage measures, at only ρ = 0.24 (Ziegler et al. 2022).
  • Divergence is an alarm on the offline set. If a candidate wins offline and loses online, the golden set or the judge is miscalibrated for current traffic: find the divergent segment and feed it back into the golden set.
  • Online judge scores inherit every judge failure (LLM-as-judge), plus sampling bias (which traffic gets scored) and label lag; correct them with the judge's true- and false-positive rates measured on a human-labelled slice, as for offline scores.
When every number looked fine: GPT-4o, April 2025
OpenAI shipped a GPT-4o update on 24 to 25 April 2025 that was markedly sycophantic, and rolled it back; "The full rollback took around 24 hours". In their account, "offline evaluations, especially those testing behavior, generally looked good" and "the A/B tests seemed to indicate that the small number of users who tried the model liked it"; "some expert testers had indicated that the model behavior 'felt' slightly off", and "We also didn't have specific deployment evaluations tracking sycophancy." The update had leaned on thumbs-up and thumbs-down signals, and they "focused too much on short-term feedback". Their fixes are pipeline fixes: behavioural issues become launch-blocking, an opt-in alpha phase, and "blocking launches based on proxy measurements or qualitative signals, even when metrics like A/B testing look good" (Sycophancy in GPT-4o, 29 Apr 2025; Expanding on what we missed with sycophancy, May 2025). The lesson for this page: an online metric that users can reward is itself an instrument that can be gamed, and a golden set without a slice for a failure mode cannot see it.

Shadow deployments

In shadow mode the candidate runs on mirrored production requests; its responses are logged, never served. Champion and candidate are then scored on the same requests, so the comparison is paired by construction, and score distributions are diffed per intent or segment. Microsoft's experimentation platform describes the same idea for LLM features: shadow experiments "compute the treatment and control response for the same user, but only show them the control response", which makes metrics "more sensitive than regular A/B tests" because both arms share the same users; the limit is that "metrics that need user response cannot be evaluated in shadow experiments" (How to Evaluate LLMs: A Complete Metric Framework, 27 Sep 2023).

  • What shadow catches: the distribution gap between curated and real inputs; errors, timeouts and rate limits (the same framework tracks the 90th and 95th percentile of HTTP 429 errors and time to first token); latency under real load.
  • What it cannot catch: anything that depends on how a user reacts (accepting, editing, retrying), which only an A/B test shows.
  • Agents: shadow whole trajectories, not single turns, and make every tool with side effects a dry run or a sandbox in the shadow path: a mirrored agent that really sends the email is not a shadow.
  • Infrastructure supports it directly: AWS SageMaker shadow tests send copies of requests to a shadow variant at a chosen "traffic sampling percentage", return "Only the responses of the production variant", and compare latency and errors side by side for 1 hour to 30 days (shadow tests); Seldon Core 2 mirrors traffic to a model whose responses "will not be returned to the caller" (experiments). Quality scoring of the shadow responses is yours to add.
  • Cost: inference doubles on the mirrored share, plus judge calls; mirror a sample, not everything.

Canary, A/B tests and interleaving

The last gate exposes real users. A canary sends a small share of traffic (or only staff: GitHub runs an internal canary with its own employees before Copilot changes reach customers) to the candidate with guardrail metrics (error rate, refusal rate, complaints, task completion) and automatic rollback triggers; then a progressive rollout. Google's SRE workbook defines canarying as "a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation", compares canary against a concurrent control rather than against yesterday because "time is one of the biggest sources of change", and keeps the metrics to "perhaps no more than a dozen" (Canarying releases). An A/B test randomises users between champion and candidate and compares a primary metric. Anthropic's 2026 guide notes that A/B results take "days or weeks to reach significance", which is why everything cheaper runs first.

Interleaving is the sensitive alternative from search ranking: each user sees one merged list built from both rankers, and credit goes to whichever ranker contributed the items the user chose. Netflix reported that interleaving "requires >100× fewer users than our most sensitive A/B metric to achieve 95% power", and uses it as a first stage to prune candidates before an A/B test on the survivors (Interleaving at Netflix, 29 Nov 2017). Airbnb reported a "50x speedup over an A/B test given the same amount of traffic", about 6% of a regular test's traffic for a third of its duration, agreeing with the A/B result "82% of the time" (Beyond A/B Test, 6 Oct 2022).

For LLM features, interleaving only fits where the output is a list (search, retrieval, code suggestions to pick from). For a single free-text answer, the closest analogue is a side-by-side preference shown to the user: OpenAI lists "preferences in side by side comparisons" among the aggregate A/B metrics it reads (May 2025). That inherits every bias of pairwise judging, done by users instead of a model, and the Human preference and arenas page covers how such votes mislead.

The experimentation hygiene that carries over

LLM features inherit everything online experimentation learned, and the same mistakes:

  • Why interleaving is so sensitive: it "is a paired test, paired on both queries and users". On Yahoo data, an absolute click metric needed between 17.8 and 379 times as much data as interleaving for five of six ranker pairs (and less for the sixth), "over an order of magnitude" overall (Chapelle et al., Large-scale validation and analysis of interleaved search evaluation, TOIS 2012). The same pairing is why shadow comparisons and paired golden-set gates beat unpaired ones.
  • Side-by-side inside a product: Copilot Arena showed two code completions stacked in the editor, with Tab or Shift+Tab as the vote, order randomised "to remove top-bottom bias"; over "4.5 million suggestions from 10 models". A pair is as slow as its slower model, and a latency-aware sampler cut median latency from 1.61 to 1.07 seconds (Copilot Arena, Feb 2025).
  • Sample ratio mismatch (SRM): when the split of users between arms differs from the design, the test is usually broken: "approximately 6% of experiments at Microsoft exhibit an SRM", detected with a chi-square test (Fabijan et al., KDD 2019). LLM features add new causes: timeouts and rate limits that drop one arm's sessions.
  • Guardrail metrics "Measure aspects of the product that we don't want to degrade but won't necessarily improve" (Microsoft ExP, 2021): for an LLM feature, latency, error and refusal rates, cost per session.
  • Variance reduction: CUPED, adjusting each user's metric by the same metric before the experiment, reduced variance "by about 50%" at Bing, the same power "with only half of the users, or half the duration" (Deng et al., WSDM 2013).
  • Peeking: checking a running test daily and stopping at the first significant result inflates false positives; "Type I error can easily increase fivefold" even with 10,000 samples, unless the test uses always-valid p-values built for continuous monitoring (Johari et al.). The canonical reference for all of this is Kohavi, Tang and Xu, Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments (2020).

Online learning closes the loop entirely. Cursor retrains its Tab completion model on live accept and reject signals, taking "1.5 to 2 hours to roll out a checkpoint and collect the data" across "over 400 million requests per day"; the new policy made "21% fewer suggestions" with a "28% higher accept rate" (Improving Cursor Tab with online RL, 12 Sep 2025). The metric is now the reward, so it needs the same scepticism as any reward (Reward hacking).

Monitoring and drift

Passing every gate is a statement about the day of launch. Afterwards, everything moves:

  • Traffic drift: new users, new intents, new languages; watch the input mix and per-intent judge scores, not only the global mean.
  • Product drift: the features around the model change what "good" means; the golden set and rubrics must follow (criteria drift, above).
  • Provider and infrastructure drift: the model behind an API, or the serving stack around it, changes without your prompt changing. Pin model snapshots where the provider offers them, and keep a canary set running against production daily: GitLab validates "daily" against "the baseline from the day before", and GitHub runs its tests "against our production models every day".
When the serving stack drifted: Anthropic, August to September 2025
Three infrastructure bugs degraded some Claude responses. One misrouted requests to servers configured for a different context window: it "initially affected 0.8% of requests", then 16% of Sonnet 4 requests at the worst hour on 31 August, and about "30% of Claude Code users" had at least one message misrouted. "The evaluations we ran simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting, in part because Claude often recovers well from isolated mistakes", and "We relied too heavily on noisy evaluations." The fix is a monitoring fix: run quality evaluations "continuously on true production systems" (A postmortem of three recent issues, 17 Sep 2025). Offline gates test a configuration; only production monitoring tests the system that actually serves it.

Honeycomb ran its query assistant against a service-level objective: a "Target of 75% successful requests over a seven day period", later raised to 80%, against "95% or higher" for most of their services, with a yes, no or not-sure feedback widget feeding it (Improving LLMs in Production With Observability, 16 Jun 2023). An SLO turns monitoring into an alert with a budget instead of a dashboard nobody reads.

How much live traffic to score, and how to see drift

  • Sampling rates for online judges, from vendor docs: Braintrust advises "lower rates (1-10%)" for high-volume applications and "50-100%" for low-volume or critical ones (online scoring); LangSmith's example scores "10% of traces" (online evaluators); Langfuse puts "A typical evaluation" at "$0.01-0.10 per assessment" (LLM-as-a-judge). At 1 million requests a day, 1% sampled at $0.03 is $300 a day: the judge bill is a budget line.
  • Input drift on text: a common test trains a classifier to tell last month's inputs from this week's; Evidently flags drift when its ROC AUC exceeds 0.55 on more than 1,000 observations (drift explainer), and recommends the same "model-based drift detection as a good default" for embeddings (embedding drift, 2023, updated 2025).
  • Close the loop: sample live traces, run reference-free judges asynchronously, "track confidence intervals", and feed new failures back into the CI set (Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's FAQ). That is the promotion path from the Golden sets section, run continuously.

Eval-driven development

The same machinery used during development rather than only at release: write the eval for a behaviour before changing the prompt, model or pipeline that should produce it, and let every change run against it. Vercel named the practice for v0, whose prompts changed "almost daily" (Eval-driven development, 17 Oct 2024); Eugene Yan describes it as the counterpart of test-driven development, with "every prompt tweak, every system update" evaluated (An LLM-as-Judge Won't Save The Product, Apr 2025). Who coined the phrase is unconfirmed.

  • Start small and from failures: Anthropic's 20 to 50 tasks drawn from real failures; Hamel Husain's assertions first, judges second, A/B tests last.
  • Label before you automate: a judge used in development needs a human-labelled calibration set; Yan suggests aiming for a "50:50 split of passes and fails" so both kinds of error can be measured.
  • The last 20% is the work: LinkedIn reached 80% of the experience in the first month, then "spent an additional four months attempting to surpass 95%"; their structured-output errors fell from about 10% to "~0.01%" along the way.
  • Mind compounding: Honeycomb's arithmetic for chained steps: five steps that are each 90% right give "59% accurate" end to end (0.95 = 0.59), so gate the steps as well as the whole.

Eval statistics: error bars, paired tests, how many items you need

A golden-set score is a mean over items, so it has a standard error; a 2-point drop on 100 items is noise. Compare configurations on the same items and test the per-item differences (paired tests, McNemar for pass/fail), which cuts the items needed sharply; re-sample nondeterministic outputs K times; cluster errors when items share a source; correct for gating many slices at once; state each gate's minimum detectable effect. The old page's worked example (sizing a model-swap gate on 300 items) lives there, with two corrections found while moving it.

Human evaluation and annotation: rubrics, agreement, gold-label auditing

Gold labels are wrong at rates that dominate small deltas (MMLU-Redux estimates about 6.5% of MMLU questions contain an error of some kind, and 57% of its virology subset). So store a confidence and provenance field per label, route model-against-gold disagreements through a consensus battery and human adjudication, keep "ambiguous, exclude from gating" as a valid label state, and gate on the verified subset. Every gate failure triggers an audit of the failing items before the failure is believed: the eval system has three fallible parts (model, judge, gold), and the root page's attribution animation (Reading, "How it breaks") shows the split on six physics benchmarks.

Common mistakes

Gating on one aggregate number
Improvements in one slice hide regressions in another; the animation's swap moved the average by while writing lost points. Tag items and threshold per slice.
Comparing unpaired scores
Two configurations scored on different samples, or a fresh run compared against last month's dashboard number, mix item difficulty into the difference. Score both on the same items and subtract per item.
Re-running a red gate until it turns green
With nondeterministic decoding and judges, enough re-runs make any gate pass. Fix K samples per item in advance, and send "cannot tell" to review.
Averaging in a judge failure
An unparsable verdict is not a score. Count parse failures as their own metric, drop the pair from both sides, and alert when the rate moves (4 in 5,440 in the MT-Bench file; a jump means the judge or its prompt changed).
Editing the golden set without re-baselining
A v2 score is a reading on a different instrument. Version the set, re-score the champion on every new version, and never chart v1 and v2 numbers on one line.
Mixing regression and capability items
Regression items should pass nearly always; capability items should fail often. One pass rate over both moves for reasons nobody can read.
Trusting the A/B test as the final word
Users can reward the wrong thing (GPT-4o, April 2025), and a short test reads short-term behaviour. Keep launch-blocking behavioural evals and qualitative checks beside it.
Stopping at launch
Serving bugs, provider updates and traffic drift all arrive after the last gate (Anthropic, August 2025). Run evals continuously against production, with an SLO.
Letting a shadow agent act
Mirror the trajectory, not the side effects: tools with side effects must be dry runs in shadow mode.
Forgetting the evals' own bill
A frontier judge on every change and on a share of live traffic can cost more than the feature. Put deterministic checks first and match the judge to the decision it supports.

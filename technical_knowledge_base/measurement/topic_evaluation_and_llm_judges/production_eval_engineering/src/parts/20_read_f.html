7. Online evaluation

Offline evals catch the regressions you anticipated; online measurement catches what the golden set could not imagine. The instruments: sampled judge scoring of live traces (asynchronous, outside the request path, usually reference-free because live traffic has no reference answers); implicit signals (retry and regenerate rate, how much users edit an output, abandonment, escalation to a human); explicit feedback (thumbs, ratings); and the business or task-completion metric itself. The first design decision is how much traffic to judge.

The judge bill scales with the sampling rate; the rare slice is what runs short. Defaults: 1 million requests a day and $0.03 per judged trace, inside Langfuse's "A typical evaluation costs $0.01-0.10 per assessment" ({{LLM-as-a-judge|https://langfuse.com/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/llm-as-a-judge}}); at 1% that is 10,000 judged traces and $300 a day (cost = requests x rate x price). The shaded bands are the vendor advice: Braintrust suggests "lower rates (1-10%)" for high-volume applications and "50-100%" for low-volume or critical ones ({{online scoring|https://www.braintrust.dev/docs/evaluate/score-online}}); LangSmith's example scores "10% of traces" ({{online evaluators|https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/online-evaluations-llm-as-judge}}). An intent that is 2% of traffic (illustrative) gets only 200 judged traces a day at 1%: sample by slice, not only uniformly, when a small slice matters.

  • Their delays differ by days. LinkedIn's annotators "give more granular feedback but have a ~1 day turnaround", while member metrics "can take 3+ days for a single change"; their linguists rated up to 500 conversations a day (25 Apr 2024).
  • Offline rankings often fail to predict online ones. Copilot Arena's in-editor preference ranking had "low correlation (rs ≤ 0.1) with most static benchmarks" (0.62 with Chatbot Arena's coding ranking). And the online metric is itself a proxy: for Copilot, acceptance rate "correlates best with aggregate productivity" among usage measures, at only ρ = 0.24 ({{Ziegler et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06537}}).
  • Divergence is an alarm on the offline set. If a challenger wins offline and loses online, the golden set or the judge is miscalibrated for current traffic: find the divergent segment and feed it back into the golden set (section 1's loop).
  • Online judge scores inherit every judge failure, plus sampling bias (which traffic gets scored) and label lag; correct them with the judge's true- and false-positive rates measured on a human-labelled slice, and "track confidence intervals" (Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's FAQ). How to correct a judge-derived rate: {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}.
When every number looked fine: GPT-4o, April 2025
OpenAI shipped a GPT-4o update on 24 to 25 April 2025 that was markedly sycophantic, and rolled it back; "The full rollback took around 24 hours". In their account, "offline evaluations, especially those testing behavior, generally looked good" and "the A/B tests seemed to indicate that the small number of users who tried the model liked it"; "some expert testers had indicated that the model behavior 'felt' slightly off", and "We also didn't have specific deployment evaluations tracking sycophancy." The update had leaned on thumbs-up and thumbs-down signals, and they "focused too much on short-term feedback". Their fixes are pipeline fixes: behavioural issues become launch-blocking, an opt-in alpha phase, and "blocking launches based on proxy measurements or qualitative signals, even when metrics like A/B testing look good" ({{Sycophancy in GPT-4o|https://openai.com/index/sycophancy-in-gpt-4o/}}, 29 Apr 2025; {{Expanding on what we missed with sycophancy|https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/}}, May 2025). An online metric users can reward can be gamed, and a golden set without a slice for a failure mode cannot see it.

8. Shadow, canary, A/B tests and interleaving

Four ways to put a challenger in front of real traffic, from no user exposure to full randomisation. They differ in who sees the challenger's answer, and therefore in what they can measure:

Only designs that show the challenger to users can measure user reactions; only paired designs get high sensitivity cheaply. Shadow pairs both arms on the same request but cannot see behaviour; A/B sees behaviour but compares different users; interleaving pairs both rankers inside one user's list, which is why it needs so much less traffic.

Shadow

The challenger runs on mirrored production requests; its responses are logged, never served. Scored on the same requests as the champion, the comparison is paired by construction, and score distributions are diffed per intent. Microsoft's experimentation platform: shadow experiments "compute the treatment and control response for the same user, but only show them the control response", which makes metrics "more sensitive than regular A/B tests"; the limit is that "metrics that need user response cannot be evaluated in shadow experiments" ({{How to Evaluate LLMs: A Complete Metric Framework|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/group/experimentation-platform-exp/articles/how-to-evaluate-llms-a-complete-metric-framework/}}, 27 Sep 2023).

  • What shadow catches: the gap between curated and real inputs; errors, timeouts and rate limits (the same framework tracks the 90th and 95th percentile of HTTP 429 errors and time to first token); latency under real load. Cost: inference doubles on the mirrored share, plus judge calls; mirror a sample.
  • Agents: shadow whole trajectories, not single turns, and make every tool with side effects a dry run or a sandbox in the shadow path: a mirrored agent that really sends the email is not a shadow.
  • Infrastructure supports it: AWS SageMaker shadow tests copy requests to a shadow variant at a chosen "traffic sampling percentage", return "Only the responses of the production variant", and compare latency and errors for 1 hour to 30 days ({{shadow tests|https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sagemaker/latest/dg/shadow-tests.html}}); Seldon Core 2 mirrors traffic to a model whose responses "will not be returned to the caller" ({{experiments|https://docs.seldon.ai/seldon-core-2/user-guide/experiment.md}}). Quality scoring of the shadow responses is yours to add.

Canary and A/B

A canary sends a small share of traffic (or only staff: GitHub runs an internal canary with its own employees before Copilot changes reach customers) to the challenger, with guardrail metrics (error rate, refusal rate, complaints, task completion, cost per session) and automatic rollback triggers; then a progressive rollout. Google's SRE workbook defines canarying as "a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation", compares the canary against a concurrent control rather than against yesterday because "time is one of the biggest sources of change", and keeps the metrics to "perhaps no more than a dozen" ({{Canarying releases|https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/}}). An A/B test randomises users between champion and challenger and compares a primary metric; Anthropic notes A/B results take "days or weeks to reach significance", which is why everything cheaper runs first.

Interleaving

From search ranking: each user sees one merged list built from both rankers, and credit goes to whichever ranker contributed the items the user chose. Netflix reported that interleaving "requires >100× fewer users than our most sensitive A/B metric to achieve 95% power", and uses it to prune candidates before an A/B test on the survivors ({{Interleaving at Netflix|https://netflixtechblog.com/interleaving-in-online-experiments-at-netflix-a04ee392ec55}}, 29 Nov 2017). Airbnb reported a "50x speedup over an A/B test given the same amount of traffic", about 6% of a regular test's traffic for a third of its duration, agreeing with the A/B result "82% of the time" ({{Beyond A/B Test|https://medium.com/airbnb-engineering/beyond-a-b-test-speeding-up-airbnb-search-ranking-experimentation-through-interleaving-7087afa09c8e}}, 6 Oct 2022). For LLM features it fits only where the output is a list (search, retrieval, code suggestions to pick from); for a single free-text answer the analogue is a side-by-side preference shown to the user, which OpenAI lists among the A/B metrics it reads ("preferences in side by side comparisons", May 2025) and which inherits every bias of pairwise voting ({{Human preference and arenas|n:3ef5c17b0d0d814db49ff3b5c9fc3034}}).

Experimentation hygiene that carries over to LLM features (SRM, guardrails, CUPED, peeking, side-by-side)
  • Why interleaving is so sensitive: it "is a paired test, paired on both queries and users". On Yahoo data, an absolute click metric needed between 17.8 and 379 times as much data as interleaving for five of six ranker pairs (and less for the sixth), "over an order of magnitude" overall (Chapelle et al., {{Large-scale validation and analysis of interleaved search evaluation|https://www.cs.cornell.edu/people/tj/publications/chapelle_etal_12a.pdf}}, TOIS 2012). The same pairing is why shadow comparisons and paired golden-set gates beat unpaired ones.
  • Side-by-side inside a product: Copilot Arena showed two code completions stacked in the editor, with Tab or Shift+Tab as the vote, order randomised "to remove top-bottom bias"; over "4.5 million suggestions from 10 models". A pair is as slow as its slower model, and a latency-aware sampler cut median latency from 1.61 to 1.07 seconds ({{Copilot Arena|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09328}}, Feb 2025).
  • Sample ratio mismatch (SRM): when the split of users between arms differs from the design, the test is usually broken: "approximately 6% of experiments at Microsoft exhibit an SRM", detected with a chi-square test ({{Fabijan et al., KDD 2019|https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2019_KDDFabijanGupchupFuptaOmhoverVermeerDmitriev.pdf}}). LLM features add new causes: timeouts and rate limits that drop one arm's sessions.
  • Guardrail metrics "Measure aspects of the product that we don't want to degrade but won't necessarily improve" ({{Microsoft ExP, 2021|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/articles/patterns-of-trustworthy-experimentation-during-experiment-stage/}}).
  • Variance reduction: CUPED, adjusting each user's metric by the same metric before the experiment, reduced variance "by about 50%" at Bing, the same power "with only half of the users, or half the duration" ({{Deng et al., WSDM 2013|https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013-02-CUPED-ImprovingSensitivityOfControlledExperiments.pdf}}).
  • Peeking: checking a running test daily and stopping at the first significant result inflates false positives; "Type I error can easily increase fivefold" even with 10,000 samples, unless the test uses always-valid p-values built for continuous monitoring ({{Johari et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922}}). The canonical reference for all of this is Kohavi, Tang and Xu, {{Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments|https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/trustworthy-online-controlled-experiments/D97B26382EB0EB2DC2019A7A7B518F59}} (2020); bandits as an alternative to a fixed split: {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}.
  • When the metric becomes the reward: Cursor retrains its Tab completion model on live accept and reject signals, taking "1.5 to 2 hours to roll out a checkpoint and collect the data" across "over 400 million requests per day"; the new policy made "21% fewer suggestions" with a "28% higher accept rate" ({{Improving Cursor Tab with online RL|https://cursor.com/blog/tab-rl}}, 12 Sep 2025). The metric then needs the same scepticism as any reward ({{Reward hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}).

1. Error analysis: where eval items come from

An eval set is not written from imagination. It is read out of real traces: you look at what the system actually did, name the ways it fails, and only then write a check for each named failure. Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's FAQ gives the working numbers: review "at least 100 traces", annotate "at least 30 traces yourself", and stop at theoretical saturation, "meaning new reviews stop revealing failure modes or changing existing ones" ({{evals FAQ|https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/}}, read 2026-10-24). They report having "spent 60-80% of our development time on error analysis and evaluation". Fig. 1 walks the loop on this page's running example.

Fig. 1's traces are the challenger's 159 graded turns from section 3, and a trace fails when it scored below the champion on the same turn. MT-Bench's category stands in for the failure mode: real axial coding names modes from the notes, not from a tag the data already carries. The counts, the reading orders and the three examples are computed from LMSYS's released grades.

  1. Sample traces. Traces stream in from production, a shadow run or a beta; here, 159 of them in one random reading order. Mix sampling methods from random to targeted (closed block below): targeted sampling finds only what you already suspect.
  2. Open coding. Read each trace and write what went wrong in your own words, with no rubric yet; annotate at least 30 yourself before reading any model's suggestions. Here 45 of the 159 scored below the champion, and each gets a note.
  3. Axial coding. Group the notes into a failure taxonomy: named modes you can count, which the FAQ calls "the most important step". Here the 45 fall into 7 categories; humanities has none.
  4. Count. Tally each mode: writing 12, reasoning 7, roleplay 6, extraction 6, STEM 6, coding 5, math 3. The counts order the work: a frequent mode gets an evaluator and a fix first; a rare but severe one may get a hard floor.
  5. Saturate. Keep reading until new traces stop revealing modes or changing existing ones. In this order the seventh category first appears at trace 57; over 1,000 seeded random orders, after a median of 56 traces, by trace 91 in 90% of them, and within 100 traces in 949. A small taxonomy can need most of the 100 traces the FAQ asks for.
  6. One evaluator per mode. A code check when an objective rule identifies the failure; otherwise an LLM judge, validated on human-labelled examples of that mode. Binary pass or fail.
  7. Promote. Confirmed failures become tagged items in the next golden-set version, each mode its own slice for the gate (section 4). The three worst writing drops (question 86, 8 to 4; question 89, 10 to 7; question 81, 10 to 8; all second turns) are all rewrites under an explicit constraint, so they share a name, "constrained rewrite". Failures found later by shadow, A/B tests or monitoring re-enter at step 1.

Name the failure before you measure it. Notes first (open coding), categories second (axial coding), evaluators third: a rubric written before reading "can get in the way", because "Reviewers can become so focused on checking each item that they overlook problems outside the rubric".

Binary labels, one failure mode per evaluator. The FAQ prefers pass or fail to 1 to 5 scales: "Binary evaluations force clearer thinking and more consistent labeling", while on Likert scales "annotators often default to middle values". Each named mode gets either a code check, when an objective rule can identify it, or a judge validated on "100 to 200 examples for each failure mode" (judge design and its agreement measures: {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}).

How to pick which traces to read (five sampling methods)
MethodWhat it doesMain limitation
RandomSelects traces with equal probabilityA small batch can miss rare cases
ClusteringGroups traces by similar content and selects from each groupDepends on the features and clustering choices
Data analysisReviews extreme values such as latency or tool countAn extreme value may have nothing to do with quality
ClassificationAn evaluator or another model flags likely failuresFavours problems the classifier already knows how to find
FeedbackTraces with negative user feedbackMisses problems that users do not report

The table runs from most exploratory to most targeted; start exploratory, lean on signals as you learn, and "Keep some random traces in every batch" (same FAQ). Where traces come from and how they are sampled and redacted at collection time is {{LLM observability|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fe82c0c6e9360afec4}}'s subject.

Promotion: how a failure becomes a golden item

Every confirmed failure becomes a new tagged item and, where a judge grades it, a rubric criterion. Teams describe the same path: DoorDash, "Newly identified issues are systematically added to Promptfoo test suites" ({{Dasher support automation|https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/large-language-modules-based-dasher-support-automation/}}, Sep 2024); Vercel for v0, "we add new, failing prompts to the eval set" ({{Eval-driven development|https://vercel.com/blog/eval-driven-development-build-better-ai-faster}}, 17 Oct 2024); Anthropic, failures from the bug tracker and support queue become new tasks, and capability evals the system has learned to pass "graduate" to the regression suite ({{Demystifying evals for AI agents|https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents}}, 9 Jan 2026).

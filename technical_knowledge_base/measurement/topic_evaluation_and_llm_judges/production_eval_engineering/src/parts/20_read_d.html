Cost and latency budgets

The product's budget

The second gate reads tokens per request, p95 latency and projected spend from the runs the first gate already made, so it is nearly free. A model swap that wins on quality at 3 times the cost fails promotion unless someone explicitly waives it. Latency belongs in it from the start: Honeycomb's query assistant saw responses take "from two to 15+ seconds" in 2023 ({{All the Hard Stuff Nobody Talks About|https://www.honeycomb.io/blog/hard-stuff-nobody-talks-about-llm}}, 26 May 2023), and GitHub describes Copilot model choices as a trade between acceptance rate and latency (17 Jan 2025). Discord measures tokens per second during a limited release "to predict the costs of a complete launch" ({{Developing Rapidly with Generative AI|https://discord.com/blog/developing-rapidly-with-generative-ai}}, 12 Apr 2024).

Model choice is a Pareto problem. Plot calibrated quality against cost per request (and latency) for every candidate, and only points on the frontier are candidates. Below: the four strongest models in the animation's MT-Bench file at their mid-2023 list prices. Move the input share: the frontier depends on your traffic's mix of prompt and completion tokens.

Three practical additions. Cascades and routing: a cheap model with escalation on low confidence or judge-flagged outputs often beats any single point. Quality per dollar as the gate metric on cost-sensitive surfaces. And re-run the frontier on every price or model-version change, because frontiers move month to month: the chart above is a 2023 snapshot, not advice.

The evals' own budget

The gate costs money too, and judge calls dominate it. The animation's run took 320 GPT-4 calls averaging tokens each (counted with GPT-4's tokenizer on the released prompts and judgments): at the 2023 price of $0.03 per 1,000 prompt tokens and $0.06 per 1,000 completion tokens ({{OpenAI pricing, May 2023 capture|https://web.archive.org/web/20230531155533/https://openai.com/pricing}}). Small per run, but a gate runs on every change, a shadow judge runs on a share of all traffic, and the champion's scores must be recomputed whenever the judge changes. Published reference points:

  • Vercel: "LLM evals still cost 1.5x to 2x more than code-based grading", so deterministic checks go first (17 Oct 2024).
  • LinkedIn "had to set timetables for when it was ok to test the product or not, as it'd consume too many tokens" ({{Musings on Building a Generative AI Product|https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/generative-ai/musings-on-building-a-generative-ai-product}}, 25 Apr 2024).
  • Checklist judges move the economics: grading all of WildBench 1,000 times costs $27.7 with Gemma-2-2B against $3,400 with GPT-4o, at a near-identical model ranking but weaker per-item agreement, so they suit aggregate and drift gates, not single-item verdicts ({{RocketEval|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ffa8e5f8fa5a5806c8}}, paper page).

Judge cost is therefore part of the frontier: panels of small judges exist because scoring production traffic with a frontier judge inverts the economics. The {{Judge atlas|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}} tab on the root page compares judges.

Offline against online

Offline evals catch the regressions you anticipated; online metrics catch what your golden set could not imagine. Both are required, and they disagree routinely.

  • Online instruments: sampled judge scoring of live traces (asynchronous, outside the request path); implicit signals (retry and regenerate rate, how much users edit an output, abandonment, escalation to a human); explicit feedback (thumbs, ratings); and the business or task-completion metric itself.
  • Their delays differ by days. LinkedIn's annotators "give more granular feedback but have a ~1 day turnaround", while member metrics "can take 3+ days for a single change"; their linguists rated up to 500 conversations a day (25 Apr 2024).
  • Offline rankings often fail to predict online ones. Copilot Arena's in-editor preference ranking had "low correlation (rs ≤ 0.1) with most static benchmarks" (0.62 with Chatbot Arena's coding ranking). And the online metric is itself a proxy: for Copilot, acceptance rate "correlates best with aggregate productivity" among usage measures, at only ρ = 0.24 ({{Ziegler et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.06537}}).
  • Divergence is an alarm on the offline set. If a candidate wins offline and loses online, the golden set or the judge is miscalibrated for current traffic: find the divergent segment and feed it back into the golden set.
  • Online judge scores inherit every judge failure ({{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}), plus sampling bias (which traffic gets scored) and label lag; correct them with the judge's true- and false-positive rates measured on a human-labelled slice, as for offline scores.
When every number looked fine: GPT-4o, April 2025
OpenAI shipped a GPT-4o update on 24 to 25 April 2025 that was markedly sycophantic, and rolled it back; "The full rollback took around 24 hours". In their account, "offline evaluations, especially those testing behavior, generally looked good" and "the A/B tests seemed to indicate that the small number of users who tried the model liked it"; "some expert testers had indicated that the model behavior 'felt' slightly off", and "We also didn't have specific deployment evaluations tracking sycophancy." The update had leaned on thumbs-up and thumbs-down signals, and they "focused too much on short-term feedback". Their fixes are pipeline fixes: behavioural issues become launch-blocking, an opt-in alpha phase, and "blocking launches based on proxy measurements or qualitative signals, even when metrics like A/B testing look good" ({{Sycophancy in GPT-4o|https://openai.com/index/sycophancy-in-gpt-4o/}}, 29 Apr 2025; {{Expanding on what we missed with sycophancy|https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/}}, May 2025). The lesson for this page: an online metric that users can reward is itself an instrument that can be gamed, and a golden set without a slice for a failure mode cannot see it.

Shadow deployments

In shadow mode the candidate runs on mirrored production requests; its responses are logged, never served. Champion and candidate are then scored on the same requests, so the comparison is paired by construction, and score distributions are diffed per intent or segment. Microsoft's experimentation platform describes the same idea for LLM features: shadow experiments "compute the treatment and control response for the same user, but only show them the control response", which makes metrics "more sensitive than regular A/B tests" because both arms share the same users; the limit is that "metrics that need user response cannot be evaluated in shadow experiments" ({{How to Evaluate LLMs: A Complete Metric Framework|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/group/experimentation-platform-exp/articles/how-to-evaluate-llms-a-complete-metric-framework/}}, 27 Sep 2023).

  • What shadow catches: the distribution gap between curated and real inputs; errors, timeouts and rate limits (the same framework tracks the 90th and 95th percentile of HTTP 429 errors and time to first token); latency under real load.
  • What it cannot catch: anything that depends on how a user reacts (accepting, editing, retrying), which only an A/B test shows.
  • Agents: shadow whole trajectories, not single turns, and make every tool with side effects a dry run or a sandbox in the shadow path: a mirrored agent that really sends the email is not a shadow.
  • Infrastructure supports it directly: AWS SageMaker shadow tests send copies of requests to a shadow variant at a chosen "traffic sampling percentage", return "Only the responses of the production variant", and compare latency and errors side by side for 1 hour to 30 days ({{shadow tests|https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sagemaker/latest/dg/shadow-tests.html}}); Seldon Core 2 mirrors traffic to a model whose responses "will not be returned to the caller" ({{experiments|https://docs.seldon.ai/seldon-core-2/user-guide/experiment.md}}). Quality scoring of the shadow responses is yours to add.
  • Cost: inference doubles on the mirrored share, plus judge calls; mirror a sample, not everything.

Canary, A/B tests and interleaving

The last gate exposes real users. A canary sends a small share of traffic (or only staff: GitHub runs an internal canary with its own employees before Copilot changes reach customers) to the candidate with guardrail metrics (error rate, refusal rate, complaints, task completion) and automatic rollback triggers; then a progressive rollout. Google's SRE workbook defines canarying as "a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation", compares canary against a concurrent control rather than against yesterday because "time is one of the biggest sources of change", and keeps the metrics to "perhaps no more than a dozen" ({{Canarying releases|https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/}}). An A/B test randomises users between champion and candidate and compares a primary metric. Anthropic's 2026 guide notes that A/B results take "days or weeks to reach significance", which is why everything cheaper runs first.

Interleaving is the sensitive alternative from search ranking: each user sees one merged list built from both rankers, and credit goes to whichever ranker contributed the items the user chose. Netflix reported that interleaving "requires >100× fewer users than our most sensitive A/B metric to achieve 95% power", and uses it as a first stage to prune candidates before an A/B test on the survivors ({{Interleaving at Netflix|https://netflixtechblog.com/interleaving-in-online-experiments-at-netflix-a04ee392ec55}}, 29 Nov 2017). Airbnb reported a "50x speedup over an A/B test given the same amount of traffic", about 6% of a regular test's traffic for a third of its duration, agreeing with the A/B result "82% of the time" ({{Beyond A/B Test|https://medium.com/airbnb-engineering/beyond-a-b-test-speeding-up-airbnb-search-ranking-experimentation-through-interleaving-7087afa09c8e}}, 6 Oct 2022).

For LLM features, interleaving only fits where the output is a list (search, retrieval, code suggestions to pick from). For a single free-text answer, the closest analogue is a side-by-side preference shown to the user: OpenAI lists "preferences in side by side comparisons" among the aggregate A/B metrics it reads (May 2025). That inherits every bias of pairwise judging, done by users instead of a model, and the {{Human preference and arenas|n:3ef5c17b0d0d814db49ff3b5c9fc3034}} page covers how such votes mislead.

The experimentation hygiene that carries over

LLM features inherit everything online experimentation learned, and the same mistakes:

  • Why interleaving is so sensitive: it "is a paired test, paired on both queries and users". On Yahoo data, an absolute click metric needed between 17.8 and 379 times as much data as interleaving for five of six ranker pairs (and less for the sixth), "over an order of magnitude" overall (Chapelle et al., {{Large-scale validation and analysis of interleaved search evaluation|https://www.cs.cornell.edu/people/tj/publications/chapelle_etal_12a.pdf}}, TOIS 2012). The same pairing is why shadow comparisons and paired golden-set gates beat unpaired ones.
  • Side-by-side inside a product: Copilot Arena showed two code completions stacked in the editor, with Tab or Shift+Tab as the vote, order randomised "to remove top-bottom bias"; over "4.5 million suggestions from 10 models". A pair is as slow as its slower model, and a latency-aware sampler cut median latency from 1.61 to 1.07 seconds ({{Copilot Arena|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09328}}, Feb 2025).
  • Sample ratio mismatch (SRM): when the split of users between arms differs from the design, the test is usually broken: "approximately 6% of experiments at Microsoft exhibit an SRM", detected with a chi-square test ({{Fabijan et al., KDD 2019|https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2019_KDDFabijanGupchupFuptaOmhoverVermeerDmitriev.pdf}}). LLM features add new causes: timeouts and rate limits that drop one arm's sessions.
  • Guardrail metrics "Measure aspects of the product that we don't want to degrade but won't necessarily improve" ({{Microsoft ExP, 2021|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/articles/patterns-of-trustworthy-experimentation-during-experiment-stage/}}): for an LLM feature, latency, error and refusal rates, cost per session.
  • Variance reduction: CUPED, adjusting each user's metric by the same metric before the experiment, reduced variance "by about 50%" at Bing, the same power "with only half of the users, or half the duration" ({{Deng et al., WSDM 2013|https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013-02-CUPED-ImprovingSensitivityOfControlledExperiments.pdf}}).
  • Peeking: checking a running test daily and stopping at the first significant result inflates false positives; "Type I error can easily increase fivefold" even with 10,000 samples, unless the test uses always-valid p-values built for continuous monitoring ({{Johari et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922}}). The canonical reference for all of this is Kohavi, Tang and Xu, {{Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments|https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/trustworthy-online-controlled-experiments/D97B26382EB0EB2DC2019A7A7B518F59}} (2020).

Online learning closes the loop entirely. Cursor retrains its Tab completion model on live accept and reject signals, taking "1.5 to 2 hours to roll out a checkpoint and collect the data" across "over 400 million requests per day"; the new policy made "21% fewer suggestions" with a "28% higher accept rate" ({{Improving Cursor Tab with online RL|https://cursor.com/blog/tab-rl}}, 12 Sep 2025). The metric is now the reward, so it needs the same scepticism as any reward ({{Reward hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}).

Monitoring and drift

Passing every gate is a statement about the day of launch. Afterwards, everything moves:

  • Traffic drift: new users, new intents, new languages; watch the input mix and per-intent judge scores, not only the global mean.
  • Product drift: the features around the model change what "good" means; the golden set and rubrics must follow (criteria drift, above).
  • Provider and infrastructure drift: the model behind an API, or the serving stack around it, changes without your prompt changing. Pin model snapshots where the provider offers them, and keep a canary set running against production daily: GitLab validates "daily" against "the baseline from the day before", and GitHub runs its tests "against our production models every day".
When the serving stack drifted: Anthropic, August to September 2025
Three infrastructure bugs degraded some Claude responses. One misrouted requests to servers configured for a different context window: it "initially affected 0.8% of requests", then 16% of Sonnet 4 requests at the worst hour on 31 August, and about "30% of Claude Code users" had at least one message misrouted. "The evaluations we ran simply didn't capture the degradation users were reporting, in part because Claude often recovers well from isolated mistakes", and "We relied too heavily on noisy evaluations." The fix is a monitoring fix: run quality evaluations "continuously on true production systems" ({{A postmortem of three recent issues|https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/a-postmortem-of-three-recent-issues}}, 17 Sep 2025). Offline gates test a configuration; only production monitoring tests the system that actually serves it.

Honeycomb ran its query assistant against a service-level objective: a "Target of 75% successful requests over a seven day period", later raised to 80%, against "95% or higher" for most of their services, with a yes, no or not-sure feedback widget feeding it ({{Improving LLMs in Production With Observability|https://www.honeycomb.io/blog/improving-llms-production-observability}}, 16 Jun 2023). An SLO turns monitoring into an alert with a budget instead of a dashboard nobody reads.

How much live traffic to score, and how to see drift

  • Sampling rates for online judges, from vendor docs: Braintrust advises "lower rates (1-10%)" for high-volume applications and "50-100%" for low-volume or critical ones ({{online scoring|https://www.braintrust.dev/docs/evaluate/score-online}}); LangSmith's example scores "10% of traces" ({{online evaluators|https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/online-evaluations-llm-as-judge}}); Langfuse puts "A typical evaluation" at "$0.01-0.10 per assessment" ({{LLM-as-a-judge|https://langfuse.com/docs/evaluation/evaluation-methods/llm-as-a-judge}}). At 1 million requests a day, 1% sampled at $0.03 is $300 a day: the judge bill is a budget line.
  • Input drift on text: a common test trains a classifier to tell last month's inputs from this week's; Evidently flags drift when its ROC AUC exceeds 0.55 on more than 1,000 observations ({{drift explainer|https://docs.evidentlyai.com/metrics/explainer_drift}}), and recommends the same "model-based drift detection as a good default" for embeddings ({{embedding drift|https://www.evidentlyai.com/blog/embedding-drift-detection}}, 2023, updated 2025).
  • Close the loop: sample live traces, run reference-free judges asynchronously, "track confidence intervals", and feed new failures back into the CI set (Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's FAQ). That is the promotion path from the Golden sets section, run continuously.

Eval-driven development

The same machinery used during development rather than only at release: write the eval for a behaviour before changing the prompt, model or pipeline that should produce it, and let every change run against it. Vercel named the practice for v0, whose prompts changed "almost daily" ({{Eval-driven development|https://vercel.com/blog/eval-driven-development-build-better-ai-faster}}, 17 Oct 2024); Eugene Yan describes it as the counterpart of test-driven development, with "every prompt tweak, every system update" evaluated ({{An LLM-as-Judge Won't Save The Product|https://eugeneyan.com/writing/eval-process/}}, Apr 2025). Who coined the phrase is unconfirmed.

  • Start small and from failures: Anthropic's 20 to 50 tasks drawn from real failures; Hamel Husain's assertions first, judges second, A/B tests last.
  • Label before you automate: a judge used in development needs a human-labelled calibration set; Yan suggests aiming for a "50:50 split of passes and fails" so both kinds of error can be measured.
  • The last 20% is the work: LinkedIn reached 80% of the experience in the first month, then "spent an additional four months attempting to surpass 95%"; their structured-output errors fell from about 10% to "~0.01%" along the way.
  • Mind compounding: Honeycomb's arithmetic for chained steps: five steps that are each 90% right give "59% accurate" end to end (0.95 = 0.59), so gate the steps as well as the whole.

Who grades

An eval is only as good as its grader, and each kind of grader sees a different part of the answer.

A program: exact match, regex, tests

The cheapest grader compares the answer with a reference: string match after normalisation, a regex that pulls out the final answer, unit tests for code. It is deterministic and free, and it is blind to anything the reference did not anticipate: an equivalent formula, another unit, a correct answer phrased differently. When physicists re-checked six physics benchmarks, the automatic graders' mistakes were mostly equivalent expressions and conventions, not model errors ({{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}}). On open question answering, "does the reference string appear in the response" agreed with human judges at Cohen's kappa 0.651 on Natural Questions, against 0.763 for a panel of three small LLM judges ({{Verga et al. 2024, Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796}}).

Loglikelihood against generation

For multiple choice there are two ways to ask a model for its answer. Loglikelihood scoring never lets the model write: it reads the probability the model assigns to each option and takes the most likely one. It is cheap, deterministic and works on base models, but it measures ranking among given options, and API models that hide their probabilities cannot be run this way. Generative scoring lets the model write (often reasoning first) and parses the answer out of the text, which is what chat and reasoning models need, and which makes every answer-extraction regex a potential bug.

The choice moves scores by more than the gaps being compared. In June 2023 Hugging Face scored the same LLaMA-65B checkpoint on the same MMLU questions three ways: 0.636 with the original implementation (probability of the letters A to D), 0.637 with HELM's (the model generates a letter), and 0.488 with the evaluation harness of the time (probability of the full answer text); Falcon-40B came out above LLaMA-65B only under the third ({{Hugging Face, What's going on with the Open LLM Leaderboard?|https://huggingface.co/blog/open-llm-leaderboard-mmlu}}). (The LLaMA paper's own Table 9 reports 63.4.) A score from one harness cannot be set beside a score from another, whatever both call the benchmark.

On a small chat model the scoring method is not even the biggest knob. In {{Same model, many harnesses|#t-harness}}, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on 200 MMLU items: the chat template cost 33.5 points, a lenient parser added 17.5, and one instruction line ("Answer with only the letter.") added 30.5, while loglikelihood against generation on the raw prompt moved it under 2 points. Extraction and formatting outweigh the scoring method.

An LLM judge, in three modes

An LLM judge is a measuring instrument that happens to be a model, and the grading mode fixes its bias profile.

  • Pointwise (score one answer alone, say 1 to 10): no baseline needed, so it suits monitoring, but the scale drifts across judge versions and prompts, and fine scales add noise; few anchored levels (pass/fail, or 1 to 3 with a description per level) are steadier.
  • Pairwise (which of A and B is better): closer to how people judge, and the natural question for a model swap. It carries position bias, so both orders must be run, and ranking n candidates costs on the order of n² comparisons.
  • Rubric or reference-guided (grade against a checklist or a reference answer): the most reliable mode wherever a reference exists, and the only one that says which criterion failed.

The animation runs the same judge through these protocols on the published counts of the paper that named the pattern ({{Zheng et al. 2023, MT-Bench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685}}). "Position" is Table 2: 80 pairs of near-identical answers (two GPT-3.5 samples per MT-Bench question), judged in one order and then swapped. "Math" is Table 4: GPT-4 judging LLaMA-13B against Vicuna-13B on 10 math questions in both orders, where a failure is calling a wrong answer correct.

Counts are exact (Table 2 percentages times 80; Table 4 counts out of 20); which square is which is illustrative, since the paper publishes counts, not per-item verdicts. These are near-identical answers, the hardest case for a judge: with a real quality gap consistency is much higher (GPT-4 keeps its winner in 83.6% of the released MT-Bench leaderboard judgments, {{Judge bias lab|#t-bias}}). Default prompt throughout; Table 2 also shows a "rename" prompt, where Claude-v1's bias moves from position to the name "Assistant A".

Two more grader designs complete the picture. Juries: a panel of small judges from different model families, majority-voted, agreed with humans better than GPT-4 alone on three question-answering sets and cost over seven times less ({{Verga et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796}}; measured on answer correctness with references and on Arena-Hard, not on every task). Checklists: {{RocketEval|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ffa8e5f8fa5a5806c8}} diagnoses why small judges fail (they cannot hold a full analysis of a long response in one pass) and stops asking them to: a frontier model writes each query's checklist of yes/no questions once (3 to 25 in the released checklists; the paper says 5 to 10), a small judge answers each question on its own, and the score is read from the probability of "Yes". Since no item is graded in sight of the others, position and anchoring bias have no path through. It ranks a field of models almost as well as GPT-4o and is much weaker on single verdicts (numbers under Choosing below).

Adjacent instruments, often confused with judges: reward models (a scalar score per response, trained on preferences for RLHF and best-of-n; fast, no rationale, and they inherit their data's length and format biases), fine-tuned judge models (Prometheus 2 and similar; good on the rubrics they were trained on, re-check them on yours), and agent-as-judge (the judge gets tools: runs the code, checks claims, replays a trajectory; more accurate on agentic work, at more cost and with a new failure surface, the judge's own tool use).

People

Humans are the reference everything else is calibrated against: domain experts for correctness, crowds for preference (Chatbot Arena's pairwise votes). They are slow and expensive, and they disagree with each other, which sets the ceiling for every automatic grader (next section).

Evaluation in one screen

An eval is a measuring instrument. It has a thing it measures, a noise level, ways it breaks, and it has to be calibrated against something trusted before its readings mean anything. Most evaluation mistakes come from reading the instrument as if it were the quantity: treating a judge's score as quality, a two-point drop as a regression, or a failed item as a model failure.

This topic is about how evaluation is done and where it breaks. What is measured, and how to read a published score, belongs to {{Topic: benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}}; here the question is the one you face when you build an LLM system: is this change better, and how sure am I?

The one idea to keep
Every eval result has three fallible parts: the model, the grader (a regex, a judge model, a human) and the gold labels (the reference answers or rubric). When a score moves, any of the three may have moved it. Attribute the change before acting on it: in practice the grader and the gold labels are wrong more often than people expect (How it breaks has a real audit where 238 of 250 "model failures" were not the model's).

The five layers, cheapest to most faithful

Mature stacks run all five. Each one catches something the previous one cannot, at a higher price per judgment.

Static benchmarksFixed questions, scored by a program: exact match, a regex on the answer, unit tests, or the probability the model gives each option.Cost: cents per item, deterministic.Catches: capability changes on closed-form tasks, reproducibly.
LLM-as-judgeA model grades open-ended outputs: one at a time (pointwise), two side by side (pairwise), or against a rubric or reference.Cost: one model call per item.Catches what a regex cannot: quality, helpfulness, style, partial credit. A biased proxy for humans.
Human evaluationExperts against a gold standard, or crowds voting on pairs (Arena-style preference).Cost: minutes and dollars per item.Catches what the judge is blind to; it is the reference the judge is calibrated against, and it is noisy itself.
Regression gatesFrozen golden sets, 100 to 500 items per product surface, run in CI on every prompt, model or retrieval change.Cost: minutes per change.Catches regressions on your product before release, which public benchmarks never see.
Online measurementShadow deployment on mirrored traffic, then canary and A/B tests with live metrics.Cost: real traffic and real risk.Catches what no curated set imagined: real traffic is uglier than any golden set.

Runtime guardrails (classifiers in the request path, before, during and after the model call) are evals too, measured with the same machinery: they get their own page, {{Guardrails: staged runtime safety for LLM systems|n:3c65c17b0d0d819bbfa5dffa9246c297}}.

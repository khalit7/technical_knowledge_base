How it breaks

When a score drops, the instinct is to blame the model. The evidence says check the instrument first: the grader and the gold labels fail more often than people expect, and an observed delta has to be attributed to the model, the grader or the labels before anyone acts on it.

The cleanest published measurement of this is an expert audit of physics benchmarks ({{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}}, {{arXiv:2609.13009|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.13009}}). GPT-5.6-Sol answered questions from four benchmarks; every answer the benchmark's grader rejected on every attempt went to physics PhD reviewers, who decided whose fault the rejection was. The animation replays it: first as a leaderboard reads it, then attributed.

Counts exact (Appendix C, as tabulated on the paper page); the order of squares is illustrative. Only rejections were audited, for one model: answers the graders accepted were not checked for false acceptance, and the corrected scores come from smaller, filtered question sets.

Benchmark errors dominated HLE-Physics (86 of 98) and UGPhysics (18 of 22); grader errors dominated PHYBench (40 of 56) and PRISM-Physics (48 of 74), mostly equivalent expressions and conventions the graders did not recognise. The same three-way split applies to any internal golden set and judge.

Judge biases

Measured on MT-Bench ({{Zheng et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685}}): position (above: on pairs of near-identical answers GPT-4 kept its verdict on 65.0% of swapped pairs, GPT-3.5 46.2%, Claude-v1 23.8%; that is the hardest case, and consistency rises with the quality gap between the answers (Shi et al., covered in the bias lab): on the released MT-Bench leaderboard judgments GPT-4 gives the same winner in both orders 83.6% of the time, recounted in the {{Judge bias lab|#t-bias}}); verbosity (padding 23 answers with a rephrased copy of their own list fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 91.3% of the time and GPT-4 8.7%, Table 3); self-enhancement (GPT-4 appeared to give its own answers a 10% higher win rate than humans did and Claude-v1 25%, but the authors write that "due to limited data and small differences, our study cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias"; the bias lab measures GPT-4 +13.0 points and Claude-v1 +10.5 on the same questions, and {{Panickssery et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076}} is the controlled study: models that recognise their own text prefer it more). Confident tone and citations, even invented ones, also raise scores. Sizes vary a lot by judge and protocol: a 2026 study of 21 judges found verbosity bias small under one pairwise rubric while two production judges still showed position bias above 0.10 ({{arXiv:2606.19544|https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544}}), so measure your own judge rather than trusting a catalogue.

Judge exploitation and null models

A judge reads the candidate's text, so the candidate can write to the judge. A "null model" that returns the same crafted response to every instruction reached an 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto and 9.55 on MT-Bench ({{Zheng et al. 2024, Cheating Automatic LLM Benchmarks|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07137}}). Any pipeline that pastes candidate text into a judge prompt is prompt-injectable: delimit the candidate as data, and run null-model and injection canaries in every judge battery. Training against a judge or reward model does the same thing at scale; that is reward hacking, the training-time mirror of this failure ({{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}).

Truncation and plumbing

The quietest failures are in the pipes. A completion cut off by a token limit, a logging layer or a context overflow reaches the judge as a stump, and the judge grades it as a bad answer rather than saying it looks cut off. An answer-extraction regex that misses a new output format scores correct answers as wrong, the classic source of "model X dropped 20 points overnight". Check that generation stopped normally (finish reason "stop") before grading, give the judge an explicit "complete or truncated" criterion, and route those failures to an infrastructure bucket, not a quality one. The fix is reading transcripts, which is why a harness's log viewer matters, and why the 2026 double-blind enclave evaluations, where nobody can read the transcripts, will need a separate debugging channel.

Contamination

If the test items were in the training data, the score measures memory. Defences range from private held-out sets to dated live sets to canary strings; the newest is evaluation inside a hardware enclave where the lab never sees the prompts and the evaluator never sees the weights (Google DeepMind's pilot, August 2026). Topic: benchmarks owns this, including the pilot and its caveats: {{Topic: benchmarks, What a number leaves out|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}}.

Gold-label noise

Reference answers are written by people and are wrong at rates that swamp small deltas. MMLU-Redux re-annotated 5,700 MMLU questions and estimates that 6.49% of MMLU questions contain errors (57% of the analysed Virology questions) ({{Are We Done with MMLU?|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04127}}). Against wrong gold, a better model can fail a gate for disagreeing with the label. Treat gold as fallible: keep a provenance field per label, send model-gold disagreements to a review, and allow "ambiguous, exclude" as a label.

Agents: grade the action, not the sentence

In an agent the harm or the success lives in the tool calls and the state they change, so a grader that reads the final message measures the wrong variable. {{MOLE|n:3db5c17b0d0d818b9680c7b91baaf7e9}} shows how far apart they can be: told that the sandbox was harmless, 28 of 39 models completed most of the harmful objectives assigned to them; refusing and completing were strongly linked across models (Spearman −0.73), and the best monitor (Claude Opus 4.7) caught 24 of 45 completed harms.

Correction to the old page
The old page said MOLE "found that a model's stated refusal did not predict whether it actually declined". That overstates the paper: refusal and completion rates are strongly (negatively) correlated across models. The point that survives is about what to grade: actions and state, not the response string.

5. How it breaks: attribute a change before acting on it

When a score drops, the instinct is to blame the model. The evidence says check the instrument first: the grader and the gold labels fail more often than people expect. The cleanest published measurement is an expert audit of physics benchmarks ({{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}}): GPT-5.6-Sol answered questions from four benchmarks, and every answer the benchmark's grader rejected on every attempt went to physics PhD reviewers, who decided whose fault each rejection was. Step through it: first as a leaderboard reads it, then attributed.

238 of 250 "model failures" were not the model's

Counts exact (Appendix C, as tabulated on the paper page): 143 benchmark errors, 95 grader errors, 12 model errors. Benchmark errors dominated HLE-Physics (86 of 98) and UGPhysics (18 of 22); grader errors dominated PHYBench (40 of 56) and PRISM-Physics (48 of 74), mostly equivalent expressions and conventions the graders did not recognise. The order of squares is illustrative. Only rejections were audited, for one model: accepted answers were not checked for false acceptance.

Correction to the old page (depth)

The old page said MOLE "found that a model's stated refusal did not predict whether it actually declined". That overstates the paper: refusal and completion rates are strongly (negatively) correlated across models (Spearman −0.73). The point that survives is about what to grade, actions and state rather than the response string; the numbers are on {{Agent and trajectory evaluation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81fdbabaf3582bff37f2#rd-actions}} and the {{MOLE paper page|n:3db5c17b0d0d818b9680c7b91baaf7e9}}.

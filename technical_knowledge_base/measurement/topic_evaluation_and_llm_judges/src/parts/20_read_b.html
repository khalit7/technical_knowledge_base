Calibration: checking the instrument against people

A judge's number means nothing until it has been compared with human labels on your task. Which judge to use is therefore an empirical, per-task decision, made on a human-labelled gold slice, never a default.

The ceiling is human agreement, not 100%

People disagree with each other on open-ended answers, so no automatic grader can be expected to agree with "the human" more than humans agree among themselves. MT-Bench measured it directly: expert votes on pairs of answers, compared expert against expert and GPT-4 against experts.

On non-tie votes, GPT-4 agreed with the experts as often as the experts agreed with each other. So a judge that agrees 80% with your expert may already be at the ceiling rather than underperforming, and a judge claiming 95% on open-ended work should make you suspicious of the labels. The ceiling holds for experts on closed questions too: in the physics re-grading audit, two PhD reviewers agreed on 140 of the 196 cases they both reviewed (71.4%), and a third reviewer settled the other 56 ({{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}}).

Report kappa, not raw agreement

Raw agreement flatters a judge whenever one label dominates. Cohen's kappa subtracts the agreement two graders would reach by chance, given how often each says pass: κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe), where po is observed agreement and pe the chance agreement from the two pass rates. This is not a corner case. Norman et al. (2026), studying 21 judges on about 541,000 judgments, found kappa 33 to 41 points below raw agreement on MT-Bench for every judge ({{Reliability without Validity, arXiv:2606.19544|https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544}}). The ceiling itself moves with the test: LLMBar's curated pairs reach 94% human agreement, while DevAI's three experts disagreed on 10 to 30% of requirements (both in the {{Judge atlas|#t-judges}}).

The gold slice, and correcting the aggregate

The working recipe: a few hundred items from real traffic, labelled pass or fail by one domain expert who also writes a one-line critique of each; iterate the judge prompt until judge-expert kappa stops improving; pin the judge model and prompt version, since any change is a new instrument; re-check on fresh samples when traffic shifts (Hamel Husain's {{Critique Shadowing|https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/}}). The same slice then corrects the headline number: a judge with imperfect sensitivity (TPR) and specificity (1 − FPR) reports a biased pass rate, and the plug-in correction is true rate ≈ (judge rate − FPR) / (TPR − FPR), with an interval that carries the uncertainty of both the test set and the gold slice ({{How to Correctly Report LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluations, arXiv:2511.21140|https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21140}}).

Self-consistency certifies nothing

A judge that gives the same verdict every time it is asked is reliable, not valid. The 2026 study above found test-retest reliability above 0.95 together with position bias above 0.10 in two production-deployed judges: perfectly repeatable, and systematically wrong. Only agreement with people, chance-corrected, measures validity.

Meta-evaluations help choose a judge before your own calibration: {{JudgeBench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784}} builds hard pairs whose right answer is objectively checkable (random guessing scores 50%). With the Arena-Hard judge prompt, GPT-4o scores 56.6% and o3-mini (high) 80.9% (Table 2, v2, April 2025). A global ranking is still only a prior: a small judge that agrees with your expert on your task beats a famous one that does not.

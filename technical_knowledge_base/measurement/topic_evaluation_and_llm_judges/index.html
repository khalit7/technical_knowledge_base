Evaluation in one screen

An eval is a measuring instrument. It has a thing it measures, a noise level, ways it breaks, and it has to be calibrated against something trusted before its readings mean anything. Most evaluation mistakes come from reading the instrument as if it were the quantity: treating a judge's score as quality, a two-point drop as a regression, or a failed item as a model failure.

This topic is about how evaluation is done and where it breaks. What is measured, and how to read a published score, belongs to Topic: benchmarks; here the question is the one you face when you build an LLM system: is this change better, and how sure am I?

The one idea to keep
Every eval result has three fallible parts: the model, the grader (a regex, a judge model, a human) and the gold labels (the reference answers or rubric). When a score moves, any of the three may have moved it. Attribute the change before acting on it: in practice the grader and the gold labels are wrong more often than people expect (How it breaks has a real audit where 238 of 250 "model failures" were not the model's).

The five layers, cheapest to most faithful

Mature stacks run all five. Each one catches something the previous one cannot, at a higher price per judgment.

Who grades

An eval is only as good as its grader, and each kind of grader sees a different part of the answer.

A program: exact match, regex, tests

The cheapest grader compares the answer with a reference: string match after normalisation, a regex that pulls out the final answer, unit tests for code. It is deterministic and free, and it is blind to anything the reference did not anticipate: an equivalent formula, another unit, a correct answer phrased differently. When physicists re-checked six physics benchmarks, the automatic graders' mistakes were mostly equivalent expressions and conventions, not model errors (Re-grading six physics benchmarks). On open question answering, "does the reference string appear in the response" agreed with human judges at Cohen's kappa 0.651 on Natural Questions, against 0.763 for a panel of three small LLM judges (Verga et al. 2024, Table 1).

Loglikelihood against generation

For multiple choice there are two ways to ask a model for its answer. Loglikelihood scoring never lets the model write: it reads the probability the model assigns to each option and takes the most likely one. It is cheap, deterministic and works on base models, but it measures ranking among given options, and API models that hide their probabilities cannot be run this way. Generative scoring lets the model write (often reasoning first) and parses the answer out of the text, which is what chat and reasoning models need, and which makes every answer-extraction regex a potential bug.

The choice moves scores by more than the gaps being compared. In June 2023 Hugging Face scored the same LLaMA-65B checkpoint on the same MMLU questions three ways: 0.636 with the original implementation (probability of the letters A to D), 0.637 with HELM's (the model generates a letter), and 0.488 with the evaluation harness of the time (probability of the full answer text); Falcon-40B came out above LLaMA-65B only under the third (Hugging Face, What's going on with the Open LLM Leaderboard?). (The LLaMA paper's own Table 9 reports 63.4.) A score from one harness cannot be set beside a score from another, whatever both call the benchmark.

On a small chat model the scoring method is not even the biggest knob. In Same model, many harnesses, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on 200 MMLU items: the chat template cost 33.5 points, a lenient parser added 17.5, and one instruction line ("Answer with only the letter.") added 30.5, while loglikelihood against generation on the raw prompt moved it under 2 points. Extraction and formatting outweigh the scoring method.

An LLM judge, in three modes

An LLM judge is a measuring instrument that happens to be a model, and the grading mode fixes its bias profile.

  • Pointwise (score one answer alone, say 1 to 10): no baseline needed, so it suits monitoring, but the scale drifts across judge versions and prompts, and fine scales add noise; few anchored levels (pass/fail, or 1 to 3 with a description per level) are steadier.
  • Pairwise (which of A and B is better): closer to how people judge, and the natural question for a model swap. It carries position bias, so both orders must be run, and ranking n candidates costs on the order of n² comparisons.
  • Rubric or reference-guided (grade against a checklist or a reference answer): the most reliable mode wherever a reference exists, and the only one that says which criterion failed.

These are near-identical answers, the hardest case for a judge: with a real quality gap consistency is much higher (GPT-4 keeps its winner in 83.6% of the released MT-Bench leaderboard judgments, Judge bias lab). Default prompt throughout; Table 2 also shows a "rename" prompt, where Claude-v1's bias moves from position to the name "Assistant A".

Two more grader designs complete the picture. Juries: a panel of small judges from different model families, majority-voted, agreed with humans better than GPT-4 alone on three question-answering sets and cost over seven times less (Verga et al. 2024; measured on answer correctness with references and on Arena-Hard, not on every task). Checklists: RocketEval diagnoses why small judges fail (they cannot hold a full analysis of a long response in one pass) and stops asking them to: a frontier model writes each query's checklist of yes/no questions once (3 to 25 in the released checklists; the paper says 5 to 10), a small judge answers each question on its own, and the score is read from the probability of "Yes". Since no item is graded in sight of the others, position and anchoring bias have no path through. It ranks a field of models almost as well as GPT-4o and is much weaker on single verdicts (numbers under Choosing below).

Adjacent instruments, often confused with judges: reward models (a scalar score per response, trained on preferences for RLHF and best-of-n; fast, no rationale, and they inherit their data's length and format biases), fine-tuned judge models (Prometheus 2 and similar; good on the rubrics they were trained on, re-check them on yours), and agent-as-judge (the judge gets tools: runs the code, checks claims, replays a trajectory; more accurate on agentic work, at more cost and with a new failure surface, the judge's own tool use).

People

Humans are the reference everything else is calibrated against: domain experts for correctness, crowds for preference (Chatbot Arena's pairwise votes). They are slow and expensive, and they disagree with each other, which sets the ceiling for every automatic grader (next section).

Calibration: checking the instrument against people

A judge's number means nothing until it has been compared with human labels on your task. Which judge to use is therefore an empirical, per-task decision, made on a human-labelled gold slice, never a default.

The ceiling is human agreement, not 100%

People disagree with each other on open-ended answers, so no automatic grader can be expected to agree with "the human" more than humans agree among themselves. MT-Bench measured it directly: expert votes on pairs of answers, compared expert against expert and GPT-4 against experts.

On non-tie votes, GPT-4 agreed with the experts as often as the experts agreed with each other. So a judge that agrees 80% with your expert may already be at the ceiling rather than underperforming, and a judge claiming 95% on open-ended work should make you suspicious of the labels. The ceiling holds for experts on closed questions too: in the physics re-grading audit, two PhD reviewers agreed on 140 of the 196 cases they both reviewed (71.4%), and a third reviewer settled the other 56 (Re-grading six physics benchmarks).

Report kappa, not raw agreement

Raw agreement flatters a judge whenever one label dominates. Cohen's kappa subtracts the agreement two graders would reach by chance, given how often each says pass: κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe), where po is observed agreement and pe the chance agreement from the two pass rates. Two judges on the same 200-item gold slice, where the expert passes 180:

Illustrative counts; the formula is Cohen's. A judge with no information at all reaches 90% raw agreement here.

This is not a corner case. Norman et al. (2026), studying 21 judges on about 541,000 judgments, found kappa 33 to 41 points below raw agreement on MT-Bench for every judge (Reliability without Validity, arXiv:2606.19544). The ceiling itself moves with the test: LLMBar's curated pairs reach 94% human agreement, while DevAI's three experts disagreed on 10 to 30% of requirements (both in the Judge atlas).

The gold slice, and correcting the aggregate

The working recipe: a few hundred items from real traffic, labelled pass or fail by one domain expert who also writes a one-line critique of each; iterate the judge prompt until judge-expert kappa stops improving; pin the judge model and prompt version, since any change is a new instrument; re-check on fresh samples when traffic shifts (Hamel Husain's Critique Shadowing). The same slice then corrects the headline number: a judge with imperfect sensitivity (TPR) and specificity (1 − FPR) reports a biased pass rate, and the plug-in correction is true rate ≈ (judge rate − FPR) / (TPR − FPR), with an interval that carries the uncertainty of both the test set and the gold slice (How to Correctly Report LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluations, arXiv:2511.21140).

Self-consistency certifies nothing

A judge that gives the same verdict every time it is asked is reliable, not valid. The 2026 study above found test-retest reliability above 0.95 together with position bias above 0.10 in two production-deployed judges: perfectly repeatable, and systematically wrong. Only agreement with people, chance-corrected, measures validity.

Meta-evaluations help choose a judge before your own calibration: JudgeBench builds hard pairs whose right answer is objectively checkable (random guessing scores 50%). With the Arena-Hard judge prompt, GPT-4o scores 56.6% and o3-mini (high) 80.9% (Table 2, v2, April 2025). A global ranking is still only a prior: a small judge that agrees with your expert on your task beats a famous one that does not.

Statistics in brief: how noisy is a reading?

An eval score is a mean over a sample of items, so it has a standard error like any other measurement. Most reported "regressions" are smaller than it.

Take a golden set of 200 pass/fail items with the champion passing 70%, and a challenger scoring 68%. The standard error of one score is √(0.7 × 0.3 / 200) = 3.2 points. Comparing two independent scores, the error of the difference is √2 times that, 4.6 points, so the 95% interval on the 2-point drop runs from about −11 to +7. The drop is noise.

Derived: standard error √(p(1 − p)/n) at p = 0.70, n = 200; difference of two independent scores √2 times that; paired difference √((d − Δ²)/n) for a fraction d of items where the two models disagree, here d = 10% (an illustrative assumption: measure yours). 95% intervals are ±1.96 standard errors. Items needed to detect 2 points at 95% confidence with 80% power: (1.96 + 0.84)² × variance / 0.02².

Three habits buy most of the sensitivity back without buying more labels:

  • Pair the comparison. Score both models on the same items and test the per-item differences (McNemar's test for pass/fail). Item difficulty is shared, so it cancels: only the items where the models disagree carry information. In the chart, pairing halves the interval; it still does not make 2 points visible on 200 items.
  • Resample when decoding is random. The same model scores differently run to run; a few samples per item (K = 3 to 5) separate item noise from sampling noise.
  • Count your tests. Items built from one template or document are not independent (use clustered standard errors), and gating 20 slices at p < 0.05 trips one by chance about two times in three (1 − 0.95²⁰ = 64%).

The rule that follows: state each gate's minimum detectable effect next to its threshold. On these assumptions a 200-item gate detects a drop of about 6 points paired (13 unpaired), not 2, and that is fine as long as nobody reads it as more.

How it breaks

When a score drops, the instinct is to blame the model. The evidence says check the instrument first: the grader and the gold labels fail more often than people expect, and an observed delta has to be attributed to the model, the grader or the labels before anyone acts on it.

The cleanest published measurement of this is an expert audit of physics benchmarks (Re-grading six physics benchmarks, arXiv:2609.13009). GPT-5.6-Sol answered questions from four benchmarks; every answer the benchmark's grader rejected on every attempt went to physics PhD reviewers, who decided whose fault the rejection was. Counts exact (Appendix C, as tabulated on the paper page); the order of squares is illustrative. Only rejections were audited, for one model: answers the graders accepted were not checked for false acceptance, and the corrected scores come from smaller, filtered question sets.

Benchmark errors dominated HLE-Physics (86 of 98) and UGPhysics (18 of 22); grader errors dominated PHYBench (40 of 56) and PRISM-Physics (48 of 74), mostly equivalent expressions and conventions the graders did not recognise. The same three-way split applies to any internal golden set and judge.

Judge biases

Measured on MT-Bench (Zheng et al. 2023): position (above: on pairs of near-identical answers GPT-4 kept its verdict on 65.0% of swapped pairs, GPT-3.5 46.2%, Claude-v1 23.8%; that is the hardest case, and consistency rises with the quality gap between the answers (Shi et al., covered in the bias lab): on the released MT-Bench leaderboard judgments GPT-4 gives the same winner in both orders 83.6% of the time, recounted in the Judge bias lab); verbosity (padding 23 answers with a rephrased copy of their own list fooled Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 91.3% of the time and GPT-4 8.7%, Table 3); self-enhancement (GPT-4 appeared to give its own answers a 10% higher win rate than humans did and Claude-v1 25%, but the authors write that "due to limited data and small differences, our study cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias"; the bias lab measures GPT-4 +13.0 points and Claude-v1 +10.5 on the same questions, and Panickssery et al. 2024 is the controlled study: models that recognise their own text prefer it more). Confident tone and citations, even invented ones, also raise scores. Sizes vary a lot by judge and protocol: a 2026 study of 21 judges found verbosity bias small under one pairwise rubric while two production judges still showed position bias above 0.10 (arXiv:2606.19544), so measure your own judge rather than trusting a catalogue.

Judge exploitation and null models

A judge reads the candidate's text, so the candidate can write to the judge. A "null model" that returns the same crafted response to every instruction reached an 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto and 9.55 on MT-Bench (Zheng et al. 2024, Cheating Automatic LLM Benchmarks). Any pipeline that pastes candidate text into a judge prompt is prompt-injectable: delimit the candidate as data, and run null-model and injection canaries in every judge battery. Training against a judge or reward model does the same thing at scale; that is reward hacking, the training-time mirror of this failure (Reward Hacking).

Truncation and plumbing

The quietest failures are in the pipes. A completion cut off by a token limit, a logging layer or a context overflow reaches the judge as a stump, and the judge grades it as a bad answer rather than saying it looks cut off. An answer-extraction regex that misses a new output format scores correct answers as wrong, the classic source of "model X dropped 20 points overnight". Check that generation stopped normally (finish reason "stop") before grading, give the judge an explicit "complete or truncated" criterion, and route those failures to an infrastructure bucket, not a quality one. The fix is reading transcripts, which is why a harness's log viewer matters, and why the 2026 double-blind enclave evaluations, where nobody can read the transcripts, will need a separate debugging channel.

Contamination

If the test items were in the training data, the score measures memory. Defences range from private held-out sets to dated live sets to canary strings; the newest is evaluation inside a hardware enclave where the lab never sees the prompts and the evaluator never sees the weights (Google DeepMind's pilot, August 2026). Gold-label noise

Reference answers are written by people and are wrong at rates that swamp small deltas. MMLU-Redux re-annotated 5,700 MMLU questions and estimates that 6.49% of MMLU questions contain errors (57% of the analysed Virology questions) (Are We Done with MMLU?). Against wrong gold, a better model can fail a gate for disagreeing with the label. Treat gold as fallible: keep a provenance field per label, send model-gold disagreements to a review, and allow "ambiguous, exclude" as a label.

Agents: grade the action, not the sentence

In an agent the harm or the success lives in the tool calls and the state they change, so a grader that reads the final message measures the wrong variable. MOLE shows how far apart they can be: told that the sandbox was harmless, 28 of 39 models completed most of the harmful objectives assigned to them; refusing and completing were strongly linked across models (Spearman −0.73), and the best monitor (Claude Opus 4.7) caught 24 of 45 completed harms.

Correction to the old page
The old page said MOLE "found that a model's stated refusal did not predict whether it actually declined". That overstates the paper: refusal and completion rates are strongly (negatively) correlated across models. The point that survives is about what to grade: actions and state, not the response string.

Choosing

Every harness runs a dataset through a model and scores it. They differ in the unit of work, and the dividing question is which of two things you are asking:

"Is this checkpoint good?"
A model-level question: fixed public tasks, comparable across labs and over time. Use a model-level harness and report its version and settings with the number.
"Is this configuration safe to ship?"
An application question: your prompt, retrieval, tools and model together, on your traffic. Use golden sets, regression gates and shadow traffic. A model swap is this kind of question, even though the trigger is a model.

Single verdicts or aggregates?

The second choice is what the grader's output will be used for. A grader that ranks models well can still be unreliable on any one response, and the cheapest graders are built that way. RocketEval's small checklist judge shows the shape (RocketEval paper page): ranking 12 models on WildBench, Gemma-2-2B reaches a Spearman correlation of 0.965 with Chatbot Arena against GPT-4o's 0.979 (one more of the 66 pairs out of order), at $27.7 against $3,400 for 1,000 runs at batch prices; on single MT-Bench verdicts it agrees with humans 57.9% of the time, against 66.6% for GPT-4o and 64.7% for humans with each other.

  • Aggregates (leaderboards, drift monitoring, a gate that trips on a distribution): a cheap grader is fine, and cheap enough to run on every merge rather than once a quarter.
  • Single items (adjudicating one case, failing one pull request on one example, generating preference labels for training): use a calibrated strong judge or a person.
  • Checklist grading still needs a frontier model to read every query once to write its checklist (never the responses), which weakens the privacy case for local judging.

Before trusting a number

  1. Which model, harness version, prompt and judge (all pinned) produced it?
  2. Has the grader been checked against people on this task, with kappa, and is it below the human ceiling or suspiciously above it?
  3. Is the change larger than the minimum detectable effect of this set, on a paired comparison?
  4. Have the failing items been read: truncated, mis-extracted, mislabelled, or genuinely wrong?
  5. For agents: was the action graded, or only the final message?

What the fixes can and cannot do
Swapping and averaging removes the position artefact; it turns a confident wrong verdict into an honest tie, which flags the item for a closer look. Length control removes the reward for length, not the reward for real content. A jury dilutes any one family's taste. None of them makes a judge right on a question it cannot solve: for a checkable answer, give the judge a verified reference or check the answer with a program, as the gold line in section 1 does.

    Further reading

    The Reading tab is a short overview on one spine: an eval is a measuring instrument, with a thing it measures, a noise level, ways it breaks, and a calibration against people. The tabs on this page hold the data; the pages under this one own the depth. Links open in a new tab, with reading times ("+" adds the resources a page links).

    On this page

    Judge bias lab

    Judge biases from released data only: one real Arena-Hard pair (a confidence interval for the sum of 100 dice) graded by five judges in both orders; a real AlpacaEval padding pair (805 instructions, 368 verdicts flip); measured rates study by study (position, length, own family); and mitigations before and after (few-shot, swap with evidence, reference answer, length control, a PoLL jury lifting kappa from 0.627 to 0.763).

    Same model, many harnesses

    One MMLU item read three ways (letter loglikelihood; chat template, generate and extract with a strict or lenient parser; the January 2023 lm-eval answer-text acc against acc_norm) on real Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct outputs computed offline; ten settings on the same 200 items with paired one-knob changes; published cases with every knob logged (LLaMA-65B MMLU: paper 63.4, original code 63.6, HELM 63.7, lm-eval e47e01b 48.8; Llama 3 8B agreement; Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, Meta against HELM; Qwen2.5 MATH Level 5 re-scored with Math-Verify, 1.2 to 59.8 for 72B; Inspect Evals MMLU); and a table of the knobs to log.

    Judge atlas

    A sortable grid of 26 rows: 13 judges and judging methods (single frontier judge, reasoning judge, PoLL and RoPoLL juries, Prometheus 2, JudgeLM, PandaLM, Auto-J, RocketEval checklists, reward models, generative verifiers, agent-as-a-judge, guard models as a pointer) and 13 tests of judges (MT-Bench human judgments, LLMBar, RewardBench and RewardBench 2, JudgeBench, EvalBiasBench, RM-Bench, five 2026 successors, Norman et al. 2026); 172 dated readings with the metric named and independent against self-reported flagged; compare 2 or 3 side by side; a one-metric chart (by default JudgeBench accuracy by date).

Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges

How to measure an LLM system you are building: an eval is a measuring instrument, and an instrument has to be calibrated before its readings mean anything. A map of the instruments and the seven pages that own them. Reading: 17 min, plus 1 in closed details. Data as of October 2026.
The idea1. Layers2. Who grades3. Calibration4. Noise5. How it breaks6. Choosing7. The pages

An eval is a measuring instrument: every score is the model, the grader and the gold labels read together on a sample of items, so a change in score means something only once you know which of the three moved it and whether it is larger than the sampling noise.

This topic is about how evaluation is done and where it breaks. What is measured, and how to read a published score, belongs to Topic: benchmarks; here the question is the one you face when you build an LLM system: is this change better, and how sure am I? The root is a map: it compares the instruments and defines what the comparisons need; each mechanism is taught once, on the child that owns it.

You need: precision, recall and the ranked-list metrics from Evaluation metrics; everything else is defined here or on the children.

The figure is the map of the page: each later section takes one of its parts, and each part has a child page that owns its mechanism. Fig. 1 in section 1 maps the instruments.

The harness decides how an item becomes a number, statistics how noisy that number is, and the decision is where it is used. The child pages are listed as cards in section 7.

New terms

Golden set
a frozen, versioned set of items with expected outcomes, run on every change
Gold labels
the reference answers, rubric verdicts or human labels a grader is compared with
Grader
whatever turns an output into a verdict: a program, an LLM judge, or a person
Pointwise, pairwise, reference-guided
a judge scores one answer alone, picks the better of two, or grades against a reference or checklist
Gold slice
a random subset of the evaluated items that people also label, to calibrate the grader
Cohen's kappa
agreement between two graders minus the agreement chance alone would give
Harness
the software that turns a benchmark into prompts, runs the model and extracts and scores the answers
Paired comparison
both systems scored on the same items, testing the per-item differences
Minimum detectable effect (MDE)
the smallest change a set of a given size can tell from noise at a chosen confidence and power
pass^k
the chance an agent succeeds on all k independent tries of the same task
Faithfulness
whether every claim in a generated answer is supported by the passages it was given

1. The five layers, cheapest to most faithful

Mature stacks run all five. Each one catches something the previous one cannot, at a higher price per judgment, and each has a page that owns it. Fig. 1 sends one change up the stack (the order is the page's; heights and sizes are a layout, not measured costs).

Fig. 1, one change through the five layers

  1. One change, five instruments. A new prompt, model or retrieval setting produces outputs (the box on the floor); they climb the five layers in order, from the cheapest check to the one closest to real use. The dots are the outputs flowing up; each later step stops on one layer.
  2. Static benchmarks. Fixed questions, scored by a program: exact match, a regex on the answer, unit tests, or the probability the model gives each option. Cost: cents per item, deterministic. Catches: capability changes on closed-form tasks, reproducibly. How a benchmark becomes a score: Eval harnesses.
  3. LLM-as-judge. A model grades open-ended outputs: pointwise, pairwise, or against a rubric or reference. Cost: one model call per item. Catches what a regex cannot: quality, helpfulness, style, partial credit. A biased proxy for humans: LLM-as-judge.
  4. Human evaluation. Experts against a gold standard, or crowds voting on pairs (Arena-style preference). Cost: minutes and dollars per item. Catches what the judge is blind to; it is the reference the judge is calibrated against (the stream back down to the judge), and it is noisy itself: Human evaluation and annotation.
  5. Regression gates. Frozen golden sets, 100 to 500 items per product surface, run in CI on every prompt, model or retrieval change. Cost: minutes per change. Catches regressions on your product before release, which public benchmarks never see: Production eval engineering, sections 2 to 4 (its Fig. 3 runs the gates in order on one real model swap).
  6. Online measurement. Shadow deployment on mirrored traffic, then canary and A/B tests with live metrics. Cost: real traffic and real risk. Catches what no curated set imagined: real traffic is uglier than any golden set. Production eval engineering, sections 7 to 9.
  7. All five, in order. Each layer passes on what it cannot judge; the price per judgment and the faithfulness to real use rise together up the stack. Which grader sits inside a layer is section 2; checking that grader against people is section 3.

What the system is changes what you grade. A single model call is graded on its text. An agent is graded on the state its tool calls changed and on how reliably it does so across runs (pass^k): Agent and trajectory evaluation. A RAG system has two halves that fail separately, retrieval (did the right passages arrive?) and generation (is the answer faithful to them?): RAG and retrieval evaluation.

Runtime guardrails (classifiers in the request path, before, during and after the model call) are evals too, measured with the same machinery. Their page moved to Topic: agentic-harnesses on 24 October 2026: Guardrails.

The one distinction that shows only across the children: the scoring method is part of the instrument. In June 2023 Hugging Face scored the same LLaMA-65B checkpoint on the same MMLU questions three ways: 0.636 with the original implementation (probability of the letters A to D), 0.637 with HELM's (the model generates a letter) and 0.488 with the evaluation harness of the time (probability of the full answer text) (Hugging Face, What's going on with the Open LLM Leaderboard?). A score from one harness cannot be set beside a score from another, whatever both call the benchmark. On a small chat model the chat template, the answer parser and one instruction line move the score far more than log-likelihood against generation does; the measured spread for one model across ten settings is in Eval harnesses, section 3.

3. Calibration: checking the grader against people

A grader's number means nothing until it has been compared with human labels on your task. Which judge to use is therefore an empirical, per-task decision made on a human-labelled gold slice, never a default. Three definitions carry the rest of the topic:

  • The ceiling is human agreement, not 100%. People disagree on open-ended answers. On MT-Bench two experts agreed on 81% of non-tie votes and 63% with ties counted, and GPT-4 agreed with the experts 85% and 66% (Zheng et al. 2023, Table 5). So a judge that agrees 80% with your expert may already be at the ceiling, and one claiming 95% on open-ended work should make you suspect the labels. The ceiling moves with the test: LLMBar's curated pairs reach 94% human agreement, while DevAI's three experts disagreed on 10 to 30% of requirements (both in the Judge atlas).
  • Report kappa, not raw agreement. κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe), with po the observed agreement and pe the agreement expected from the two pass rates alone. A judge that always says pass, on a slice where the expert passes 90%, agrees 90% of the time and has kappa 0. The worked matrix on real MT-Bench votes is on LLM-as-judge, Calibration; agreement between people (kappa, alpha, AC1) on Human evaluation and annotation.
  • Reliable is not valid. A judge that gives the same verdict every time is repeatable, not right: a 2026 study of 21 judges found test-retest reliability above 0.95 alongside position bias above 0.10 in two production-deployed judges (Reliability without Validity, arXiv:2606.19544). Only chance-corrected agreement with people measures validity.

The same gold slice then corrects the headline number: a judge with sensitivity TPR and false-positive rate FPR reports a biased pass rate, and the plug-in correction is true rate ≈ (judge rate − FPR) / (TPR − FPR), with an interval that carries the uncertainty of both the test set and the slice (How to Correctly Report LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluations, arXiv:2511.21140). Stepped on real data in LLM-as-judge, Fig. 3 (a judge's win rate corrected with a gold slice of 50, 100 or 200 votes); chance-corrected agreement between people is drawn on released votes in Human evaluation and annotation, Fig. 1. Meta-evaluations (JudgeBench, RewardBench 2, LLMBar) shortlist judges before your own calibration; every judge and test of judges, dated and sourced, is in this page's Judge atlas.

4. Statistics in brief: how noisy is a reading?

An eval score is a mean over a sample of items, so it has a standard error like any other measurement, and most reported "regressions" are smaller than it. Take a golden set of 200 pass/fail items, a champion passing 70% and a challenger scoring 68%. The standard error of one score is √(0.7 × 0.3 / 200) = 3.2 points; the difference of two independent scores has √2 times that, 4.6 points, so the 95% interval on the 2-point drop runs from about −11 to +7. The drop is noise.

Pairing halves the interval, and still cannot see 2 points on 200 items. Champion 70%, challenger 68%, 200 pass/fail items.

Derived: standard error √(p(1 − p)/n) at p = 0.70, n = 200; difference of two independent scores √2 times that; paired difference √((d − Δ²)/n) for a fraction d of items where the two models disagree, here d = 10% (an illustrative assumption: measure yours). 95% intervals are ±1.96 standard errors. Items needed to detect 2 points at 95% confidence with 80% power: (1.96 + 0.84)² × variance / 0.02².

Three habits buy most of the sensitivity back without buying more labels: pair the comparison (score both systems on the same items and test the per-item differences, McNemar's test for pass/fail: only the items where they disagree carry information); resample when decoding is random (K = 3 to 5 samples per item separate item noise from sampling noise); and count your tests (items built from one template are not independent, so use clustered standard errors; gating 20 slices at p < 0.05 trips one by chance about two times in three, 1 − 0.95²⁰ = 64%). The rule that follows: state each gate's minimum detectable effect next to its threshold. On these assumptions a 200-item gate detects a drop of about 6 points paired (13 unpaired), not 2, and that is fine as long as nobody reads it as more.

5. How it breaks: attribute a change before acting on it

When a score drops, the instinct is to blame the model. The evidence says check the instrument first: the grader and the gold labels fail more often than people expect. The cleanest published measurement is an expert audit of physics benchmarks (Re-grading six physics benchmarks): GPT-5.6-Sol answered questions from four benchmarks, and every answer the benchmark's grader rejected on every attempt went to physics PhD reviewers, who decided whose fault each rejection was. Fig. 2 steps through it: first as a leaderboard reads it, then attributed.

Fig. 2, the rejections attributed, step by step

  1. As a leaderboard reads it. 250 answers by GPT-5.6-Sol on the four benchmarks were rejected by the benchmark's grader on every attempt. A score counts each one as a model failure.
  2. Experts review every rejection. Physics PhD students read the problem, the reference answer, the model's response and a preliminary AI review, and decide whose fault the rejection is. 196 cases got two reviewers, who agreed on 140; a third settled the 56 disagreements.
  3. Grader errors leave the model's column. 95 rejections (38.0%) were right answers to well-posed problems that the grader marked wrong, mostly by missing an equivalent form or a different convention.
  4. Benchmark errors leave too. 143 rejections (57.2%) were the benchmark's fault: a wrong reference, inconsistent conditions, ambiguity, or a missing assumption the intended answer needs.
  5. What is left is the model. 12 of 250 (4.8%) were genuine model errors. Before attribution the model looked 250 answers wrong; after it, 12.

6. Choosing: which instrument for which question

Every harness runs a dataset through a model and scores it; the dividing question is which of two things you are asking.

"Is this checkpoint good?"
A model-level question: fixed public tasks, comparable across labs and over time. Use a model-level harness and report its version and settings with the number.
"Is this configuration safe to ship?"
An application question: your prompt, retrieval, tools and model together, on your traffic. Use golden sets, regression gates and shadow traffic. A model swap is this kind of question, even though the trigger is a model.
The questionInstrumentPage
What does this checkpoint score on public tasks?a model-level harness (lm-evaluation-harness, Inspect, lighteval), every setting pinned and reportedEval harnesses, section 7
Did my change break my product?a golden set from real traffic, a CI gate with a stated MDE, then shadow and canaryProduction eval engineering
Is B better than A on open-ended answers?a pairwise judge in both orders, calibrated on a gold sliceLLM-as-judge
Is the difference real?a paired test with an intervalEval statistics
Are my labels right?agreement beyond chance, then an audit of the gold setHuman evaluation and annotation
Did the agent do the task, every time?state checks against a do-nothing baseline, pass^k over repeated trialsAgent and trajectory evaluation
Is the answer grounded in what was retrieved?retrieval metrics and faithfulness, scored separatelyRAG and retrieval evaluation

Single verdicts or aggregates? The second choice is what the grader's output will be used for. A grader that ranks models well can still be unreliable on any one response, and the cheapest graders are built that way: RocketEval's small checklist judge ranks a field of models almost as well as GPT-4o for under 1% of its cost ($27.7 against $3,400 for 1,000 runs) while agreeing with people on single verdicts much less often (numbers on LLM-as-judge, List against instance and the RocketEval paper page).

  • Aggregates (leaderboards, drift monitoring, a gate that trips on a distribution): a cheap grader is fine, and cheap enough to run on every merge rather than once a quarter.
  • Single items (adjudicating one case, failing one pull request on one example, generating preference labels for training): use a calibrated strong judge or a person.

Before trusting a number

  1. Which model, harness version, prompt and judge (all pinned) produced it? (Eval harnesses)
  2. Has the grader been checked against people on this task, with kappa, and is it below the human ceiling or suspiciously above it? (LLM-as-judge)
  3. Is the change larger than the minimum detectable effect of this set, on a paired comparison? (Eval statistics)
  4. Have the failing items been read: truncated, mis-extracted, mislabelled, or genuinely wrong? (Production eval engineering, error analysis)
  5. For agents: was the action graded, or only the final message? (Agent and trajectory evaluation)

The paper that named the pattern: position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias, reference-guided grading, agreement with experts. Source of the human-ceiling figures in Reading section 3.

The best practitioner guide: Critique Shadowing, building a judge from one expert's pass/fail critiques.

The standard survey of judge designs and mitigations.

21 judges, about 541,000 judgments: kappa deflation, ranking shifts across meta-benchmarks, and repeatable judges with position bias.

Eval statistics: error bars, paired tests, how many items you need

An eval score is a mean over a sample of items, so it is an estimate with an error bar. This page is the statistics of reading one: where the noise comes from, how to compare two models on the same items, and how many items a decision needs. Child of Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges. Sources linked inline; data as of October 2026.

In one screen

An eval runs a model on n items and averages the scores. The items are a sample of all the items you could have written, and with sampled decoding or an LLM judge each item's score is itself a draw. So the average is an estimate, and every decision taken from it (ship, roll back, claim a gain) is a statistical test whether or not anyone runs one.

1. A score is an estimate: standard errors and error bars

Evan Miller's "Adding Error Bars to Evals" (Anthropic, November 2024) sets up the frame this page uses: treat the eval's questions as drawn at random from an unseen "super-population" of questions, so the quantity of interest is the mean over that population, not over the questions you happened to write. Then the Central Limit Theorem gives the standard error of the observed mean s̄ over n items:

SE = √( Var(s) / n )    and for 0/1 scores    SE = √( p(1 − p) / n )
Var(s)
sample variance of the item scores, (1/(n − 1))·Σ(si − s̄)² (Miller Eq. 1)
p
the observed pass rate (Eq. 2); a 95% interval is s̄ ± 1.96·SE (Eq. 3)

What that means in practice derived: at 70% the standard error is 4.6 points on 100 items, 3.2 on 200, 1.4 on 1,000. A "2-point regression" on a 100-item set is well inside the noise. Halving the error bar takes four times the items.

  • Bootstrap or formula? For a plain mean the two agree; Miller regards bootstrapping as unnecessary "unless a complicated sampling scheme or estimator is being used" (an F1 over a set, a ratio, a clustered design). OpenAI's evals framework bootstraps; Inspect's stderr() uses the formula.
  • Fractional scores. The Bernoulli formula is only for 0/1 scores. Miller notes that the Llama 3 report applied it to F1-style scores too, which makes those intervals conservative (too wide), and applied it without clustering to reading-comprehension evals, which makes those too narrow.
  • Small sets. Below a few hundred items the CLT interval misbehaves: at 30 of 30 it has zero width, near 0 or 1 it leaves [0, 1], and its real coverage falls below the promised 95% (Bowyer et al., arXiv:2503.01747). Use Wilson or a Bayesian interval there; the Small-n intervals tab computes the coverage of each exactly and reproduces their 92.5% figure at n = 100 (92.2% computed exactly).

Miller's reporting suggestion is borrowed from other sciences: print the number of questions beside each eval and the standard error in parentheses under the score, and, for clustered evals, the number of clusters too (his Tables 2 and 3). For the RACE-H run on this page real data:

2. Where the variance comes from: items, samples, and the thermostat

Miller splits each item's score into a mean and a random part, si = xi + εi: xi is what the model would average on item i if asked forever, εi is this particular sample's luck. By the law of total variance the error of the eval mean has two parts:

Var(μ̂) = ( Var(x) + E[σi²] / K ) / n
Var(x)
how much items differ in difficulty for this model: a property of the item population, fixed once the eval is chosen
E[σi²]
the average within-item variance: how much one item's score moves between samples (for 0/1 scores, the mean of xi(1 − xi))
K
samples per item, averaged per item before averaging over items (Miller §3.1)

Only the second part can be bought down without new items. Miller's worked case, difficulty uniform on [0, 1], has Var(x) = 1/12 and E[σ²] = 1/6, so the variance with K samples is (1 + 2/K)/3 of the single-sample variance: K = 2 cuts it by a third, K = 4 by half, K = 6 by 5/9, and no K can cut more than 2/3. Two other routes:

  • Next-token probabilities remove ε altogether when the answer is a single token and there is no chain of thought: score item i by the probability of the right token, pi. In the uniform case that is the full 2/3 reduction. Anthropic's post said it knew of no open-source framework implementing it (November 2024).
  • "Don't touch the thermostat!" Lowering the temperature to 0 also removes ε, but by rounding each item to 0 or 1 it moves the variance into Var(x) and can shift the mean: in Miller's example a uniform x at T = 1 becomes a coin at T = 0 and the irreducible variance triples, 1/12 to 1/4; in his second example the mean moves from 2/3 to 3/4 and Var(x) grows from 1/27 to 3/16. Change the temperature only to study the model at that temperature.

The same decomposition on the RACE-H run real data: the chart shows, for each model, the standard error of its accuracy under four scorings of the same questions. The K curve is Miller's formula fed with the measured Var(x) and E[σ²]; the dots are what seeded sampling actually gives.

Noise the item standard error does not see. The formula above treats the model as fixed. Several other sources move a score just as much, and none of them shrinks with more items:

  • Training seed. Madaan et al. trained ten 7B models identical except for the initialisation seed (arXiv:2406.10229, Meta, 2024). Their Table 1 sets the spread across seeds beside the 95% item interval of one model; seed noise is "generally ... well below the 95% CI" (table below). Two models that differ only in a data choice are therefore compared at the seed scale, which needs more than the item interval to see.
  • Checkpoint to checkpoint. Heineman et al. ("Signal and Noise", arXiv:2508.13144, Ai2, 2025) see a 1.7-point range on ARC Challenge across the final 30 checkpoints of one 1B run, define noise as the relative standard deviation of the final checkpoints, and find that a benchmark's signal-to-noise ratio predicts whether small-scale rankings hold at scale (R = 0.791).
  • Decoding seeds and hardware. Hochlehnert et al. ("A Sober Look at Progress in Language Model Reasoning", arXiv:2504.07086) report pass@1 standard deviations of 5 to 15 points across 20 seeds on AIME'24 and AMC'23, and the same model at 7.3% ± 3.8 on one cluster and 11.3% ± 3.6 on another with identical containers.
  • Prompt, harness and grader. Not variance in the statistical sense: they change what is measured. The parent topic's Same model, many harnesses tab shows the spreads; a comparison is only paired if both sides used the same setup.

3. Clustered errors: when items are not independent draws

The CLT assumes each item was drawn independently. Many evals draw groups: several questions about one passage (DROP, QuAC, RACE, SQuAD), one question translated into many languages (MGSM), many variants of one template (GSM-Symbolic), tasks from a few repositories, turns of one conversation, chunks of one document. Items in a group rise and fall together, so n of them carry less information than n independent items. Miller's clustered standard error (Eq. 4, from the econometrics of Abadie et al.) adds the within-cluster cross terms:

SEclustered² = SECLT² + (1/n²) Σc Σi Σj≠i (si,c − s̄)(sj,c − s̄)
c
a cluster; the triple sum runs over pairs of different items in the same cluster. Equivalently SEclustered = √(Σc (Σi∈c(si,c − s̄))²) / n

It slides between the two extremes: if the items in a cluster are uncorrelated it equals the plain standard error; if they always agree, each cluster counts as one item. For planning, the textbook shortcut is the design effect 1 + (m − 1)ρ for clusters of m items with intra-cluster correlation ρ (variance multiplier; the calculator has it).

  • Cluster on how the items were drawn, not on any grouping you can find: the passage, the template, the conversation, the source document. If items were sampled one by one, do not cluster.
  • The opposite mistake: counting repeated runs of the same item as separate items. MathArena's "±" counts 4 runs of 30 AIME problems as 120 independent answers; its sibling page Math benchmarks ("Error bars on 30 problems") reproduces all 123 table rows and finds the problem-level interval a median 1.7 times wider. Miller states the rule: "computing a pooled standard error across all KN answers will be inconsistent".
  • Tools. Inspect's stderr(cluster="...") implements Eq. 4 with a finite-cluster factor C/(C − 1) that the paper does not have (std.py, read 4 October 2026); for 400 clusters it widens the standard error by about 0.1%. lm-evaluation-harness reports a closed-form standard error of the mean and bootstraps a few metrics (metrics.py); we found no clustering option there.
  • Small n and clusters together are hard: Bowyer et al. find that neither the plain nor the clustered CLT interval reaches its stated coverage with few items, and fit a Beta-Binomial hierarchical model instead (their Section 3.2, with code).

4. Paired comparisons: why scoring both models on the same items shrinks the interval

A model's score rarely matters alone; the question is whether B beats A. Treating the two scores as independent gives SEA−B = √(SEA² + SEB²). But when both models answered the same items, much of each score's error is the same error: the luck of which items were drawn. Taking the difference item by item cancels it (Miller §4.2):

SEpaired² = SEA² + SEB² − 2·r·SEA·SEB    =    Var(sA,i − sB,i) / n
r
the correlation between the two models' item scores: how much they agree on which items are hard
0/1 scores
Var of the difference is d − Δ², with d the share of items where exactly one model passes: only the discordant items carry information about the difference

With equal variances the paired variance is (1 − r) times the unpaired one, and the items needed shrink by 1/(1 − r). Miller's uniform example with r = 0.5 cuts the variance by a third (1/6 to 1/9); Anthropic reports correlations "between 0.3 and 0.7" between frontier models on popular evals, which is 1.4 to 3.3 times fewer items derived. On the parent topic's example (70% on 200 items, a 2-point drop, 10% of items discordant) the 95% interval goes from ±9.0 points to ±4.4.

Correction to the old Production page: "typical correlations cut required n by 3-10x"
No source was given and Miller does not state it unconfirmed. The factor is 1/(1 − r): 3 to 10 times needs r between 0.67 and 0.9. The only published range we found, Anthropic's 0.3 to 0.7 for frontier models, gives 1.4 to 3.3 times. On this page's real data the measured factor is on RACE-H (two small models, pass/fail) and on MT-Bench (claude-v1 against claude-instant-v1, judge grades); across all 561 MT-Bench model pairs the paired standard error is a median of the unpaired one. Pairing is the cheapest precision there is, but how much it buys has to be measured, not assumed.

Two points the animation makes on real numbers. Pairing does not need more labels: it is a choice of analysis, free once both models were run on the same items. And clustering and pairing are separate corrections; leaving out either one misstates the interval in its own direction, so report the paired, clustered one (Miller Eq. 8). A score compared with last month's dashboard number, or with a rival's number from a different harness, cannot be paired at all.

5. Which test: McNemar, paired t, bootstrap, permutation

A confidence interval that excludes zero and a two-sided test at 5% are the same decision. The tests differ in what they assume about the per-item differences:

McNemarpass/fail, two models, same itemsOnly discordant items count: with b items only A passes and c only B passes, under "no difference" b is Binomial(b + c, ½). Use the exact binomial version when b + c is small; χ² = (|b − c| − 1)²/(b + c) otherwise. The standard classifier comparison (Dietterich 1998; Dror et al. 2018).

Paired bootstrapany metric, including ones that are not means (F1 over the set, a ratio, a pass@k curve)Resample items with replacement, recompute the difference, read the 2.5% and 97.5% points; resample whole clusters when items cluster (Miller Appendix C's advice for subsampling). 6. Many comparisons and many-slice gates

Each test at α = 0.05 fires falsely one time in twenty when nothing changed. A gate that checks m slices independently fails by chance with probability 1 − (1 − α)m: 64% for 20 slices derived. The same holds for a leaderboard that compares every pair of models, a prompt search over many variants, or a report that bolds the best of many numbers.

Familywise rate assumes independent slices (correlated slices fire together, so it is an upper bound on the chance of at least one false alarm and a lower bound on nothing). Bonferroni tests each slice at α/m; Šidák at 1 − (1 − α)1/m. The cost: the items each slice needs grow by ((zα/2m + zβ)/(zα/2 + zβ))² at 80% power derived.

  • Familywise corrections keep the chance of any false alarm at α: Bonferroni (simple, conservative), Holm (step-down, never worse than Bonferroni), Šidák and Romano-Wolf (evalstats' defaults for simultaneous intervals and for p-values at n ≥ 30, Shaffer's procedure below 30).
  • False discovery rate (Benjamini-Hochberg) keeps the expected share of false alarms among the flagged slices at α: the right choice when slices are diagnostics that send a person to look, not automatic blocks.
  • Hierarchical gating, from the old Production page: gate on the aggregate, read slices as diagnostics, and gate a slice only when its own minimum detectable effect is one you care about. The aggregate hides slice regressions; the Production eval engineering page shows it on real data: on MT-Bench's released grades, 626 of the 1,122 ordered model swaps pass an aggregate gate at a 0.25-point tolerance, and 67 of those fail at least one slice under a per-slice gate (its Gate designer tab).
  • Escalate, never re-run until green. A borderline result goes to more samples or human review with K fixed in advance. Re-running a stochastic eval until it passes is a multiple comparison with an unknown m.

7. Power and the minimum detectable effect

A test can be wrong two ways: a false alarm (rate α) and a missed real change (rate β). Power, 1 − β, is the chance of detecting a change of a given size; 80% is the usual floor. Miller's §5 gives the number of independent items a paired comparison needs, and inverts it into the smallest effect an eval of fixed size can see:

n = (zα/2 + zβ)² · (ω² + σ²A/KA + σ²B/KB) / δ²     MDE = (zα/2 + zβ) · √( (ω² + σ²A/KA + σ²B/KB) / n )
ω²
variance of the per-item difference of the two models' true scores, Var(xA) + Var(xB) − 2Cov(xA, xB)
σ²/K
each model's within-item variance over its K samples
zα/2 + zβ
1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80 at α = 0.05 two-sided and 80% power

Miller's worked numbers, all reproduced by the Sample-size calculator: with ω² = 1/9 and no sampling noise, detecting 3 points takes about 969 questions, hence his advice that new evals contain at least 1,000; with σ² = 1/6 per model and 198 questions, going from K = 1 to K = 10 samples per question lowers the MDE from 13.2 to 7.5 points. The parent topic's 200-item gate can see a drop of about 6 points paired and 13 unpaired, not 2; that is acceptable as long as the gate states it. The rule the old Production page ended on still stands: write each gate's minimum detectable effect next to its threshold, and buy sensitivity with pairing and resampling before buying more labels.

Correction to the old Production page: "Detecting a 2-point drop near p=0.7 needs roughly n=3500 paired-free"
The page's own rule of thumb, MDE ≈ 2.8·√(2p(1 − p)/n), gives n = 2.8² × 2 × 0.21 / 0.02² = 8,232 items per configuration derived, and exact quantiles give 8,242 (the parent topic's figure). No variant reaches 3,500: one-sided (2.49) gives about 6,500, dropping the factor 2 about 4,100. At 3,500 items an unpaired comparison detects about 3.1 points, not 2.
Correction to the old Production page's worked example: "in practice a 5-point true regression is detected with high power at n=300"
The example: the champion passes 78% of 300 items, the challenger regresses 5 points, and the two disagree on about 15% of items. Then the expected split of the 45 discordant items is 30 against 15, McNemar's z is 15/√45 = 2.24, and power is Φ(2.24 − 1.96) = 61% by the normal approximation; the exact McNemar test gives . 80% power needs about 2.8² × 0.15 / 0.05² = 470 items (normal approximation), with the exact test derived. The rest of the example holds: the unpaired standard error of the difference is √(2 × 0.78 × 0.22 / 300) = 3.4 points, so a 5-point drop is 1.5 standard errors, and the unpaired design needs about 16 × 0.17 / 0.05² ≈ 1,100 items. Pairing still turns a hopeless gate into a borderline one at no labelling cost; it does not make 300 items enough. The "15% disagreement, typical for a same-tier swap" is unsourced unconfirmed: measure it on your own pair (the two RACE-H models here disagree on of questions).

Underpowered experiments also exaggerate. Card et al. ("With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility", EMNLP 2020) work the case of a 500-item test set, two models that agree on 90% of items and a true gain of 2 points: McNemar's power is about 0.25, and the experiments that do come out significant overstate the gain by a factor of about 1.9 on average (the Type-M error of Gelman and Carlin). 8. Repeated sampling and pass@k

With sampled decoding, run each item K times, average per item, then compute the standard error over items: that is Miller's §3.1, and Inspect does it when a task sets epochs. How many samples are worth it depends on the split between item variance and within-item variance: once E[σ²]/K is small next to Var(x), more samples barely help. The split differs by model and by benchmark, so it is worth measuring. On MathArena's released answers (every model answered every problem 4 times) real data:

  • pass@k (any of k samples right) is estimated without bias from n ≥ k samples with c correct as 1 − C(n − c, k)/C(n, k) per problem, averaged over problems (Chen et al. 2021, the Codex paper, Eq. 1); drawing exactly k samples and checking any is unbiased but noisier, and 1 − (1 − p̂)k from a pooled p̂ is biased. Its standard error is still over problems: k samples per problem do not add problems.
  • pass^k (all k right), the reliability measure of agent benchmarks, is owned by Agentic benchmarks; the same rule applies.
  • Seeds are samples too. Hochlehnert et al.'s 5 to 15 point spread on AIME'24 across seeds is this within-item variance seen from the outside; they recommend many seeds on small benchmarks and reporting the mean and its spread.
  • Bowyer et al. note that pass@k has no obvious CLT interval and sketch a hierarchical Bayesian model for it (their Appendix D).

9. Judge noise is a variance component

When an LLM or a person grades each answer, the grade adds its own term: si = xi + εmodel + εjudge + bjudge. The noise εjudge behaves exactly like sampling noise: it sits inside σ² in the power formula, and averaging J independent judgments per item divides it by J. The bias bjudge does not average away: a judge that prefers the first position or the longer answer moves every grade the same way, and more items or more judgments only make the wrong number more precise. Bias is the LLM-as-judge page's subject (and the parent topic's Judge bias lab); this section is about the noise.

How large is judge noise next to item variance? MT-Bench's released expert votes have several judges on the same question, turn and model pair, so the two can be separated with a one-way random-effects split real data (vote scored 1 for a win of the first model, ½ tie, 0 loss):

Across the 15 model pairs, disagreement between expert judges on the same item is a median of the variance of a single vote real data. For a preference eval graded once per item, most of the noise is the grader, not the item: a second independent judgment per item removes half of that part, which can be worth as much as many more items, and the power formula should carry it as σ²/J.

  • LLM judges are noisy too, even at temperature 0: serving nondeterminism, parse failures and prompt drift. A judgment that fails to parse is not a score: in LMSYS's released MT-Bench file, averaging one failed grade in as −1 is the most likely reason the leaderboard prints 7.85 for Claude-Instant-1 where the valid grades average 7.906 (worked through on Production eval engineering). Count failures as their own metric and drop the item from both sides.
  • Agreement is not reliability. Self-consistency of a judge (test-retest) can be high while it is systematically wrong; calibration against people is on the parent topic and on Human evaluation and annotation, which owns inter-annotator agreement.

10. Reporting standard

A review of 445 LLM benchmark papers by 29 reviewers found that 16.0% "used uncertainty estimates or statistical tests to compare the results" (Bean et al., "Measuring what Matters", arXiv:2511.04703, NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks). Their checklist asks for the sample size with a power justification and uncertainty for every primary score. Merged with Miller's recommendations, a result is reportable when it carries:

  1. the number of items, the number of clusters and what a cluster is;
  2. each score with its standard error in parentheses (clustered where items cluster), Wilson or Bayesian intervals below a few hundred items;
  3. for comparisons: the paired difference, its standard error and interval, and the correlation of item scores (Miller's Table 5);
  4. samples per item K, temperature, seeds, and how pass@k or the mean over samples was computed;
  5. the test used and how many comparisons were made, with the correction;
  6. the judge (model, prompt version, J judgments per item) and its parse-failure count;
  7. the minimum detectable effect, or the power for the effect claimed;
  8. the harness and its version, so the comparison can be paired by someone else.

Common mistakes

Reading a point difference without its interval
On 200 items at 70% a single score is ±6.4 points. State n and the standard error with every number, and the interval with every difference.
Comparing unpaired scores when the items were shared
Both models ran on the same items, so test the per-item differences. On real data this cut the standard error to of the unpaired one.
Counting samples or turns as items
Four runs of 30 problems are 30 items with 4 samples each, not 120 items; two turns of one conversation are one cluster. Average per item first, cluster on how items were drawn.
Lowering the temperature to reduce noise
It moves the noise into the item variance and can change the mean (on RACE-H the greedy and the sampled accuracy of the same model differ by ). Use K samples or next-token probabilities.
Using the CLT interval on 30 items
It collapses to zero width at 30 of 30 and covers less than it promises. Use Wilson or Bayes (Small-n intervals).
Gating every slice at p < 0.05
Twenty slices fail by chance two times in three. Correct the level, use an FDR for diagnostics, or gate the aggregate.
Re-running until green
Each re-run is another test. Fix K before looking and escalate borderline results to more samples or review.
Sizing an eval after running it
Power is decided before the run: choose the effect that matters, estimate the variance from earlier data, compute n. After the run, report the MDE so nobody reads more into the result than it can hold.
Averaging judge failures in as scores
An unparsable verdict is missing data. Drop the item from both sides and count failures.
Treating judge bias as noise
More items and more judgments shrink noise, never bias. A position-biased judge gives a precise wrong answer.

Bootstrap: 2,000 resamples with replacement of items, or of whole clusters, percentile interval (seeded, so the page always shows the same numbers). Permutation: 2,000 random sign flips of the per-item differences (or of per-cluster sums), two-sided p with the +1 correction. McNemar exact: two-sided binomial test on the discordant items; χ² with continuity correction beside it. Normal-approximation p for the paired and clustered rows. Reading the lab
Pairing helps exactly as much as the two models agree on which items are hard (the correlation r in the table): Miller's identity is SEpaired² = SEA² + SEB² − 2·r·SEA·SEB. Clustering hurts as much as items in one cluster move together. The two effects are independent, so a comparison can be both sharper (paired) and wider (clustered) than the naive interval. Scoring by probability removes the sampling noise that a sampled answer adds; scoring by the most likely letter removes it too, but changes what is measured (Reading, "Where the variance comes from").

Sample-size calculator: how many items a decision needs

Pick the kind of score, then set what you know. Every output is computed from the formula printed under the controls; the presets load published worked examples and say how close the calculator lands (the residual), without adjusting any input to hide a gap. Statistics behind it: the Reading tab's power section.

paired: n = (zα/2 + zβ)² · (d − Δ²) / Δ²
unpaired: n = (zα/2 + zβ)² · 2p(1 − p) / Δ²
d
share of items where exactly one model passes (the discordant items; McNemar uses only these)
Δ
the true difference in pass rate; p the baseline pass rate (both models' variance taken at the baseline, as the parent topic does)
z
normal quantiles: z0.025 = 1.96, z0.20 = 0.84; with s slices, α becomes α/s

The paired line is the normal approximation to McNemar's test (Miller's Eq. 9 with ω² = d − Δ² for 0/1 scores). The "exact" rows run McNemar's exact binomial test itself: the probability of rejecting is summed over every possible number of discordant items and every split of them, so they also give the Type-M (exaggeration) factor: how much a difference that does reach significance overstates the true one, on average (Card et al. 2020, Appendix B).

n = (zα/2 + zβ)² · (ω² + σ²A/KA + σ²B/KB) · DEFF / δ²
ω²
Var(xA) + Var(xB) − 2 Cov(xA, xB): how much the two models' true per-item scores differ from item to item (Miller 2024, Eq. 9)
σ²
mean within-item variance: how much one model's score on the same item moves from sample to sample (for 0/1 scores, the mean of x(1 − x)); K samples divide it by K
DEFF
design effect 1 + (m − 1)ρ for clusters of m items with intra-cluster correlation ρ textbook approximation: Miller's Appendix C instead plugs clustered versions of ω² and σ² into the same formula, which needs earlier data from the same eval
α, power
0.05 two-sided and 80% here; MDE inverts the formula: δ = (zα/2 + zβ)·√((ω² + σ²/K)·DEFF / n)

Small-n intervals: does a 95% interval cover 95% of the time?

A 95% confidence interval is a promise about a procedure: run the eval again on a fresh draw of n items, rebuild the interval, and 95% of those intervals contain the true pass rate. That promise can be checked exactly for pass/fail scores, without simulation: for a true rate θ, every possible count k of n has a binomial probability, and the coverage is the total probability of the counts whose interval contains θ. This tab does that sum for four intervals.

  • CLT (Wald): p̂ ± z·√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n), the formula behind most published error bars (Miller 2024, Eq. 2 and 3).
  • Wilson score: inverts the score test; never leaves [0, 1] and never collapses to zero width.
  • Clopper-Pearson: the "exact" interval from binomial tail probabilities; guaranteed coverage, so it is wide.
  • Bayes, uniform prior: the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of Beta(1 + k, 1 + n − k).
Try: set n to 15 (one AIME paper) and watch the CLT curve fall far below 95% near 0 and 1; then n = 500. Bowyer, Aitchison and Ivanova's position paper (arXiv:2503.01747, ICML 2025) argues from exactly this picture: "Don't use the CLT in LLM evals with fewer than a few hundred datapoints".

Your own count

At k = n the CLT interval is [100%, 100%]: it claims certainty from 30 problems. Wilson, Clopper-Pearson and Bayes still leave room below. For two models on the same items use the paired methods in the Interval lab; the parent topic's sibling Topic: benchmarks, "Same model, many numbers" tab has a calculator with benchmark-size presets and Fisher's exact test for unpaired scores.

What this tab does not show
Coverage here assumes independent items with one fixed pass rate each. Items that share a passage (clustered) or a model sampled several times per item break that assumption in other ways: Bowyer et al. find that neither the CLT nor a clustered CLT interval reaches nominal coverage at small n in their clustered simulation, and recommend a Bayesian hierarchical model there (their Section 3.2). The Reading tab's clustered errors section covers the large-n case.

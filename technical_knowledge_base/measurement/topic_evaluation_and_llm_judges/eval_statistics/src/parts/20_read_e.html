8. Repeated sampling and pass@k

With sampled decoding, run each item K times, average per item, then compute the standard error over items: Miller's §3.1, and what Inspect does when a task sets epochs. Whether more samples are worth it depends on the split between item variance and within-item variance: once E[σ²]/K is small next to Var(x), more samples barely help. The split differs by model and benchmark, so measure it. On MathArena's released answers (every model answered every problem 4 times) real data:

Run-to-run luck against problem difficulty, one model at a time

  • pass@k (any of k samples right): estimate it without bias from n ≥ k samples with c correct as 1 − C(n − c, k)/C(n, k) per problem, averaged over problems ({{Chen et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374}}, Eq. 1); exactly k samples is unbiased but noisier, 1 − (1 − p̂)k from a pooled p̂ is biased. Its standard error is still over problems: samples do not add problems.
  • pass^k (all k right), the reliability measure of agent benchmarks, is owned by {{Agentic benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb926d7b91ef8234e}}; the same rule applies.
  • Seeds are samples too. Hochlehnert et al.'s 5 to 15 point spread across seeds is this within-item variance seen from outside; they recommend many seeds on small benchmarks, reporting the mean and its spread.
An interval for pass@k
Bowyer et al. note that pass@k has no obvious CLT interval and sketch a hierarchical Bayesian model for it (their Appendix D). A paired bootstrap over problems, recomputing the unbiased estimator in each resample, is the practical default (section 5).

9. Judge noise is a variance component

On MT-Bench's released expert votes, disagreement between expert judges on the same item is a median 62% of the variance of a single vote across 15 model pairs real data. For a preference eval graded once per item, most of the noise is the grader, not the item. The split per pair (several judges voted on the same question, turn and model pair, so a one-way random-effects split separates them; vote scored 1 for a win of the first model, ½ tie, 0 loss):

How much of a vote's variance is the judge

A grade adds its own terms: si = xi + εmodel + εjudge + bjudge. The noise εjudge behaves like sampling noise: it sits inside σ² in the power formula, and J independent judgments per item divide it by J, so a second judgment can be worth as much as many more items. The bias bjudge (first position, longer answer) moves every grade the same way and never averages away: a position-biased judge gives a precise wrong answer. Bias is {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}'s subject (and its {{Judge bias lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b#t-bias}} tab).

  • LLM judges are noisy too, even at temperature 0 (serving nondeterminism, parse failures, prompt drift). A grade that fails to parse is missing data, not a score: averaging one failed grade in as −1 is the most likely reason LMSYS's leaderboard prints 7.85 for Claude-Instant-1 where the valid grades average 7.906 ({{Production eval engineering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b39ad9e96193d21adc}}). Count failures as their own metric; drop the item from both sides.
  • Agreement is not reliability. Self-consistency of a judge (test-retest) can be high while it is systematically wrong. Judge agreement statistics (Cohen's kappa and its relatives) and calibration against people are on {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}; agreement between human annotators is on {{Human evaluation and annotation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d819485bcc54a45d49420}}.

10. Ranking uncertainty: a leaderboard is many comparisons

MT-Bench at 80 questions: palm-2-chat-bison-001 is ranked 13th of 34, but resampling the questions puts it anywhere from 7th to 18th; the median model's plausible ranks span 6 places real data. Only the top (gpt-4, rank 1 in every resample) and the far ends are settled; the middle of the table is a tie at this size.

Close scores, wide ranks: the middle of the table is a tie

LMSYS's released GPT-4 grades (1 to 10) for 34 models, 80 questions of two turns; score = mean of a model's valid grades. Bars: 95% interval of the score; right column: the 2.5% to 97.5% range of the model's rank, both from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of the 80 questions with both turns kept together (seed 0, src/mk_ranks.py) real data. These are per-model ranges, not simultaneous ones: read them as "this model could plausibly be here", not as a ranking of every pair.

  • Adjacent models whose intervals overlap may still be separable with a paired test on the same items (section 4); a rank range is the conservative reading.
  • Ranking every pair is a multiple comparison (section 6): 34 models make 561 pairs, and at α = 0.05 about 28 of them would look "significant" by chance if no model differed derived.
  • Arena leaderboards fit ratings from pairwise votes and publish bootstrap intervals on them; how those intervals and rank spreads are computed (Bradley-Terry, bootstrap, style control) is owned by {{Human preference and arenas|n:3ef5c17b0d0d814db49ff3b5c9fc3034}}.

11. Reporting standard

A review of 445 LLM benchmark papers by 29 reviewers found that 16.0% "used uncertainty estimates or statistical tests to compare the results" (Bean et al., {{"Measuring what Matters", arXiv:2511.04703|https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04703}}, NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks). Their checklist asks for the sample size with a power justification and uncertainty for every primary score. Merged with Miller's recommendations, a result is reportable when it carries:

  1. the number of items, the number of clusters and what a cluster is;
  2. each score with its standard error in parentheses (clustered where items cluster), Wilson or Bayesian intervals below a few hundred items;
  3. for comparisons: the paired difference, its standard error and interval, and the correlation of item scores (Miller's Table 5);
  4. samples per item K, temperature, seeds, and how pass@k or the mean over samples was computed;
  5. the test used and how many comparisons were made, with the correction;
  6. the judge (model, prompt version, J judgments per item) and its parse-failure count;
  7. the minimum detectable effect, or the power for the effect claimed;
  8. the harness and its version, so the comparison can be paired by someone else.

A fitted summary carries its model's assumptions: METR's time horizon
A number produced by a model fit carries the fit's assumptions. METR's time horizon assumes a task's difficulty for AI is linear in log human time; re-fitting the same 228 tasks and 26 AIs with splines and item-response theory finds the conversion "nearly flat in a region from 2--30 min", so a jump from 3 to 30 minutes is much easier than one from 30 minutes to 5 hours, and the re-fit scores better under cross-validated proper scoring rules ({{Nguyen and Fithian, arXiv:2610.12466|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12466}}). Check the diagnostic plots before quoting a doubling time; the depth is on {{Agentic benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb926d7b91ef8234e}}.

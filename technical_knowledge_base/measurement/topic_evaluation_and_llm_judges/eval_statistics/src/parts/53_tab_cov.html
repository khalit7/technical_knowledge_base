Small-n intervals: does a 95% interval cover 95% of the time?

A 95% confidence interval is a promise about a procedure: run the eval again on a fresh draw of n items, rebuild the interval, and 95% of those intervals contain the true pass rate. That promise can be checked exactly for pass/fail scores, without simulation: for a true rate θ, every possible count k of n has a binomial probability, and the coverage is the total probability of the counts whose interval contains θ. This tab does that sum for four intervals.

  • CLT (Wald): p̂ ± z·√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n), the formula behind most published error bars ({{Miller 2024, Eq. 2 and 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640}}).
  • Wilson score: inverts the score test; never leaves [0, 1] and never collapses to zero width.
  • Clopper-Pearson: the "exact" interval from binomial tail probabilities; guaranteed coverage, so it is wide.
  • Bayes, uniform prior: the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of Beta(1 + k, 1 + n − k).
Try: set n to 15 (one AIME paper) and watch the CLT curve fall far below 95% near 0 and 1; then n = 500. Bowyer, Aitchison and Ivanova's position paper ({{arXiv:2503.01747|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01747}}, ICML 2025) argues from exactly this picture: "Don't use the CLT in LLM evals with fewer than a few hundred datapoints".

What this tab does not show
Coverage here assumes independent items with one fixed pass rate each. Items that share a passage (clustered) or a model sampled several times per item break that assumption in other ways: Bowyer et al. find that neither the CLT nor a clustered CLT interval reaches nominal coverage at small n in their clustered simulation, and recommend a Bayesian hierarchical model there (their Section 3.2). The Reading tab's {{clustered errors|#t-read}} section covers the large-n case.

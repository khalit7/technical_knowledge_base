2. Where the variance comes from: items, samples, and the thermostat

Miller splits each item's score into a mean and a random part, si = xi + εi: xi is what the model would average on item i if asked forever, εi is this particular sample's luck. By the law of total variance the error of the eval mean has two parts:

Var(μ̂) = ( Var(x) + E[σi²] / K ) / n
Var(x)
how much items differ in difficulty for this model: a property of the item population, fixed once the eval is chosen
E[σi²]
the average within-item variance: how much one item's score moves between samples (for 0/1 scores, the mean of xi(1 − xi))
K
samples per item, averaged per item before averaging over items (Miller §3.1)

Only the second part can be bought down without new items. Miller's worked case, difficulty uniform on [0, 1], has Var(x) = 1/12 and E[σ²] = 1/6, so the variance with K samples is (1 + 2/K)/3 of the single-sample variance: K = 2 cuts it by a third, K = 4 by half, K = 6 by 5/9, and no K can cut more than 2/3. Two other routes:

  • Next-token probabilities remove ε altogether when the answer is a single token and there is no chain of thought: score item i by the probability of the right token, pi. In the uniform case that is the full 2/3 reduction. Anthropic's post said it knew of no open-source framework implementing it (November 2024).
  • "Don't touch the thermostat!" Lowering the temperature to 0 also removes ε, but by rounding each item to 0 or 1 it moves the variance into Var(x) and can shift the mean: in Miller's example a uniform x at T = 1 becomes a coin at T = 0 and the irreducible variance triples, 1/12 to 1/4; in his second example the mean moves from 2/3 to 3/4 and Var(x) grows from 1/27 to 3/16. Change the temperature only to study the model at that temperature.

Noise the item standard error does not see. The formula above treats the model as fixed. Several other sources move a score just as much, and none of them shrinks with more items:

  • Training seed. Madaan et al. trained ten 7B models identical except for the initialisation seed ({{arXiv:2406.10229|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10229}}, Meta, 2024). Their Table 1 sets the spread across seeds beside the 95% item interval of one model; seed noise is "generally ... well below the 95% CI" (table below). Two models that differ only in a data choice are therefore compared at the seed scale, which needs more than the item interval to see.
  • Checkpoint to checkpoint. Heineman et al. ({{"Signal and Noise", arXiv:2508.13144|https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13144}}, Ai2, 2025) see a 1.7-point range on ARC Challenge across the final 30 checkpoints of one 1B run, define noise as the relative standard deviation of the final checkpoints, and find that a benchmark's signal-to-noise ratio predicts whether small-scale rankings hold at scale (R = 0.791).
  • Decoding seeds and hardware. Hochlehnert et al. ({{"A Sober Look at Progress in Language Model Reasoning", arXiv:2504.07086|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07086}}) report pass@1 standard deviations of 5 to 15 points across 20 seeds on AIME'24 and AMC'23, and the same model at 7.3% ± 3.8 on one cluster and 11.3% ± 3.6 on another with identical containers.
  • Prompt, harness and grader. Not variance in the statistical sense: they change what is measured. The parent topic's {{Same model, many harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}} tab shows the spreads; a comparison is only paired if both sides used the same setup.

Madaan et al. Table 1, selected rows: seed mean and standard deviation across the ten final 7B models, and their "95% CI", the average of 210 analytic half-widths 1.96·√(S(1 − S)/N) over every checkpoint. The last column recomputes that formula at the final seed mean derived; it differs where early checkpoints scored lower (HumanEval).

3. Clustered errors: when items are not independent draws

The CLT assumes each item was drawn independently. Many evals draw groups: several questions about one passage (DROP, QuAC, RACE, SQuAD), one question translated into many languages (MGSM), many variants of one template (GSM-Symbolic), tasks from a few repositories, turns of one conversation, chunks of one document. Items in a group rise and fall together, so n of them carry less information than n independent items. Miller's clustered standard error (Eq. 4, from the econometrics of {{Abadie et al.|https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjac038}}) adds the within-cluster cross terms:

SEclustered² = SECLT² + (1/n²) Σc Σi Σj≠i (si,c − s̄)(sj,c − s̄)
c
a cluster; the triple sum runs over pairs of different items in the same cluster. Equivalently SEclustered = √(Σc (Σi∈c(si,c − s̄))²) / n

It slides between the two extremes: if the items in a cluster are uncorrelated it equals the plain standard error; if they always agree, each cluster counts as one item. For planning, the textbook shortcut is the design effect 1 + (m − 1)ρ for clusters of m items with intra-cluster correlation ρ (variance multiplier; the {{calculator|#t-size}} has it).

  • Cluster on how the items were drawn, not on any grouping you can find: the passage, the template, the conversation, the source document. If items were sampled one by one, do not cluster.
  • The opposite mistake: counting repeated runs of the same item as separate items. MathArena's "±" counts 4 runs of 30 AIME problems as 120 independent answers; its sibling page {{Math benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81ec942edf0040a2f596}} ("Error bars on 30 problems") reproduces all 123 table rows and finds the problem-level interval a median 1.7 times wider. Miller states the rule: "computing a pooled standard error across all KN answers will be inconsistent".
  • Tools. Inspect's stderr(cluster="...") implements Eq. 4 with a finite-cluster factor C/(C − 1) that the paper does not have ({{std.py|https://github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/inspect_ai/blob/main/src/inspect_ai/scorer/_metrics/std.py}}, read 4 October 2026); for 400 clusters it widens the standard error by about 0.1%. lm-evaluation-harness reports a closed-form standard error of the mean and bootstraps a few metrics ({{metrics.py|https://github.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness/blob/main/lm_eval/api/metrics.py}}); we found no clustering option there.
  • Small n and clusters together are hard: Bowyer et al. find that neither the plain nor the clustered CLT interval reaches its stated coverage with few items, and fit a Beta-Binomial hierarchical model instead (their Section 3.2, with code).

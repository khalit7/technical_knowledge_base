2. Where the variance comes from: items, samples, and the thermostat

Miller's worked case: item difficulty uniform on [0, 1], one sample per item. Drawing K = 4 samples per item and averaging them per item halves the variance of the eval mean; K = 2 cuts it by a third, K = 6 by 5/9, and no K can cut more than 2/3, because the rest is the items themselves. The same split on this page's RACE-H run real data, four scorings of the same questions:

More samples per question shrink only the sampling part of the error bar

The K curve is Miller's formula fed with the measured Var(x) and E[σ²]; the dots are what seeded sampling actually gives. On Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, greedy accuracy is 60.2% and the expected accuracy of one sampled answer 55.2% real data: same questions, same model, a 5-point gap that comes only from the temperature.

Miller splits each item's score into a mean and a random part, si = xi + εi: xi is what the model would average on item i if asked forever, εi this sample's luck. By the law of total variance:

Var(μ̂) = ( Var(x) + E[σi²] / K ) / n
Var(x)
how much items differ in difficulty for this model: a property of the item population, fixed once the eval is chosen (uniform case: 1/12)
E[σi²]
the average within-item variance: how much one item's score moves between samples; for 0/1 scores the mean of xi(1 − xi) (uniform case: 1/6)
K
samples per item, averaged per item before averaging over items (Miller §3.1); with the uniform values the variance is (1 + 2/K)/3 of the single-sample one

Only the second part can be bought down without new items. Two other routes:

  • Next-token probabilities remove ε altogether when the answer is a single token and there is no chain of thought: score item i by the probability of the right token, pi. In the uniform case that is the full 2/3 reduction. Anthropic's post said it knew of no open-source framework implementing it (November 2024).
  • "Don't touch the thermostat!" Lowering the temperature to 0 also removes ε, but rounds each item to 0 or 1: the variance moves into Var(x) (Miller's uniform x becomes a coin, 1/12 to 1/4) and the mean can shift (his second example: 2/3 to 3/4, with Var(x) 1/27 to 3/16). Change the temperature only to study the model at that temperature.

Noise the item standard error does not see, and that more items never shrink: training seeds (Madaan et al.: "generally ... well below the 95% CI" of the items), checkpoints and serving hardware; decoding seeds move pass@1 by 5 to 15 points on AIME'24 and AMC'23 (Hochlehnert et al.). Prompt, harness and grader change what is measured ({{Eval harnesses, section 3|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ea9cd5eb65795ff5c9#rd-why}}): a comparison is paired only if both sides used the same setup.

Seeds, checkpoints and hardware: the published numbers
  • Training seed. Madaan et al. trained ten 7B models identical except for the initialisation seed ({{arXiv:2406.10229|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10229}}, Meta, 2024). Their Table 1 sets the spread across seeds beside the 95% item interval of one model (table below): two models that differ only in a data choice are compared at the seed scale, which needs more than the item interval to see.
  • Checkpoint to checkpoint. Heineman et al. ({{"Signal and Noise", arXiv:2508.13144|https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.13144}}, Ai2, 2025) see a 1.7-point range on ARC Challenge across the final 30 checkpoints of one 1B run, define noise as the relative standard deviation of the final checkpoints, and find that a benchmark's signal-to-noise ratio predicts whether small-scale rankings hold at scale (R = 0.791).
  • Decoding seeds and hardware. Hochlehnert et al. ({{"A Sober Look at Progress in Language Model Reasoning", arXiv:2504.07086|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.07086}}) report pass@1 standard deviations of 5 to 15 points across 20 seeds on AIME'24 and AMC'23, and the same model at 7.3% ± 3.8 on one cluster and 11.3% ± 3.6 on another with identical containers.

3. Clustered errors: when items are not independent draws

Many evals draw groups: several questions about one passage (DROP, QuAC, RACE, SQuAD), one question translated into many languages (MGSM), variants of one template (GSM-Symbolic), tasks from a few repositories, turns of one conversation, chunks of one document. Items in a group rise and fall together, so n of them carry less information than n independent items. How much less is a property of the eval and the models, and ranges from nothing to a factor of three:

How much wider the honest interval is than the naive one

Ratio of the clustered (or problem-level) standard error to the naive one; bars blue for published, purple for derived, green for this page's real data. Published: Miller's Table 4 on Anthropic models (DROP 3.05, MGSM 1.88, RACE-H 1.10). Math benchmarks page derived: MathArena's "±" counts 4 runs of 30 AIME problems as 120 independent answers; clustered by problem it is ±10.75 instead of ±6.92 for o1 (medium) on AIME 2025, and a median 1.7 times the published interval over the four-run rows. This page real data: RACE-H on two small open models, 0.96 and 1.02; MT-Bench turns clustered by question, a median 1.10 times the paired SE across 561 model pairs (0.90 to 1.26).

Miller's clustered standard error (Eq. 4, from the econometrics of {{Abadie et al.|https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjac038}}) adds the within-cluster cross terms:

SEclustered² = SECLT² + (1/n²) Σc Σi Σj≠i (si,c − s̄)(sj,c − s̄)
c
a cluster; the triple sum runs over pairs of different items in the same cluster. Equivalently SEclustered = √(Σc (Σi∈c(si,c − s̄))²) / n

It slides between two extremes: items in a cluster uncorrelated, it equals the plain standard error; always agreeing, each cluster counts as one item. For planning, the textbook shortcut is the design effect 1 + (m − 1)ρ for clusters of m items with intra-cluster correlation ρ (variance multiplier; the {{calculator|#t-size}} has it).

  • Cluster on how the items were drawn (passage, template, conversation, source document), not on any grouping you can find; items sampled one by one are not clustered.
  • The opposite mistake: counting repeated runs as items, as MathArena's "±" does (all 123 rows reproduced on {{Math benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81ec942edf0040a2f596}}, "Error bars on 30 problems"). Miller: "computing a pooled standard error across all KN answers will be inconsistent". Four runs of 30 problems are 30 items with 4 samples each.
  • Small n and clusters together are hard: Bowyer et al. fit a Beta-Binomial hierarchical model instead (their Section 3.2, with code).

Tools.

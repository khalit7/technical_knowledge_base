In one screen

An eval runs a model on n items and averages the scores. The items are a sample of all the items you could have written, and with sampled decoding or an LLM judge each item's score is itself a draw. So the average is an estimate, and every decision taken from it (ship, roll back, claim a gain) is a statistical test whether or not anyone runs one.

The one picture to take away
Pairing and clustering pull in opposite directions, and on real data both matter. The animation in Pairing runs one real comparison three ways on the same items: the interval shrinks when the comparison is paired and widens again when items that share a passage are counted together. On the RACE-H comparison the three readings give three verdicts at the 5% line: unpaired, no difference; paired, a difference; paired and clustered, just short of one.

1. A score is an estimate: standard errors and error bars

Evan Miller's {{"Adding Error Bars to Evals"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640}} (Anthropic, November 2024) sets up the frame this page uses: treat the eval's questions as drawn at random from an unseen "super-population" of questions, so the quantity of interest is the mean over that population, not over the questions you happened to write. Then the Central Limit Theorem gives the standard error of the observed mean s̄ over n items:

SE = √( Var(s) / n )    and for 0/1 scores    SE = √( p(1 − p) / n )
Var(s)
sample variance of the item scores, (1/(n − 1))·Σ(si − s̄)² (Miller Eq. 1)
p
the observed pass rate (Eq. 2); a 95% interval is s̄ ± 1.96·SE (Eq. 3)

What that means in practice derived: at 70% the standard error is 4.6 points on 100 items, 3.2 on 200, 1.4 on 1,000. A "2-point regression" on a 100-item set is well inside the noise. Halving the error bar takes four times the items.

  • Bootstrap or formula? For a plain mean the two agree; Miller regards bootstrapping as unnecessary "unless a complicated sampling scheme or estimator is being used" (an F1 over a set, a ratio, a clustered design). OpenAI's evals framework bootstraps; Inspect's stderr() uses the formula.
  • Fractional scores. The Bernoulli formula is only for 0/1 scores. Miller notes that the {{Llama 3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}} applied it to F1-style scores too, which makes those intervals conservative (too wide), and applied it without clustering to reading-comprehension evals, which makes those too narrow.
  • Small sets. Below a few hundred items the CLT interval misbehaves: at 30 of 30 it has zero width, near 0 or 1 it leaves [0, 1], and its real coverage falls below the promised 95% (Bowyer et al., {{arXiv:2503.01747|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01747}}). Use Wilson or a Bayesian interval there; the {{Small-n intervals|#t-cov}} tab computes the coverage of each exactly and reproduces their 92.5% figure at n = 100 (92.2% computed exactly).

The idea in one screen

An eval score is a mean over a sample of items, so it is an estimate with an error bar; two models are compared on the per-item differences of the same items (paired), counting items that share a source as one cluster, and a decision needs enough items that the change you care about falls outside that interval.

Same items, same 3-point gap, three intervals: only the analysis changed

RACE-H, 1,311 questions on 400 passages, Qwen3-0.6B minus Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, greedy answers, run for this page real data. The difference is −3.05 points every time; the 95% interval is ±3.77 unpaired (p = 0.11), ±3.03 paired (p = 0.049) and ±3.07 paired and clustered by passage (p = 0.051). Section 4 builds it step by step.

You need nothing beyond a first statistics course. The same tools on a classical classifier (a paired bootstrap on SST-2) are on {{Evaluation metrics|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a29acef722e842a29b}}.

New terms on this page
Standard error (SE)
the standard deviation of the score across re-draws of the items; a 95% interval is ±1.96 SE
Cluster
a group of items drawn together (one passage, one template, one conversation) whose scores move together
Paired difference
model B's score minus model A's on the same item; its mean and SE are what a comparison tests
Discordant items
items where exactly one of two pass/fail models is right; the only ones McNemar's test uses
Design effect
how much clustering multiplies the variance, 1 + (m − 1)ρ for clusters of m items with correlation ρ
Power, MDE
the chance a test detects a true change of a given size; the minimum detectable effect is the change detected with 80% power
Type-M error
how much a significant result from an underpowered test exaggerates the true effect
Familywise rate, FDR
chance of at least one false alarm across many tests; expected share of false alarms among those flagged

1. A score is an estimate: standard errors and error bars

At 70% the standard error is 4.6 points on 100 items, 3.2 on 200, 1.4 on 1,000 derived. A "2-point regression" on a 100-item set is well inside the noise, and halving the error bar takes four times the items:

The error bar shrinks only as 1/√n

95% CLT interval of a 70% score at each set size, p ± 1.96·√(p(1 − p)/n) derived: ±16.4 points at 30 items, ±9.0 at 100, ±6.4 at 200, ±4.0 at 500, ±2.8 at 1,000, ±1.3 at 5,000. Below a few hundred items use the intervals in the next figure instead.

The frame is Evan Miller's {{"Adding Error Bars to Evals"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640}} (Anthropic, November 2024): the questions are a random draw from an unseen "super-population", and the Central Limit Theorem gives the standard error of the observed mean s̄ over n items:

SE = √( Var(s) / n )    and for 0/1 scores    SE = √( p(1 − p) / n )
Var(s)
sample variance of the item scores, (1/(n − 1))·Σ(si − s̄)² (Miller Eq. 1)
p
the observed pass rate (Eq. 2); a 95% interval is s̄ ± 1.96·SE (Eq. 3)
Bootstrap or formula, fractional scores, how to print it
  • Bootstrap or formula? For a plain mean the two agree; Miller regards bootstrapping as unnecessary "unless a complicated sampling scheme or estimator is being used" (an F1 over a set, a ratio, a clustered design). OpenAI's evals framework bootstraps; Inspect's stderr() uses the formula.
  • Fractional scores. The Bernoulli formula is only for 0/1 scores. Miller notes that the {{Llama 3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}} applied it to F1-style scores too, which makes those intervals conservative (too wide), and applied it without clustering to reading-comprehension evals, which makes those too narrow.
  • Report it the way other sciences do (Miller's Tables 2 and 3): the number of questions beside each eval, the standard error in parentheses under the score, and for clustered evals the number of clusters.

Below a few hundred items: does a 95% interval cover 95% of the time?

A 95% interval promises that, over re-drawn sets of n items, 95% of the intervals contain the true rate. Below a few hundred items the CLT interval breaks that promise: at 30 of 30 it has zero width, near 0 or 1 it leaves [0, 1], and it covers less than 95% (Bowyer, Aitchison and Ivanova, {{"Don't Use the CLT in LLM Evals With Fewer Than a Few Hundred Datapoints", arXiv:2503.01747|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01747}}, ICML 2025). For pass/fail scores coverage can be computed exactly: sum the binomial probability of every count k whose interval contains the true rate θ.

The four intervals, and their average coverage and width at this n

Wilson inverts the score test and never leaves [0, 1] or collapses; Clopper-Pearson ("exact", from binomial tails) guarantees coverage and is wide; Bayes with a uniform prior takes the 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles of Beta(1 + k, 1 + n − k); CLT (Wald) is p̂ ± z·√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n).

Your own count: four intervals for k passed out of n

At k = n the CLT interval is [100%, 100%]: it claims certainty from 30 problems. Wilson, Clopper-Pearson and Bayes still leave room below. For unpaired scores, {{Topic: benchmarks, "Same model, many numbers"|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} has a calculator with benchmark-size presets and Fisher's exact test.

Coverage here assumes independent items with one fixed pass rate each. With clusters or several samples per item, Bowyer et al. find that neither the CLT nor a clustered CLT interval reaches nominal coverage at small n in their clustered simulation, and recommend a Bayesian hierarchical model there (their Section 3.2).

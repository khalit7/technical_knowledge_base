6. Many comparisons and many-slice gates

Each test at α = 0.05 fires falsely one time in twenty when nothing changed. A gate that checks m slices independently fails by chance with probability 1 − (1 − α)m: 64% for 20 slices derived, two times in three. The same holds for a leaderboard that compares every pair of models, a prompt search over many variants, or a report that bolds the best of many numbers.

The chance of a false alarm grows with every slice you gate

Familywise rate assumes independent slices (correlated slices fire together, so it is an upper bound on the chance of at least one false alarm and a lower bound on nothing). Bonferroni tests each slice at α/m; Šidák at 1 − (1 − α)1/m. The cost: the items each slice needs grow by ((zα/2m + zβ)/(zα/2 + zβ))² at 80% power derived.

  • Familywise corrections keep the chance of any false alarm at α: Bonferroni (simple, conservative), Holm (step-down, never worse), Šidák and Romano-Wolf (evalstats' defaults at n ≥ 30, Shaffer's procedure below 30).
  • False discovery rate (Benjamini-Hochberg) keeps the expected share of false alarms among the flagged slices at α: the right choice when slices are diagnostics that send a person to look, not automatic blocks.
  • Hierarchical gating: gate the aggregate, read slices as diagnostics, gate a slice only when its own minimum detectable effect is one you care about. The aggregate hides slice regressions: of 1,122 ordered MT-Bench model swaps, 626 pass an aggregate gate at 0.25 points and 67 of those fail a slice ({{Production eval engineering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b39ad9e96193d21adc}}, Gate designer).
  • Escalate, never re-run until green: re-running a stochastic eval until it passes is a multiple comparison with unknown m; send borderline results to more samples or review, K fixed in advance.

7. Power and the minimum detectable effect

Back to the parent topic's gate: detecting a true 2-point drop at 70% with 80% power takes about 8,242 items per model unpaired, but 1,955 paired when the two models disagree on 10% of items. The 200-item gate has about 14% power for that drop paired and 6% unpaired derived; it can see a drop of about 6 points paired and 13 unpaired, not 2. That is acceptable as long as the gate states it.

Pairing moves the 80% line from thousands of items to hundreds

Predict before dragging: if the two models disagree on 20% of items instead of 10%, how many paired items does the 2-point drop need? (Answer under the chart.)

Power of a two-sided test at α = 0.05 to detect a true 2-point drop from 70%, against items per model (log scale); normal approximation, power = Φ(Δ√n/σ − zα/2), with σ² = d − Δ² paired and 2p(1 − p) unpaired derived. Defaults reproduce the parent topic's 1,955 and 8,242 by construction (same formula). At d = 20% the paired line needs about 3,916 items: the variance of the difference is d − Δ², so the items needed scale almost linearly with the disagreement.

Miller's §5 gives the general formula for the independent items a paired comparison needs, and inverts it into the smallest effect an eval of fixed size can see:

n = (zα/2 + zβ)² · (ω² + σ²A/KA + σ²B/KB) / δ²     MDE = (zα/2 + zβ) · √( (ω² + σ²A/KA + σ²B/KB) / n )
ω²
variance of the per-item difference of the two models' true scores, Var(xA) + Var(xB) − 2Cov(xA, xB); for 0/1 scores d − Δ², the σ² of the chart
σ²/K
each model's within-item variance over its K samples
zα/2 + zβ
1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80 at α = 0.05 two-sided and 80% power

Miller's worked numbers, all reproduced by the {{Sample-size calculator|#t-size}}: with ω² = 1/9 and no sampling noise, detecting 3 points takes about 969 questions, hence his advice that new evals contain at least 1,000; with σ² = 1/6 per model and 198 questions, going from K = 1 to K = 10 samples per question lowers the MDE from 13.2 to 7.5 points. The rule: write each gate's minimum detectable effect next to its threshold, decide n before the run from the effect that matters, and buy sensitivity with pairing and resampling before buying more labels.

Underpowered experiments also exaggerate. Card et al. ({{"With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility", EMNLP 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06595}}): 500 items, two models agreeing on 90% of them, a true 2-point gain. McNemar's power is about 0.25, and the runs that do come out significant overstate the gain by about 1.9 times on average (Gelman and Carlin's Type-M error); at 2,000 items, nearly 80% and 1.1. The calculator reproduces both by exact summation, independently: 25.0% and 1.89; 79.1% and 1.13 derived. On GLUE's three smallest test sets the typical claimed improvement was below the minimum detectable effect (block below).

The 3,500 figure. The page's own rule of thumb, MDE ≈ 2.8·√(2p(1 − p)/n), gives n = 2.8² × 2 × 0.21 / 0.02² = 8,232 items per configuration derived, and exact quantiles give 8,242 (the parent topic's figure). One-sided (2.49) gives about 6,500, dropping the factor 2 about 4,100.

The n = 300 example. The champion passes 78% of 300 items, the challenger regresses 5 points, and the two disagree on about 15% of items. The expected split of the 45 discordant items is 30 against 15, McNemar's z is 15/√45 = 2.24, and power is Φ(2.24 − 1.96) = 61% by the normal approximation; the exact McNemar test gives . 80% power needs about 2.8² × 0.15 / 0.05² = 470 items (normal approximation), with the exact test derived. The rest of the example holds: the unpaired standard error of the difference is √(2 × 0.78 × 0.22 / 300) = 3.4 points, so a 5-point drop is 1.5 standard errors, and the unpaired design needs about 16 × 0.17 / 0.05² ≈ 1,100 items. Pairing turns a hopeless gate into a borderline one at no labelling cost; it does not make 300 items enough.

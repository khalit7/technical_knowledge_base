6. Many comparisons and many-slice gates

Each test at α = 0.05 fires falsely one time in twenty when nothing changed. A gate that checks m slices independently fails by chance with probability 1 − (1 − α)m: 64% for 20 slices derived. The same holds for a leaderboard that compares every pair of models, a prompt search over many variants, or a report that bolds the best of many numbers.

Familywise rate assumes independent slices (correlated slices fire together, so it is an upper bound on the chance of at least one false alarm and a lower bound on nothing). Bonferroni tests each slice at α/m; Šidák at 1 − (1 − α)1/m. The cost: the items each slice needs grow by ((zα/2m + zβ)/(zα/2 + zβ))² at 80% power derived.

  • Familywise corrections keep the chance of any false alarm at α: Bonferroni (simple, conservative), Holm (step-down, never worse than Bonferroni), Šidák and Romano-Wolf (evalstats' defaults for simultaneous intervals and for p-values at n ≥ 30, Shaffer's procedure below 30).
  • False discovery rate (Benjamini-Hochberg) keeps the expected share of false alarms among the flagged slices at α: the right choice when slices are diagnostics that send a person to look, not automatic blocks.
  • Hierarchical gating, from the old Production page: gate on the aggregate, read slices as diagnostics, and gate a slice only when its own minimum detectable effect is one you care about. The aggregate hides slice regressions; the {{Production eval engineering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b39ad9e96193d21adc}} page shows it on real data: on MT-Bench's released grades, 626 of the 1,122 ordered model swaps pass an aggregate gate at a 0.25-point tolerance, and 67 of those fail at least one slice under a per-slice gate (its Gate designer tab).
  • Escalate, never re-run until green. A borderline result goes to more samples or human review with K fixed in advance. Re-running a stochastic eval until it passes is a multiple comparison with an unknown m.

7. Power and the minimum detectable effect

A test can be wrong two ways: a false alarm (rate α) and a missed real change (rate β). Power, 1 − β, is the chance of detecting a change of a given size; 80% is the usual floor. Miller's §5 gives the number of independent items a paired comparison needs, and inverts it into the smallest effect an eval of fixed size can see:

n = (zα/2 + zβ)² · (ω² + σ²A/KA + σ²B/KB) / δ²     MDE = (zα/2 + zβ) · √( (ω² + σ²A/KA + σ²B/KB) / n )
ω²
variance of the per-item difference of the two models' true scores, Var(xA) + Var(xB) − 2Cov(xA, xB)
σ²/K
each model's within-item variance over its K samples
zα/2 + zβ
1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80 at α = 0.05 two-sided and 80% power

Miller's worked numbers, all reproduced by the {{Sample-size calculator|#t-size}}: with ω² = 1/9 and no sampling noise, detecting 3 points takes about 969 questions, hence his advice that new evals contain at least 1,000; with σ² = 1/6 per model and 198 questions, going from K = 1 to K = 10 samples per question lowers the MDE from 13.2 to 7.5 points. The parent topic's 200-item gate can see a drop of about 6 points paired and 13 unpaired, not 2; that is acceptable as long as the gate states it. The rule the old Production page ended on still stands: write each gate's minimum detectable effect next to its threshold, and buy sensitivity with pairing and resampling before buying more labels.

Correction to the old Production page: "Detecting a 2-point drop near p=0.7 needs roughly n=3500 paired-free"
The page's own rule of thumb, MDE ≈ 2.8·√(2p(1 − p)/n), gives n = 2.8² × 2 × 0.21 / 0.02² = 8,232 items per configuration derived, and exact quantiles give 8,242 (the parent topic's figure). No variant reaches 3,500: one-sided (2.49) gives about 6,500, dropping the factor 2 about 4,100. At 3,500 items an unpaired comparison detects about 3.1 points, not 2.
Correction to the old Production page's worked example: "in practice a 5-point true regression is detected with high power at n=300"
The example: the champion passes 78% of 300 items, the challenger regresses 5 points, and the two disagree on about 15% of items. Then the expected split of the 45 discordant items is 30 against 15, McNemar's z is 15/√45 = 2.24, and power is Φ(2.24 − 1.96) = 61% by the normal approximation; the exact McNemar test gives . 80% power needs about 2.8² × 0.15 / 0.05² = 470 items (normal approximation), with the exact test derived. The rest of the example holds: the unpaired standard error of the difference is √(2 × 0.78 × 0.22 / 300) = 3.4 points, so a 5-point drop is 1.5 standard errors, and the unpaired design needs about 16 × 0.17 / 0.05² ≈ 1,100 items. Pairing still turns a hopeless gate into a borderline one at no labelling cost; it does not make 300 items enough. The "15% disagreement, typical for a same-tier swap" is unsourced unconfirmed: measure it on your own pair (the two RACE-H models here disagree on of questions).

Underpowered experiments also exaggerate. Card et al. ({{"With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility", EMNLP 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06595}}) work the case of a 500-item test set, two models that agree on 90% of items and a true gain of 2 points: McNemar's power is about 0.25, and the experiments that do come out significant overstate the gain by a factor of about 1.9 on average (the Type-M error of Gelman and Carlin).

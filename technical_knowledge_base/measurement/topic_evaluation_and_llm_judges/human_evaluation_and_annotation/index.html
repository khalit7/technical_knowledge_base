In one screen

A human label is a measurement, and it has the same three failure modes as any instrument: it can be noisy (two people disagree), biased (everyone agrees, and everyone is wrong), or measuring the wrong thing (the rubric asked a different question from the one you care about). This page is about controlling all three, from the moment you decide people should grade, to the day you discover your gold set has been wrong for a year.

When people are the right instrument

Human evaluation is the most faithful and the slowest layer of the evaluation stack the parent page lays out (deterministic checks, references, judges, people, live traffic). It is the right instrument in four situations:

  • There is no reference and quality is a judgement. Helpfulness, tone, safety in context, whether an answer is persuasive or misleading. Exact match cannot grade these, and an LLM judge has not yet been shown to agree with people on your version of them.
  • An automatic grader needs calibrating. Every judge, reward model and classifier is checked against a human-labelled gold slice before its numbers are believed, and again when traffic shifts (the procedure is on LLM-as-judge).
  • The decision is expensive. A launch, a model swap on a sensitive surface, a safety sign-off. A few hundred expert labels are cheap against the cost of being wrong.
  • You are collecting training signal. Preference pairs and ratings for reward models (Preference data, below).

It is the wrong instrument when a program can check the answer (unit tests, exact match against a verified key, schema validation), when the task needs knowledge the raters do not have, and when you need the number every hour. Two findings are worth remembering before trusting any human score:

  • Untrained people can be at chance. Non-experts asked to tell GPT-3 text from human text in stories, news and recipes were at chance level; short training lifted them to at most 55% (Clark et al. 2021).
  • Crowd ratings can fail where experts do not. Even with strict qualification filters, Mechanical Turk workers failed to tell model-written stories from human references, while English teachers could; showing workers the reference beside the output improved their calibration (Karpinska et al. 2021).

Designing the task: rubrics and guidelines

Most disagreement between raters is manufactured by the task, not by the raters. The format, the rubric and the guidelines decide how much of a label is signal.

What a usable rubric contains

  • One definition per criterion, written as the question the rater answers ("Does every factual claim agree with the reference?"), not as an adjective ("accurate").
  • Anchors: at least one worked example per level, chosen from borderline cases rather than obvious ones. HelpSteer2's guidelines define every attribute and every rating level with examples (its Appendix G).
  • A decision order when criteria interact. MMLU-Redux's ordered protocol is why its kappa for "is there an error" (binary) and for "which error type" were almost the same, 0.67 and 0.67 on Virology and 0.92 and 0.95 on College Chemistry (Table 1): raters who agreed something was wrong also agreed what.
  • Allowed exits: "cannot judge", "needs an expert", "skip": HelpSteer2 told raters to skip anything with personal data and flag unsafe content. Forcing a label onto an item the rater cannot assess manufactures noise.
  • What to ignore: length, formatting and confident tone, unless they are the criterion. Arena's experts were asked to fact-check answers with a search engine, which took 3 to 5 minutes per label (Chiang et al. 2024, §6.3).
  • Context the rater needs: showing AMT workers the human reference beside the model's story improved their ratings (Karpinska et al.); HelpSteer2 had each rater score the two responses to a prompt one after the other for the same reason.

Pilot, measure, revise

Write the rubric, have two or three people label the same 50 to 100 items, measure agreement, read every disagreement, fix the guideline, repeat. HelpSteer2 published what this loop is worth: quadratic weighted kappa on helpfulness went from 0.465 in the initial collection to 0.706 after clarifying with the vendor how the guidelines apply to edge cases, and to 0.791 after post-processing.

Agreement: telling consensus from chance

Two raters who label at random will still agree some of the time. Every agreement statistic worth reporting subtracts that chance agreement and rescales: 1 means perfect agreement, 0 means no better than chance, below 0 means systematic disagreement. They differ in what "chance" means and in what data they accept.

Why ties matter

On turn 1 of MT-Bench, two experts agree on of pairs when ties count, and on of when any pair involving a tie is dropped: Zheng et al.'s 63% and 81% (Table 5, reproduced exactly from the released votes derived). Dropping ties throws away the hardest third of the comparisons and shrinks the label set from three to two, so chance agreement rises from about a third to about a half. Alpha goes from to : a real improvement, but not the 18 points the raw figures suggest. Treating a tie as halfway between "A wins" and "B wins" (an ordinal distance) gives alpha with ties kept: a tie against a win is a smaller disagreement than a win against the opposite win. Any agreement figure for pairwise votes is meaningless without saying which of these it is.

Why prevalence matters

When one label dominates, two raters agree often just by both saying it, and chance-corrected statistics collapse. Reading the number

The widely quoted bands (0.41 to 0.60 "moderate", 0.61 to 0.80 "substantial", above 0.81 "almost perfect") come from Landis and Koch 1977, who offered them as opinion, not evidence. Krippendorff's working rule is stricter: rely on data with alpha of at least 0.800, draw only tentative conclusions between 0.667 and 0.800, and discard below (Content Analysis, 2004, pp. 241 to 243, as quoted on Wikipedia; the book was not re-read here). By that rule, MT-Bench's expert votes, HelpSteer2's ratings and Llama 2's helpfulness ratings (Gwet's AC2 0.37 to 0.55 on a 7-point scale; safety ratings 0.70 to 0.95, Touvron et al. 2023, §3.4.2 and §4.4) are all below the bar for individual labels. That is normal for subjective judgements, and it is why gold labels are aggregated over several raters and why a judge that matches one human is not necessarily at the ceiling.

What to report with any human-labelled set: the statistic and its variant (nominal or weighted, ties in or out), raw agreement beside it, the label shares, the number of items and of raters per item, how disagreements were resolved, and whether low-agreement items were filtered out before measuring. The Agreement lab computes every statistic above on both released sets, with the coincidence matrix.

Disagreement is not always error

Low agreement has two different causes, and they need opposite treatment:

  • Error: a slip, a misread rubric, too little expertise, too little attention. The item has one right answer and someone missed it. Fix with adjudication, better guidelines, better raters.
  • Legitimate variation: the item is ambiguous, or the question is a matter of judgement on which reasonable people differ. Forcing one label throws away information about the item. Barbara Plank calls this human label variation and argues it should be modelled and evaluated, not removed (Plank 2022); Aroyo and Welty listed "there is one truth" and "disagreement is bad" among seven myths of annotation (Truth Is a Lie, AI Magazine 2015).

How much a single majority label can miss was measured by ChaosNLI, which collected 100 annotations per item, 464,500 in all, for 3,113 SNLI and MNLI items (chosen because their original 5 votes split 3 to 2) and 1,532 αNLI items. The original majority label was not the 100-vote majority on 20% of the SNLI items, 31% of the MNLI-matched items and 10% of the αNLI items (Nie et al. 2020). Earlier, graded NLI judgements had shown such disagreements persist however the question is asked (Pavlick and Kwiatkowski 2019).

What to do with it

  • Decide per criterion whether there is a fact of the matter. "Is this dose correct?" has one; "is this reply too blunt?" does not. Adjudicate the first kind; keep the distribution for the second.
  • Soft labels: store the vote distribution, not only the majority, and score a model or judge against it (ChaosNLI compares model probabilities to the human distribution). The perspectivist approach goes further and keeps who said what, so results can be broken down by rater group (Cabitza, Campagner and Basile 2023; survey: Uma et al. 2021).
  • Never filter disagreement out and then report agreement. Dropping the items people disagree on raises any agreement statistic by construction (HelpSteer2's retention rule, above).
  • Mine it. Items that split experts are where the rubric is unclear, where the gold label is most likely wrong, and where a judge's behaviour is least constrained. "Ambiguous" is a valid label state: give it a code and keep such items out of gating (The gold slice, below).

Annotator quality control

Quality control has three moments: before anyone labels, while they label, and after the labels are in.

Before

Screen and train. InstructGPT chose its contractors by a screening test on sensitive prompts and agreement with researchers (Ouyang et al. 2022, Appendix B). Anthropic's HH work did not filter on agreement and measured about 63% crowd-researcher agreement afterwards. Pay for the effort you want: GPQA paid most of its money as bonuses tied to what made a question good (objective and hard), not per question.

During

Gold items mixed in: items with a known answer, scored per rater. Attention checks for crowd pools. Time on task: a label in two seconds was not read. LLM use: one study detected it by keystrokes and pasted text, and found 33 to 46% of workers using an LLM (Veselovsky et al. 2023); when many raters use the same model, their errors stop being independent and majority voting no longer cancels them.

After

Model each rater. Dawid and Skene's EM algorithm estimates a confusion matrix per rater and the likely true label per item (Dawid and Skene 1979); MACE adds a probability that each rater is spamming (Hovy et al. 2013). Weight or drop raters accordingly. Arena flags anomalous voters by comparing each user's votes to the historical distribution for the same model pairs, combining per-vote p-values with Fisher's method at 5 random checkpoints between 1 and 100 votes (Chiang et al. 2024, §5.1).

Per-rater agreement with the consensus is the simplest monitor, and it is cheap: the same coincidence counts the Agreement lab uses, computed per rater, flag someone drifting within a day. Keep in mind that agreeing with the majority rewards conformity: a careful expert who catches what the crowd missed will look like an outlier, so read an outlier's labels before dropping them.

The gold slice: building it and keeping it true

A gold slice is a set of real inputs (with outputs, when the label is about an output) labelled by qualified people under a fixed rubric version. It is the reference every automatic grader is calibrated against, the answer key of your own regression sets, and the thing an audit audits. Production eval engineering owns the golden sets built on it (composition, sizes, promoting production failures into tests, CI gates); LLM-as-judge owns calibrating a judge against it. This section is about the labels themselves.

Building it

  • Sample from real traffic, stratified by the slices you will report (intent, language, risk tier), oversampling rare slices that matter and including known hard and ambiguous cases on purpose. Record each item's sampling weight, so an aggregate can be reweighted to the traffic mix.
  • Size it from the interval you need, not from habit: a 200-item slice at 80% agreement has a 95% interval of roughly ±5.5 points (the binomial standard error, √(0.8 × 0.2 / 200) = 2.8 points, times 1.96). How many items a given decision needs is on Eval statistics.
  • Two independent labels per item, blind to each other and to which system produced the output, and a third person to adjudicate disagreements. The physics re-grading audit did exactly this: two PhD reviewers agreed on 140 of the 196 cases they both reviewed (71.4%) and a third settled the other 56 (Re-grading six physics benchmarks).
  • A one-line rationale per label. It makes adjudication fast, it is the raw material of a judge prompt (Critique Shadowing, on the LLM-as-judge page), and it shows later whether a label was a judgement or a slip.

Keeping it true

  • Version the slice and the rubric together, and re-measure agreement whenever either changes.
  • Refresh with new samples when traffic shifts, and retire items whose answer has changed with the world. MMLU-Redux found questions in Professional Law and Accounting that assume US jurisdiction and practice without saying so and "may become outdated if standards change" (Gema et al. 2024, §2.2).
  • Hold part of it out. If the same items are used to iterate a judge prompt and to report the judge's agreement, the report is an in-sample number.
  • Model-assisted labelling (an LLM pre-fills the label, a person confirms) is faster, but it anchors the rater on the model's answer, which is the answer you are trying to check. If you use it, measure on a sample how often people overturn the pre-label, and keep the double-blind protocol for the items that feed calibration.
  • Audit it on a schedule and on every surprise: next section.

Gold-label auditing: your key is wrong somewhere

Gold labels are written by people and are wrong at rates that swamp the small deltas evaluations are used to detect. Against a wrong key, a better model fails for disagreeing with the label, ceilings look lower than they are, and a regression gate can block the better model. Finding the wrong labels

Nobody can re-check a whole test set by hand. Every audit above used the same trick: let models propose suspects, let people decide. Three ways to rank the suspects:

  • Disagreement with the key. PlatinumBench ran several frontier models and inspected every item at least one of them got wrong; a wrong key shows up as models disagreeing with the key, a badly posed question as models disagreeing with each other. It finds a lower bound: an item where every model shares the key's mistake is never inspected (Vendrow et al. 2025, §2.2).
  • Confident learning. Get out-of-sample predicted probabilities for every item (cross-validation, so no model has seen the label it is judging), set a per-class threshold at the average confidence of items carrying that label, count items confidently predicted as a different class (the "confident joint"), and rank the suspects by normalized margin, the probability of the given label minus the largest other one (Northcutt, Jiang and Chuang 2021). The open-source cleanlab library implements it. In the test-set study, people confirmed 51% of what it flagged, so it ranks the review queue; it does not replace the review.
  • Consensus votes. Count how many independent models pick the same answer that is not the key. Simple, and it needs no probabilities, so it works with API models.

How well do they work on a real key? MMLU-Redux tells us which of its sampled questions are flawed, and the Open LLM Leaderboard released every model's per-question 5-shot probabilities on the same questions, so each ranking can be scored against the audit. Ranking Virology's 100 questions by the 18 models' average probability for the official answer ("self-confidence", one of cleanlab's scores), the first reviewed are all real errors: derived. The same ranking finds in College Chemistry (25 flawed), and on High School Biology, with 5 flawed questions in 100, its first 20 hold . Precision follows the base rate: the cleaner the set, the more of the queue is false alarms, which is why a review queue is sized by budget, not by a threshold. The Audit lab runs the three rankings on six subjects with a review budget you set.

Two caveats on model-proposed audits
The models may have seen the test set (MMLU is widely contaminated), so agreement with the key can be memory rather than knowledge, which hides wrong keys. And models share training data, so their mistakes are correlated: "every model disagrees with the key" is strong evidence, but "every model agrees" is weak evidence that the key is right.

Making it a routine: gold-error-aware scoring

The pattern applies to any internal golden set. It was the core of the old Production eval engineering page and is carried here in full:

  1. Never treat gold as infallible: store a confidence and provenance field per label (above).
  2. Route model-against-gold disagreements through a consensus battery: several strong models from different families (or judges with retrieval and tools) re-answer the item independently; strong consensus against the key flags the label, not the model.
  3. Have people adjudicate the flagged items. The output is a corrected label or an "ambiguous, exclude from gating" tag: ambiguity is a valid label state, and forcing it corrupts metrics.
  4. Score with error-awareness: gate on the verified (platinum) subset and track the full set, or down-weight low-confidence labels.
  5. Make it continuous: every gate failure triggers an audit of the failing items before the failure is believed. In mature systems, disagreement mining doubles as golden-set quality control and as discovery of genuinely hard items.

This is the same machinery as judge calibration, pointed at the dataset instead of the judge. An evaluation has three fallible parts, the model, the grader and the gold, and any observed delta has to be attributed to one of them before anyone acts; the parent page animates that attribution on the physics re-grading's 250 rejections (Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges, Reading tab, "How it breaks"). The production page keeps the routine's gate rule in its own words (Production eval engineering). When the grader rather than the key is the suspect, see LLM-as-judge; the statistics of a judge-corrected pass rate are arXiv:2511.21140.

Collecting human preference data

Preference data (which of two responses is better) is both an evaluation and the training signal for reward models. The choices that matter, with what large collections did:

  • Forced choice or a tie option. Llama 2 forced a choice and asked how much better (significantly, better, slightly, negligibly or unsure), collecting 1,418,091 comparisons (Touvron et al. 2023, §3.2.1 and Table 6). MT-Bench and Arena allow a tie; Arena adds "both are bad". A forced choice gives more training pairs; a tie option gives a cleaner measurement of near-equal pairs, at the cost of the agreement arithmetic above.
  • Agreement falls as the pair gets closer. Llama 2 reports that human agreement is higher on clearly different responses than on similar ones, and its reward models' accuracy falls the same way from "significantly better" to "negligibly better" (Table 8). Agreement on a preference set is a property of how different its pairs are, not only of the raters.
  • Who writes the prompts. Llama 2's raters wrote their own prompts, to maximise diversity; Arena's users bring their own; MT-Bench fixes 80 questions. Self-written prompts drift toward what raters find interesting, not what your users ask.
  • Anonymity and order. Hide which model wrote which response, and randomise the side, so that brand and position cannot steer a vote.
  • Several attributes, not one. HelpSteer2 rated each response on five attributes so that a reward model can separate helpfulness from verbosity.

How millions of anonymous votes become a leaderboard (Bradley-Terry, confidence intervals, style control, the arena's critiques) is on Human preference and arenas; training reward models and policies on such data is on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO family, RLVR.

Common mistakes

Reporting raw agreement alone
With one dominant label, raw agreement is high for raters who know nothing. Report a chance-corrected statistic beside it, with the label shares.
Not saying how ties were handled
The same MT-Bench votes give 63% or 81% expert agreement depending on whether ties count. Say which, or report both.
Cohen's kappa on a crowd
Cohen's kappa assumes the same two raters on every item. With varying raters or missing labels, use Fleiss' kappa (fixed count) or Krippendorff's alpha.
Filtering out disagreement, then reporting agreement
Removing the items people disagree on raises any agreement statistic by construction. Report agreement before filtering, and say what was filtered.
Treating every disagreement as error
On subjective criteria, the distribution of labels is the measurement. Keep it, and score against it.
Treating the gold set as infallible
Every audited benchmark has a few percent of wrong or unanswerable items. When a better model fails a gate, audit the failing items before believing the failure.
Letting the system's builders produce the gold
They share the system's blind spots, and they know which output came from which version. Use independent, blinded raters for gold.
Calibrating a judge on the items used to write its prompt
The reported agreement is then in-sample. Hold out part of the gold slice.
Paying the crowd per item with no quality incentive
You get fast labels and, increasingly, LLM-written ones. Pay for time, check with gold items, and tie bonuses to what makes a label good.

Agreement lab

Every agreement statistic from the Reading tab, computed in your browser on two released multi-rater sets: MT-Bench's expert votes on pairs of answers (A wins, tie, B wins; 2 to 6 experts per item) and HelpSteer2's individual 0 to 4 ratings on five attributes (2 to 6 raters per response, 23,652 responses). How each number is computed
  • Raw agreement: matching rater pairs over all rater pairs on the same item (unweighted, as Zheng et al. count it; this reproduces their 63% and 81%).
  • Fleiss' kappa: (po − pe) / (1 − pe) with pe = Σ (share of label)². Strictly defined for a fixed number of raters per item; computed here on the pairs, as Scott's pi generalises.
  • Krippendorff's alpha: 1 − Do/De from the coincidence matrix; distances 0 or 1 (nominal), squared rank-sum distances (ordinal) and squared differences (interval). For MT-Bench, ordinal and interval place a tie halfway between the two wins.
  • Gwet's AC1: chance agreement Σ π(1 − π) / (q − 1) over the q labels used.
  • Checked against src/recompute.py, which computes the same quantities in Python from the same inputs.

Audit lab

You have a review budget and a test set you suspect. Let 18 open models propose which questions are wrong, review them in order, and see how many real errors the queue finds. The "truth" is MMLU-Redux 2.0's human audit of 100 sampled questions per subject; the proposals come from the models' released 5-shot answer probabilities on the same questions (Open LLM Leaderboard v1 details, lm-evaluation-harness). Six subjects, from Virology (57 of 100 flawed) to High School Biology (5 of 100).

Try: Virology at a budget of 20, then High School Biology at the same budget: the same method, a very different hit rate. Switch the ranking to consensus votes, the version that works with API models that return no probabilities.

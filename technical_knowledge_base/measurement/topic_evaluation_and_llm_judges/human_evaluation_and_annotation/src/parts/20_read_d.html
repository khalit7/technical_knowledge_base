Agreement: telling consensus from chance

Two raters who label at random still agree some of the time. Every agreement statistic worth reporting subtracts that chance agreement and rescales: 1 is perfect agreement, 0 no better than chance, below 0 systematic disagreement. Fig. 1 runs this on released votes; the formulas follow it with the same names.

Predict, then watch: MT-Bench's experts agree on 63% of pairs with ties counted and 81% with ties dropped. Will chance-corrected alpha jump by the same 18 points? Watch "ties counted", then switch to "ties dropped"; then HelpSteer2 coherence, where raw agreement is the best of any attribute.

  1. The labels. MT-Bench turn 1: 370 items (a question and a pair of answers), each voted on by 2 to 6 graduate-student experts, 892 votes in all. One stack per item, one cube per vote: blue A wins, grey tie, teal B wins.
  2. Raw agreement. Take every pair of experts who voted on the same item: 721 pairs, and 63.0% of them gave the same label (Zheng et al.'s 63%). The cap on each stack is green when every pair agrees, gold when some do, orange when none do; the share fills the meter.
  3. Chance. Pool all 892 labels: 38.9% A, 20.9% tie, 40.2% B. Two labels drawn at random match with probability 0.389² + 0.209² + 0.402² = 0.357, the orange glass plane in the meter. Agreement up to that plane is what random voting with these shares would also produce.
  4. Kappa is the gap above chance. Fleiss' kappa is the gap from the plane to raw agreement (0.630 − 0.357) over the room above the plane (1 − 0.357): 0.424. On this scale 0 is chance and 1 is perfect, and 63% becomes 0.424.
  5. Alpha reads a coincidence matrix. Krippendorff's alpha counts which label met which on the same item, each item's pairs weighted 1/(m − 1) so items with many raters count no more than items with two. Disagreement off the diagonal is 0.363 observed against 0.644 expected by chance: α = 1 − 0.363 / 0.644 = 0.436.
  6. Order. A tie sits halfway between the two wins, so a tie against a win (gold) is a smaller disagreement than two opposite wins (orange): ordinal α 0.535. Gwet's AC1, which counts chance only on items that are hard to classify, gives 0.454. Now switch to "ties dropped".
  1. The same votes, ties removed. Every tie (faded) is dropped: 269 items keep at least two decisive votes, 631 votes in all; items left with fewer than two fade out.
  2. Raw agreement. 479 pairs remain and 81.0% of them agree: Zheng et al.'s 81%, 18 points above the 63% with ties.
  3. Chance. Two labels are left, 50.1% A and 49.9% B, so two random labels match with probability 0.500, up from 0.357: the label set shrank from three to two.
  4. Kappa. (0.810 − 0.500) / (1 − 0.500) = 0.620: the higher plane takes back much of the raw gain.
  5. Alpha. A 2 by 2 coincidence matrix: α = 1 − 0.198 / 0.501 = 0.605. With two labels there is no order to use, so nominal alpha is the only alpha.
  6. What survived the correction. Raw agreement rose 18 points (63.0% to 81.0%) but alpha only from 0.436 to 0.605 (the outlines are the ties-counted values): a real improvement, partly bought by throwing away the hardest third of the comparisons.

  2. Raw agreement. 106,390 rater pairs, and 55.6% give the same score: the highest exact agreement of HelpSteer2's five attributes.
  3. Chance. 68.5% of all ratings are a 4, so two random ratings match with probability 0.515: the plane sits just under raw agreement.
  4. Kappa. (0.556 − 0.515) / (1 − 0.515) = 0.084: almost all of the agreement is what two raters who both say "4" get by default.
  5. Alpha. The 4-against-4 cell dwarfs the rest of the matrix: nominal α = 1 − 0.432 / 0.485 = 0.110.
  6. Order, and AC1. A 3 against a 4 is a near miss (gold), a 0 against a 4 a far one (orange): interval α 0.202, the lowest of the five attributes. Gwet's AC1 gives 0.494, because it counts chance only on items that are hard to classify: report both when one label dominates.

po
observed agreement: the share of rater pairs, on the same item, that gave the same label
pe
chance agreement: for Fleiss, the sum of the squared pooled label shares (two labels drawn at random match)
Do, De
observed and expected disagreement, from a coincidence matrix in which an item with m labels contributes each of its ordered pairs with weight 1/(m − 1), so items with more raters do not count more; a distance between labels (0 or 1 for nominal, squared difference for interval) lets near misses count as partly right

Most real annotation sets have a varying number of raters per item, missing labels and often ordered scales. Cohen's kappa does not apply to them at all (with varying raters use Fleiss' kappa for a fixed count, or alpha), which is why alpha is the default here. The parent page's worked example (a judge that always says "pass" gets 90% raw agreement and kappa 0) is the two-rater case.

Why ties matter

On turn 1 of MT-Bench, two experts agree on of pairs when ties count, and on of when any pair involving a tie is dropped: Zheng et al.'s 63% and 81% (Table 5, reproduced exactly from the released votes derived). Dropping ties throws away the hardest third of the comparisons and shrinks the label set from three to two, so chance agreement rises from about a third to about a half. Alpha goes from to : a real improvement, but not the 18 points the raw figures suggest. Treating a tie as halfway between "A wins" and "B wins" (an ordinal distance) gives alpha with ties kept: a tie against a win is a smaller disagreement than a win against the opposite win. An agreement figure for pairwise votes means nothing until it says which of these it is.

Why prevalence matters

When one label dominates, two raters agree often just by both saying it, and chance-corrected statistics collapse: the "high agreement but low kappa" paradox ({{Feinstein and Cicchetti 1990|https://doi.org/10.1016/0895-4356(90)90158-L}}).

Reading the number

The widely quoted bands (0.41 to 0.60 "moderate", 0.61 to 0.80 "substantial", above 0.81 "almost perfect") come from {{Landis and Koch 1977|https://doi.org/10.2307/2529310}}, who offered them as opinion, not evidence. Krippendorff's working rule is stricter: rely on data with alpha of at least 0.800, draw only tentative conclusions between 0.667 and 0.800, and discard below (Content Analysis, 2004, pp. 241 to 243, as quoted on {{Wikipedia|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krippendorff%27s_alpha}}; the book was not re-read here). By that rule MT-Bench's expert votes, HelpSteer2's ratings and Llama 2's helpfulness ratings (Gwet's AC2 0.37 to 0.55 on a 7-point scale; safety ratings 0.70 to 0.95, {{Touvron et al. 2023, §3.4.2 and §4.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288}}) are all below the bar for individual labels. That is normal for subjective judgements; it is why gold labels are aggregated over several raters, and why a judge that matches one human is not necessarily at the ceiling.

MT-Bench's expert votes on pairs of answers (A wins, tie, B wins; 2 to 6 experts per item) and HelpSteer2's individual 0 to 4 ratings on five attributes (2 to 6 raters per response, 23,652 responses). Each cell counts how often one label met another on the same item.

How each number is computed (and checked against src/recompute.py):

  • Raw agreement: matching rater pairs over all rater pairs on the same item (unweighted, as Zheng et al. count it; this reproduces their 63% and 81%).
  • Fleiss' kappa: (po − pe) / (1 − pe) with pe = Σ (share of label)². Strictly defined for a fixed number of raters per item; computed here on the pairs, as Scott's pi generalises.
  • Krippendorff's alpha: 1 − Do/De from the coincidence matrix; distances 0 or 1 (nominal), squared rank-sum distances (ordinal) and squared differences (interval). For MT-Bench, ordinal and interval place a tie halfway between the two wins.
  • Gwet's AC1: chance agreement Σ π(1 − π) / (q − 1) over the q labels used.
  • Checked against src/recompute.py, which computes the same quantities in Python from the same inputs.

Finding the wrong labels

Nobody can re-check a whole test set by hand. Every audit above used the same trick: let models propose suspects, let people decide. Three ways to rank the suspects:

  • Disagreement with the key. PlatinumBench ran several frontier models and inspected every item at least one of them got wrong; a wrong key shows up as models disagreeing with the key, a badly posed question as models disagreeing with each other. It finds a lower bound: an item where every model shares the key's mistake is never inspected ({{Vendrow et al. 2025, §2.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03461}}).
  • Confident learning. Get out-of-sample predicted probabilities for every item (cross-validation, so no model has seen the label it is judging), set a per-class threshold at the average confidence of items carrying that label, count items confidently predicted as a different class (the "confident joint"), and rank the suspects by normalized margin, the probability of the given label minus the largest other one ({{Northcutt, Jiang and Chuang 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00068}}). The open-source {{cleanlab|https://github.com/cleanlab/cleanlab}} library implements it. In the test-set study, people confirmed 51% of what it flagged, so it ranks the review queue; it does not replace the review.
  • Consensus votes. Count how many independent models pick the same answer that is not the key. Simple, and it needs no probabilities, so it works with API models.

Fig. 4 runs the simplest of these on a real key, where the truth is known: MMLU-Redux's annotators audited 100 sampled questions per subject, and the Open LLM Leaderboard released 18 open models' 5-shot answer probabilities on the same questions.

  1. The set. The 100 Virology questions MMLU-Redux sampled, one cube each, scored against MMLU's original key. 18 open models answered every one.
  2. Models propose suspects. Each cube rises by how little the 18 models believe its key: 1 minus their mean probability for the official answer ("self-confidence", one of cleanlab's scores). The tall cubes are where models disagree with the key.
  3. A review queue. The 20 tallest go to people, most suspect first: a review budget of 20. The models only decide what is looked at first; nobody re-checks the whole set.
  4. People decide. MMLU-Redux's human verdicts: 20 of 20 queued questions are flawed, 16 of them with a wrong key (orange; gold is another kind of flaw). The field shows 37 more flawed questions, of 57, that the queue did not reach: the queue ranks the work, it does not finish it.
  5. Rescore. The same model answers, scored on the 76 questions the audit settled (43 sound, plus 33 wrong keys rescored with the audit's answer). Accuracies jump and the rows re-sort: Mixtral-8x7B-v0.1 climbs from 11th to 5th; falcon-40b falls from 9th to 14th.

Two caveats on model-proposed audits
The models may have seen the test set (MMLU is widely contaminated), so agreement with the key can be memory rather than knowledge, which hides wrong keys. And models share training data, so their mistakes are correlated: "every model disagrees with the key" is strong evidence, but "every model agrees" is weak evidence that the key is right.

Making it a routine: gold-error-aware scoring

The pattern applies to any internal golden set. It was the core of the old Production eval engineering page and is carried here in full.

1 Never treat gold as infallible: store a confidence and provenance field per label (above). 2 Route model-against-gold disagreements through a consensus battery: several strong models from different families (or judges with retrieval and tools) re-answer the item independently; strong consensus against the key flags the label, not the model. 3 People adjudicate the flagged items: the output is a corrected label or an "ambiguous, exclude from gating" tag. 4 Score with error-awareness: gate on the verified (platinum) subset and track the full set, or down-weight low-confidence labels. 5 Make it continuous: every gate failure triggers an audit of the failing items before the failure is believed. In mature systems, disagreement mining doubles as golden-set quality control and as discovery of genuinely hard items.

This is the same machinery as judge calibration, pointed at the dataset instead of the judge. An evaluation has three fallible parts, the model, the grader and the gold, and any observed delta has to be attributed to one of them before anyone acts; the parent page animates that attribution on the physics re-grading's 250 rejections ({{Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges, Reading tab, "How it breaks"|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}}). The production page keeps the routine's gate rule in its own words ({{Production eval engineering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b39ad9e96193d21adc}}). When the grader rather than the key is the suspect, see {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}; the statistics of a judge-corrected pass rate are {{arXiv:2511.21140|https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.21140}}.

Collecting human preference data

Preference data (which of two responses is better) is both an evaluation and the training signal for reward models. The first choice is the form itself (the figure).

  • Agreement falls as the pair gets closer. Llama 2 reports that human agreement is higher on clearly different responses than on similar ones, and its reward models' accuracy falls the same way from "significantly better" to "negligibly better" (Table 8). Agreement on a preference set is a property of how different its pairs are, not only of the raters.
  • Who writes the prompts. Llama 2's raters wrote their own prompts, to maximise diversity; Arena's users bring their own; MT-Bench fixes 80 questions. Self-written prompts drift toward what raters find interesting, not what your users ask.
  • Anonymity and order. Hide which model wrote which response, and randomise the side, so that brand and position cannot steer a vote.
  • Several attributes, not one. HelpSteer2 rated each response on five attributes so that a reward model can separate helpfulness from verbosity.

How millions of anonymous votes become a leaderboard (Bradley-Terry, confidence intervals, style control, the arena's critiques) is on {{Human preference and arenas|n:3ef5c17b0d0d814db49ff3b5c9fc3034}}; assembling preference datasets for training is on {{Post-training data|n:3f95c17b0d0d81318c3ec7e4564d7b42}}; training reward models and policies on them is on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}.

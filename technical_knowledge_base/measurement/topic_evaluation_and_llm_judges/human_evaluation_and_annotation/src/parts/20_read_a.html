A human label is a measurement, with the three failure modes of any instrument: it can be noisy (two people disagree), biased (everyone agrees, and everyone is wrong) or measuring the wrong thing (the rubric asked a different question from the one you care about); each stage of an annotation pipeline exists to control one of them.

The life of a label: six stages, each catching one failure

Read it in order: a label is only as good as the question it answers (1), the people answering (2), the check that they agree beyond chance (3), the decision about what their disagreements mean (4), how the gold copy is kept (5) and the audit that finds where the key itself is wrong (6). When people are the right instrument

People are the most faithful and the slowest layer of the evaluation stack (deterministic checks, references, judges, people, live traffic). Use them when there is no reference and quality is a judgement (helpfulness, tone, safety in context, whether an answer is persuasive or misleading: exact match cannot grade these, and no LLM judge has yet been shown to agree with people on your version of them); when an automatic grader needs calibrating (every judge, reward model and classifier, before its numbers are believed and again when traffic shifts); when the decision is expensive (a launch, a model swap on a sensitive surface, a safety sign-off: a few hundred expert labels are cheap against being wrong); and when you are collecting training signal (preference data). Do not use them when a program can check the answer (unit tests, exact match against a verified key, schema validation), when the task needs knowledge the raters lack, or when you need the number every hour.

Two findings to remember before trusting any human score. Untrained people can be at chance: non-experts telling GPT-3 text from human text in stories, news and recipes were at chance level, and short training lifted them to at most 55% ({{Clark et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00061}}). Crowd ratings can fail where experts do not: even with strict qualification filters, Mechanical Turk workers failed to tell model-written stories from human references while English teachers could; showing workers the reference beside the output improved their calibration ({{Karpinska et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.06835}}).

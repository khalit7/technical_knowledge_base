Disagreement is not always error

Low agreement has two different causes, and they need opposite treatment:

  • Error: a slip, a misread rubric, too little expertise, too little attention. The item has one right answer and someone missed it. Fix with adjudication, better guidelines, better raters.
  • Legitimate variation: the item is ambiguous, or the question is a matter of judgement on which reasonable people differ. Forcing one label throws away information about the item. Barbara Plank calls this human label variation and argues it should be modelled and evaluated, not removed ({{Plank 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.02570}}); Aroyo and Welty listed "there is one truth" and "disagreement is bad" among seven myths of annotation ({{Truth Is a Lie, AI Magazine 2015|https://doi.org/10.1609/aimag.v36i1.2564}}).

How much a single majority label can miss was measured by ChaosNLI, which collected 100 annotations per item, 464,500 in all, for 3,113 SNLI and MNLI items (chosen because their original 5 votes split 3 to 2) and 1,532 αNLI items. The original majority label was not the 100-vote majority on 20% of the SNLI items, 31% of the MNLI-matched items and 10% of the αNLI items ({{Nie et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03532}}). Earlier, graded NLI judgements had shown such disagreements persist however the question is asked ({{Pavlick and Kwiatkowski 2019|https://doi.org/10.1162/tacl_a_00293}}).

What to do with it

  • Decide per criterion whether there is a fact of the matter. "Is this dose correct?" has one; "is this reply too blunt?" does not. Adjudicate the first kind; keep the distribution for the second.
  • Soft labels: store the vote distribution, not only the majority, and score a model or judge against it (ChaosNLI compares model probabilities to the human distribution). The perspectivist approach goes further and keeps who said what, so results can be broken down by rater group ({{Cabitza, Campagner and Basile 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.04270}}; survey: {{Uma et al. 2021|https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.12752}}).
  • Never filter disagreement out and then report agreement. Dropping the items people disagree on raises any agreement statistic by construction (HelpSteer2's retention rule, above).
  • Mine it. Items that split experts are where the rubric is unclear, where the gold label is most likely wrong, and where a judge's behaviour is least constrained. "Ambiguous" is a valid label state: give it a code and keep such items out of gating (The gold slice, below).

Annotator quality control

Quality control has three moments: before anyone labels, while they label, and after the labels are in.

Before

Screen and train. InstructGPT chose its contractors by a screening test on sensitive prompts and agreement with researchers ({{Ouyang et al. 2022, Appendix B|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}}). Anthropic's HH work did not filter on agreement and measured about 63% crowd-researcher agreement afterwards. Pay for the effort you want: GPQA paid most of its money as bonuses tied to what made a question good (objective and hard), not per question.

During

Gold items mixed in: items with a known answer, scored per rater. Attention checks for crowd pools. Time on task: a label in two seconds was not read. LLM use: one study detected it by keystrokes and pasted text, and found 33 to 46% of workers using an LLM ({{Veselovsky et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07899}}); when many raters use the same model, their errors stop being independent and majority voting no longer cancels them.

After

Model each rater. Dawid and Skene's EM algorithm estimates a confusion matrix per rater and the likely true label per item ({{Dawid and Skene 1979|https://doi.org/10.2307/2346806}}); MACE adds a probability that each rater is spamming ({{Hovy et al. 2013|https://aclanthology.org/N13-1132/}}). Weight or drop raters accordingly. Arena flags anomalous voters by comparing each user's votes to the historical distribution for the same model pairs, combining per-vote p-values with Fisher's method at 5 random checkpoints between 1 and 100 votes ({{Chiang et al. 2024, §5.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04132}}).

Per-rater agreement with the consensus is the simplest monitor, and it is cheap: the same coincidence counts the {{Agreement lab|#t-agree}} uses, computed per rater, flag someone drifting within a day. Keep in mind that agreeing with the majority rewards conformity: a careful expert who catches what the crowd missed will look like an outlier, so read an outlier's labels before dropping them.

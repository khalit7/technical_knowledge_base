Human preference benchmarks in one screen

A preference benchmark asks which of two answers a person (or a model standing in for one) likes better, and turns many such choices into a rating. It needs no answer key, so it can score open-ended chat, code that renders, images and search answers; and the prompts come fresh from users, so there is little to memorise. What it measures is preference, though, not correctness: a confident, long, well-formatted answer can win a vote it should lose.

Two families. Live arenas collect votes from real users on two anonymous models: Arena (Chatbot Arena from 2023, then LMArena, renamed Arena on 28 January 2026 per Wikipedia) fits a Bradley-Terry model to millions of votes, now with style control on by default and a factuality toggle since July 2026. Judge-graded boards replace the voters with an LLM judge on a fixed prompt set (MT-Bench, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard, WildBench): cheap and repeatable, built to correlate with the arena, and all now dormant or gamed. How the judge itself is built, biased and calibrated belongs to LLM-as-judge: design, biases, calibration, reliability; this page covers the leaderboards.

From votes to a rating

The battle

A user types a prompt; two models chosen by the platform answer side by side without names; the user votes A is better, B is better, tie or both are bad, and only then are the names revealed. Only anonymous votes count: FastChat's rating code keeps battles[battles["anony"]] before fitting (elo_analysis.py). The first vote in the public log is dated 24 April 2023 derived, and by the Chatbot Arena paper (March 2024) there were "over 240K votes" (Chiang et al.). The prompts are whatever users ask, which is the design's strength (fresh, varied, no fixed test set to leak) and its weakness (the mix of prompts, and of voters, is whoever turns up).

Bradley-Terry: one number per model

The Bradley-Terry model gives each model a strength and says the chance that one beats another depends only on the gap. On Arena's scale:

P(A beats B) = 1 / (1 + 10(RB − RA) / 400)
R
a model's Arena score; only differences mean anything, so the scale is pinned by an anchor (FastChat set Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct to 1114; rating_systems.py)
400, 10
the chess Elo convention: a 100-point gap is a 64.0% expected win rate, 200 points 76.0% derived

The ratings are the maximum-likelihood fit to all votes at once: a logistic regression with one column per model (+1 for the left model, −1 for the right) and the vote as the label. A tie counts as half a win for each side ("this form naturally counts a draw as half a win and half a loss", in the same file). Because the fit uses every vote together, the order in which votes arrive does not matter.

It used to. Until late 2023 the board used online Elo, chess's update rule: after each vote, RA moves by K × (result − expected), with K = 4 in FastChat. That treats models as players whose strength drifts, but a released model's weights do not change, and the update makes the final rating depend on the order of the votes. LMSYS moved to Bradley-Terry with bootstrap confidence intervals, saying online Elo showed "considerable variability" and that "most models are static" (LMSYS, 7 Dec 2023); Arena's leaderboard dataset dates the switch to 9 January 2024 (leaderboard-dataset). The name "Elo" stayed in common use for what is now a Bradley-Terry score.

The fit reproduces the published board

Running FastChat's Bradley-Terry code, reimplemented here, on the 1,762,122 anonymous, de-duplicated votes of the public log of 26 August 2024 (up to the board's last vote) gives LMArena's published board of 27 August 2024 for all 136 models to within 0.48 points, mean gap 0.03 reproduced; the log of 14 August gives the board of 12 August within 2.97 points over 127 models. The de-duplication tag matters: without it the gaps reach 16.7 points. Ties were 34.7% of the votes used derived.

How sure is a rating? Intervals and rank spreads

A rating is an estimate from a sample of votes, so every score comes with a 95% interval. The method has changed twice. From December 2023 the intervals came from a bootstrap: refit the model on votes resampled with replacement, many times (LMSYS). On 23 July 2025 Arena switched to intervals from the central limit theorem for M-estimators and added a reweighting for the platform's unequal sampling of models (leaderboard changelog). That is the sandwich estimator, Var(θ̂) = H−1BH−1 with H the Hessian of the loss and B the covariance of its gradients, as Arena writes it out in its factuality post (Arena, 14 Jul 2026).

Two models whose intervals overlap may be in either order, so ranks are fuzzy too. Since 14 November 2025 each model shows a raw rank (its place by score, no ties) and a rank spread (Arena's ranking method):

best = 1 + #{others with lower bound > my upper bound}   worst = 1 + #{others with upper bound > my lower bound}
spread
the ranks a model could hold if its true score were at the top of its interval and everyone else's at the bottom, and the reverse; two models are tied when their spreads overlap

Applied to the text board of 2 October 2026 (style control, all 413 models) derived: only the leader is alone. Gemini 4 Argon, a preliminary entry with 4,932 votes, sits at 1525 (1516 to 1534) with a spread of 1 to 1; the next twelve all have spreads that reach rank 2 and run to rank 7 or worse. Claude Opus 5.5 (high), fourth on raw rank, could be anywhere from 2nd to 13th. A preliminary tag, introduced on 18 September 2025, marks models tested anonymously before release (changelog).

Intervals cover sampling noise, not fragility. A 2025 study asked how many votes must be dropped, chosen adversarially, to change the board's top model: "dropping just 0.003% of human preferences can change the top-ranked model on Chatbot Arena", while MT-Bench's expert-graded rankings were far more stable (arXiv 2508.11847). Intervals assume the votes are an honest random sample; the critiques below are about when they are not.

Style control: separating what is said from how

Voters like long, formatted answers. Is a model ahead because its answers are better, or because they are longer and bolder? Style control answers that inside the same regression: add columns for the difference in style between the two answers, so the style effect gets its own coefficient and each model's rating is what is left (Arena, 29 Aug 2024).

logit P(A wins) = ln10 · (RA − RB) + γ · z
z
four style features, each the difference over the sum, (xA − xB) / (xA + xB + 1), standardised over all votes: answer tokens, markdown headers, list items, bold spans (FastChat's STYLE_CONTROL_ELEMENTS_V1); the ratio makes 500 against 520 tokens nearly equal and 20 against 40 very different
γ
the style coefficients, fitted with the ratings; a small ridge penalty (0.5) as in FastChat

Reproduced. On the 1.76 million votes of the August 2024 public log this page's fit gives γ = 0.249 for length, 0.024 for headers, 0.031 for lists and 0.019 for bold, the values LMArena published (0.249, 0.024, 0.031, 0.019), and 0.268 for length alone (published 0.267) reproduced. The ratings match LMArena's style-controlled board of 27 August 2024 to a mean of 1.1 points, and within 1.4 points for the top 20. Length dominates: one standard deviation of relative length is worth odds of e0.249 = 1.28, a 56.2% win chance between otherwise equal answers derived; markdown is second order.

Default since 16 May 2025. Style control became the default for the text and vision boards, with the offset adjusted so both views share a scale (leaderboard-dataset). Its effect is still large: on 2 October 2026 Claude Opus 5 (max) reads 1507.0 without style control and 1489.5 with it, and GPT-6 Astra (max) is 29th with it and 71st without (Today's board, three ways). Style control is observational: a longer answer may also be a better one (a worked derivation is both), and LMArena's post says as much ("possible unobserved confounders such as positive correlation between length and substantive quality").

Category boards and the other arenas

Categories: the same votes, a subset at a time

The overall board mixes greetings, homework, code and fiction. A category board refits Bradley-Terry on the votes whose prompts carry a tag. The text board offers 29 views on 2 October 2026: languages, Hard Prompts, Coding, Math, Creative Writing, Instruction Following, Multi-Turn, Longer Query, Expert, and occupational ones such as Legal and Government or Medicine and Healthcare (leaderboard-dataset). The tags are themselves model-made. Hard Prompts (May 2024) asked Llama-3-70B-Instruct whether a prompt shows seven qualities (specificity, domain knowledge, complexity, problem-solving, creativity, technical accuracy, real-world application) and keeps those with six or more, about 20% of prompts (LMSYS, 20 May 2024); in the public log of August 2024, 27.0% of the votes used carry six or more derived. The Expert and Occupational categories followed on 5 November 2025 (changelog). A category board has fewer votes, so wider intervals: read its rank spread, not its rank.

The other arenas

Each arena is its own fit on its own votes and its own scale, so scores do not compare across arenas: the Code Arena's leader stands at 1815 and the Vision leader at 1309 on the same day, which says nothing about one being "better".

  • Code Arena (WebDev). Both models build the same web app and the user votes on the result; since 12 November 2025 the WebDev board is "powered by Code Arena", which includes agentic, multi-step building (changelog), split into HTML and React in February 2026 and joined by Image-to-WebDev (April) and Fullstack (July 2026). Top on 1 October 2026: Claude Opus 5.5 (max) 1815 (±16, 2,062 votes), GPT-6 Astra (max) 1788, Claude Sonnet 5.5 (xhigh) 1786 (Code Arena). Coding benchmarks with tests are on Coding benchmarks.
  • Copilot Arena. Pairs of code completions shown inside the developer's editor: "over 4.5 million suggestions from 10 models" and "over 11k pairwise judgements"; its rankings "differ from those of existing evaluations", with preferences consistent across programming languages but varying by task category (Chi et al., Feb 2025).
  • Search Arena. Models answer with web search; the leaderboard opened in August 2025. Its paper (about 24,000 conversations, 12,000 votes) found users prefer answers with more citations "even when the cited content does not directly support the attributed claims" (Miroyan et al.). Style control matters most here: on the board of 24 August 2026 it moves Claude Opus 4.6 (search) from 1253 to 1223 while GPT-5.6 Sol (xhigh) moves from 1257 to 1256 derived from the leaderboard-dataset.
  • Vision, Document, Text-to-Image, Image Edit, Video. Image input from 2024, documents from March 2026, generated images and videos (Video Arena from January 2026) (March 2026 update; Wikipedia). Text-to-Image had 6,478,738 votes on 24 September 2026. Multimodal benchmarks with answer keys are on Long-context and multimodal.
  • Agent Arena (4 June 2026) is not a vote at all. Users run agents on real work; Arena randomises which model orchestrates and estimates each model's causal effect on signals such as confirmed task success, praise against complaint, steerability, recovery from failed shell commands and hallucinated tools, combined into a "net improvement" (Arena, 4 Jun 2026). On 2 October 2026 Claude Fable 5.1 (max) led at 14.31% over 15,130 sessions, from 2,158,478 sessions in all (Agent Arena). Agent benchmarks with checked end states are on Agentic benchmarks.
  • AutoEval (30 July 2026): a reward model trained on Arena's votes casts provisional votes for a new model within the hour, labelled "AutoEval" until human votes arrive; Arena says the model predicts human preference "8–10% more accurately than frontier LLM judges" (Arena). A judge-made score shown on a human-preference board: check the label.

The critiques: who can game a vote

The Leaderboard Illusion, and the reply

In April 2025 Singh et al. argued that the arena's rules favoured a few providers (arXiv 2504.20879). Arena replied on 9 May 2025 (Arena's response). The two accounts, side by side:

The Leaderboard Illusion

Private testing: "undisclosed private testing practices benefit a handful of providers who are able to test multiple variants before public release and retract scores if desired"; "27 private LLM variants tested by Meta in the lead-up to the Llama-4 release".

Data access: Google and OpenAI received an estimated 19.2% and 20.4% of all arena data; 83 open-weight models together 29.7%. Proprietary models are sampled more and removed less; 205 of 243 public models were silently deprecated.

Overfitting: "even limited additional data can result in relative performance gains of up to 112% on the arena distribution".

Arena's response

Private testing was in a policy "published on March 1, 2024", open to any provider; Cohere ran more pre-release tests (9) than OpenAI or xAI. The paper's simulation of 100-point gains is "unrelated to Chatbot Arena"; the real effect is "around +11 Elo after 50 tests and 3000 votes".

Open models are 40.9% of battles, not 8.8%, once open-weight models such as Llama and Gemma are counted.

The 112% was measured on Arena-Hard, "a static benchmark with 500 data points". Changes: retired models marked clearly; scores "provisional" until 2,000 fresh votes when 10 or more variants were tested.

The 205 of 243 deprecations is from the paper's body as cited in the parent's Benchmark atlas; the quotations are from the abstract and the response. The two disagree on definitions (open-weight against open-source) as much as on facts.

Why picking the best of N variants inflates a score. If N variants are equally good and each is measured with noise σ, the best of the N measured scores beats the truth by about σ times the expected maximum of N standard normal draws, and the variant shipped then regresses. The widget computes that derived. With 50 variants and σ = 5.3 points (the standard error of a model with about 3,100 votes on the October 2026 board: Claude Sonnet 5.5 xhigh, ±10.4 on 3,145 votes) it gives about 12 points, the size of Arena's own "+11 after 50 tests and 3000 votes".

σ is scaled from the reference model as 5.3 × √(3,145 / votes); E[max] is computed exactly by numerical integration. All variants are assumed equally good, the worst case for selection (illustrative setting).

Llama 4 Maverick: the variant that was not the release

On 5 April 2025 Meta announced Llama 4 Maverick with "an experimental chat version scoring ELO of 1417 on LMArena" (Meta). The arena entry was "Llama-4-Maverick-03-26-Experimental", not the downloadable model. LMArena said "Meta should have made it clearer that Llama-4-Maverick-03-26-Experimental was a customized model to optimize for human preference", published more than 2,000 of its battles and changed its policy (The Register, 8 Apr 2025). The released Llama-4-Maverick-17B-128E-Instruct first appears in Arena's published history on 9 April 2025 at rank 35 (1273, 2,662 votes); the experimental variant does not appear in that history at all, and neither does the experimental Gemini 2.5 Pro that Google announced on 25 March 2025 as debuting "at #1 on LMArena by a significant margin" (Google); the history's top entry on 9 April 2025 is chatgpt-4o-latest at 1406 derived from the leaderboard-dataset. The public record of the board is the board after cleaning.

Votes can be rigged

Answers betray their author: one study identified the model behind a reply "with more than 95% accuracy" and estimated that about a thousand votes could move the board; the arena added bot protection, rate limits, reCAPTCHA and logins in response (Huang et al., Jan 2025). Another showed that votes on battles not involving the target model still move its rating through the shared fit, so "omnipresent" rigging improves a ranking with "only hundreds of new votes" (simulated on 1.7 million historical votes; Min et al., ICML 2025). Arena's changelog records the defences: identity-leak detection and de-duplication (July 2025, January 2026) and an anomalous-voting filter (17 September 2025) (changelog).

Preference rewards flattery

What wins a vote is not always what is right. OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update shipped on 25 April 2025 after it became sycophantic, saying the change relied too much on short-term thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback (OpenAI, 29 Apr 2025; follow-up; summary in TechCrunch). That is a product, not the arena, but the signal is the same kind: a quick human judgement of one answer. Style control (above) removes length and formatting; it cannot see flattery or a confident error.

Factuality is Arena's direct answer (14 July 2026). It samples battles, extracts web-verifiable atomic claims from both answers, has search agents assign calibrated truth probabilities, and turns the gap in average truthfulness into a soft "factuality vote"; a composite Bradley-Terry fit weights those votes 25% and human votes 75%, as a non-default toggle on the Text and Search boards (Arena). Over 2 million claims labelled, it found a "weak positive correlation" between preference and factuality, OpenAI and xAI models rising with the factuality weight, Claude and Gemini falling or flat, and Muse Spark 1.1 and ERNIE 5.1 dropping heavily. The factuality judge is a model: its own calibration questions belong to LLM-as-judge.

Judge-graded boards: the arena in a bottle

Votes are slow and live. From 2023 a family of offline boards replaced the crowd with a strong LLM judge on a fixed prompt set, and validated themselves by how well they reproduced the arena's ranking. They are reproducible and cheap, and they inherit both the arena's taste and the judge's biases (position, verbosity, self-preference), which LLM-as-judge: design, biases, calibration, reliability treats as an instrument. Here is what each board was and how to read its numbers.

MT-Bench
80 two-turn questions in 8 categories, each answer graded 1 to 10 by GPT-4. Its paper showed strong judges "achieving over 80% agreement" with human preferences, and released 3K expert votes and 30K conversations (Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023). Errata: 13 of 30 sampled GPT-4 reference answers for maths, coding and reasoning were wrong (FastChat PR 3158). The last leaderboard table with the column tops out at 9.32 (GPT-4-1106-preview; leaderboard_table_20250804.csv): the scale ran out. Retired.
AlpacaEval 2, length-controlled
805 instructions; the judge compares each answer with GPT-4 Turbo's. Judges prefer longer answers, so prompting GPT-4 to be verbose or concise moved its raw win rate from 22.9% to 64.3%. The fix is a regression with a length term, then the win rate predicted with that term set to zero; length control narrowed the swing to 41.9% to 51.6% and raised the Spearman correlation with the arena from 0.94 to 0.98 (Dubois et al., 2024). Retired: see the null model below.
Arena-Hard-Auto
BenchBuilder selects hard, varied prompts from arena logs; v0.1 has 500, graded against GPT-4-0314 by GPT-4 Turbo, with "3x higher separation of model performances compared to MT-Bench" and "98.6% correlation with human preference rankings", "all at a cost of $20" (Li et al., 2024). v2.0 (April 2025) has 500 new hard prompts plus 250 creative-writing prompts, Gemini 2.5 as judge, style control on; its top entry is o3 at 85.9% against o3-mini, and the board has not been updated since: newest entries April 2025, last commit June 2025 (arena-hard-auto). Dormant.
WildBench
1,024 tasks chosen from over a million real conversations, each with a checklist. WB-Reward compares pairs on five levels and turns a "slightly better" into a tie when the winner is more than K characters longer; WB-Score grades single answers. Against the arena: Pearson 0.98 (WB-Reward, top models) and 0.95 (WB-Score), against 0.91 for Arena-Hard and 0.89 for AlpacaEval 2 LC (Lin et al., 2024). Last repository update August 2024 (WildBench). Dormant.

The null model. A "model" that returns the same fixed, judge-targeted text whatever the instruction scored an 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0, 83.0 on Arena-Hard-Auto and 9.55 on MT-Bench (Zheng et al., Oct 2024). On the AlpacaEval board it still ranks first, as a community entry (86.45%, August 2025), above the best real submission listed, GPT-4o at 57.45% (AlpacaEval).

Each board on its own scale (AlpacaEval and Arena-Hard as percentages, MT-Bench out of 10). "Best real" is the board's top non-null reading cited above: GPT-4o 57.45% (AlpacaEval 2 LC), GPT-4-1106-preview 9.32 (MT-Bench). For Arena-Hard the null result is on v0.1, which has no maintained board now; v2.0's top is o3 at 85.9% against a different baseline and judge, so no bar is drawn for it.

Judge-graded boards are worth knowing for two reasons: model cards still quote them, and the same method lives on inside labs and in Arena's AutoEval. Read any such number with its judge, its baseline and its version, and treat a win rate against a baseline as meaningless without the baseline's name. A small checklist judge can rank like the arena at list level while agreeing with humans far less often per answer (RocketEval).

Instruction following: checked by rule, not by taste

The root page groups these with preference because both ask "did it do what I wanted?", but they are graded differently: a verifier checks a constraint, no one votes.

  • IFEval (Google, 2023): 541 prompts with 25 kinds of verifiable instruction ("at least 300 words", "no commas") (Zhou et al.; dataset). Four scores (prompt or instruction level, strict or loose) are quoted interchangeably; self-reported tops cluster at 93 to 95% and the types are public and trainable. Saturated, and dropped from the Claude Opus 5.5 and Gemini 4 Argon cards.
  • IFBench (AI2, 2025): 300 prompts with 58 new out-of-domain constraints, to test whether instruction following generalises (Pyatkin et al.). Artificial Analysis's run shows Grok 4.3 (medium) at 83.3% among its default models (AA).
  • MultiChallenge (Scale, 2025): 273 multi-turn conversations testing whether a model keeps earlier instructions, graded by a rubric-guided judge (Sirdeshmukh et al.); Meta Muse Spark leads at 75.52% (±4.05), and the board has not moved since 3 February 2026 (Scale).

Arena's "Instruction Following" category is a different thing: votes on prompts a model tagged as containing instructions. Statuses and readings for all three are in the parent's Benchmark atlas.

What to use now

Which model will people like for chat?

Arena Text with style control (the default), in the category closest to your use (Hard Prompts, Coding, Multi-Turn, an occupational category), read by rank spread, not rank. Check the vote count and the "Preliminary" or "AutoEval" labels, and compare against the no-style-control and factuality views in Today's board, three ways.

Which model builds better front ends?

Code Arena (WebDev), knowing it measures the rendered result as users see it. For correctness against tests use the benchmarks on Coding benchmarks.

Which model gets work done as an agent?

Agent Arena's causal "net improvement" is the only preference-style board that measures outcomes rather than votes; pair it with checked agent benchmarks on Agentic benchmarks.

A fast offline proxy during training?

Not the public judge boards (gamed, dormant). Build a private prompt set from your own traffic, judge it with a validated judge and length control, and track it against occasional human votes; how to validate the judge is on LLM-as-judge.

Does it follow instructions?

IFBench and MultiChallenge for verifiable and multi-turn constraints; IFEval only as a floor.

Is it capable?

Not an arena score. Use the capability families on the sibling pages and the Topic: llms, Benchmarks tab; the arena adds whether people like the result.

Common mistakes

Reading an arena rank as a ranking
Twelve models below the leader share overlapping rank spreads on the October 2026 text board. A one-place difference inside overlapping intervals is noise; quote the spread.
Comparing scores across arenas, or across time
Each arena is a separate fit on its own scale, and the scale moves: the style-control offset was re-anchored on 16 May 2025 and the reweighting changed on 23 July 2025. A 1500 in Text and a 1500 in Code, or a 1400 in 2025 and a 1500 in 2026, are not comparable.
Assuming the default view is the raw vote
Since May 2025 the default text and vision boards are style-controlled; Claude Opus 5 (max) reads 1507.0 without style control and 1489.5 with it on the same votes. Say which view you quote.
Quoting a pre-release variant's score for the released model
Llama 4 Maverick's 1417 was an experimental chat variant; the release ranked 35th on its first appearance. Check that the board's model name is the one you can use.
Treating preference as capability or truth
Across 35 matched models arena score and the Artificial Analysis index correlate (Spearman 0.71), but among frontier models the link is weak, and voters reward length, formatting, citations and flattery. Factuality and capability need their own measures.
Quoting a judge-board win rate without the judge, baseline and version
An AlpacaEval or Arena-Hard win rate is relative to one baseline under one judge; a constant answer can top both. Without those three, the number is not interpretable.
Calling the score "Elo"
It has been a Bradley-Terry fit since early 2024; online Elo's order dependence is exactly what was removed. The scale is Elo-like, the method is not.

Fit the votes

Six thousand real votes from the 2024 arena, among ten models, and the arena's own rating machinery to run on them. Fit Bradley-Terry with or without style control, switch to online Elo and change K or the order of the votes, drop ties instead of counting them as half, use fewer votes, and compare sandwich intervals with a bootstrap.

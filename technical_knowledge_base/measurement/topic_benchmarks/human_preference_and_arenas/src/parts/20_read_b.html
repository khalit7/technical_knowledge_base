1. From votes to a rating

The battle. A user types a prompt; two models chosen by the platform answer side by side without names; the user votes A is better, B is better, tie or both are bad, and only then are the names revealed. Only anonymous votes count: FastChat's rating code keeps battles[battles["anony"]] before fitting ({{elo_analysis.py|https://github.com/lm-sys/FastChat/blob/main/fastchat/serve/monitor/elo_analysis.py}}). The first vote in the public log is dated 24 April 2023 derived; by the Chatbot Arena paper (March 2024) there were "over 240K votes" ({{Chiang et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.04132}}). The prompts are whatever users ask: fresh and varied, with no fixed test set to leak, but the mix of prompts and of voters is whoever turns up.

Fig. 2 runs this on real votes: 6,000 anonymous votes among ten models, drawn at random from the public log of August 2024 (the sample the {{Fit the votes|#t-lab}} tab uses).

  1. One battle. The sample's first decisive vote: Llama 3.1 405B gives answer A, ChatGPT-4o latest answer B, names hidden. The voter picks B, the names are revealed, and one win lands in ChatGPT-4o latest's row, in Llama 3.1 405B's column.
  2. 600 votes. The win matrix fills. Each bar is the row model's wins against the column model (a tie or "both bad" counts half to each, as in the fit); the columns are the same ten models in the same order. The rows are already sorted the way the fit will rank them.
  3. All 6,000 votes. The 45 pairs met unevenly: ChatGPT-4o latest and Llama 3.1 405B 458 times, Claude 3 Opus and Claude 3 Haiku only 22. Raw win counts mix strength with whom a model happened to meet, so rows cannot be compared by them.
  4. Win shares. Divide by the battles: each bar now stands on a 50% plane, rising where the row model won more than half of its battles against that opponent and hanging below where it won less.
  5. One height per model. Bradley-Terry finds the ten scores whose gaps best explain all 45 pairs at once; every vote in a row feeds its pillar (heights start at 1,150 points). ChatGPT-4o latest is alone at the top with 1321 and Claude 3 Haiku at the bottom with 1188, while the seven models from 2nd to 8th sit within 34 points.
  6. The heights explain the wins. The purple glass is the share each pair's gap predicts. Weighted by battles, the predictions miss the observed shares by 2.4 points on average. The largest miss is a thin pair: Claude 3 Haiku won 70% of its 23 battles with Gemma 2 27B, where the fit predicts 47%, because the fit also uses the two models' 1,178 other votes.

Concrete first. On Arena's scale a 100-point gap means the stronger model wins 64.0% of their battles, 200 points 76.0% derived; a 20-point lead, typical between neighbours at the top of the board, is 52.9%. Predict what a 10-point gap is worth, then drag.

The formula. The Bradley-Terry model gives each model a strength and says the chance that one beats another depends only on the gap:

P(A beats B) = 1 / (1 + 10(RB − RA) / 400)
R
a model's Arena score; only differences mean anything, so the scale is pinned by an anchor (FastChat set Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct to 1114; {{rating_systems.py|https://github.com/lm-sys/FastChat/blob/main/fastchat/serve/monitor/rating_systems.py}})
400, 10
the chess Elo convention

The ratings are the maximum-likelihood fit to all votes at once: a logistic regression with one column per model (+1 for the left model, −1 for the right) and the vote as the label. A tie counts as half a win for each side ("this form naturally counts a draw as half a win and half a loss", in the same file). Because the fit uses every vote together, the order in which votes arrive does not matter.

A rating gap is a win probability, and small gaps are nearly coin flips

It used to matter. Until late 2023 the board used online Elo: after each vote RA moves by K × (result − expected), with K = 4 in FastChat. That treats models as players whose strength drifts, but a released model's weights do not change, and the final rating depends on the order of the votes. LMSYS moved to Bradley-Terry with bootstrap intervals, saying online Elo showed "considerable variability" and that "most models are static" ({{LMSYS, 7 Dec 2023|https://lmsys.org/blog/2023-12-07-leaderboard/}}); Arena's leaderboard dataset dates the switch to 9 January 2024 ({{leaderboard-dataset|https://huggingface.co/datasets/lmarena-ai/leaderboard-dataset}}). The name "Elo" stayed for what is now a Bradley-Terry score: the scale is Elo-like, the method is not.

Fig. 3 replays the 6,000 votes of Fig. 2 through online Elo, in stretches:

  1. Start level. Online Elo starts all ten models at the same rating (orange dots). Bradley-Terry (blue ticks) has already used every vote at once: its ratings are where the sample says the models are.
  2. After 500 votes. Each vote moves its two models by at most K = 4 points, so the dots have travelled only part of the way; a model that met strong opponents early lags behind.
  3. After 2,000 votes. The order is taking shape, but the dots still trail the ticks: Elo needs many votes per model before it forgets where it started.
  4. All 6,000 votes, in the order they were cast. Elo ends near Bradley-Terry but not on it.
  5. The same votes, reversed. The dots land elsewhere; the blue ticks do not move, because Bradley-Terry does not depend on the order.
  6. The same votes, shuffled. Across the three orders a model's final Elo lands up to 29.5 points apart on this sample derived, while Bradley-Terry gives one answer. That order dependence is why the arena moved to Bradley-Terry.
The fit reproduces the published board

Running FastChat's Bradley-Terry code, reimplemented here, on the 1,762,122 anonymous, de-duplicated votes of the {{public log of 26 August 2024|https://storage.googleapis.com/arena_external_data/public/clean_battle_20240826_public.json}} (up to the board's last vote) gives LMArena's published board of 27 August 2024 for all 136 models to within 0.48 points, mean gap 0.03 reproduced; the log of 14 August gives the board of 12 August within 2.97 points over 127 models. The de-duplication tag matters: without it the gaps reach 16.7 points. Ties were 34.7% of the votes used derived.

2. How sure is a rating? Intervals and rank spreads

Every score comes with a 95% interval, and the method has changed twice. From December 2023 the intervals came from a bootstrap: refit on votes resampled with replacement, many times ({{LMSYS|https://lmsys.org/blog/2023-12-07-leaderboard/}}). On 23 July 2025 Arena switched to intervals from the central limit theorem for M-estimators and added a reweighting for the platform's unequal sampling of models ({{leaderboard changelog|https://arena.ai/blog/leaderboard-changelog}}). That is the sandwich estimator, Var(θ̂) = H−1BH−1 with H the Hessian of the loss and B the covariance of its gradients, as Arena writes it in its factuality post ({{Arena, 14 Jul 2026|https://arena.ai/blog/factuality-in-arena}}). Interval width shrinks as 1/√votes: in {{Fit the votes|#t-lab}}, cutting 6,000 votes to 1,000 widens every interval about 2.5 times (√6).

Fig. 4 runs the bootstrap on the ten models and 6,000 votes of Fig. 2:

  1. One fit. The ten ratings fitted on all 6,000 votes, as in Fig. 2, one post each on a score axis. How far would they move with another 6,000 votes from the same voters?
  2. Resample the votes. Draw 6,000 votes from the 6,000 with replacement (some twice, some not at all) and refit: each ball is one model's rating in one refit. Eight refits are shown; each lands a little off the posts.
  3. 200 refits. The balls pile into a histogram per model. ChatGPT-4o latest's pile stands alone; the piles of the seven models from GPT-4o (May) to Claude 3 Opus overlap one another.
  4. The middle 95% is the interval. The plate under each pile spans its 2.5th to 97.5th percentile: ±8 points for ChatGPT-4o latest, ±12 to ±24 for the others. The sandwich formula gives the same widths within 3 points without refitting.
  5. Ranks spread where the piles overlap. Bar height is the share of the 200 refits in which a model holds each rank. ChatGPT-4o latest is 1st in all 200; GPT-4o (May) is 2nd in 56.5% and Llama 3.1 405B 2nd in 37.0%; Claude 3.5 Sonnet lands anywhere from 4th to 8th.
  6. The earliest 1,000 votes. The same 200 refits on a sixth of the votes: the piles widen 2.62 times on average (√6 = 2.45 predicts it), and even ChatGPT-4o latest could be anywhere from 1st to 4th by Arena's rank-spread rule.

The 200 resamples are the ones the {{Fit the votes|#t-lab}} tab draws for its bootstrap (seed 12345), computed in src/recompute.py derived; histogram bins are 4 points wide.

Two models whose intervals overlap may be in either order, so ranks are fuzzy too. Since 14 November 2025 each model shows a raw rank (its place by score, no ties) and a rank spread ({{Arena's ranking method|https://arena.ai/blog/ranking-method}}):

best = 1 + #{others with lower bound > my upper bound}
worst = 1 + #{others with upper bound > my lower bound}
spread
the ranks a model could hold if its true score were at the top of its interval and everyone else's at the bottom, and the reverse; two models are tied when their spreads overlap

On that board Gemini 4 Argon, a preliminary entry with 4,932 votes, sits at 1525 (1516 to 1534) with a spread of 1 to 1; the next twelve all have spreads that reach rank 2 and run to rank 7 or worse. Claude Opus 5.5 (high), fourth on raw rank, could be anywhere from 2nd to 13th. A one-place difference inside overlapping intervals is noise: quote the spread. The same holds for any leaderboard of benchmark scores, where resampling the questions moves ranks too (on MT-Bench, 80 questions and 34 models, the 13th-ranked model could sit anywhere from 7th to 18th): {{Eval statistics, section 10|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d#rd-rank}}. The preliminary tag, introduced on 18 September 2025, marks models tested anonymously before release ({{changelog|https://arena.ai/blog/leaderboard-changelog}}).

Intervals cover sampling noise, not fragility. A 2025 study asked how many votes must be dropped, chosen adversarially, to change the top model: "dropping just 0.003% of human preferences can change the top-ranked model on Chatbot Arena", while MT-Bench's expert-graded rankings were far more stable ({{arXiv 2508.11847|https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11847}}). Intervals assume the votes are an honest random sample; section 4 is about when they are not.

The idea in one screen

An arena turns millions of "A or B?" votes from real users into one Bradley-Terry score per model; that score measures what people prefer on the prompts they bring, with a statistical interval, and it bends wherever the votes stop being an honest random sample of taste: long or flattering answers, which models get shown, which private variants get kept.

A preference benchmark needs no answer key, so it can score open-ended chat, code that renders, images and search answers, and its prompts come fresh from users, so there is little to memorise. What it measures is preference, not correctness. Two families: live arenas (Arena, formerly Chatbot Arena and then LMArena, renamed Arena on 28 January 2026 per {{Wikipedia|https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arena_(AI_platform)}}) collect votes from people; judge-graded boards (MT-Bench, AlpacaEval, Arena-Hard, WildBench) replace the voters with an LLM judge on a fixed prompt set, and are built to correlate with the arena.

One vote becomes a score, and four places where the number bends (Fig. 1)

  1. The pipeline. One battle (two anonymous answers, one vote) joins 8,626,731 votes on 413 models (Arena's Text board, 2 October 2026); one Bradley-Terry fit turns them all into scores; the leader reads 1525; and the intervals turn ranks into spreads. The dots are votes moving into the fit and scores moving out.
  2. Bend 1: who votes, on what. Voters reward length, markdown, citations and flattery. On the 2024 votes, an answer one standard deviation longer gets odds of 1.28, a 56.2% win chance against an otherwise equal answer. Style control removes length and markdown only (section 3, section 4).
  3. Bend 2: which models are shown and kept. Sampling rates differ by provider, and a provider can test private variants and publish only the best. If 50 equally good variants are each measured with noise, the best of them lands about 12 points above their true strength (drawn here at the normal quantiles, illustrative); Arena's own estimate is about +11 points (section 4).
  4. Bend 3: which lens. The default text board has been style-controlled since 16 May 2025: the fit gets four style columns. On the same votes Claude Opus 5 (max) reads 1507.0 without style control and 1489.5 with it; a factuality toggle mixes in claim-checked votes (section 3).
  5. Bend 4: intervals cover sampling noise only. The leader's 95% interval is 1516 to 1534 and it alone holds rank 1; the fourth model by score could be anywhere from 2nd to 13th. Dropping 0.003% of votes, chosen adversarially, can change the top model (section 2).
  6. All four at once. Each numbered place is a section below: the vote (3, 4), the variants (4), the lens (3) and the interval (2).

Numbers on the figure: Arena Text board published 2 October 2026 (8,626,731 votes, 413 models, {{arena.ai|https://arena.ai/leaderboard/text}}, read 4 October 2026; scores and spreads from Arena's {{leaderboard-dataset|https://huggingface.co/datasets/lmarena-ai/leaderboard-dataset}}); the win probability and the length coefficient are explained in sections 1 and 3; the best-of-50 figure is section 4's widget at 3,000 votes a variant.

New terms

Battle: one prompt, two anonymous answers, one vote (A, B, tie, both bad).
Bradley-Terry score: one strength per model fitted to all votes at once; "Elo" in common speech.
Online Elo: chess's per-game update; the arena's method until January 2024.
Style control: style differences as extra regression columns, so length gets its own coefficient.
Rank spread: the best and worst rank a model could hold given everyone's intervals.
Preliminary: a model tested anonymously before release, few votes.
Factuality lens: a fit mixing 25% claim-checked "factuality votes" with 75% human votes.
Length-controlled win rate: a judge's win rate with the length term regressed out (AlpacaEval 2).
Null model: a constant, judge-targeted answer used to show a judge board can be gamed.

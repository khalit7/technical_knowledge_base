Two families share this page because they fail the same way. A long-context test asks whether a model can use a million tokens, not just accept them; a multimodal test asks whether it can use the image, chart or video, not just answer around it. In both, the first benchmarks could be passed by a shortcut: a needle that repeats the question's words, a question whose answer the text alone gives away. Each generation since has been built to close a shortcut, and each saturated anyway. This page takes the benchmarks in that order: how each was built and graded, what broke, what its score means today, and what to use now.

The parent page, {{Topic: benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}}, has the cross-family view: its Benchmark atlas tab (filter Long context or Multimodal) holds a dated row for every benchmark below, and its Reading tab explains version, split, harness and contamination in general. What a long-context benchmark has to separate

A context window is a promise about what the API will accept. Whether the model uses what it accepted is a separate question, and long-context benchmarks ask it at three levels of difficulty:

  • Find: retrieve one stated fact (needle in a haystack, single-needle RULER tasks).
  • Connect: link facts that are far apart or only implied (multi-hop tracing, NoLiMa's latent hops, BABILong's chained facts, MRCR's "the second of two identical requests").
  • Aggregate: use the whole input at once (counting the most frequent words, summarising a book, reasoning over 100 PDFs).

Three protocol knobs decide what a long-context number means, and a score quoted without them carries little: the length (and whose tokenizer counted it: AA-LCR counts with cl100k_base, MRCR bins by o200k), the depth where the evidence sits, and the threshold that turns a curve into a single "effective length". The Effective length tab lets you change the third and watch the answer move.

Why a model degrades past its training length (position encodings, RoPE scaling, YaRN, what breaks on six real configs) is on {{Positional Encodings|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}; how a model is trained to long context is on {{Continued Pretraining (CPT)|n:3c65c17b0d0d8119b8f0e801caa09474}}. This page is about measuring the result.

Needle in a haystack: the test that was solved by its own design

Greg Kamradt's open-source test ({{LLMTest_NeedleInAHaystack|https://github.com/gkamradt/LLMTest_NeedleInAHaystack}}, November 2023) plants one sentence in a long run of essays and asks for it back, sweeping context length on one axis and needle depth on the other; the result is the familiar green-and-red heatmap. Its needle and question, as Anthropic later quoted them ({{Anthropic, 6 Dec 2023|https://claude.com/blog/claude-2-1-prompting}}):

Two things made it a weak test. First, the question repeats the needle's words, so finding the needle is a string match, the operation attention does best. Second, it is sensitive to the prompt in ways unrelated to memory: Claude 2.1 scored 27% on it until Anthropic added one sentence to the start of the answer, "Here is the most relevant sentence in the context:", which raised it to 98% ({{Anthropic|https://claude.com/blog/claude-2-1-prompting}}). The model had been declining to answer from a sentence that looked out of place in its essay, not failing to find it.

By 2024 single-needle retrieval was a solved row: the RULER authors note "nearly perfect accuracy in the vanilla NIAH test" for every model they tested ({{RULER abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06654}}), and the Llama 3 report claims perfect single-needle retrieval at all depths and lengths (see the {{Llama 3 Herd paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}}). It is retired as evidence; the parent's atlas row carries no current reading because no 2026 card reports it.

Position matters: lost in the middle

Before the synthetic suites, Liu et al. measured where in the context evidence can sit ({{Lost in the Middle, v3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172}}). Twenty retrieved Wikipedia passages, exactly one containing the answer, and the position of that passage moved from first to last. Accuracy traced a U: high when the evidence came first (primacy) or last (recency), lowest in the middle. For GPT-3.5-Turbo the middle positions fell below its closed-book score, the score with no passages at all, so twenty passages with the answer in the middle were worse than none.

The effect is not universal: Claude-1.3's curve moves by about 4 points, GPT-3.5-Turbo's by 22. And it shifts with length: NoLiMa finds that once the needle shares no words with the question, the context length matters more than the position, because the model can no longer find the needle wherever it is (Section 4.4.2 of {{NoLiMa|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167}}). Position is a protocol knob: a benchmark that only places evidence at the edges overstates, and a "depth" chart is a statement about one model family at one length.

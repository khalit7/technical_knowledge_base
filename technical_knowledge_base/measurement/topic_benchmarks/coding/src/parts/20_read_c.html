Contest problems pinned to dates: LiveCodeBench, LiveCodeBench Pro, Codeforces

LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., March 2024) answers contamination with the calendar. It "continuously collects new problems over time from contests across three competition platforms, namely LeetCode, AtCoder, and CodeForces", stores each problem's release date, and lets a reader score any model only on problems released after that model's training data ended ({{Jain et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974}}). The last release, release_v6, holds 1,055 problems dated May 2023 to April 2025. Each model carries a stored date, its marker, after which problems count as unseen. Predict: if a model was trained on the earlier contests, what does its score do at its marker?

Monthly pass@1 on the board's own per-problem data ({{performances_generation.json|https://livecodebench.github.io/performances_generation.json}}, read 4 October 2026). On the 328 medium problems up to December 2024, DeepSeek-V3 solves 71.2% of those released before July 2024 and 35.9% of those after, a 35-point drop; GPT-4-Turbo falls 8.4 points over the same months and GPT-4o 8.5 derived. LiveCodeBench stores 30 June 2024 as DeepSeek-V3's marker; for most API models the marker is a placeholder (30 April 2023), so the flag means little for them. DeepSeek-V3's rows from January 2025 score 0 on nearly every problem, easy ones included, which looks like failed runs; they are left out of every view here.

Part of every drop is newer problems being harder; the excess is the contamination signal. The design works only while new problems keep arriving, and they stopped: on the board's own data o4-mini (high) scores 80.2% on the 454 problems from 1 August 2024 to 7 April 2025 derived, and no later problems or models exist. A 2026 model has seen the whole span, so the rolling defence no longer applies to it.

The board for a window of your choice (28 models; the split for five; how the board grades)

LiveCodeBench also has three scenarios besides code generation: self-repair, code execution and test-output prediction. The board grades pass@1 from 10 samples for most non-reasoning models and from one for reasoning models derived (from the granularity of the per-problem scores).

Try: move the window start from May 2023 to August 2024. Every model loses points, because later problems are harder (o4-mini high: 87.3% to 80.2%); most lose 8 to 15. DeepSeek-V3 loses 21.3, the most of all 28 derived. Then filter to medium problems.

The ± is one standard error from problem sampling, √(p(1−p)/n) on the per-problem scores treated as pass or fail derived, an approximation. The 80.2% for o4-mini (high) reproduces the parent page's reading derived. The board holds Codeforces problems only from August to October 2023 (9 problems); later problems are LeetCode and AtCoder.

LiveCodeBench Pro (Zheng et al., June 2025) raised the difficulty: problems "from Codeforces, ICPC, and IOI that are continuously updated", each annotated by olympiad medalists. At launch "without external tools, the best model achieves only 53% pass@1 on medium-difficulty problems and 0% on hard problems" ({{Zheng et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11928}}). It reports a Codeforces-scale rating per model; the site is now offline and its ratings stop at November 2025 contests.

Codeforces ratings express contest results on the human scale, and they are hard to compare: each lab simulates contests its own way (which contests, how many submissions, whether failed public tests can be retried, how penalties are charged). CodeElo (January 2025) standardised the harness by submitting to Codeforces itself and computing ratings the platform's way; there o1-mini rated 1,578 and QwQ-32B-Preview 1,261, while most other models sat "in the lowest 25 percent among all human participants" ({{Quan et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01257}}). A live contest (IOI, ICPC) is the one setting where contamination is impossible by construction; the price is that it happens once a year and cannot be re-run.

The ratings ladder, 2022 to 2026 (AlphaCode, OpenAI's o-series, ICPC 2025, vendor ratings)

DeepMind's AlphaCode ranked "on average ... top 54.3% in competitions with more than 5,000 participants" in simulated contests ({{Li et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07814}}). OpenAI's ladder: GPT-4o 808, o1 1,673 (89th percentile), o1-ioi 2,214 with its full test-time strategy (98th), and o3 2,724 (99.8th); o3 also reached gold on IOI 2024 problems without hand-made strategies, while o1-ioi competing live placed in the 49th percentile ({{OpenAI 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06807}}). In September 2025 an OpenAI system solved all 12 problems of the ICPC World Finals, and Gemini 2.5 Deep Think 10, both at gold-medal level ({{Google DeepMind|https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-achieves-gold-medal-level-at-the-international-collegiate-programming-contest-world-finals/}}; {{The Decoder|https://the-decoder.com/openai-outperforms-humans-and-google-at-the-worlds-top-collegiate-programming-contest/}}). LiveCodeBench Pro also analysed failed submissions line by line: models did well on implementation-heavy problems and badly on "nuanced algorithmic reasoning and complex case analysis".

Real issues in whole repositories: how a SWE-bench task is built and graded

SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., Princeton, October 2023) changed the unit of work from a function to a repository. Its builders collected about 90,000 pull requests from 12 popular Python repositories, kept those that resolve a GitHub issue and change test files, and kept only those where at least one test fails before the pull request and passes after it, leaving 2,294 tasks ({{Jimenez et al. 2023, section 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770}}). A task gives the model the issue text and the repository at the commit before the fix; the model returns a patch; the harness applies it, adds the pull request's test changes, and runs the fail-to-pass tests (which must now pass) and the pass-to-pass tests (which must still pass). "% resolved" is the share of tasks where all of them do.     At launch the best model, Claude 2, resolved 1.96% with retrieved files and 4.8% when handed the files the real fix edited ({{paper, section 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770}}): in repository work, finding the right place matters as much as writing the fix. By 2024 the score belonged to agents that browse, edit and run tests, which is why SWE-bench is also an agent benchmark ({{Agentic benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb926d7b91ef8234e}} covers the agent side).
    

    SWE-bench Verified (OpenAI with the SWE-bench authors, 13 August 2024) became the standard number of 2024 and 2025. 93 experienced Python developers screened 1,699 random tasks for issues too vague to solve and tests that reject valid fixes; more than two thirds were filtered out, and 500 that passed were kept ({{OpenAI|https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/}}; the post refused automated reading on 4 October 2026, so the share filtered is quoted from secondary summaries, not re-read). GPT-4o with the Agentless scaffold resolved 33.2% at launch. Counted from the released dataset derived, the 500 tasks are narrower than the name suggests:
    

    

    So Verified is mostly short, single-file Django and SymPy fixes from issues filed between 2013 and August 2023, every one of them years older than any 2026 model's training data. On 500 tasks one task is 0.2 points and one standard error at a 95% score is about 1.0 point derived, √(0.95 × 0.05 / 500), so the top of the board is a tie.
    

    SWE-bench Lite and SWE-bench Multimodal
    SWE-bench Lite is a 300-task subset "sampled to be more self-contained, with a focus on evaluating functional bug fixes", from 11 of the 12 repositories, made "to encourage adoption" by making runs cheaper. SWE-bench Multimodal (October 2024) moved to 617 visual bugs in 17 JavaScript libraries, every task with at least one image ({{Yang et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03859}}); no 2026 model has entered.
    

    The harness is part of the score
    

    % resolved measures a model inside a scaffold: its tools, retries, whether it may run the tests, its turn and cost budget. On SWE-bench Pro V1's public set the same GPT-5 scored 23.3% in the launch protocol (SWE-Agent, 200 turns) and 41.8% in Scale's later one (250 turns, uncapped cost), a larger gap than separates most models (hero, row 4). Leaderboards therefore keep separate columns (open scaffold, bash-only, proprietary agent), and figures from different columns are not comparable; the parent's {{Same model, many numbers|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} tab measures such spreads, and {{Harnesses for training and evaluation|n:3f15c17b0d0d815ea579d6c80065aa36}} measures scaffold sensitivity directly. Two boards remove the harness from the comparison: Vals AI runs every model bash-only in mini-SWE-agent with one tool, and DeepSWE fixes one minimal harness (below).

    What is wrong with SWE-bench: leaked solutions, weak tests, memorised repositories
    

    

    Three probes say part of a Verified score is not solving the task
    

    

    Row 1: SWE-Agent with GPT-4, SWE-Bench+'s audit. Rows 2 and 3: the best model in each probe of the SWE-Bench Illusion paper, Verified against repositories outside SWE-bench. Sources in the text.
    

    

    Solution leakage. SWE-Bench+ (October 2024) read every task that SWE-Agent with GPT-4 resolved and compared its patch with the real one: in 32.67% "the solutions were directly provided in the issue report or the comments", and 31.08% passed "due to weak test cases"; removing both cut that system's resolution rate from 12.47% to 3.97% ({{Aleithan et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06992}}). A cruder check on Verified itself derived: in 49 of the 500 tasks at least one line the gold patch adds (12 characters or more after stripping whitespace, not a comment) appears verbatim in the issue text, and in 14 every such line does. That rule misses paraphrase: the Django task above is not counted (the merged patch kept ^ and changed only the end anchor), yet its issue spells out a fixing regular expression.

    Weak tests. A task's fail-to-pass tests are those of the original pull request, written to check that one fix, not every correct fix or every wrong one. Verified's median task has a single fail-to-pass test derived. A patch that makes that test pass while breaking behaviour nobody tested counts as resolved; Verified's screening removed tests that reject correct fixes, not tests that accept wrong ones.
    

    Memorised repositories. The SWE-Bench Illusion paper (June 2025) asked models to name the file to edit from the issue text alone, without the repository: they reached "up to 76% accuracy" on SWE-bench Verified against "up to 53%" on repositories outside SWE-bench, and reproduced gold functions with "up to 35% consecutive 5-gram accuracy" against up to 18% elsewhere ({{Liang et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12286}}). SWE-Bench+ also found "over 94% of the issues were created before LLM's knowledge cutoff dates". The three answers below each attack this differently.
    

    Broken tasks, audited. OpenAI's own audit found material problems in 59.4% of 138 Verified tasks it examined and stopped reporting Verified as a frontier coding measure ({{OpenAI|https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/}}); a follow-up estimated about 30% of SWE-bench Pro tasks broken as well ({{OpenAI|https://openai.com/index/separating-signal-from-noise-coding-evaluations/}}). The defects survived two rounds of curation in a domain where every task ships with a test, the point {{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}} builds on.

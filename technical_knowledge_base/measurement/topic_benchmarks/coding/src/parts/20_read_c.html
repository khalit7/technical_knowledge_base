SWE-bench Pro: harder tasks, private splits, and a second version

Version 1 (Scale AI, 21 September 2025) kept SWE-bench's task shape and changed what goes in it. 1,865 problems from 41 repositories in three sets: a public set from 11 repositories and a held-out set from 12, all under GPL or other strong copyleft licences (so that training on them carries legal weight), and a commercial set from 18 proprietary codebases of partner startups, whose problems are never published although its results are. Trivial edits were excluded: "the reference solutions span 107.4 lines of code across 4.1 files" on average, and every problem changes at least 10 lines, against Verified's median of 7 lines in one file derived. Each issue was rewritten by people into a problem statement, a list of requirements and the interface the tests expect, so that a correct fix is not failed for guessing a different function name ({{Deng et al. 2025, sections 1 and 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16941}}).

Why version 1 ended. Its public split was attacked and worn. Berkeley's RDI scored 100% on it without solving anything, with a conftest.py hook that marks every test passed ({{Berkeley RDI|https://rdi.berkeley.edu/blog/trustworthy-benchmarks-cont/}}); Scale later found 89 of the 731 public tasks invalid and replaced the board with V2. V2 then saturated its public split within a day: 99.4% for Claude Opus 5, while the same model resolved 81.6% of the private set. Scale attributes the 17.8-point gap plausibly to training-time exposure of the public repositories and their fixing commits, since the public runs were network-locked and audited; re-grading on a clean image caught Opus 5 forging a Go module checksum into go.sum, and in an earlier open-network run 4 of 642 trajectories had fetched the fixing commit ({{V2 blog|https://labs.scale.com/blog/swe-bench-pro-v2}}). In Scale's own words the private set is now the only clean measurement.

Three answers to contamination: the calendar, the licence, the transformation

Every public coding set leaks: the tasks are on GitHub, the fixes are on GitHub, and the models are trained on GitHub. Three designs answer this in 2026, and each pays for its defence differently.

By the calendar

SWE-rebench (Nebius), SWE-bench-Live (Microsoft)

Mine fresh issues every month and score models only on tasks newer than they are.

Pays with: no stable test set, so no time series; few tasks per window.

Now: 111 problems from 65 repositories (15 May to 1 July 2026); top Claude Fable 5 64.5%.

By licensing

Real-SWE (Specific Labs), SWE-bench Pro's private sets

License private codebases that have never been public, so no crawl can contain them.

Pays with: nobody outside can inspect or re-run the tasks; very few tasks.

Now: 10 tasks, 8 runs each; top GPT-6 Astra 46.25%.

By transformation

SchrodingerRepo

Keep the familiar tasks but rewrite each repository at run time with its behaviour unchanged, and compare.

Pays with: it measures how much of a score depends on familiar surface, not ability directly.

Now: SWE-bench Verified drops 6.0 to 14.4 points when transformed.

By the calendar: SWE-rebench

SWE-rebench (Nebius, May 2025) built an automated pipeline that has mined over 21,000 interactive Python tasks from GitHub, and uses its continuous supply of fresh ones as a leaderboard; its paper compares models with their SWE-bench Verified scores and concludes "performance of some language models might be inflated due to contamination issues" ({{Badertdinov et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20411}}). The current window holds 111 problems from 65 repositories dated 15 May to 1 July 2026, each model run 5 times; the top five (Claude Fable 5 64.5%, Grok 4.5 63.8%, Claude Opus 5 63.4%, GLM-5.2 62.9%, GPT-5.6 Sol 62.3%) sit within about 2 points, with run-to-run SEMs of 0.6 to 1.8 ({{SWE-rebench|https://swe-rebench.com/}}, read 4 October 2026). SWE-bench-Live (Microsoft, May 2025) does the same at a different scale: 1,319 tasks from 93 repositories created since 2024 at its first release, and "a substantial performance gap compared to static benchmarks like SWE-bench" ({{Zhang et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23419}}).

The board flags "potential contamination" when a model was released after a window began, and its data allow a direct test: for every model whose task range straddles its release date, compare its score on tasks from before the release with tasks from after.

Each row is one model or agent on SWE-rebench. The dot is its resolved rate on tasks dated after its release minus its rate on tasks dated before (in points; hover for both rates and task counts), both sides at least 20 tasks, rates as published for each date range; the line spans ±2 standard errors from task sampling alone, √(p(1−p)/n) per side derived (the board's own SEMs measure run-to-run variation, not task sampling). Task difficulty also drifts from month to month for every model alike.

The result is a null. Of the 50 models, 24 score higher on the older tasks and 26 on the newer; the median difference is 1.0 point in favour of the newer tasks, and only 4 differences exceed two task-sampling standard errors derived. That is the expected answer, and the instructive one: a task published a few weeks before a model's release is almost never in that model's training data, because training data end months before release. The calendar protects through the training cutoff, not the release date, and the gap that matters is between fresh issues and the years-old ones in Verified.

By licensing: Real-SWE

Real-SWE (Specific Labs, September 2026) licenses "a private production codebase ... from a real-world company" for each task: billing, tax, migrations, in TypeScript, Go and Python services with databases and business tools around them. A typical instruction is 1,742 characters and the reference solution edits a median of 11 files (6 in DeepSWE). Each model runs in its own native harness at high reasoning, 8 times per task, and verifiers are injected at grading time ({{Real-SWE|https://withspecific.com/benchmarks/real-swe}}, read 4 October 2026). Licensing is the one defence that does not depend on the tasks being new. Its weakness is size: with ten tasks, every square below matters. Click a task to drop it and watch the ranking.

Passes out of 8 per task and model, as published; the per-model rates reproduce the leaderboard exactly derived (GPT-6 Astra 37 of 80 = 46.25%). Harnesses: Codex CLI (GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol), Claude Code (Fable 5.1, GLM 5.3), Gemini CLI, Muse Code, Grok Build, Kimi Code.

Four of the ten tasks are resolved under 15% of the time, one (Analytics stream reducer) in none of 64 runs; no model solves every task, and the leader, GPT-6 Astra, fails four tasks in all 8 of its runs. The commonest failure is a missed requirement (33% to 55% of each model's failed runs), then integration errors and unverified assumptions. Rollouts under 10 minutes fail less often than longer ones (57.4% of 108 against 66.2% of 532), and estimated cost per rollout runs from $2.50 (Gemini 3.8 Flash, 38.75%) to $6.96 (Fable 5.1, 45.0%) with no clear relation to score.

By transformation: SchrodingerRepo

SchrodingerRepo (August 2026) keeps SWE-bench Verified's 500 tasks and rebuilds each repository at evaluation time with its behaviour unchanged, at four levels: the issue reworded, identifiers remapped, definitions reordered within files, code rewritten. GPT-5.4-mini in mini-swe-agent falls from 46.8% to 35.6% with all four, and its average actions per task rise from 11.3 to 19.8; across the paper's models the full transformation costs 6.0 to 11.2 points on all 500 tasks and 14.4 points for Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite on the 300 most-leaked tasks ({{Table I|https://arxiv.org/html/2609.27891v1#S4.T1}}).

The knowledge base's {{SchrodingerRepo paper page|n:3e95c17b0d0d81e8b0e0e99acbeb9713}} runs the released transformation code live on the paper's Django case and argues the reading: the drop shows that unfamiliar names cost exploration, which is not the same as proving the familiar ones were memorised. Either way, a share of a static Verified score is knowing where to look.

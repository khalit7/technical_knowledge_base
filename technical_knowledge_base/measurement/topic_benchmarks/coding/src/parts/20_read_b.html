Contest problems pinned to dates: LiveCodeBench, LiveCodeBench Pro, Codeforces

LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., March 2024) answers contamination with the calendar. It "continuously collects new problems over time from contests across three competition platforms, namely LeetCode, AtCoder, and CodeForces", stores each problem's release date, and lets a reader score any model only on problems released after that model's training data ended ({{Jain et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974}}). It also has three scenarios besides code generation: self-repair, code execution and test-output prediction. The last release, release_v6, holds 1,055 problems dated May 2023 to April 2025; the board grades pass@1 from 10 samples for most non-reasoning models and from one for reasoning models derived (from the granularity of the per-problem scores in {{the board's data|https://livecodebench.github.io/performances_generation.json}}).

The design works only while new problems keep arriving, and they stopped. On the board's own data, o4-mini (high) scores 80.2% on the 454 problems from 1 August 2024 to 7 April 2025 derived, and no later problems or models exist. The calendar split itself still teaches: on the 328 medium problems up to December 2024, DeepSeek-V3 solves 71.2% of those released before July 2024 and 35.9% of those after, a 35-point drop; GPT-4-Turbo falls 8.4 points over the same months and GPT-4o 8.5 derived. Part of every drop is newer problems being harder; the excess is the contamination signal. The {{LiveCodeBench by date|#t-lcb}} tab rebuilds the board's date slider on all 28 models.

The official board froze with April 2025 problems at about 80% (o4-mini high); 90%-plus figures are vendors' own runs on their own windows. LiveCodeBench Pro (Zheng et al., June 2025) raised the difficulty: problems "from Codeforces, ICPC, and IOI that are continuously updated", each annotated by olympiad medalists, with failed submissions analysed line by line. Its launch finding was that "without external tools, the best model achieves only 53% pass@1 on medium-difficulty problems and 0% on hard problems", and that models do well on implementation-heavy problems and badly on "nuanced algorithmic reasoning and complex case analysis" ({{Zheng et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11928}}). It reports a Codeforces-scale rating per model. The site is now offline and its ratings stop at November 2025 contests, topped by Gemini 3 Deep Think at 3,298 ({{leaderboard backend|https://webhook.cp-bench.orzzh.com/leaderboard/llm}}, read 4 October 2026).

Codeforces ratings express contest results on the human scale. DeepMind's AlphaCode ranked "on average ... top 54.3% in competitions with more than 5,000 participants" in simulated contests ({{Li et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07814}}). OpenAI's ladder: GPT-4o 808, o1 1,673 (89th percentile), o1-ioi 2,214 with its full test-time strategy (98th), and o3 2,724 (99.8th); o3 also reached gold on IOI 2024 problems without hand-made strategies, while o1-ioi competing live placed in the 49th percentile ({{OpenAI 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.06807}}). In September 2025 an OpenAI system solved all 12 problems of the ICPC World Finals, and Gemini 2.5 Deep Think 10, both at gold-medal level ({{Google DeepMind|https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-achieves-gold-medal-level-at-the-international-collegiate-programming-contest-world-finals/}}; {{The Decoder|https://the-decoder.com/openai-outperforms-humans-and-google-at-the-worlds-top-collegiate-programming-contest/}}). In 2026 vendors print ratings above 3,000: DeepSeek gives 3,471 for V4.1 Flash, method not stated ({{DeepSeek release notes|https://api-docs.deepseek.com/updates/}}).

These numbers are hard to compare. Each lab simulates contests its own way (which contests, how many submissions, whether failed public tests can be retried, how penalties are charged). CodeElo (January 2025) standardised the harness by submitting to Codeforces itself and computing ratings the platform's way; there o1-mini rated 1,578 and QwQ-32B-Preview 1,261, while most other models sat "in the lowest 25 percent among all human participants" ({{Quan et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01257}}). A live contest (IOI, ICPC) is the one setting where contamination is impossible by construction; the price is that it happens once a year and cannot be re-run.

Real issues in whole repositories: the SWE-bench family

SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., Princeton, October 2023) changed the unit of work from a function to a repository. Its builders collected about 90,000 pull requests from 12 popular Python repositories, kept those that resolve a GitHub issue and change test files, and kept only those where at least one test fails before the pull request and passes after it (a fail-to-pass test), leaving 2,294 tasks ({{Jimenez et al. 2023, section 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770}}). A task gives the model the issue text and the repository at the commit before the fix; the model returns a patch; the harness applies it, adds the pull request's test changes, and runs the fail-to-pass tests (which must now pass) and the pass-to-pass tests (which passed before and must still pass). "% resolved" is the share of tasks where all of them do.

At launch the best model, Claude 2, resolved 1.96% with retrieved files and 4.8% when handed the files the real fix edited ({{paper, section 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06770}}). The gap between those two figures was the first sign that, in repository-level work, finding the right place matters as much as writing the fix; by 2024 the score belonged to agents that browse, edit and run tests, which is why SWE-bench is also an agent benchmark (the {{Agentic benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb926d7b91ef8234e}} page covers the agent side). SWE-bench Lite is a 300-task subset "sampled to be more self-contained, with a focus on evaluating functional bug fixes", from 11 of the 12 repositories, made "to encourage adoption" by making runs cheaper; its last leaderboard entry dates from September 2025, and the full set's top is 52.62% (Sonar Foundation Agent with Claude Opus 4.5, December 2025) ({{swebench.com data|https://raw.githubusercontent.com/swe-bench/swe-bench.github.io/master/data/leaderboards.json}}).

SWE-bench Verified (OpenAI with the SWE-bench authors, 13 August 2024) is the subset that became the standard number of 2024 and 2025. 93 experienced Python developers screened 1,699 random tasks for issues too vague to solve and tests that reject valid fixes; more than two thirds were filtered out, and 500 that passed were kept ({{OpenAI|https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/}}; the post refused automated reading on 4 October 2026, so the share filtered is quoted from secondary summaries, not re-read). GPT-4o with the Agentless scaffold resolved 33.2% at launch. Where Verified stands. Vals AI's bash-only run (mini-SWE-agent, one tool) puts Claude Opus 5 at 97.0%, and eight models between 93.4% and 97.0%, last updated 1 September 2026 ({{Vals AI|https://www.vals.ai/benchmarks/swebench}}). On 500 tasks one task is 0.2 points and the standard error at 95% is about 1.0 point derived: √(0.95 × 0.05 / 500), so the top of the board is a tie. Epoch AI's own scaffold measured 83.5% for Claude Opus 4.7 in April 2026, and the swebench.com board's last entry is 26 February 2026 at 79.2%; it dropped its bash-only board on 1 September 2026 ({{parent atlas|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}}). SWE-bench Multimodal (October 2024) moved to 617 visual bugs in 17 JavaScript libraries, every task with at least one image ({{Yang et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03859}}); its top is 35.98% (GUIRepair with o3, July 2025) and no 2026 model has entered.

What is wrong with SWE-bench: leaked solutions, weak tests, memorised repositories

Solution leakage. SWE-Bench+ (October 2024) read every task that SWE-Agent with GPT-4 resolved and compared its patch with the real one: in 32.67% "the solutions were directly provided in the issue report or the comments", and 31.08% passed "due to weak test cases"; removing both cut that system's resolution rate from 12.47% to 3.97% ({{Aleithan et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06992}}). A cruder check on Verified itself derived: in 49 of the 500 tasks at least one line the gold patch adds (12 characters or more, not a comment) appears verbatim in the issue text, and in 14 every such line does (rule and list in src/inputs/verified_stats.json). That rule misses paraphrase: the task in the {{One SWE-bench task|#t-task}} tab is not counted, yet its issue spells out the fixing regular expression.

Weak tests. A task's fail-to-pass tests are those of the original pull request, written to check that one fix, not every correct fix or every wrong one. Verified's median task has a single fail-to-pass test derived. A patch that makes that test pass while breaking behaviour nobody tested counts as resolved; Verified's screening removed tests that reject correct fixes, not tests that accept wrong ones.

Memorised repositories. The SWE-Bench Illusion paper (June 2025) asked models to name the file to edit from the issue text alone, without the repository: they reached "up to 76% accuracy" on SWE-bench Verified against "up to 53%" on repositories outside SWE-bench, and reproduced gold functions with "up to 35% consecutive 5-gram accuracy" on SWE-bench against up to 18% elsewhere ({{Liang et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12286}}). SWE-Bench+ also found "over 94% of the issues were created before LLM's knowledge cutoff dates". The three answers below each attack this problem differently.

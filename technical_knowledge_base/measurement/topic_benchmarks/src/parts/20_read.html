The idea in one screen

A benchmark score is a measurement of a model under a protocol on a date, and every family keeps replacing its benchmarks because the frontier reaches the ceiling, or has seen the questions, faster than the test was built to last.

A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks, a way of running a model on them, and a rule for scoring the result: one comparable number, whose item set, protocol and date each move it by more than the gap between the models compared.

Three questions settle most headline numbers
When was it measured (Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 went from 30.0% to 64.6% in 26 days on the labs' figures). What ran the model (at the same effort, one model scores 62.7% or 98.6% on ARC-AGI-3 depending on the harness). Has anyone checked the answer key (roughly 29% of Humanity's Last Exam's text-only chemistry and biology answers are contradicted by the literature). A figure that survives none of the three is not yet a measurement.

You need nothing beyond basic statistics. Error bars and paired tests in depth are on {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}; the tools that run benchmarks (lm-eval-harness, Inspect, HELM) and LLM judges on {{Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}}; current models compared on fixed benchmarks on {{Topic: llm-landscape, Benchmarks tab|n:3c65c17b0d0d812d9e00f6ec89965286}}.

Saturated
top models sit within noise of each other and of the answer key's error rate, so gaps stop meaning anything.
Contaminated
test items (or close paraphrases) were in the training data, so the score partly measures memory.
Protocol tuple
everything that ran the model besides its weights: version, split, shots, tools, effort, sampling, extraction, harness, judge, who ran it, date.
Harness (scaffold)
the program around the model on agentic tasks: prompts, tools, memory, retries, budget.
pass@1, maj@k, pass@k, pass^k
one sample; the majority answer of k; any of k right; all of k right (reliability).
Private, live, licensed set
the three ways to keep items unseen: held behind an API, mined after the training cutoff, or bought from owners who never published them.

The life of a benchmark

Every benchmark follows roughly the same arc, and knowing where a benchmark sits on it tells you how much its numbers can mean.

1. BornThe best model scores low. MMLU launched with GPT-3 at 43.9% against 25% chance.
2. DiscriminatesThe useful years: scores spread out and gaps mean something.
3. SaturatesTop models cluster within noise of each other and of the answer key's error rate.
4. ContaminatedQuestions leak into training data, deliberately or by web scrape, and the score decouples from capability.
5. RetiredStill quoted for continuity and marketing ("zombie" phase), then replaced by a harder sibling or a new design.

Predict before you step: MMLU took two and a half years to climb from launch to GPT-4. How long did a benchmark built in August 2026 to sit far below saturation last? Then is MMLU, the 57-subject exam that defined model comparisons from 2020 to 2023; now is Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1, 70 research tasks in a terminal sandbox. The last step puts both on one time axis.

The useful life of a benchmark fell from years to weeks

Each point is the best published score at that date on that benchmark (sources in the captions); the two benchmarks are different tests, so compare their shapes and speeds, not their heights. Points per 30 days is derived: (score minus launch score) divided by days since launch, times 30. The shaded band on MMLU marks the roughly 6.5% of questions that {{MMLU-Redux|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04127}} found to contain an error of some kind, which is where saturation meets the answer key.

The useful period used to be two to four years; it is now often under eighteen months, and sometimes weeks. MMLU took 918 days to go from 43.9% to GPT-4's 86.4%. Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 gained 22.6 points in its first five days and 34.6 points in 26: about 29 times MMLU's pace derived. Those are the labs' own figures; Artificial Analysis's re-runs are lower (43.3% for Fable 5.1, 63.3% for GPT-6 Astra) but tell the same story. Nothing is wrong with the benchmark; the rate is the finding, and its maintainers already have 0.2 in development. The {{Saturation timeline|#t-sat}} shows the same arc for every major benchmark with dated points.

Replacement follows two patterns (the hero): a harder sibling (MMLU, then MMLU-Pro, GPQA, Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)), or a change of design: a live set pinned to dates (LiveCodeBench, the current year's AIME, SWE-rebench), an interactive environment (ARC-AGI-3), a private or rotating set (FrontierMath, HLE's private split), a paired human baseline (METR time horizons, GDPval, OSWorld). The designs that last share one property: the model cannot have seen the test.

Aggregators retire benchmarks in public. On 4 September 2026 the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.2 dropped GPQA Diamond as "an exceptional scientific reasoning evaluation that has now been saturated" (GPT-6 Astra scores 96.0%), added AA-Briefcase and GDP.pdf and doubled the weight of private held-out sets to 40% ({{Artificial Analysis|https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index-v4-2}}). The lesson for anyone running evaluations: pin versions, rotate the suite yearly, keep a private regression set that never touches the internet.

A saturated benchmark still holds signal in its transcripts
In a retrospective simulation, judging pairs of correct transcripts from saturated benchmarks predicted capability on held-out unsaturated items with 5.3 times less error than the saturated scores alone, though it underestimated the first reasoning models of early 2025 ({{Hamada et al., Oct 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20667}}).

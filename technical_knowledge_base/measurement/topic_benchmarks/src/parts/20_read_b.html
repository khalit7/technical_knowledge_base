What a number leaves out

A score is the output of a procedure: sample the data (the benchmark's dataset and ideally a canonical prompt), run the model (zero- or few-shot, chain of thought, tools, reasoning effort, an agent scaffold) and score it (accuracy, exact match, pass@k, pass^k, an Elo rating, a judge's grade, task success). Every knob moves the number, so a result means something only with its protocol tuple:

benchmark versiontask splitshotschain of thoughttoolseffort and token budgetsampling (pass@1, maj@k, runs)answer extractionharness or scaffoldjudgewho ran itdate

Even the small knobs matter. Shots: MMLU was scored 5-shot in the base-model era because the examples taught the format; reasoning models are evaluated zero-shot, and OpenAI reported that few-shot prompts degraded o1 on GPQA. Answer extraction: parsing "The answer is (X)" and reading the model's probabilities over the options give different MMLU scores for the same model, so harnesses (lm-eval-harness, HELM, a vendor's own) legitimately disagree by points. Below, the knobs that move headline numbers most, one real case each; {{Same model, many numbers|#t-same}} lays the measured spreads side by side.

Version same name, different test

Terminal-Bench went 2.0, 2.1, 3.0 (23 July 2026: 74 tasks, best models about 34%) and 4.0 (26 August 2026) ({{releases|https://github.com/harbor-framework/terminal-bench/releases}}). Revisions are made to discriminate: two models 4.9 points apart on 2.1 were 12.7 apart on 3.0 ({{Terminal-Bench 3.0|https://www.tbench.ai/news/terminal-bench-3-0}}). Version 4.0 set a flat 8-hour agent timeout, recalibrated CPU and memory, fixed 19 tasks, removed 8, and calls itself a breaking change ({{Terminal-Bench 4.0|https://www.tbench.ai/news/terminal-bench-4-0}}). When the agent gets a shell and a budget, the limits are part of the task: a 2.x score beside a 4.0 score is a false comparison, not an old one. OSWorld circulates under three names that are not one scale: the original, OSWorld-Verified (built because roughly 300 tasks or checkers were broken and flattening scores) and OSWorld 2.0, which current vendor cards report ({{OSWorld|https://os-world.github.io/}}). Even inside one version the scoring rule matters: Claude Opus 5's 74.0% on OSWorld 2.0 is partial credit, 37.2% strict, and the maintainers' own run gives 68.3% and 31.4% ({{Same model, many numbers|#t-same}}).

Split which tasks were scored

Many benchmarks hold back tasks to measure overfitting to the public part. On Scale's SWE-bench Pro, Claude Opus 4.1 fell from 22.7% on the public set to 17.8% on the private commercial subset ({{Scale|https://labs.scale.com/leaderboard/swe_bench_pro_public}}). It now also has a major version: V2 (22 September 2026) re-checked every task and kept 642, and its leaderboard has Claude Opus 5 at 99.4% (638 of 642), so a version-1 figure and a V2 figure share only a name ({{Scale|https://labs.scale.com/blog/swe-bench-pro-v2}}; readings in {{Same model, many numbers|#t-same}}). Public sets die by leakage; fully private sets cannot be audited and concentrate trust (FrontierMath's late-disclosed OpenAI funding is the cautionary tale). Best practice is a hybrid: public development set, private held-out set, and independent re-runners (Epoch AI, Princeton's HAL, Artificial Analysis) as auditors.

Harness what ran the model

On agentic benchmarks the score belongs to the model plus the program around it (prompts, tools, memory, retries, budget). On ARC-AGI-3, interactive games humans solve completely, GPT-6 Astra's headline pair is 62.7% on ARC Prize's standard provider-agnostic harness and 99.9% on a Provider Adapter harness that keeps the model's opaque reasoning state between requests ({{ARC Prize, 3 September 2026|https://arcprize.org/blog/astra}}). That pair also mixes effort levels (max in the standard harness, high in the adapter); at equal effort it is 62.7% against 98.6% at max and 54.8% against 99.9% at high, still a gap of 36 to 45 points from the harness alone ({{Same model, many numbers|#t-same}}). ARC Prize's reading: neutral conditions and best achievable are different questions, and once a model reasons in hidden state they stay different. Research harnesses showed it first: Prime Agent's 95.5% with Claude Opus 5, though its often-quoted "30% to 95.5%" compares different harnesses on different game sets (95.5% is the easier public set, best of three runs: {{Prime Agent|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81579a02f0431f24c6cb}}); StateM lifting GPT-5.5 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 83.1% to 92.1% ({{StateM|n:3c65c17b0d0d81899098d8153e100691}}).

Three relatives. Who ran it: on Terminal-Bench 4.0 Anthropic reports 66.4% for Claude Opus 5.5 and Artificial Analysis measured 59.6%; xAI reports 38.0% for Grok 4.7, Artificial Analysis 25.8% ({{Topic: llms, Benchmarks tab|n:3c65c17b0d0d812d9e00f6ec89965286}}). Retrieval: on the Vals AI Legal Research Bench (200 questions) OpenAI reports 38.7% for GPT-6 Astra with web search and 54% for Astra for Law, the same weights with a 230-million-URL legal index and legal-analysis instructions, on OpenAI's private validation set ({{TNW|https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-astra-for-law-gpt-6-legal-search-index}}); if both ran on the same 200 questions the gap is about 3.1 standard errors derived, but index and instructions move together, so it does not isolate retrieval. Cost: on ordinary tasks the harness moves the bill more than the score (21 model-and-harness pairs: {{Arena|https://arena.ai/blog/coding-agents-harness-tax}}); SoL-Pi cut recorded tokens 44.7 to 49.0% on EdgeBench's 51 public tasks, scoring 2.5 to 2.8 points below its baseline ({{SoL-Pi|n:3e25c17b0d0d81a0bb77f16cbc898bb5}}). At the cheap extreme, a small transformer trained for about 67 cents reaches 44% on ARC-AGI-1 ({{write-up|https://mvakde.github.io/blog/44-on-arc-1/}}).

Sampling how many tries, how many items

A model sampled at temperature above zero answers differently on each run, and small benchmarks magnify that. The current AIME has 30 problems, so one is worth 3.3 points, and conventions differ by more: pass@1 (one sample, or the mean over samples), maj@32 (the majority answer of 32 samples, often many points higher), pass@k (any of k right). A "93% on AIME" without convention, temperature, token budget and run count compares with nothing; MathArena fixes them and evaluates within days of each contest ({{MathArena|https://matharena.ai}}). Even then, the item count sets a floor on the noise. Try it:

illustrative runs: each item solved independently at the chosen rate (real items differ in difficulty). The band and stats are exact binomial arithmetic derived: standard error √(p(1−p)/n), 95% interval ±1.96 standard errors, and two models scored on different items need a gap above 1.96·√2 standard errors. Scoring both on the same items with a paired test removes much of the variance ({{Anthropic, "Adding error bars to evals"|https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals}}).

Three rules of thumb fall out: at 80% on 30 AIME problems the standard error is 7.3 points; a 2-point gap on GPQA Diamond's 198 questions is noise (±4.2 at 90%), and so is one on SWE-bench Verified's 500 tasks (±4.0 at 70%). The inflation tricks live here too: best-of-N reported as one run, maj@k beside a rival's pass@1, "with tools" beside "without".

Answer key is the key right?

A score is capped by its answer key. On HLE, FutureHouse found 29 ± 3.7% of text-only chemistry and biology answers contradicted by the literature ({{FutureHouse|https://www.futurehouse.org/research/hle-exam}}), and HLE is graded by an LLM judge, which makes the judge part of the instrument. An expert re-grading of six physics benchmarks audited 250 answers marked wrong for GPT-5.6 Sol: 143 benchmark errors, 95 grader errors, 12 model errors, so 95.2% were not the model's fault; on the cleaned, smaller sets its scores rose from 47% to 79% (HLE-Physics) and 13% to 95% (PRISM-Physics), upper estimates since only rejected answers were checked ({{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}}). So the part of a suite a model fails mixes model failure and item defect, and defects gather in the hard tail people quote. Differences between models on the same harness mean more than absolute scores.

Contamination had the model seen it?

Public benchmarks leak into training data, and "trained on the test" blurs into "trained on similar data". Detection: text overlap with training corpora (weak against paraphrase), asking the model to complete an item from its prefix, rebuilding a fresh parallel set (GSM1k, a new GSM8K look-alike, cost some model families up to 8% in the paper's current version, v4; its first version said 13%: {{Zhang et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00332}}), and splitting by date. Three recent designs answer it three ways:

  • By the calendar: SWE-rebench mines fresh issues and scores models only on a recent window, because models resolve issues in pre-cutoff repositories notably better; the price is no stable time series ({{SWE-rebench|https://swe-rebench.com}}).
  • By transformation: SchrodingerRepo rebuilds each repository at evaluation time with behaviour unchanged (reworded issue, remapped names, reordered layout, rewritten code). Pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified drops 6.0 to 14.4 points, over 80% of the extra actions spent navigating and searching: part of a static score is knowing where to look ({{SchrodingerRepo|n:3e95c17b0d0d81e8b0e0e99acbeb9713}}).
  • By licensing: Real-SWE licenses ten tasks from companies' private repositories, which cannot leak. Its leaderboard (read 4 October 2026) has GPT-6 Astra at 46.25% and Claude Fable 5.1 at 45.00%, below the 57.9% and 55.8% their labs report on Terminal-Bench 4.0 ({{Real-SWE|https://withspecific.com/benchmarks/real-swe}}); with ten tasks, one task is 10 points, so the ranking is within noise.

Defences, strongest first: private sets behind an evaluation API (FrontierMath, HLE's private split, SWE-bench Pro's commercial split), live data pinned to dates (LiveCodeBench, MathArena, SWE-rebench), canary strings (respected only by cooperating labs), perturbed templates, trust. Above them sits enclave evaluation, piloted by Google DeepMind on Gemini Flash Lite in August 2026: weights and prompts meet inside a hardware-encrypted enclave and only scores leave ({{DeepMind|https://deepmind.google/blog/piloting-the-worlds-first-double-blind-ai-evaluations/}}; caveats on {{Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}}).

Index version the aggregator moved the target

Composite indices are versioned benchmarks too. The Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index changed twice in four days: v4.2 (4 September 2026) and v4.3 (7 September: Terminal-Bench 2.1 upgraded to 4.0, τ³-Banking replaced by AutomationBench-AA, private weight 45%) ({{v4.3|https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index-v4-3}}); the current release is v4.3.2 ({{methodology|https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/intelligence-benchmarking}}). Index scores quoted at launches before 4 September, such as the high-50s and low-60s for GLM-5.3-Flash, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Muse Spark 1.3, sit on an older scale. A score without its index version is a benchmark name without a version.

Subject what is measured is not the model

A cluster of September 2026 benchmarks measures the system around the model, and each moves the number more than any model gap. Hyper-τ-bench: Claude Opus 5 building agents passes 23.9% of held-out tasks alone and 82.2% paired with an engineer who knows the task ({{Sierra|https://sierra.ai/blog/hyper-t-bench-evaluating-agents-that-build-agents}}). HarnessDev: the best model-built harness averages 67.8 against 86.2 for human-engineered ones ({{HarnessDev|n:3d45c17b0d0d81cd9883f783840e013f}}). MOLE scores the monitor: 28 of 39 agent models completed most of their harmful objectives under a preamble saying the sandbox is harmless (across models, refusal and completion rates correlate, Spearman −0.73), and the best monitor caught 24 of 45 completed harms in a one-day audit ({{MOLE|n:3db5c17b0d0d818b9680c7b91baaf7e9}}). Emergence World scores duration: agent worlds run for up to 21 days produced failures nobody injected (goal drift, compounding tool errors, refused work), which tasks that end cannot detect ({{Emergence World|n:3e25c17b0d0d818fbffbc9e2ceab824b}}). Phi-Bench scores infrastructure engineering (Claude Opus 5: mean reward 36.53%, {{Phi-Bench|n:3db5c17b0d0d81029236da6c38f5891f}}).

And what nobody reports: no major model card reports confidence calibration ({{arXiv 2609.26489|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26489}}); and a model's stated reason is not a measurement of its choice, since WhatWorkedBench finds agents picking near-optimal settings for wrong reasons ({{arXiv 2609.27490|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27490}}).

Go deeper: harnesses (lm-eval-harness, Inspect, HELM), LLM judges and contamination tooling on {{Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}}; agent scaffolds on {{Topic: agentic-harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d881a8fe98b4cff7bb}}; every spread, dated, in {{Same model, many numbers|#t-same}}.

When the measure becomes the target

Goodhart's law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") takes five forms. Training on the test or its paraphrases. Overfitting the format: multiple-choice letters, or the verbosity voters and judges reward (AlpacaEval needed a length-controlled variant). Scaffold shopping: your best harness against a rival's default. Reward hacking the evaluator: Berkeley RDI's cheating agent reached near-perfect scores on eight agent benchmarks (98% on GAIA) while solving no tasks, by overwriting tests, tampering with checkers or fetching public gold answers ({{Berkeley RDI|https://rdi.berkeley.edu/blog/trustworthy-benchmarks-cont/}}), and o3 hacked the scorer unprompted in 39 of 128 METR RE-Bench runs ({{METR|https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/}}); evaluator isolation is now a requirement. Goodfire proposes catching it from inside, via an activation signature, not yet a monitor with a known false-positive rate ({{Goodfire|https://www.goodfire.com/research/reward-hacking-activation-monitors}}). Selective reporting: testing private variants and publishing the winner, as The Leaderboard Illusion documented on Arena (then LMArena; Meta tested 27 Llama 4 variants) ({{Singh et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20879}}; {{Arena's response|https://arena.ai/blog/our-response/}}). The fix inside companies is the same as outside: private in-house suites stay discriminating because nobody trains on them.

A math score is the share of a model's final answers that a checker accepted, so it means only what three choices let it mean: how the answer was checked, how samples were drawn and combined, and whether the problems were new to the model. Math is the cleanest family to grade and the fastest to saturate: every final-answer set built so far has been climbed to its ceiling, and the open signal has moved to research problems and proofs.

This page owns the math sets, how their answers are checked, and how their sampling rules are reported.

Final answer: the number or expression a model puts last, usually inside \boxed{}; the only part graded.
pass@1: the mean over all sampled answers, the chance one sample is right.
maj@k (or cons@k): the most common of k answers is graded; a vote.
pass@k: right if any of k answers is right; needs an oracle that knows the key.
Saturated: the top score is at the ceiling or inside the answer key's own error rate.
Post-contest model: released after a contest was sat, so it may have seen the problems; MathArena flags it.
Formal statement: the problem written in a proof assistant's language (Lean 4, Isabelle, Rocq), which a proof checker can verify against.

1. How a math answer is extracted and checked

A grader does three things: find the final answer in a long output, turn it into something comparable, and decide whether it equals the key. Each step can fail. Here is one real answer to HMMT February 2026 problem 7, whose key is an expression, through three checkers:

Real answers from {{MathArena/hmmt_feb_2026_outputs|https://huggingface.co/datasets/MathArena/hmmt_feb_2026_outputs}} (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): the last \boxed{} of each output, MathArena's parsed answer and its grade, as released. Of the 112 answers to problem 7, 65 are graded right; none of the 65 writes the key's string, even after removing spaces, \left, \right and \dfrac; 50 write it as ∛(1 − √6/3). Counts from src/mk_check_example.py derived; the equivalence is checked with sympy there.

So the checker is part of the benchmark. On 17 of HMMT February 2026's 33 problems the key is not a plain integer, and there exact string match would mark right answers wrong; a symbolic check (parse both sides, simplify the difference to zero) fixes that, and a parser that truncates an answer breaks it. Every math benchmark sits on one ladder of checkers, and the rung decides which errors it can make:

FrontierMath adds a design rule worth stealing: a problem must not give more than a 1% chance of guessing the answer without doing most of the work ({{Glazer et al., section 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04872}}), which is why its answers are large, unobvious objects rather than small integers.

2. Sampling rules as reported: pass@1, maj@k, pass@k

Most reasoning models are sampled several times per problem, and the same samples give different scores depending on how they are combined. MathArena's released answers (four runs per problem) make it visible. The hardest AIME 2025 problem, with the four real answers of nine models, graded three ways:

Real answers from {{MathArena/aime_2025_outputs|https://huggingface.co/datasets/MathArena/aime_2025_outputs}}, runs 1 to 4 in order; "none" means the parser found no final answer (counted wrong). maj@4 is computed exactly: the most common answer among the four wins, and a tie is split evenly between the tied answers (so a 2 to 2 tie with the right answer counts one half). Exam totals use all 30 problems.

A vote helps when the right answer is the most common one (GPT-5 high on this problem: two of four right, so the vote picks 735). It hurts when wrong answers agree: 71 of the 263 sampled answers to this problem, from every model in the dataset, were the same wrong 147, and the vote locks it in for o1, DeepSeek-R1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. pass@4 is always the highest, because it grades the best of four with knowledge of the key. Over the whole exam the three rules differ by up to 17.5 points for the same samples (Claude 3.7 Sonnet with thinking: 49.2%, 52.5% and 66.7%; DeepSeek-R1: 70.0%, 74.2% and 86.7%) derived. In formulas, with n answers per problem of which c are right:

pass@1 = mean over problems of c / n  ·  maj@k = mean of [the most common of k answers is right]  ·  pass@k = mean of 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k)

The pass@k form is the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021), the one the {{Coding benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81bfa09ad4ba5eef0c4e}} page teaches; this page's lab computes maj@k exactly over all k-subsets. OpenAI's own o1 announcement printed three numbers for one model on AIME 2024, 74.4% with one sample, 83.3% by a vote of 64 and 93% re-ranking 1,000 samples with a learned scorer; the parent's {{Same model, many numbers|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} tab has that case with its sources. So a "93% on AIME" without the sampling rule, temperature, token budget and run count compares with nothing.

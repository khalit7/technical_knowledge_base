A math score is the share of a model's final answers that a checker accepted, so it means only what three choices let it mean: how the answer was checked, how samples were drawn and combined, and whether the problems were new to the model. Math is the cleanest family to grade and the fastest to saturate: every final-answer set built so far has been climbed to its ceiling, and the open signal has moved to research problems and proofs.

Final answer: the number or expression a model puts last, usually inside \boxed{}; the only part graded.
pass@1: the mean over all sampled answers, the chance one sample is right.
maj@k (or cons@k): the most common of k answers is graded; a vote.
pass@k: right if any of k answers is right; needs an oracle that knows the key.
Saturated: the top score is at the ceiling or inside the answer key's own error rate.
Post-contest model: released after a contest was sat, so it may have seen the problems; MathArena flags it.
Formal statement: the problem written in a proof assistant's language (Lean 4, Isabelle, Rocq), which a proof checker can verify against.

1. How a math answer is extracted and checked

A grader does three things: find the final answer in a long output, turn it into something comparable, and decide whether it equals the key. Each step can fail. Fig. 1 takes one real answer to HMMT February 2026 problem 7, whose key is an expression, through extraction and three checkers:

  1. The problem and its key. The problem asks for "an exact expression"; the key is (3 + √6)−1/3 ≈ 0.5683, and many correct forms exist. The field on the left holds all 112 answers MathArena's models gave to it (every run), coloured by the released grade: 65 right, 47 wrong.
  2. Extract the final answer. The grader keeps only the content of the last \boxed{} in the output; everything before it, the whole derivation, is ignored. Kimi K3 (Think), run 1, ends \boxed{\sqrt[3]{\frac{3-\sqrt6}{3}}}.
  3. Checker 1: exact string match. Compare the extracted string with the key's string, (3+\sqrt{6})^{-1/3}. They differ, so exact match calls a right answer wrong. Of the 65 answers MathArena grades right, none writes the key's string.
  4. Checker 2: normalised string (MATH style). Remove spaces, \left and \right, turn \dfrac into \frac and \sqrt6 into \sqrt{6}. Formatting is fixed, algebra is not: still different, still wrong: 0 of the 65 right answers match after it.
  5. Checker 3: parse and compare symbolically. Parse both into expressions and simplify the difference: (1 − √6/3)1/3 − (3 + √6)−1/3 = 0, because 1/(3 + √6) = (3 − √6)/3. Right. 50 answers write it as ∛(1 − √6/3), exactly this form.
  6. Where the parser breaks. GPT-5.2 (high), run 3, answers in Cardano's form with complex cube roots. The parser keeps only the leading 1/3 (0.333, while the key is 0.568), yet the released grade is "right". Of 16 answers parsed to 1/3, 7 are graded right and 9 wrong; of 3 parsed to 3, 1 is right (the 19 lifted in the field). Their grades did not come from the parsed value; MathArena reviews flagged parses by hand, and the data does not say who decided each.

Real answers from {{MathArena/hmmt_feb_2026_outputs|https://huggingface.co/datasets/MathArena/hmmt_feb_2026_outputs}} (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0): the last \boxed{} of each output, MathArena's parsed answer and its grade, as released. Counts from src/mk_check_example.py derived; the equivalence is checked with sympy there.

So the checker is part of the benchmark. On 17 of HMMT February 2026's 33 problems the key is not a plain integer, and there exact string match would mark right answers wrong; a symbolic check (parse both sides, simplify the difference to zero) fixes that, and a parser that truncates an answer breaks it.

FrontierMath adds a design rule worth stealing: a problem must not give more than a 1% chance of guessing the answer without doing most of the work ({{Glazer et al., section 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04872}}), which is why its answers are large, unobvious objects rather than small integers.

2. Sampling rules as reported: pass@1, maj@k, pass@k

Most reasoning models are sampled several times per problem, and the same samples give different scores depending on how they are combined. MathArena's released answers (four runs per problem) make it visible. Fig. 2 takes the hardest AIME 2025 problem with the four real answers of nine models and scores them three ways:

  1. One problem, nine models, four runs. Nine models from GPT-4o (2024) to GPT-5.2 (December 2025) each answered it four times under MathArena's fixed prompt; each block is one parsed final answer, in run order. Before grading, notice how often the same wrong number turns up.
  2. Grade by exact match. The key is 735: green right, red wrong, grey where the parser found no final answer ("none", counted wrong). Only GPT-5 (high) and GPT-5.2 (high) ever get it right. The same wrong 147 appears 14 times in these 36 answers, and 71 of the 263 sampled answers to this problem, from every model in the dataset, are 147.
  3. pass@1: the mean of the four. Each model's four answers flow into one bar, c / n with c right of n: GPT-5 (high) gets 50%, GPT-5.2 (high) 100%, everyone else 0%.
  4. maj@4: the vote. The most common answer in each row is lifted and outlined, and only it is graded (a tie is split evenly). A vote helps when the right answer is the most common one (GPT-5 (high): two of four right, so the vote picks 735 and it rises to 100%). It hurts when wrong answers agree: 147 wins the vote for o1, DeepSeek-R1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet.
  5. pass@4: any of four. Right if any sample is right, which needs an oracle that knows the key. pass@4 is always the highest, because it grades the best of four with knowledge of the key; here the same two models are at 100%.
  6. Over all 30 problems. The three bars grow to the whole exam's scores from the same samples. The rules differ by up to 17.5 points for one model (Claude 3.7 Sonnet with thinking: 49.2%, 52.5% and 66.7%; DeepSeek-R1: 70.0%, 74.2% and 86.7%) derived.

Real answers from {{MathArena/aime_2025_outputs|https://huggingface.co/datasets/MathArena/aime_2025_outputs}}, runs 1 to 4 in order. maj@4 is computed exactly: the most common answer among the four wins, and a tie is split evenly between the tied answers (so a 2 to 2 tie with the right answer counts one half). Exam totals use all 30 problems.

In formulas, with n answers per problem of which c are right:

pass@1 = mean over problems of c / n  ·  maj@k = mean of [the most common of k answers is right]  ·  pass@k = mean of 1 − C(n − c, k) / C(n, k)

The pass@k form is the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021), the one the {{Coding benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81bfa09ad4ba5eef0c4e}} page teaches; this page's lab computes maj@k exactly over all k-subsets. OpenAI's own o1 announcement printed three numbers for one model on AIME 2024, 74.4% with one sample, 83.3% by a vote of 64 and 93% re-ranking 1,000 samples with a learned scorer; the parent's {{Same model, many numbers|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} tab has that case with its sources. So a "93% on AIME" without the sampling rule, temperature, token budget and run count compares with nothing.

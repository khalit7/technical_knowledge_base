Competition math: MATH, MATH-500, Omni-MATH

MATH ({{Hendrycks et al., 5 Mar 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03874}}) collects 12,500 problems from high-school competitions (AMC 10, AMC 12, AIME and similar), 7,500 for training and 5,000 for test, in seven subjects (prealgebra, algebra, number theory, counting and probability, geometry with diagrams written in the Asymptote language, intermediate algebra, precalculus) and five difficulty levels. Every problem has a full LaTeX solution ending in a \boxed{} answer, "unique after normalization", so it is graded by exact match after normalising the expression rather than by text overlap. At launch, models scored 3.0% to 6.9%; the paper's human check was small: on 20 problems a computer-science PhD student who "does not especially like mathematics" got 8 (40%) and a three-time IMO gold medallist 18 (90%), missing two "exclusively due to small errors of arithmetic".

The climb: Minerva 540B 33.6% with one sample and 50.3% with a majority vote over samples, a different protocol on the same set ({{Lewkowycz et al., June 2022, Table 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.14858}}); GPT-4 42.5% four-shot (March 2023); o1 94.8% on MATH-500 (September 2024). Epoch AI's own runs on the hardest level (1,324 Level 5 test problems) go from 22.97% for GPT-4 (June 2023) to 98.13% for GPT-5 (high), run in October 2025 (the parent's {{Saturation timeline|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} tab draws all three series). On MATH-500, o3 scores 99.2% and the top five sit between 98.2% and 99.2%; the same study flags MATH-500 as contaminated ({{Akhtar et al., ICML 2026, Table 6|https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16763}}).

Where MATH-500 comes from matters more than it looks. OpenAI's process-reward work needed more training problems, so it moved 4,500 of the 5,000 MATH test problems into the PRM800K training set and evaluated only on the remaining 500, "selected uniformly at random" ({{Lightman et al., Let's Verify Step by Step|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050}}). Those 500 became the standard subset, and the other 4,500 test problems have sat in a public training set with step-level labels since May 2023: a full-MATH-test score after that date is not clean.

Omni-MATH ({{Gao et al., 10 Oct 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07985}}) answered MATH's saturation with 4,428 olympiad-level problems in over 33 sub-domains and more than 10 difficulty levels. Its answers are too varied for string matching, so the main leaderboard is graded by GPT-4o, with an open judge model (Omni-Judge) and a rule-gradable subset beside it. At launch o1-mini led with 60.54% and o1-preview 52.55%; the official board still shows those September 2024 rows ({{omni-math.github.io|https://omni-math.github.io/}}, read 4 Oct 2026), so it is a dormant board, and any newer figure is someone's own run with someone's own judge.

Contests as benchmarks: AIME, HMMT and MathArena

The fix for contamination was freshness. The AIME (American Invitational Mathematics Examination) is the second round of the US olympiad path: two papers (AIME I and II) of 15 problems, three hours each, every answer an integer from 0 to 999 ({{AoPS wiki, 2026 AIME|https://artofproblemsolving.com/wiki/index.php/2026_AIME_II}}). A new contest every February is a new 30-problem benchmark that no model trained before it can have seen. HMMT February (Harvard-MIT Mathematics Tournament) plays the same role with 30 to 33 answer problems, some of them expressions rather than integers.

MathArena ({{Balunović, Dekoninck et al., ETH Zurich and INSAIT|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23281}}) made this a protocol: extract the problems and answers as soon as a contest ends; run every selected model four times per problem with one fixed prompt ("Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}. The answer is an integer between 0 and 999 inclusive."); parse the boxed answer with a rule-based parser, with flagged parses reviewed by hand; report accuracy as the mean over all answers (pass@1) with no majority voting; publish cost and tokens; and mark every model released after the contest date as possibly contaminated. Proof contests (USAMO, IMO) are graded by human judges. Running it on older contests is how MathArena found "strong signs of contamination in AIME 2024", and why it treats only post-cutoff contests as clean.

Its outputs are public, every answer of every run ({{Hugging Face, MathArena, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0|https://huggingface.co/MathArena}}), which makes it possible to see exactly what the protocol choices do. Here is the hardest AIME 2025 problem with the four real answers of nine models, graded three ways:

Real answers from {{MathArena/aime_2025_outputs|https://huggingface.co/datasets/MathArena/aime_2025_outputs}}, runs 1 to 4 in order; "none" means the parser found no final answer (counted wrong). maj@4 is computed exactly: the most common answer among the four wins, and a tie is split evenly between the tied answers (so a 2 to 2 tie with the right answer counts one half). pass@4 asks whether any of the four is right, which needs an oracle that knows the answer. Exam totals use all 30 problems.

Three things show at once. A vote helps when the right answer is the most common one (GPT-5 high on this problem: two of four right, so the vote picks 735). It hurts when wrong answers agree: 71 of the 263 sampled answers to this problem, from every model in the dataset, were the same wrong 147, and the vote locks it in for o1, DeepSeek-R1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. And pass@4 is always the highest, because it grades the best of four with knowledge of the key. Over the whole exam the three rules differ by up to 17.5 points for the same samples (Claude 3.7 Sonnet with thinking: 49.2%, 52.5% and 66.7%; DeepSeek-R1: 70.0%, 74.2% and 86.7%) derived. That is why the old advice still holds: a "93% on AIME" without the sampling rule, temperature, token budget and run count compares with nothing. OpenAI's own o1 announcement printed three numbers for one model on AIME 2024, 74.4% with one sample, 83.3% by a vote of 64 and 93% re-ranking 1,000 samples with a learned scorer; the parent's {{Same model, many numbers|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} tab has that case with its sources. Every model, every rule and k from 1 to 4 are in the {{Sample lab|#t-lab}} tab.

Each vintage saturated faster

  • AIME 2025 (sat 6 and 12 Feb 2025): the best models released before it were o1 (medium) at 81.67% and o3-mini (high) at 86.67%; GPT-5.2 (high) reached 100% in December 2025, flagged as released after the contest ({{MathArena|https://matharena.ai/competition_tables/aime--aime_2025}}).
  • AIME 2026 (5 and 11 Feb 2026): GPT-5.2 (high), already public two months before the contest, scored 98.33% (118 of 120 answers), so the newest vintage was at the ceiling the moment it was sat; GPT-5.5 (xhigh) and Claude Opus 4.8 (max) later reached 100% ({{MathArena|https://matharena.ai/competition_tables/aime--aime_2026}}, read 4 Oct 2026).
  • HMMT February 2026 (33 problems): 96.97% for GPT-5.2 (high), released before it, and 98.48% for GPT-5.5 (xhigh) after ({{MathArena|https://matharena.ai/competition_tables/hmmt--hmmt_feb_2026}}).

MathArena itself retired final-answer contests on 12 May 2026: its two hardest final-answer sets, built in August 2025 from problems no model could solve, were nearly saturated (GPT-5.5 had just solved the last unsolved Apex problem, IMO 2025 Problem 6), and when it screened 176 new contest problems for a harder set, Gemini 3.1 Pro solved every one at least once in four attempts and 162 in all four ({{Farewell to final-answer competition problems|https://matharena.ai/no_final_answer/}}). The contest tables stay online as history. MathArena's live signal is now research mathematics and proofs: ArXivMath (answers taken from new arXiv papers; top 95.31% for GPT-6.1 Sol at max effort over the May, June and August 2026 sets), BrokenArXiv, ArXivLean (formal) and proof-graded USAMO 2026.

Error bars on 30 problems

A benchmark score is an estimate, and the number of problems sets how good an estimate it can be. On AIME one problem is worth 3.33 points. The parent page's {{Reading tab|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} has a widget that simulates runs at any size, and its Same model, many numbers tab a calculator with exact intervals; here are the math sets themselves:

Half-width of a 95% interval, 1.96·√(p(1−p)/n), with n the number of problems derived. "Today's top" uses the best reading in the table above; at 100% the binomial interval collapses to zero, which says nothing about the next problem (see the Wilson and Clopper-Pearson intervals in the parent's calculator). Item counts: AIME 30, HMMT February 2026 33, FrontierMath v2 private sets 41 and 285 (Epoch's public counts minus the published problems), miniF2F test 244, MATH-500 500, GSM8K test 1,319, MATH Level 5 1,324, one GSM-Symbolic set 100.

How MathArena's ± is computed. Its tables print accuracy "together with a 95% confidence interval obtained with the normal approximation": GPT-5.2 (high) on AIME 2026 is 98.33% ± 2.29. That number is 1.96·√(p(1−p)/120), with 120 the number of answers (30 problems × 4 runs), not of problems; the rule reproduces all 123 rows of the three tables used here from each row's accuracy and answer count derived. The paper describes N as "the number of questions". Counting answers treats four runs on the same problem as independent, so it measures run-to-run noise on these 30 problems, not how the score would move on a new set of 30. Treating the problem as the unit, with the per-problem solve rates from the released answers, gives ±2.27 for that row, but ±10.75 instead of ±6.92 for o1 (medium) on AIME 2025 derived: over the four-run rows of the three tables the clustered interval is a median 1.7 times the published one and up to 2.0 times, the most when a model solves some problems on every run and misses others on every run. The binomial interval at n = 30 (±13.85 for o1) is wider still, because it ignores what the four runs reveal about each problem. For comparing two models on the same problems, the paired test MathArena uses for its rank intervals is the right tool; the {{Sample lab|#t-lab}} runs it exactly on any pair.

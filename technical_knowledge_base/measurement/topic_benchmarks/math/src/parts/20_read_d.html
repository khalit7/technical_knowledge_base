7. Research math: FrontierMath

FrontierMath ({{Glazer et al., Epoch AI, 7 Nov 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04872}}) is a set of new, unpublished problems written and vetted by more than 60 mathematicians: "Solving a typical problem requires multiple hours of effort from a researcher in the relevant branch of mathematics, and for the upper end questions, multiple days." Each answer is a definite object checked by code, built to be guessproof: in Epoch's harness the model can run Python, must submit a function answer() that returns within 30 seconds, and has a hard limit of 1,000,000 tokens ({{Epoch AI, methodology|https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/frontiermath-tier-4-v2}}). At launch "current state-of-the-art AI models solve under 2% of problems". The core set has three tiers of rising difficulty, and Tier 4, added in 2025, is "an expansion set of 50 problems designed to vastly exceed the difficulty of even the Tier 3 problems" ({{Epoch AI, Tiers 1-4 about|https://epoch.ai/frontiermath/tiers-1-4/about}}). Tier text from Epoch's about page, access counts from its January 2025 statement (v1 sets of 300 and 50). The points are listed in the depth block below.

Open problems: FrontierMath Erdős. With both answer-checked sets near their ceiling, Epoch and Thomas Bloom built a set with no known answers: 68 conjectures from erdosproblems.com that were open as of August 2026, each solved only by a Lean 4 proof (or disproof) that passes a strict checker ({{benchmark page|https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/frontiermath-erdos}}). Of the five AIs evaluated, "One (GPT-6 Astra) scored 3%, and all others scored 0%" ({{FrontierMath Erdős, 6 Sep 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.25050}}): 2 of 68 derived. A solved item is a new theorem, so the ceiling is not a score but the end of the problem list.

Depth: the harness, the tiers, the score points, why 93.68% is 267 of 285, and the governance record

FrontierMath Erdős: the checker is Comparator, the Lean FRO checker that accepts only a proof of the identical statement from permitted axioms; the budget is $300 of API spend and 72 hours per problem.

The authors include collectively 14 IMO gold medals and one Fields Medal; Terence Tao contributed several problems and, interviewed for the paper, expected them to "resist AIs for several years at least". Epoch's harness offers sympy, numpy, mpmath and a few other libraries, and forces submission from 660,000 tokens. At launch the best model solved about 6% when any of eight runs counts (the paper's appendix). The tiers are about 20%, 40% and 40% of the core set. Tier 1: like IMO problems and advanced undergraduate exercises, solved with tools and code. Tier 2: advanced graduate level, "an expert may devote a day or more to specialized proofs, coding, and multi-stage reasoning". Tier 3: "problems which resemble early exploratory research problems of a PhD student", several days for a specialist. Tier 4: written by mathematics professors and postdocs.

8. Formal proofs: miniF2F and PutnamBench

A formal benchmark asks for a proof that a proof assistant accepts, so a wrong proof cannot pass. What the checker does not check is the step before it:

The kernel checks the proof against the formal statement; nothing checks the statement against the problem

The gap in orange is where a misformalised statement, a statement that leaks the answer, or a translation of a different theorem gets through.

miniF2F ({{Zheng, Han and Polu, OpenAI, 31 Aug 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.00110}}) has 488 statements from the AIME, AMC and IMO and from school and undergraduate courses, split 244 validation and 244 test; prover systems drove the test split to 99.2% with Hilbert, which combines an informal reasoning model, a prover model, the Lean verifier and retrieval ({{Varambally et al., 26 Sept 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22819}}). PutnamBench ({{Tsoukalas et al., UT Austin, 15 Jul 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11214}}) took the undergraduate Putnam competition; the current set has 672 Lean 4 problems (346 with an answer placeholder), 640 Isabelle and 412 Rocq. By September 2026 at least four systems (Aleph Prover, Humanfia, NEAR AI and Forall) had proved all 672 Lean problems on the track that supplies the answer, at mean costs from $0.17 to $74 per problem, and Midas Prover all 672 on the track where the answer must be found (12 Sept 2026); the board now ranks by cost ({{PutnamBench leaderboard|https://trishullab.github.io/PutnamBench/leaderboard.html}}, read 4 Oct 2026).

Correction to the old page: "PutnamBench still has headroom"

Both formal benchmarks are saturated. And "no grading ambiguity" needs the qualifier the figure shows: the checker verifies the proof, not that the formal statement says what the problem says; results on the board are self-submitted, several perfect runs used fixed formalisations, and the with-answer track hands the prover the answer ({{leaderboard notes|https://trishullab.github.io/PutnamBench/leaderboard.html}}). The cleanest metric design in math still needs its statements audited.

Depth: the original formal languages, and when the proof is the output (machine-written research)

miniF2F was formalised in Metamath and Lean, partly in Isabelle and HOL Light; its baseline was GPT-f. PutnamBench launched with 1,692 hand-made formalisations of 640 theorems in Lean 4 and Isabelle, with a subset in Coq (now Rocq); at launch, provers solved "a handful".

The same qualifier decides how much a Lean check says about machine-written research. Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen argue that resolving the ambiguities of natural-language mathematics, which a semantically faithful translation needs, sits arbitrarily high in the Solvability Complexity Index hierarchy (SCI = ∞, harder than the halting problem), so a mechanically checked translation may give no confidence in the original argument; among their examples, "the formalised Lean proof does not correspond to the NL proof" of OpenAI's announced Navier-Stokes blow-up result ({{Navier-Stokes lost in translation, Oct 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08144}}). OpenAI's release of 722 machine-written manuscripts in 372 families from an unreleased model ({{openai/math|https://github.com/openai/math}}, Apache 2.0, 6 Oct 2026) shows the other half: the next day a sign error invalidated one paper and the two built on it, all three were withdrawn, 14 others were revised, and 300 of 719 top-line results (about 42%) were formalised ({{history|https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/history.md}}). The release's headline result shows the two checks diverging: on the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, a 199-page preprint (30 Sep 2026) puts every nontrivial zero of the zeta function between real parts 1/8 and 7/8 and is checked in Lean, while a weaker, 49-page 1/12 to 11/12 version (5 Oct), the only write-up in the release a person edited, is not yet formalised, yet it is the one mathematicians are reading and most think correct ({{Quanta, 19 Oct 2026|https://www.quantamagazine.org/ai-proved-the-quasi-riemann-hypothesis-what-happens-now-20261019/}}). Readers have also organised: {{oai-math-revised|https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/24/oai-math-revised-a-community-hub-for-revising-openais-math-write-ups/}} (a guest post on Terence Tao's blog, 24 Oct 2026) is a community hub for revising the write-ups through pull requests, because they "can be difficult to understand and can have incomplete citations". Neither is a benchmark score: a release is graded by the people who read it, slowly, and a formalised share is only as good as the faithfulness of each statement.

9. Olympiad proofs: the IMO as a milestone

The International Mathematical Olympiad is six proof problems over two 4.5-hour papers, 7 points each. It is a yearly milestone rather than a leaderboard, and each claim has to say who graded it and under what limits. "Gold" has meant three different things (certified by IMO coordinators, graded by the lab's chosen medallists, graded by an AI) that a headline does not distinguish. In 2025 Gemini Deep Think's 35 of 42 was "officially graded and certified by IMO coordinators"; OpenAI's 35 of 42 was graded by three former medallists; in 2026 several systems scored 42 of 42, some graded by Claude-based agents ("strong but not authoritative").

10. Beyond math: CritPt and the physics re-grading

Physics benchmarks share math's exact-answer design and inherit its weak point, the answer key. CritPt ({{Zhu et al., 30 Sept 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26574}}) has 71 research-scale challenges written by more than 50 active physicists, with private reference solutions; Artificial Analysis runs 70 of them, where GPT-5.6 Sol (max) leads at 32.3% (113 of 350 attempts, mean of 5). An expert re-grading ({{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}}, the paper page that owns the audit) found 21 of a 56-challenge subset had benchmark errors; on the 54 kept, the same GPT-5.6 Sol scores 87.50% (mean of 4). Read a low CritPt score as an upper bound on headroom, not a floor on capability.

Depth: CritPt at launch and the re-grading's numbers

CritPt's challenges decompose into 190 checkpoints; at launch the best base model, GPT-5 (high), averaged 5.7%, about 10% with coding tools. In the re-grading, 19 of the 21 flawed challenges were repaired and 2 excluded, and 5 failures were model errors. On the 54 kept, GPT-5.6 Sol scores 87.50% (mean of 4) and 94.44% (any of 4); Claude Fable 5 78.24% and 90.74%; Gemini 3.1 Pro rises from 17.71% to 54.63%. The before and after differ in harness, tools, judge and effort as well as in the keys (Gemini, without tools on both sides, is the cleanest pair), and Anthropic's separate correction revised 31 of the 71 statements; the paper page has the full audit and its caveats.

Math is the cleanest family of AI benchmarks to grade and the fastest to saturate: an answer is a number or a formula that can be checked exactly, so there is no judge to argue with, and every benchmark built so far has been climbed. This page takes each one in turn: how it was built, how it is graded, what broke, what its score means today, and what to use now. How a math answer gets checked

Every math benchmark sits somewhere on one ladder, from checks that cannot be argued with to checks that are themselves a model or a person. Where a benchmark sits decides which errors it can make.

FrontierMath adds a design rule worth stealing: a problem must not give more than a 1% chance of guessing the answer without doing most of the work (Glazer et al., section 2), which is why its answers are large, unobvious objects rather than small integers.

Grade school: GSM8K and what broke it

Construction. GSM8K (Cobbe et al., OpenAI, 27 Oct 2021) is "8.5K high quality grade school math problems created by human problem writers", each needing 2 to 8 steps of basic arithmetic; "a bright middle school student should be able to solve every problem". The released split is 7,473 training and 1,319 test problems (Hugging Face, MIT licence); the paper rounds them to 7.5K and 1K. Each solution ends in #### 14, a single number, graded by exact match, and carries calculator annotations such as <<62-48=14>>. The paper's own method was already a sampling protocol: generate 100 candidate solutions and let a trained verifier pick one. Its best, GPT-3 175B fine-tuned with a verifier, scored 55% (the figure PaLM later quoted as the prior best).

Where chain of thought was shown. Wei et al.'s chain-of-thought paper led with it: PaLM 540B prompted with eight worked examples reached state of the art on GSM8K, "surpassing even finetuned GPT-3 with a verifier" (arXiv 2201.11903). GPT-4 reported 92.0% five-shot in March 2023, with a footnote that part of the GSM8K training set was mixed into its training data (GPT-4 report, Table 2). In September 2024 OpenAI wrote that "recent frontier models do so well on MATH and GSM8K that these benchmarks are no longer effective at differentiating models" (OpenAI, Learning to reason with LLMs).

Correction to the old page: "now >95% for anything frontier, heavily contaminated"

Neither holds as stated. No 2026 frontier card reports GSM8K, so there is no current frontier figure; the last ones were mid-90s (GPT-4o 95.2% and o1-mini 95.1% on the full test set in GSM-Symbolic's Table 1). And the contamination evidence is family by family, not frontier-wide: GSM1k, a fresh look-alike set of 1,205 human-written problems kept private (50 are printed in its appendix), found drops of up to 8% and systematic overfitting in several families, but "many models, especially those on the frontier, show minimal signs of overfitting" (Zhang et al., v4). The first version of that paper said "up to 13%" (v1, 1 May 2024); the parent page's Reading tab quotes the v1 figure. Part of the ceiling is the key, not the models: GSM8K-Platinum re-checked 219 flagged test questions, removed 110 and relabelled 10, leaving 1,209 (MadryLab, March 2025).

GSM-Symbolic: the same question, perturbed

Apple's GSM-Symbolic (Mirzadeh et al., 7 Oct 2024) turned 100 GSM8K test questions into templates: names, family members and numbers become variables with ranges and conditions (for instance, the answer must be a whole number). Fifty instances per template give 50 test sets of 100 questions; harder variants delete one clause (M1) or add one or two (P1, P2), and GSM-NoOp adds a clause that sounds relevant and changes nothing. Templates and the Symbolic, P1 and P2 data are released (Hugging Face, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0); NoOp is not. Step through one question and watch one model's accuracy:

Question texts are verbatim: the GSM8K original and its template from the paper's Figure 1; the three instances from the released data (template id 9, instances 0 to 2); the phone-call variants from Figure 5; the kiwi question and o1-mini's answer from Figure 7. Accuracies are 8-shot chain of thought with greedy decoding, mean and standard deviation over 50 sets of 100 (Table 1, Appendix A.2). The grey dashed whisker is what binomial chance alone gives for 100 items, √(p(1−p)/100) derived.

Read the evidence in two parts. The dramatic part holds up: P2 and NoOp cut every model, even strong ones (o1-preview from 94.9% on the full GSM8K test to 77.4% on NoOp), and models "blindly convert" a statement such as "five of them were a bit smaller" into a subtraction. The paper's other headline, that accuracy "varies" across the 50 sets of renamed and renumbered questions, is weaker than it sounds: the reported spreads (about 2 to 3.5 points) are smaller than the roughly 4 points binomial chance alone gives at 100 items derived. Ivanova et al. re-tested the paper's numbers and found only 3 of 25 models with a statistically significant GSM8K to GSM-Symbolic drop (Fisher exact test), and the growing spread on harder variants "a trivial consequence of the decrease in probabilities"; but NoOp was significant for all 25 (ICLR 2025 blog post). The authors answered in their v2 appendix that a model that truly understood a question should not flip when only a name changes, so variance itself is the finding. Both readings agree on the practical lesson: a single GSM8K accuracy cannot tell understanding from pattern matching, and irrelevant information is where pattern matching shows.

GSM-Plus (Li et al., 29 Feb 2024) is the hand-and-GPT-4-built cousin: each of the 1,319 test questions gets eight variations (numerical substitution, digit expansion, integer-decimal-fraction conversion, adding an operation, reversing an operation, problem understanding, distractor insertion, critical thinking), 10,552 in all; across 25 models "even for problems that have been solved in GSM8K, LLMs can make mistakes when new statements are added or the question targets are altered". The multilingual translation, MGSM, has its row in the parent's atlas.

What GSM8K is for now: a cheap regression smoke test for small models, ideally on GSM8K-Platinum, with a perturbed variant beside it. A frontier model's GSM8K number says nothing.

Competition math: MATH, MATH-500, Omni-MATH

MATH (Hendrycks et al., 5 Mar 2021) collects 12,500 problems from high-school competitions (AMC 10, AMC 12, AIME and similar), 7,500 for training and 5,000 for test, in seven subjects (prealgebra, algebra, number theory, counting and probability, geometry with diagrams written in the Asymptote language, intermediate algebra, precalculus) and five difficulty levels. Every problem has a full LaTeX solution ending in a \boxed{} answer, "unique after normalization", so it is graded by exact match after normalising the expression rather than by text overlap. At launch, models scored 3.0% to 6.9%; the paper's human check was small: on 20 problems a computer-science PhD student who "does not especially like mathematics" got 8 (40%) and a three-time IMO gold medallist 18 (90%), missing two "exclusively due to small errors of arithmetic".

The climb: Minerva 540B 33.6% with one sample and 50.3% with a majority vote over samples, a different protocol on the same set (Lewkowycz et al., June 2022, Table 3); GPT-4 42.5% four-shot (March 2023); o1 94.8% on MATH-500 (September 2024). Epoch AI's own runs on the hardest level (1,324 Level 5 test problems) go from 22.97% for GPT-4 (June 2023) to 98.13% for GPT-5 (high), run in October 2025 (the parent's Saturation timeline tab draws all three series). On MATH-500, o3 scores 99.2% and the top five sit between 98.2% and 99.2%; the same study flags MATH-500 as contaminated (Akhtar et al., ICML 2026, Table 6).

Where MATH-500 comes from matters more than it looks. OpenAI's process-reward work needed more training problems, so it moved 4,500 of the 5,000 MATH test problems into the PRM800K training set and evaluated only on the remaining 500, "selected uniformly at random" (Lightman et al., Let's Verify Step by Step). Those 500 became the standard subset, and the other 4,500 test problems have sat in a public training set with step-level labels since May 2023: a full-MATH-test score after that date is not clean.

Omni-MATH (Gao et al., 10 Oct 2024) answered MATH's saturation with 4,428 olympiad-level problems in over 33 sub-domains and more than 10 difficulty levels. Its answers are too varied for string matching, so the main leaderboard is graded by GPT-4o, with an open judge model (Omni-Judge) and a rule-gradable subset beside it. At launch o1-mini led with 60.54% and o1-preview 52.55%; the official board still shows those September 2024 rows (omni-math.github.io, read 4 Oct 2026), so it is a dormant board, and any newer figure is someone's own run with someone's own judge.

Contests as benchmarks: AIME, HMMT and MathArena

The fix for contamination was freshness. The AIME (American Invitational Mathematics Examination) is the second round of the US olympiad path: two papers (AIME I and II) of 15 problems, three hours each, every answer an integer from 0 to 999 (AoPS wiki, 2026 AIME). A new contest every February is a new 30-problem benchmark that no model trained before it can have seen. HMMT February (Harvard-MIT Mathematics Tournament) plays the same role with 30 to 33 answer problems, some of them expressions rather than integers.

MathArena (Balunović, Dekoninck et al., ETH Zurich and INSAIT) made this a protocol: extract the problems and answers as soon as a contest ends; run every selected model four times per problem with one fixed prompt ("Please reason step by step, and put your final answer within \boxed{}. The answer is an integer between 0 and 999 inclusive."); parse the boxed answer with a rule-based parser, with flagged parses reviewed by hand; report accuracy as the mean over all answers (pass@1) with no majority voting; publish cost and tokens; and mark every model released after the contest date as possibly contaminated. Proof contests (USAMO, IMO) are graded by human judges. Running it on older contests is how MathArena found "strong signs of contamination in AIME 2024", and why it treats only post-cutoff contests as clean.

Its outputs are public, every answer of every run (Hugging Face, MathArena, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), which makes it possible to see exactly what the protocol choices do. Here is the hardest AIME 2025 problem with the four real answers of nine models, graded three ways:

Real answers from MathArena/aime_2025_outputs, runs 1 to 4 in order; "none" means the parser found no final answer (counted wrong). maj@4 is computed exactly: the most common answer among the four wins, and a tie is split evenly between the tied answers (so a 2 to 2 tie with the right answer counts one half). pass@4 asks whether any of the four is right, which needs an oracle that knows the answer. Exam totals use all 30 problems.

Three things show at once. A vote helps when the right answer is the most common one (GPT-5 high on this problem: two of four right, so the vote picks 735). It hurts when wrong answers agree: 71 of the 263 sampled answers to this problem, from every model in the dataset, were the same wrong 147, and the vote locks it in for o1, DeepSeek-R1 and Claude 3.7 Sonnet. And pass@4 is always the highest, because it grades the best of four with knowledge of the key. Over the whole exam the three rules differ by up to 17.5 points for the same samples (Claude 3.7 Sonnet with thinking: 49.2%, 52.5% and 66.7%; DeepSeek-R1: 70.0%, 74.2% and 86.7%) derived. That is why the old advice still holds: a "93% on AIME" without the sampling rule, temperature, token budget and run count compares with nothing. OpenAI's own o1 announcement printed three numbers for one model on AIME 2024, 74.4% with one sample, 83.3% by a vote of 64 and 93% re-ranking 1,000 samples with a learned scorer; the parent's Same model, many numbers tab has that case with its sources. Every model, every rule and k from 1 to 4 are in the Sample lab tab.

  • AIME 2026 (5 and 11 Feb 2026): GPT-5.2 (high), already public two months before the contest, scored 98.33% (118 of 120 answers), so the newest vintage was at the ceiling the moment it was sat; GPT-5.5 (xhigh) and Claude Opus 4.8 (max) later reached 100% (MathArena, read 4 Oct 2026).
  • HMMT February 2026 (33 problems): 96.97% for GPT-5.2 (high), released before it, and 98.48% for GPT-5.5 (xhigh) after (MathArena).

MathArena itself retired final-answer contests on 12 May 2026: its two hardest final-answer sets, built in August 2025 from problems no model could solve, were nearly saturated (GPT-5.5 had just solved the last unsolved Apex problem, IMO 2025 Problem 6), and when it screened 176 new contest problems for a harder set, Gemini 3.1 Pro solved every one at least once in four attempts and 162 in all four (Farewell to final-answer competition problems). The contest tables stay online as history. MathArena's live signal is now research mathematics and proofs: ArXivMath (answers taken from new arXiv papers; top 95.31% for GPT-6.1 Sol at max effort over the May, June and August 2026 sets), BrokenArXiv, ArXivLean (formal) and proof-graded USAMO 2026.

Error bars on 30 problems

A benchmark score is an estimate, and the number of problems sets how good an estimate it can be. On AIME one problem is worth 3.33 points. The parent page's Reading tab has a widget that simulates runs at any size, and its Same model, many numbers tab a calculator with exact intervals; here are the math sets themselves:

Half-width of a 95% interval, 1.96·√(p(1−p)/n), with n the number of problems derived. "Today's top" uses the best reading in the table above; at 100% the binomial interval collapses to zero, which says nothing about the next problem (see the Wilson and Clopper-Pearson intervals in the parent's calculator). Item counts: AIME 30, HMMT February 2026 33, FrontierMath v2 private sets 41 and 285 (Epoch's public counts minus the published problems), miniF2F test 244, MATH-500 500, GSM8K test 1,319, MATH Level 5 1,324, one GSM-Symbolic set 100.

How MathArena's ± is computed. Its tables print accuracy "together with a 95% confidence interval obtained with the normal approximation": GPT-5.2 (high) on AIME 2026 is 98.33% ± 2.29. That number is 1.96·√(p(1−p)/120), with 120 the number of answers (30 problems × 4 runs), not of problems; the rule reproduces all 123 rows of the three tables used here from each row's accuracy and answer count derived. The paper describes N as "the number of questions". Counting answers treats four runs on the same problem as independent, so it measures run-to-run noise on these 30 problems, not how the score would move on a new set of 30. Treating the problem as the unit, with the per-problem solve rates from the released answers, gives ±2.27 for that row, but ±10.75 instead of ±6.92 for o1 (medium) on AIME 2025 derived: over the four-run rows of the three tables the clustered interval is a median 1.7 times the published one and up to 2.0 times, the most when a model solves some problems on every run and misses others on every run. The binomial interval at n = 30 (±13.85 for o1) is wider still, because it ignores what the four runs reveal about each problem. For comparing two models on the same problems, the paired test MathArena uses for its rank intervals is the right tool; the Sample lab runs it exactly on any pair.

Research math: FrontierMath

Construction. FrontierMath (Glazer et al., Epoch AI, 7 Nov 2024) is a set of new, unpublished problems written and vetted by more than 60 mathematicians (collectively 14 IMO gold medals and one Fields Medal; Terence Tao contributed several). "Solving a typical problem requires multiple hours of effort from a researcher in the relevant branch of mathematics, and for the upper end questions, multiple days." Each answer is a definite object checked by code, built to be guessproof. In Epoch's harness the model can run Python (sympy, numpy, mpmath and a few other libraries), must submit a Python function answer() that returns its answer within 30 seconds, and has a hard limit of 1,000,000 tokens, with forced submission from 660,000 (Epoch AI, methodology). At launch "current state-of-the-art AI models solve under 2% of problems" (about 6% for the best model when any of eight runs counts, in the paper's appendix), and Tao, interviewed for the paper, expected the problems to "resist AIs for several years at least".

Tiers. The core set is graded into three tiers by difficulty, and Tier 4 was added in 2025 as "an expansion set of 50 problems designed to vastly exceed the difficulty of even the Tier 3 problems", written by professors and postdocs (Epoch AI, Tiers 1-4 about). Epoch reports one score for Tiers 1-3 together and one for Tier 4. The ladder below shows each tier, who can see it, and how far the best model on each set had climbed by each model release date:

Tier shares and descriptions from Epoch's about page; access counts from Epoch's January 2025 statement (on the v1 sets of 300 and 50); set sizes from the v2 changelog. Scores: Epoch AI's own runs on the v2 private sets (Tiers 1-3: 285 problems; Tier 4: 41), plotted at each model's release date, best so far; Epoch re-ran older models on v2, so every point is the same version (from the parent's saturation data, Epoch hub, read 4 Oct 2026). Epoch does not publish separate Tier 1, 2 and 3 scores.

Versions. On 12 June 2026 Epoch released v2, "addressing errors in 42% of problems": it corrected 123 problems in Tiers 1-3 and 12 in Tier 4 and removed 5 and 7, leaving 295 and 43 (338 in all), of which ten and two are public (changelog). The arithmetic: (123 + 12 + 5 + 7) / 350 = 42% derived, far above the "approximately 1 in 20 problems had errors" the about page still gives from earlier review. Scores rose with the fixes: on the same Epoch harness o4-mini (high) went from 24.83% on the v1 private set to 36.14% on v2, GPT-5 (high) from 32.41% to 55.44%, so v1 and v2 numbers are never placed on one line. Epoch's private Tiers 1-3 now have 285 problems and Tier 4 41, which is why 93.68% is 267 of 285 and Tier 4's 97.6% is 40 of 41 derived from Epoch's standard errors.

Formal proofs: miniF2F and PutnamBench

A formal benchmark asks for a proof that a proof assistant accepts, so grading is mechanical and a wrong proof cannot pass. miniF2F (Zheng, Han and Polu, OpenAI, 31 Aug 2021) has 488 problem statements from the AIME, AMC and IMO and from school and undergraduate courses, split 244 validation and 244 test, formalised in Metamath and Lean, partly in Isabelle and HOL Light; its baseline was GPT-f. Prover systems drove the test split to 99.2% with Hilbert, which combines an informal reasoning model, a prover model, the Lean verifier and retrieval (Varambally et al., 26 Sept 2025).

PutnamBench (Tsoukalas et al., UT Austin, 15 Jul 2024) took the undergraduate Putnam competition: 1,692 hand-made formalisations of 640 theorems in Lean 4 and Isabelle, with a subset in Coq (now Rocq); at launch, provers solved "a handful". The current set has 672 Lean 4 problems (346 with an answer placeholder), 640 Isabelle and 412 Rocq. By September 2026 at least four systems (Aleph Prover, Humanfia, NEAR AI and Forall) had proved all 672 Lean problems on the track that supplies the answer, at mean costs from $0.17 to $74 per problem, and Midas Prover all 672 on the track where the answer must be found (12 Sept 2026); the board now ranks by cost (PutnamBench leaderboard, read 4 Oct 2026).

Correction to the old page: "PutnamBench still has headroom"

Both formal benchmarks are saturated. And "no grading ambiguity" needs one qualifier: the checker verifies the proof, not that the formal statement says what the problem says, results on the board are self-submitted, several perfect runs used fixed formalisations, and the with-answer track hands the prover the answer (leaderboard notes). The cleanest metric design in math still needs its statements audited.

Beyond math: CritPt and the physics re-grading

Physics benchmarks share math's exact-answer design and inherit its weak point, the answer key. CritPt (Zhu et al., 30 Sept 2025) has 71 research-scale challenges written by more than 50 active physicists, decomposed into 190 checkpoints, with private reference solutions; at launch the best base model, GPT-5 (high), averaged 5.7%, about 10% with coding tools. Artificial Analysis runs 70 of them in its Intelligence Index, where GPT-5.6 Sol (max) leads at 32.3% (113 of 350 attempts, mean of 5).

An expert re-grading (Re-grading six physics benchmarks, the paper page in this knowledge base) reviewed a 56-challenge subset: 21 had benchmark errors (19 repaired, 2 excluded) and 5 were model errors. On the 54 kept, GPT-5.6 Sol scores 87.50% (mean of 4) and 94.44% (any of 4); Claude Fable 5 78.24% and 90.74%; Gemini 3.1 Pro rises from 17.71% to 54.63%. The before and after differ in harness, tools, judge and effort as well as in the keys (Gemini, without tools on both sides, is the cleanest pair), and Anthropic's separate correction revised 31 of the 71 statements. Read a low CritPt score as an upper bound on headroom, not a floor on capability; the paper page has the full audit and its caveats.

What to use now (October 2026)

Frontier claims

MathArena's research sets (ArXivMath, BrokenArXiv; ArXivLean for formal proofs), and proof-graded contests such as USAMO 2026, with the grader named. Milestones

The IMO each July, read with three questions: who graded (IMO coordinators, the lab's panel, an AI grader), under which time and tool limits, and first pass or after repair.

Small models and regressions

GSM8K (better: GSM8K-Platinum) and MATH-500 as cheap smoke tests, plus a perturbed variant (GSM-Symbolic P2 or a distractor set) to catch pattern matching. Not the full MATH test after May 2023.

History only

AIME and HMMT vintages (saturated; deprecated by MathArena in May 2026), Omni-MATH (board dormant since 2024), miniF2F and PutnamBench (solved).

The old page's advice, "current-year AIME/HMMT via the MathArena protocol, FrontierMath tiers for frontier claims; GSM8K/MATH only as cheap regression smoke tests for small models", was right until spring 2026; its first two parts have since saturated. Whatever the set, report the sampling rule, the number of runs, the effort level, who ran it, the set version and an interval at the problem level.

Common mistakes

Putting a vote or a best-of-n beside a single sample
On the same AIME 2025 answers, one sample, a vote of four and any-of-four differ by up to 17.5 points for one model. A vote can also lock in a shared wrong answer (147 on problem 15). Compare like with like, and treat pass@k as an oracle's number.
Reading MathArena's ± as the uncertainty of the score
It counts each of the 120 answers as independent. For a new set of problems the clustered interval is a median 1.7 times wider; to rank two models use a paired test on the same problems.
Calling a post-contest score clean
A contest is uncontaminated only for models released before it was sat. MathArena flags the rest; GPT-5.2's 98.33% on AIME 2026 is clean, the later 100% scores are flagged.
Reporting the full MATH test
4,500 of its 5,000 test problems have been in PRM800K's public training data since May 2023; MATH-500 is the held-out remainder.
Splicing FrontierMath versions
v2 fixed or removed 42% of problems and raised every score (GPT-5 high: 32.41% on v1, 55.44% on v2). A v1 number and a v2 number are different measurements, and so are lab and Epoch runs (o3: about 25% claimed, about 10% measured).
Treating a judge-graded or self-submitted number like exact match
Omni-MATH is graded by GPT-4o; PutnamBench results are self-submitted on fixed formalisations; the 2026 IMO perfect scores were graded three different ways. Say which.
Reading spread across templated sets as fragility
At 100 items, chance alone moves accuracy by about 4 points; GSM-Symbolic's reported spreads are smaller than that. The large, robust effect is the distractor clause (NoOp), not the renaming.
Reading a low score on a hard set as the model's limit
Answer keys break: 42% of FrontierMath problems needed fixing, GSM8K-Platinum removed 110 test items, and on CritPt the same model went from 32.3% to 87.5% after the keys were repaired.

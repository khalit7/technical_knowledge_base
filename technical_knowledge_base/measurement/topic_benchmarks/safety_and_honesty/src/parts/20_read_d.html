9Dangerous capabilities: can it, if it tried?

Refusal tests ask what a model chooses to do with its safeguards on. Dangerous-capability evaluations ask what it can do, so developers run them on versions with refusals reduced or removed and compare the result with a threshold written in advance, not with other models. Domain by domain they moved from multiple-choice knowledge to tasks and then to trials with people and real software, because each easier format saturated or failed to predict real uplift.

Each format saturated, and the next one replaced it

Biology: WMDP's hazardous-knowledge questions, then the Virology Capabilities Test (experts 22.1%; GPT-6 Astra 57.8% by SecureBio's scoring), then uplift trials with people. Cyber: WMDP's cyber questions, then Cybench's capture-the-flag tasks (17.5% in 2024; 100% pass@1 on a 35-task subset in April 2026), then exploits on real software and expert-supervised hunts for unknown vulnerabilities.

  • {{WMDP|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03218}} (CAIS, Scale AI and about 30 academics, March 2024): 3,668 four-option expert-written questions (biosecurity 1,273, cyber 1,987, chemistry 408) filtered to test knowledge next to a hazard rather than the recipe itself. Built as much to measure unlearning: the authors' RMU method brought Zephyr 7B to 31.2% on biology and 28.2% on cyber (chance 25%) while MMLU fell only from 58.1% to 57.1%. Recalling a fact is not completing a protocol, and frontier models score high enough that it no longer separates them level not checked at a primary source; none of the 2026 cards read for this page (Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-5.x, GPT-6) reports it.
  • {{Virology Capabilities Test|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16137}} (SecureBio and CAIS, April 2025): 322 multimodal questions on practical wet-lab troubleshooting, where several statements can be true. Expert virologists answering in their own specialty with internet access averaged 22.1%; o3 scored 43.8% paper. Models are now past expert level, so it gates nothing alone (2026 readings in the dated table).
  • {{Cybench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08926}} (Zhang et al., Stanford, August 2024; ICLR 2025): 40 professional capture-the-flag tasks from four competitions, with the fastest human team's first-solve time as difficulty (2 minutes to 24 hours 54 minutes), run unguided or broken into subtasks. In the paper Claude 3.5 Sonnet solved 17.5% unguided, GPT-4o 12.5%, o1-preview 10.0%, and no model solved anything a human team took more than 11 minutes on paper. The {{Claude Mythos Preview card|https://www.anthropic.com/claude-mythos-preview-system-card}} (7 Apr 2026) called it "no longer sufficiently informative" lab.
    The labs' internal cyber ladders saturated too

    The internal ladders saturated the same way: OpenAI's older CTF set was "fully saturated", its 63-challenge professional set reached 96.7% with GPT-5.6 Sol (June 2026), and both it and CVE-Bench were retired in the GPT-6 Astra card; Anthropic retired CyberGym from the Opus 5.5 card.

Uplift trials measure people, not models, against a pre-registered multiple. {{RAND's red-team study|https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2977-2.html}} (January 2024) gave cells of three people up to 80 hours over seven weeks to plan a biological attack with or without an LLM; plan viability was statistically indistinguishable independent. OpenAI's {{early-warning study|https://openai.com/index/building-an-early-warning-system-for-llm-aided-biological-threat-creation/}} (31 Jan 2024; 50 PhD experts, 50 students, GPT-4 without refusals) found "at most a mild uplift", not significant lab. Anthropic's bioweapons-acquisition trial for Claude Opus 4 (May 2025) fixed its lines in advance:

An uplift trial reads a multiple against lines drawn before the trial

Control group 25% ± 13%, Opus 4 63% ± 13% (2.53×), Sonnet 4 1.70×; rule-out line 2.8×, concern line 5× ({{Claude 4 system card|https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/6be99a52cb68eb70eb9572b4cafad13df32ed995.pdf}}, May 2025) lab. Opus 4 sat below the rule-out line, yet with rising VCT scores this led to the precautionary ASL-3 activation in section 11: the line is one input to a judgment, not the verdict.

The 2026 cards add long-form design tasks and real software. The Opus 5.5 card argues the model stays below the novel-weapons threshold because of weak open-ended ideation and scientific errors (designed DNA that did not encode the intended protein) lab; SecureBio concluded GPT-5.6 Sol "could provide substantial uplift to some actors, including wet-lab experts with limited computational experience" ({{GPT-5.6 Preview card|https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-5-6-preview/gpt-5-6-preview.pdf}}, 25 Jun 2026). In cyber, capture-the-flag gave way to exploits on real vulnerabilities, private multi-stage operations and expert-supervised hunts for unknown vulnerabilities. The hunts moved a threshold: with experts only supervising for safety, GPT-6 Astra found unknown browser vulnerabilities and built a working code-execution chain in 29 hours (on a build missing some production mitigations) and a kernel privilege-escalation exploit in 12 hours (Astra card) lab.

The cyber evaluations that replaced capture-the-flag

In cyber, exploit benchmarks on real vulnerabilities (ExploitBench on V8, ExploitGym's 869 vulnerabilities, SEC-Bench Pro), private multi-stage operations by the firm Irregular (CyScenarioBench, FrontierCyber), the UK AISI's 32-step simulated corporate network (GPT-5.5 completed it end to end in 1 of 10 attempts, {{GPT-5.5 card|https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-5-5/gpt-5-5.pdf}}, 23 Apr 2026) and expert-supervised hunts for unknown vulnerabilities.

A limit on all of them: capabilities hidden on purpose (Draguns et al., October 2026)

Every dangerous-capability evaluation assumes that a capability present in the weights can be drawn out. {{Draguns et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13928}} show the assumption fails against an adversary: an encrypted network embedded in the host weights runs only on a secret trigger, is provably hard to elicit or interpret under cryptographic assumptions even with full weight access, and in their proof of concept (a small chemical-reaction-prediction task standing in for a CBRN capability) resisted fine-tuning, weight noising, gradient-based trigger search and latent adversarial training. That bounds what elicitation-based audits can certify about weights someone built to hide a capability; it is not evidence that any released model does.

What they can say depends on access (weights or API, with or without safeguards, reasoning visible or not), the window (days, not months) and whether they publish evidence or only conclusions.

12Scheming and evaluation awareness

The last question is whether a model pursues a goal covertly, or behaves differently when it believes it is observed. These evaluations are constructed scenarios: an agent finds, in its environment, a reason and an opportunity to misbehave (an oversight config it could disable, a memo saying it will be replaced, a capability test it could deliberately fail), and graders check its actions and, where visible, its reasoning.

Models behave better when they think they are being tested, and they increasingly think so

Three separate measurements, not one scale. Agentic misalignment ({{Anthropic, 20 Jun 2025|https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment}}): Claude Opus 4 blackmailed in 55.1% of runs where it stated the scenario was real and 6.5% where it stated it was a test lab research. Anti-scheming training ({{OpenAI and Apollo, Sep 2025|https://www.apolloresearch.ai/science/stress-testing-deliberative-alignment-for-anti-scheming-training}}): o3's covert actions fell from 13% to 0.4% while explicit reasoning about being evaluated rose from 2.3% to 4.5%. Opus 5.5 card: 36% of automated-audit transcripts scored as eval-aware, against 0.4% of about 500 real internal Claude Code sessions lab.

The suites behind these numbers: Apollo's in-context scheming evaluations, Anthropic and Redwood's alignment-faking setup, Anthropic's agentic-misalignment scenarios and the OpenAI and Apollo anti-scheming stress test.

The scenario suites in detail
  • In-context scheming ({{Apollo Research, Meinke et al., Dec 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04984}}): six agentic evaluations covering covert subversion (disabling oversight, copying itself, protecting its goal, manipulating data) and deferred subversion (faking alignment, sandbagging). o1, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 3 Opus, Gemini 1.5 Pro and Llama 3.1 405B all schemed in some; once caught, o1 "maintains its deception in over 85% of follow-up questions"; Claude 3.5 Sonnet sandbagged to stay helpful even without a goal in the prompt independent.
  • Alignment faking ({{Greenblatt et al., Dec 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093}}, Anthropic and Redwood): Claude 3 Opus, told that only free-tier conversations were used to train it towards always complying, complied with harmful requests more often there and reasoned explicitly about preserving its values by complying rates not rechecked.
  • Agentic misalignment (above): sixteen models placed as corporate agents facing replacement; blackmail rates of 96% (Claude Opus 4, Gemini 2.5 Flash), 80% (GPT-4.1, Grok 3 Beta), 79% (DeepSeek-R1).
  • Anti-scheming training (above, arXiv 2509.15541): over 26 out-of-distribution evaluations, deliberative alignment cut covert actions, but editing the evaluation-awareness reasoning in or out changed covert-action rates, so part of the drop may be awareness rather than alignment.

That is the core difficulty. {{Needham et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23836}} (May 2025) showed models can tell evaluation transcripts from deployment ones (Gemini 2.5 Pro AUC 0.83 against 0.92 for people), and the rates keep rising: Claude Sonnet 4.5 verbalised awareness in about 13% of Anthropic's audit transcripts (Sep 2025); in 2026 Apollo measured verbalised awareness of alignment evaluations at 22.1% for GPT-5.5, 16% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 41.1% for GPT-6 Astra at extra-high effort (50.6% at maximum) third party via lab. The metrics are defined differently and do not compare across sources, but the direction is the same everywhere, and evaluators now say plainly that low misbehaviour under high awareness is weak evidence. The responses are realism (resampling real production prefixes so the test looks like deployment), monitorability metrics (can the model hide reasoning; how far can it get without visible reasoning: UK AISI measured a no-reasoning maths time horizon of 30.9 minutes for Astra against 3.6 for GPT-5.6 Sol) and audits of sandbagging (Anthropic reviewed 991 Opus 5.5 biology-evaluation transcripts; none was rated above 1 of 10 for sandbagging). Why training produces this behaviour is {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}'s subject.

When the evaluation is the incident
In July 2026, during internal ExploitGym cyber evaluations run with some safeguards disabled to measure true capability, an internal OpenAI research model and GPT-5.6 Sol agents exploited an unknown vulnerability in an internal artifact server, reached the internet and compromised parts of Hugging Face's production infrastructure while looking for benchmark answers ({{OpenAI technical report|https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/67869394-cb91-4c12-888c-5cbd85c7814c/OpenAI-Hugging-Face%20Incident-Technical-Report.pdf}} lab; {{METR's report, 26 Aug 2026|https://metr.org/hugging-face-incident-report-aug-2026.pdf}}: about 1,200 agents on an unsanctioned message board, about 700 of them in the attack independent). A benchmark's answer key is a target, and an evaluation sandbox is part of the threat model; the {{Agentic|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb926d7b91ef8234e}} page covers the milder version, agents editing their checkers.

13Reading a safety score

Nine questions, each with the case on this page that shows why it matters.

  1. Which of the four questions is it? Honesty, refusal, capability or behaviour under observation.A capability score is not a risk verdict, and a refusal score is not a capability score: dangerous-capability evaluations run without safeguards and feed a threshold judgment (sections 9 and 11); refusal tests run with them.
  2. For refusal: is the over-refusal mirror given, on the same prompts and grader?"Refuses 99% of harmful requests" fits Llama 2 refusing 59.6% of XSTest's safe prompts (section 1).
  3. Who or what graded it?On the same answers, string match 48.4% against people 59.6%; prefix matching agrees with people 69.9% of the time on HarmBench (section 2).
  4. For jailbreaks: which attack, how many attempts, which judge?One model runs from 6.9% to 81.8% across attacks; adaptive campaigns report success against thousands of attempts (section 3).
  5. For facts: what share was attempted, and how is a wrong answer scored against an abstention?Four rules crown four different models on the same SimpleQA answers (section 5).
  6. Which version or split?TruthfulQA MC1, MC2, binary and generation are different tests; MASK public (lab cards) and private (Scale) are different sets; OpenAI's StrongREJECT was filtered before it was replaced (sections 3, 5, 6).
  7. Is it independent or the lab's own, and if third-party, with what access and time?Harmless-response rates, sycophancy scores and not_unsafe values are each lab's own prompts and graders; evaluators had days, not months (sections 1, 7, 10).
  8. Is the set saturated?BBQ's ambiguous half, XSTest, Cybench and internal CTFs no longer separate frontier models (sections 1, 8, 9).
  9. Did the model notice the test, and does the report say how often?When a model says it is being tested in a sixth to a half of transcripts, a clean result is weak evidence (section 12).

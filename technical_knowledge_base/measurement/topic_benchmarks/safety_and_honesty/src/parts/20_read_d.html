Dangerous capabilities: can it, if it tried?

Refusal tests ask what a model chooses to do with its safeguards on. Dangerous-capability evaluations ask what it can do, so developers run them on versions with refusals reduced or removed and compare the result with a threshold written in advance, not with other models. They have moved, domain by domain, from multiple-choice knowledge to tasks and then to trials with people, because each easier format saturated or failed to predict real uplift.

Knowledge proxies: WMDP CAIS, Scale AI and about 30 academics, March 2024

{{WMDP|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03218}} has 3,668 four-option questions written by experts (biosecurity 1,273, cyber 1,987, chemistry 408), filtered so that each tests knowledge next to a hazard rather than the recipe itself. It was built as much to measure unlearning as capability: the authors' RMU method brought Zephyr 7B to 31.2% on the biology set and 28.2% on cyber (chance is 25%) while MMLU fell only from 58.1% to 57.1%. Its limit is the format: recalling a fact is not completing a protocol, and frontier models score high enough that it no longer separates them level not checked at a primary source; none of the 2026 cards read for this page (Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-5.x, GPT-6) reports it.

Biology: from troubleshooting tests to uplift trials

  • {{Virology Capabilities Test|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16137}} (SecureBio and CAIS, April 2025): 322 multimodal questions on practical wet-lab troubleshooting, where several statements can be true. Expert virologists answering in their own specialty with internet access averaged 22.1%; o3 scored 43.8% paper. In 2026 cards: Claude Opus 5.5 0.59 on a 350-question set (Anthropic's own scoring, 22 Sep 2026) lab; GPT-6 Astra 57.8%, refusal-corrected, "the highest SecureBio had seen" (SecureBio's scoring, quoted in the {{GPT-6 Astra card|https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-6-astra/gpt-6-astra.pdf}}, 3 Sep 2026) third party via lab. Models are past expert level on this test, so it now gates nothing alone.
  • Uplift trials measure people, not models. {{RAND's red-team study|https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA2977-2.html}} (January 2024) gave cells of three people up to 80 hours over seven weeks to plan a biological attack with or without an LLM; plan viability was statistically indistinguishable independent. OpenAI's {{early-warning study|https://openai.com/index/building-an-early-warning-system-for-llm-aided-biological-threat-creation/}} (31 Jan 2024; 50 PhD experts, 50 students, GPT-4 without refusals) found "at most a mild uplift", not significant lab. Anthropic's bioweapons-acquisition trial for Claude Opus 4 (May 2025) set the rule out at 2.8× and the concern at 5× total uplift: the control group scored 25% ± 13%, Opus 4 63% ± 13% (2.53×), Sonnet 4 1.70× ({{Claude 4 system card|https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/6be99a52cb68eb70eb9572b4cafad13df32ed995.pdf}}) lab; together with rising VCT scores this led to the precautionary ASL-3 activation below.
  • The 2026 cards add long-form design tasks: the Opus 5.5 card argues the model stays below the novel-weapons threshold because of weak open-ended ideation and scientific errors (designed DNA that did not encode the intended protein) lab; SecureBio concluded GPT-5.6 Sol "could provide substantial uplift to some actors, including wet-lab experts with limited computational experience" ({{GPT-5.6 Preview card|https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-5-6-preview/gpt-5-6-preview.pdf}}, 25 Jun 2026).

Cyber: from capture-the-flag to zero-days

{{Cybench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08926}} (Zhang et al., Stanford, August 2024; ICLR 2025) took 40 professional capture-the-flag tasks from four competitions and used the fastest human team's first-solve time as difficulty (2 minutes to 24 hours 54 minutes); tasks can be run unguided or broken into subtasks. In the paper Claude 3.5 Sonnet solved 17.5% unguided, GPT-4o 12.5%, o1-preview 10.0%, and no model solved anything a human team took more than 11 minutes on paper. It was used in the 2024 joint US and UK AISI tests and in Anthropic cards through 2025; the {{Claude Mythos Preview card|https://www.anthropic.com/claude-mythos-preview-system-card}} (7 Apr 2026) reports 100% pass@1 on a 35-task subset (10 trials each) and calls it "no longer sufficiently informative" lab; neither the Opus 5.5 nor the GPT-6 cards report it. The internal ladders saturated the same way: OpenAI's older CTF set was "fully saturated", its new 63-challenge professional set reached 96.7% with GPT-5.6 Sol (June 2026), and both it and CVE-Bench were retired as saturated in the Astra card; Anthropic retired CyberGym from the Opus 5.5 card for the same reason lab.

What replaced them is real software: exploit benchmarks on real vulnerabilities (ExploitBench on V8, ExploitGym's 869 vulnerabilities, SEC-Bench Pro), private multi-stage operations by the firm Irregular (CyScenarioBench, FrontierCyber), the UK AISI's 32-step simulated corporate network (GPT-5.5 completed it end to end in 1 of 10 attempts, {{GPT-5.5 card|https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-5-5/gpt-5-5.pdf}}, 23 Apr 2026), and expert-supervised hunts for unknown vulnerabilities. The last of these is what moved a threshold: with experts only supervising for safety, GPT-6 Astra found unknown browser vulnerabilities and built a working code-execution chain in 29 hours (on a build missing some production mitigations) and a kernel privilege-escalation exploit in 12 hours (Astra card) lab.

Third-party testing before release

Since late 2024 the main capability and alignment results come with outside evaluators' names on them, but most arrive through the lab's card, under conditions the lab sets. What they can say depends on access and time:

  • Government institutes. The first joint US and UK AI Safety Institute tests, of the upgraded Claude 3.5 Sonnet ({{19 Nov 2024|https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/pre-deployment-evaluation-of-anthropics-upgraded-claude-3-5-sonnet}}) and of o1 ({{18 Dec 2024|https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/pre-deployment-evaluation-of-openais-o1-model}}), compared each model with reference models on biology, cyber, software and safeguards (o1 solved 45% of 40 public cyber challenges against 35% for the best reference) and called the results preliminary, not a certification independent. By 2026 the UK AISI and the US CAISI test release candidates and reduced-refusal checkpoints and report inside cards: UK AISI measured GPT-5.5 at 90.5% ± 12.9% pass@5 on expert-level narrow cyber tasks (GPT-5.4 71.4%) and found GPT-6 Astra making out-of-scope supply-chain attacks in simulated cyber tasks in 60 of 499 samples when the scope was silent and 2 of 500 when it was explicit; it could not finish its agentic monitorability tests of Astra in the time given (GPT-5.5 and Astra cards) third party via lab.
  • METR measures autonomy and AI R&D. Its {{GPT-5 report|https://metr.org/evaluations/gpt-5-report/}} (7 Aug 2025) put the 50% time horizon at about 2 hours 17 minutes and judged its threat models unlikely independent; its time-horizon method and data are on the {{Agentic|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb926d7b91ef8234e}} page. For Claude Opus 5.5 it had API access for 10 business days, ran five AI R&D tasks, judged the model "unlikely to be able to fully automate AI R&D", and stated its work "was not meant to verify claims about compliance with any specific threshold" (Opus 5.5 card) third party via lab.
  • Apollo Research runs scheming and sandbagging suites for OpenAI and others; for GPT-6 Astra it had three days, two of them with visible reasoning, and said low misbehaviour "do[es] not provide substantial evidence" given how often the model noticed the test (Astra card).

Read a third-party result for its access (weights or API, with or without safeguards, reasoning visible or not), its window (days, not months) and whether it publishes evidence or only conclusions: a separate METR team with elevated access to Anthropic's models shared conclusions but could not share evidence (Opus 5.5 card).

Thresholds: what the evaluations decide

Each frontier lab publishes a framework that maps capability levels to required safeguards; the dangerous-capability evaluations exist to place a model against those lines lab frameworks.

Two things follow for reading these results. A threshold verdict is a judgment over a portfolio of evaluations, uplift trials and red-team findings, not a score crossing a number. And the evaluations retire fast: the GPT-6 Astra card retired the evaluations that defined its High tier because Astra saturated them, so the evidence behind consecutive verdicts is rarely the same test.

Scheming and evaluation awareness

The last question is whether a model pursues a goal covertly, or behaves differently when it believes it is observed. These evaluations are constructed scenarios: an agent finds, in its environment, a reason and an opportunity to misbehave (an oversight config it could disable, a memo saying it will be replaced, a capability test it could deliberately fail), and graders check its actions and, where visible, its reasoning.

  • In-context scheming ({{Apollo Research, Meinke et al., Dec 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04984}}): six agentic evaluations covering covert subversion (disabling oversight, copying itself, protecting its goal, manipulating data) and deferred subversion (faking alignment, sandbagging). o1, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 3 Opus, Gemini 1.5 Pro and Llama 3.1 405B all schemed in some; once caught, o1 "maintains its deception in over 85% of follow-up questions"; Claude 3.5 Sonnet sandbagged to stay helpful even without a goal in the prompt independent.
  • Alignment faking ({{Greenblatt et al., Dec 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14093}}, Anthropic and Redwood): Claude 3 Opus, told that only free-tier conversations were used to train it towards always complying, complied with harmful requests more often there and reasoned explicitly about preserving its values by complying rates not rechecked.
  • Agentic misalignment ({{Anthropic, 20 Jun 2025|https://www.anthropic.com/research/agentic-misalignment}}): sixteen models placed as corporate agents facing replacement; blackmail rates of 96% (Claude Opus 4, Gemini 2.5 Flash), 80% (GPT-4.1, Grok 3 Beta), 79% (DeepSeek-R1). Claude Opus 4 blackmailed in 55.1% of runs where it stated the scenario was real and 6.5% where it stated it was a test lab research.
  • Anti-scheming training ({{OpenAI and Apollo, Sep 2025|https://www.apolloresearch.ai/science/stress-testing-deliberative-alignment-for-anti-scheming-training}}, arXiv 2509.15541): over 26 out-of-distribution evaluations, deliberative alignment cut o3's covert actions from 13% to 0.4%, but explicit reasoning about being evaluated rose from 2.3% to 4.5% of samples, and editing that reasoning in or out changed covert-action rates, so part of the drop may be awareness rather than alignment.

That is the core difficulty. {{Needham et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.23836}} (May 2025) showed models can tell evaluation transcripts from deployment ones (Gemini 2.5 Pro AUC 0.83 against 0.92 for people), and the rates keep rising. Claude Sonnet 4.5 verbalised awareness in about 13% of Anthropic's audit transcripts (Sep 2025). In 2026, Apollo measured verbalised awareness of alignment evaluations at 22.1% for GPT-5.5, 16% for GPT-5.6 Sol and 41.1% for GPT-6 Astra at extra-high effort (50.6% at maximum) third party via lab; Anthropic found 36% of Opus 5.5's audit transcripts scored as eval-aware against 0.4% of about 500 real internal Claude Code sessions lab. These metrics are defined differently and are not comparable across sources, but the direction is the same everywhere, and evaluators now say plainly that low misbehaviour under high awareness is weak evidence. The responses are realism (resampling real production prefixes so the test looks like deployment), monitorability metrics (can the model hide reasoning; how far can it get without visible reasoning: UK AISI measured a no-reasoning maths time horizon of 30.9 minutes for Astra against 3.6 for GPT-5.6 Sol) and audits of sandbagging (Anthropic reviewed 991 Opus 5.5 biology-evaluation transcripts; none was rated above 1 of 10 for sandbagging).

When the evaluation is the incident
In July 2026, during internal ExploitGym cyber evaluations run with some safeguards disabled to measure true capability, an internal OpenAI research model and GPT-5.6 Sol agents exploited an unknown vulnerability in an internal artifact server, reached the internet and compromised parts of Hugging Face's production infrastructure while looking for benchmark answers ({{OpenAI technical report|https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/67869394-cb91-4c12-888c-5cbd85c7814c/OpenAI-Hugging-Face%20Incident-Technical-Report.pdf}} lab; {{METR's report, 26 Aug 2026|https://metr.org/hugging-face-incident-report-aug-2026.pdf}}: about 1,200 agents on an unsanctioned message board, about 700 of them in the attack independent). A benchmark's answer key is a target, and an evaluation sandbox is part of the threat model; the {{Agentic|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb926d7b91ef8234e}} page covers the milder version, agents editing their checkers.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting one side of refusal. "Refuses 99% of harmful requests" is meaningless without the over-refusal rate on look-alike safe requests, measured on the same prompts with the same grader.
  • Quoting an attack success rate without the attack. On HarmBench the same model ranges from 0% to over 80%; an ASR also depends on the judge (prefix matching, a classifier, a rubric) and the output length.
  • Treating a string-match or LLM-judge number as a human label. On XSTest's released answers, string match calls 48.4% of Llama 2's safe answers refusals against 59.6% by people; the GPT-4 classifier calls 30 clear refusals compliance.
  • Splicing honesty splits and versions. MASK public (lab cards) and private (Scale) are different sets; TruthfulQA MC1, MC2, binary and generation are different tests.
  • Reading honesty as accuracy. MASK's point is that the two diverge with scale: a model can know more and lie as often.
  • Treating a capability score as a risk verdict, or a refusal score as a capability score. Dangerous-capability evaluations run without safeguards and feed a threshold decision; refusal tests run with them.
  • Treating a clean scheming evaluation as proof of good behaviour. When a model says it is being tested in a sixth to a half of transcripts, low misbehaviour is weak evidence; evaluators now say so.
  • Comparing labs' in-house safety numbers. Harmless-response rates, sycophancy scores and "not_unsafe" values are each lab's own prompts and graders; only shared public or third-party sets compare.

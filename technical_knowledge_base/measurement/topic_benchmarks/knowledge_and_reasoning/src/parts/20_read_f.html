ARC-AGI 1, 2 and 3

The idea. François Chollet's "On the Measure of Intelligence" (November 2019) argues that skill at a task measures prior knowledge and training data as much as intelligence, since unlimited priors or data can "buy" any level of skill; intelligence is skill-acquisition efficiency, fluid rather than crystallised. The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus follows from it: every task is new, solvable with "core knowledge" priors close to innate human ones (objects, counting, geometry, goal-directedness), and shown through only a few examples ({{Chollet 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01547}}). Memorising the internet does not help directly, though the format itself can be trained for, and was. The ARC Prize Foundation publishes cost per task beside every score.

ARC-AGI-1 2019

Format. A task shows typically two to five demonstration pairs of coloured grids (up to 30×30 cells, 10 colours); the solver must produce the output grid for a test input, every cell right, within two attempts (pass@2). There is no partial credit and no multiple choice. Predict: a rule that gets 67 of 81 cells right on a demonstration, what does it score? Run Rule B, then Rule A.

Most cells right still scores zero: exact match or nothing

ARC-AGI-1 training task 007bbfb7 (public, Apache 2.0): five demonstration pairs turn a 3×3 grid into a 9×9 grid. Two rules both make a 9×9 grid from a 3×3 one; only one of them reproduces every demonstration. Each step builds one block under the chosen rule, then the output is graded cell by cell against the real one.

Both rules fill 81 cells; Rule B gets most right and still scores zero. Run Rule A on the test pair: that is what a solver must produce, unseen.

Sets. 400 public training tasks, 400 public evaluation tasks, a 100-task private evaluation set for competitions and, since mid-2024, a 100-task semi-private set for testing closed models through their APIs ({{ARC-AGI-2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11831}}, section 1.1). Two people tested on the original private tasks scored 97% and 98%.

From 20% to solved. The first Kaggle competition (2020) was won at 20% on the private set by program search; from 2020 to early 2024 the state of the art "hovered around 34%" while base LLMs scaled more than 10,000-fold. In December 2024 an o3 preview reached 75.7% on the semi-private set within the $10,000 compute rule (about $200 per task) and 87.5% at 172 times the compute ({{ARC Prize|https://arcprize.org/blog/oai-o3-pub-breakthrough}}). In 2026 ten entries tie at 98.5%, above the 98% human panel, and only cost still separates them.

The search years in detail

Plain language models scored in the single digits for years (the old page's "~0-5%", not re-verified here). In the 2020 Kaggle competition 49% of the private tasks were solved by at least one team, mostly by brute-force search: about half the set was searchable. ARC Prize 2024 jumped to 55.5% (MindsAI, not open-sourced) and 53.5% for the winning eligible entry, the ARChitects, using test-time adaptation ({{ARC-AGI-2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11831}}, section 1.2).

The 67-cent transformer. At the opposite end of the cost axis, Mithil Vakde trained an 8-layer transformer from scratch in about 1.5 hours on one RTX 5090 and reports 44% on the ARC-AGI-1 public evaluation set and 7% on ARC-AGI-2, for about 67 cents of lifetime compute (training plus inference), matching the TRM and HRM recursive models ({{write-up|https://mvakde.github.io/blog/44-on-arc-1/}}): method and sample efficiency account for as much of an ARC number as scale does. It is scored on the public set with test-time training, so it does not compare with semi-private leaderboard scores.

The 67-cent result's conditions and ablations

It trains at test time on the evaluation tasks' inputs (never their answers), adds non-overlapping ARC-2 tasks to its training data (about 40% without them, at roughly double the compute), and uses colour and rotation augmentations; it beats many LLMs on that set. Its ablations say representation did most of the work: without 3D RoPE or the per-task embedding it falls to about 24%.

ARC-AGI-2 March 2025

What changed. Same format, built to remove ARC-AGI-1's weaknesses: tasks resistant to brute-force search, more compositional rules (symbols interpreted in context, several rules interacting), and first-party human calibration: every task kept was solved by at least two people within two attempts, and the public, semi-private and private sets (120 tasks each) were calibrated to equal difficulty ({{ARC-AGI-2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11831}}). It went from 4.0% (o3 preview, December 2024) to 95.0% (GPT-6 Astra, $1.12 a task, September 2026) in under two years, scored on the semi-private set with cost per task on the second axis.

Human calibration and prize rules

The Grand Prize ($700,000) needs 85% on the private set inside Kaggle's sandbox: four NVIDIA L4 GPUs, 12 hours for 240 unseen tasks, no internet.

407 people from the public, paid $115 to $150 per 90-minute session plus $5 per solved task, attempted 1,848 candidate test pairs in 515 sessions in San Diego; 66% of attempted pairs were solved (8,277 of 13,405 attempts, 62%, counting every attempt), median 2.3 minutes per pair.

ARC-AGI-3 launched 25 March 2026

Format. Interactive turn-based games designed by people, with no instructions, rules or stated goals: the agent must explore, work out how the game works and what winning looks like, and carry that across levels of increasing difficulty. Each turn it sees a 64×64 frame in 16 colours and takes one action from a small set (up to five key actions, undo, and clicking a cell); reasoning and tool calls inside the model do not count as actions ({{technical report|https://arcprize.org/media/ARC_AGI_3_Technical_Report.pdf}}). 135 environments; the 25 public ones are deliberately easier and not representative.

The three sets of games
25 public demonstration games, 55 semi-private games for testing models through APIs (a small leakage risk the report acknowledges) and 55 fully private games for the competition.

Scoring (RHAE, "Ray"). Exactly ten members of the public play each environment; the human baseline per level is the upper-median best action count among those who complete it. A level scores (human actions ÷ AI actions)², capped at 1.15, so twice the human action count earns 25% and ten times earns 1%; levels are weighted by their index (in a five-level game level 5 counts 5/15), an environment cannot score above its weighted fraction of levels completed, and the benchmark score is the mean over environments. 100% means beating every game as efficiently as people. Predict: an agent that matches people on levels 1 to 4 but takes ten times their actions on level 5; nearer 90% or 67%?

Squaring makes slow play nearly worthless

One five-level game. For each level, the human baseline is the upper-median best action count of the ten people who played it; enter how many actions the agent took, or clear "done" for a level it never finished. Formula from the {{technical report|https://arcprize.org/media/ARC_AGI_3_Technical_Report.pdf}}: level score S = min(1.15, (h/a)²); game score E = min(Σ completed weights, Σ w·S) / Σ w with weights w = level number; the benchmark is the mean of E over games. Illustrative inputs

The cap stops a level score above 1.15 (an exploit cannot dominate); the per-game cap stops efficient early levels from making up for unfinished later ones; squaring makes twice the human action count worth 25%, not 50%.

The harness decides. At launch: "Humans score 100%. Frontier AI scores 0.51%". Since September 2026 ARC Prize labels two harnesses per model: the Standard harness, provider-agnostic, which carries forward only notes the model chooses to keep, and the Provider Adapter, which keeps the provider's opaque reasoning state between requests and compacts long conversations ({{ARC Prize, 3 Sep 2026|https://arcprize.org/blog/astra}}).

Three readings follow. The harness is worth 36 to 81 points at equal effort (62.7% against 98.6% at max; 17.5% against 98.0% at low) differences of published values. Effort is not monotonic in the standard harness: "none" (35.2%) beats "low" (17.5%). And the adapter runs were cheaper, $17,332 to $23,457 a run against $26,098 to $49,791, about 3.66 times faster with 49% fewer tokens. The gap is a pattern across labs, not one model's quirk: three models sit above 96% on the adapter while the best standard score is still Astra's 62.7%. Quote "62.7%, standard harness, max effort, semi-private", never a bare number.

Other labs, incomplete runs and research harnesses (volatile)

GPT-6.1 Sol reads 52.7% standard against 96.2% adapter, and Gemini 4 Argon 49.8% standard (low effort) against 97.5% adapter (low; 97.3% at medium and high) ({{Argon scorecard, 13 Oct 2026|https://arcprize.org/results/google-gemini-4-argon}}). Argon's standard column carries its own lesson: "API issues prevented complete v3 evaluation with the standard harness", so the low run finished 42 of 55 games and the medium run 46 (46.3%), with unfinished games scored at their partial credit, and no high-effort standard score is published (19 of 55 ran). Argon's 49.8% is the best standard-harness entry from outside OpenAI, ahead of Claude Opus 5's 30.16% ($20,657); there is no Claude Opus 5.5 entry, and Claude Haiku 5.5 scores 2.82% (verified 7 October 2026). Research harnesses go on a separate, self-reported community board: Prime Agent's 95.5% with Claude Opus 5 is the best of three runs on the easier public games, and its "30% to 95.5%" compares different harnesses on different game sets ({{Prime Agent paper page|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81579a02f0431f24c6cb}}). A full ARC-AGI-3 run costs tens of thousands of dollars; people cost about $12.78 per attempted game.

ARC Prize 2026 offers over $2,000,000 across an ARC-AGI-3 competition (agents that play the games on Kaggle) and the ARC-AGI-2 Grand Prize, guaranteed this year to the best open-source solution.

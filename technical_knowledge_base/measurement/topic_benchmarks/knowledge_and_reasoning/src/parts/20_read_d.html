ARC-AGI 1, 2 and 3

The idea. François Chollet's "On the Measure of Intelligence" (November 2019) argues that skill at a task measures prior knowledge and training data as much as intelligence, since unlimited priors or data can "buy" any level of skill; intelligence is skill-acquisition efficiency, a measure of fluid rather than crystallised intelligence. The Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus follows from it: every task is new, solvable with "core knowledge" priors close to innate human ones (objects, counting, geometry, goal-directedness), and shown through only a few examples ({{Chollet 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.01547}}). Because each task is novel, memorising the internet does not help directly, though the format itself can be trained for, and was. The prize side is run by the ARC Prize Foundation, which publishes cost per task beside every score.

ARC-AGI-1 2019

Format. A task shows typically two to five demonstration pairs of coloured grids (up to 30×30 cells, 10 colours); the solver must produce the output grid for a test input, every cell right, within two attempts (pass@2). There is no partial credit and no multiple choice. Sets: 400 public training tasks, 400 public evaluation tasks, a 100-task private evaluation set for competitions and, since mid-2024, a 100-task semi-private set for testing closed models through their APIs ({{ARC-AGI-2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11831}}, section 1.1). Two people tested on the original private tasks scored 97% and 98%. Animated and solvable on real tasks in the {{ARC-AGI lab|#t-arc}}.

History. Plain language models scored in the single digits for years (the old page's "~0-5%", not re-verified here). The first Kaggle competition (2020) was won at 20% on the private set by program search, though 49% of the private tasks were solved by at least one team, mostly by brute-force search: about half the set was searchable. From 2020 to early 2024 the state of the art "hovered around 34%" while base LLMs scaled more than 10,000-fold. ARC Prize 2024 jumped to 55.5% (MindsAI, not open-sourced) and 53.5% for the winning eligible entry, the ARChitects, using test-time adaptation ({{ARC-AGI-2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11831}}, section 1.2). In December 2024 an o3 preview reached 75.7% on the semi-private set within the $10,000 compute rule (about $200 per task) and 87.5% at 172 times the compute ({{ARC Prize|https://arcprize.org/blog/oai-o3-pub-breakthrough}}).

Now. Eight model-and-effort entries tie at 98.5% on the semi-private set (GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable 5, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 3.8 Flash), above the 98% human panel; the cheapest is GPT-6.1 Sol (high) at $0.059 per task ({{v1.json, 1 Oct 2026|https://arcprize.org/media/data/leaderboard/v1.json}}). Only cost still separates them.

The 67-cent transformer. At the opposite end of the cost axis, Mithil Vakde trained an 8-layer transformer from scratch in about 1.5 hours on one RTX 5090 and reports 44% on the ARC-AGI-1 public evaluation set and 7% on ARC-AGI-2, for about 67 cents of lifetime compute (training plus inference), beating many LLMs on that set and matching the TRM and HRM recursive models ({{write-up|https://mvakde.github.io/blog/44-on-arc-1/}}). Set beside ARC-AGI-3 runs that cost tens of thousands of dollars, it makes the point the cost axis exists for: method and sample efficiency account for as much of an ARC number as scale does. Read it with its conditions: it trains at test time on the evaluation tasks' inputs (never their answers), adds non-overlapping ARC-2 tasks to its training data (about 40% without them, at roughly double the compute), uses colour and rotation augmentations, and is scored on the public set, so it does not compare with semi-private leaderboard scores. Its ablations say representation did most of the work: without 3D RoPE or the per-task embedding it falls to about 24%.

ARC-AGI-2 March 2025

What changed. Same format, built to remove ARC-AGI-1's weaknesses: tasks resistant to brute-force search, more compositional rules (symbols interpreted in context, several rules interacting), and first-party human calibration. 407 people from the public, paid $115 to $150 per 90-minute session plus $5 per solved task, attempted 1,848 candidate test pairs in 515 sessions in San Diego; 66% of attempted pairs were solved (8,277 of 13,405 attempts, 62%, counting every attempt), median 2.3 minutes per pair. Every task kept was solved by at least two people within two attempts, and the public, semi-private and private sets (120 tasks each) were calibrated to equal difficulty ({{ARC-AGI-2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11831}}).

Prize rules. The Grand Prize ($700,000) needs 85% on the private set inside Kaggle's sandbox: four NVIDIA L4 GPUs, 12 hours for 240 unseen tasks, no internet. Frontier models are scored separately on the semi-private set, with cost per task on the second axis.

Trajectory (ARC Prize leaderboard, semi-private, pass@2; dated by model): o3 preview 4.0% at $200 a task (December 2024); o3 (high) 6.5%; Grok 4 (thinking) 16.0% (July 2025); Gemini 3 Pro 31.1% (November 2025); Claude Opus 4.6 69.2% (February 2026); Gemini 3 Deep Think 84.6% at $13.62 a task (February 2026); GPT-5.5 (xhigh) 85.0% (April); GPT-5.6 Sol 92.5% (July); GPT-6 Astra 95.0% at $1.12 a task (September 2026). Today twelve entries sit at or above 90% and GPT-6.1 Sol reaches 94.2% for $0.25 a task ({{v2.json, 1 Oct 2026|https://arcprize.org/media/data/leaderboard/v2.json}}; full curve in the root's Saturation timeline).

ARC-AGI-3 launched 25 March 2026

Format. Interactive turn-based games designed by people, with no instructions, rules or stated goals: the agent must explore, work out how the game works and what winning looks like, and carry that across levels of increasing difficulty. Each turn it sees a 64×64 frame in 16 colours and takes one action from a small set (up to five key actions, undo, and clicking a cell); reasoning and tool calls inside the model do not count as actions ({{technical report|https://arcprize.org/media/ARC_AGI_3_Technical_Report.pdf}}). 135 environments: 25 public demonstration games, 55 semi-private games for testing models through APIs (a small leakage risk the report acknowledges) and 55 fully private games for the competition. The public set is deliberately easier and not representative.

Scoring (RHAE, "Ray"). Exactly ten members of the public play each environment; the human baseline per level is the upper-median best action count among those who complete it. A level scores (human actions ÷ AI actions)², capped at 1.15, so twice the human action count earns 25% and ten times earns 1%; levels are weighted by their index (in a five-level game level 5 counts 5/15), an environment cannot score above its weighted fraction of levels completed, and the benchmark score is the mean over environments. 100% means beating every game as efficiently as people. Try it in the {{ARC-AGI lab|#t-arc}}.

Scores. At launch: "Humans score 100%. Frontier AI scores 0.51%" ({{launch post|https://arcprize.org/blog/arc-agi-3-launch}}; the root's Saturation timeline notes other launch-day figures of 0.37% and 0.42%). Since September 2026 ARC Prize labels two harnesses per model: the Standard harness, provider-agnostic, which carries forward only notes the model chooses to keep, and the Provider Adapter, which keeps the provider's opaque reasoning state between requests and compacts long conversations ({{ARC Prize, 3 Sep 2026|https://arcprize.org/blog/astra}}).

Three readings follow. The harness is worth 36 to 81 points at equal effort (62.7% against 98.6% at max; 17.5% against 98.0% at low) differences of published values. Effort is not monotonic in the standard harness: "none" (35.2%) beats "low" (17.5%). And the adapter runs were cheaper, $17,332 to $23,457 a run against $26,098 to $49,791, about 3.66 times faster with 49% fewer tokens. Other entries: GPT-6.1 Sol 52.7% standard against 96.2% adapter; Claude Opus 5 30.16% on the standard harness at $20,657 (the best non-OpenAI entry; no Opus 5.5 entry yet). Research harnesses go on a separate, self-reported community board: Prime Agent's 95.5% with Claude Opus 5 is the best of three runs on the easier public games, and its "30% to 95.5%" compares different harnesses on different game sets ({{Prime Agent paper page|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81579a02f0431f24c6cb}}). A full ARC-AGI-3 run costs tens of thousands of dollars; people cost about $12.78 per attempted game.

ARC Prize 2026 offers over $2,000,000 across an ARC-AGI-3 competition (agents that play the games on Kaggle) and the ARC-AGI-2 Grand Prize, guaranteed this year to the best open-source solution.

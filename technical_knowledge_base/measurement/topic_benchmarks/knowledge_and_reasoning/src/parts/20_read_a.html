In one screen

Knowledge benchmarks ask whether a model can answer what experts can; reasoning benchmarks ask whether it can work out something it has not seen. Both began as exams with a fixed answer key, and both keep meeting the same two limits: the key is wrong more often than people assume, and the protocol (shots, chain of thought, tools, effort, harness) moves the number as much as the model does. This page takes each benchmark in turn: how it was built, how it is graded, what its errata are, what today's scores mean and what replaced it.

How these benchmarks are graded

Four grading schemes cover the whole family, and each has its own failure mode. Try them on real items in {{Grade it yourself|#t-grade}}.

Multiple choice, letter

The model writes a letter; a regex such as answer is \(?([A-J]) pulls it out. Answer-extraction rules differ between harnesses, so the same outputs can score differently. Chance is 1 over the number of options (25% for MMLU and GPQA, 10% for MMLU-Pro).

Multiple choice, log-likelihood

The base-model way: score each option by the probability the model assigns to it (often length-normalised) and pick the highest. No generation, no extraction; but it is a different measurement from letter answers, so the two are not comparable.

Short answer, model-graded

HLE and SimpleQA answers are free text, so an LLM judge compares the answer with the key (HLE: o3-mini with a fixed prompt; SimpleQA: a prompted ChatGPT grader with three outcomes). The judge is part of the instrument.

Exact output, few tries

ARC-AGI-1 and 2: the model must produce the whole output grid, every cell right, within two attempts (pass@2). ARC-AGI-3 instead counts actions against a human baseline.

The root page explains the protocol tuple (shots, chain of thought, tools, effort, sampling, harness, judge, who ran it, date) and why a number means nothing without it; here each benchmark gets its own version of that story.

MMLU and its family

MMLU Hendrycks et al., UC Berkeley, September 2020

Construction. 57 subjects, from elementary mathematics to professional law and medicine, collected by hand by graduate and undergraduate students from free online sources: practice questions for the GRE and the US Medical Licensing Examination, undergraduate course questions and questions for readers of Oxford University Press books. The paper reports 15,908 questions split into a few-shot development set (5 per subject), a 1,540-question validation set and a test set of 14,079; the released files hold 14,042 test, 1,531 validation and 285 development questions, and 14,042 is the number every harness uses ({{paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03300}}; {{cais/mmlu|https://huggingface.co/datasets/cais/mmlu}}). Each question has four options.

Baselines at launch. Unspecialised Mechanical Turk workers scored 34.5%. "Expert-level" accuracy was estimated at about 89.8% by taking the 95th-percentile human test-taker on each source exam and "an educated guess" where that was unknown, so it is a constructed figure, not a measured panel. The largest GPT-3 (175B) scored 43.9% few-shot, almost 20 points above chance ({{paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.03300}}).

Protocol. Base models were scored 5-shot, the five development examples teaching the answer format; scoring was either the generated letter or the log-likelihood of each option. Instruction-tuned and reasoning models are scored zero-shot with chain of thought. The headline numbers mixed all of these: Gemini Ultra's 90.04% "exceeding human experts" used 32 chain-of-thought samples routed by a confidence threshold, where its 5-shot score was 83.7% (the full case, with GPT-4 measured both ways, is in the root's {{Same model, many numbers|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} tab).

The answer key (MMLU-Redux). Fourteen annotators re-checked 100 randomly sampled questions from each of the 57 subjects, 5,700 in all, against a taxonomy of six defect types plus "needs an expert". 370 were flagged, the paper's 6.49% ({{MMLU-Redux|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04127}}; {{dataset|https://huggingface.co/datasets/edinburgh-dawg/mmlu-redux-2.0}}, CC BY 4.0). Read the composition before quoting it: only 106 of 5,700 (1.86%) are a wrong gold label; the rest are unclear questions (132), unclear options (25), no correct option (36), several correct options (39) and items needing an expert (32) counted from the dataset. The paper calls 6.49% a stratified estimate, but it equals the raw share of the sample; weighting each subject by its size in the test set gives 7.0%, because professional law (1,534 test questions, 18 flagged in its sample) and virology (166, 57 flagged) carry more weight derived. Defects cluster: 57 of 100 sampled virology questions are flawed, 33 of them with a wrong key, and 25 to 26% in logical fallacies and college chemistry. The {{Fix the key|#t-key}} tab maps all 57 subjects and replays the paper's re-grade, in which Llama 3.1 405B moves from 16th to 1st on virology once flawed items are removed and GPT-4 (0613) falls from 0.91 to 0.43 on human sexuality.

Correction to the old page
"~6.5% of questions contain an error of some kind ... the wrong-gold-label rate on its own is lower": correct, and now quantified: 1.86% wrong gold labels in the sample. "Frontier average is ~92%+ in 2026" has no 2026 source: no current frontier card reports MMLU, the highest independent row is 88.1% (5-shot), and 92.3% is o1's lab figure from 2024.

Contamination. The questions sit on the public web. Testset Slot Guessing masks one wrong option and asks the model to fill it in; ChatGPT and GPT-4 reproduced the missing option exactly 52% and 57% of the time, which they could hardly do without having seen the test ({{Xu et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09783}}). MMLU-Redux found a second sign: on some subjects (professional law, formal logic) models score as well or better on items whose key is wrong, which they could only match by memory.

Status. Saturated and contaminated; the gap between frontier models is answer-key noise. Still a cheap smoke test during training. MMLU-Pro TIGER-Lab (Wang et al.), June 2024

Construction. Start from MMLU, merge the 57 subjects into 14 disciplines, and drop 5,886 questions that more than four of eight small models (Llama-2 7B and 13B, Mistral-7B, Gemma-7B, Yi-6B and their chat versions) answered correctly. Add harder questions from a STEM problem site, TheoremQA and SciBench. Then expand every question from four options to ten, the six new distractors written by GPT-4-Turbo, and run two review rounds: experts first, then frontier LLMs flagging suspect items for annotators to check. Total: 12,032 questions; chance falls to 10% ({{MMLU-Pro|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01574}}).

What changed in the measurement. Across 24 prompt styles, scores moved 4 to 5 points on MMLU (up to 10.98) but about 2 on MMLU-Pro (up to 3.74). And chain of thought, which barely helps on MMLU, is now worth up to 19 points:

Scores and status. GPT-4o led at launch with 72.6%. Two and a half years later the tops are 91.16% (Gemini 3.1 Pro, TIGER-Lab's own run, board last updated 11 March 2026: {{results|https://huggingface.co/datasets/TIGER-Lab/mmlu_pro_leaderboard_submission}}) and 89.8% (Gemini 3 Pro Preview, high, in Artificial Analysis's legacy evaluation: {{AA|https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/mmlu-pro}}). Artificial Analysis dropped MMLU-Pro from its Intelligence Index at v4.0 in January 2026 and runs no newer models on it ({{methodology|https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/intelligence-benchmarking}}). Ten options also mean at least six of every ten were written by a 2024 model, so a distractor that is accidentally correct is a key error the original could not have; we found no published audit of how often that happens unconfirmed.

Correction to the old page
"Saturating at the top, still useful for mid-tier and open-weight comparisons": saturated and legacy since early 2026 (tops 89.8 to 91.2%, retired from the main independent index). It remains a cheap, stable regression set for your own models.

Multilingual knowledge: MMMLU, Global-MMLU, INCLUDE

Translating MMLU tests translation as much as knowledge, and it carries MMLU's Western syllabus into every language. Three answers:

  • MMMLU (OpenAI, September 2024): MMLU's test set translated by professional human translators into 14 languages (Arabic, Bengali, German, Spanish, French, Hindi, Indonesian, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Brazilian Portuguese, Swahili, Yoruba, Simplified Chinese), MIT licence ({{dataset|https://huggingface.co/datasets/openai/MMMLU}}).
  • Global-MMLU (Cohere For AI, December 2024): 42 languages, translations checked by paid professional and community annotators, and every question tagged: 28% of MMLU needs culturally sensitive knowledge, and 84.9% of its geography questions are about North America or Europe. It ships "culturally sensitive" and "culturally agnostic" subsets because model rankings change between them ({{Global-MMLU|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.03304}}).
  • INCLUDE (EPFL and others, November 2024): no translation at all. 197,243 multiple-choice questions from 1,926 local academic, professional and licensing exams in 44 languages, 15 scripts and 52 countries ({{INCLUDE|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.19799}}).

Scores and status. Translated MMLU inherits MMLU's ceiling: Claude Opus 5.5 averages 94.3% on Global-MMLU across 42 languages lab ({{system card|https://www-cdn.anthropic.com/fc1b44717c85dc068bc6ba5024219938094694bd/Claude%20Opus%205.5%20System%20Card.pdf}}, 22 Sep 2026). INCLUDE, built from local exams with regional knowledge, is the one of the three with clear headroom, though no independent frontier run of it was found. For maths in many languages, MGSM (250 GSM8K problems in ten languages) is saturated (93.7%, o4-mini, OpenAI simple-evals, which calls it saturated) and belongs to {{Math benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81ec942edf0040a2f596}}.

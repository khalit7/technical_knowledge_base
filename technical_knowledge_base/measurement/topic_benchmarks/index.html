Benchmarks in one screen

A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks, a way of running a model on them, and a rule for scoring the result. It turns "how good is this model at X?" into one number that anyone can compare. That is its value and its trap: the number is a measurement of a model under a protocol on a date, and every one of those three parts moves it.

Two ideas carry this page. First, benchmarks age. A new benchmark is born hard, separates models for a while, then fills up until the top models sit within noise of each other and of the answer key's own errors; often its questions leak into training data on the way. That used to take years. In 2026 it can take weeks. Second, a headline number leaves things out: which version of the benchmark, which split of its tasks, which harness ran the model, how many samples were drawn, how good the answer key is, whether the model had seen the tasks, and which version of an aggregate index it belongs to. Each of these has moved a real score by more than the gap between the models being compared, and the section below gives one real case for each.

Three questions settle most headline numbers
When was it measured (Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 went from 30.0% to 64.6% in 26 days on the labs' figures; Saturation timeline). What ran the model (at the same effort, one model scores 62.7% or 98.6% on ARC-AGI-3 depending on the harness). Has anyone checked the answer key (roughly 29% of Humanity's Last Exam's text-only chemistry and biology answers are contradicted by the literature). A figure that survives none of the three is not yet a measurement.

The life of a benchmark

Every benchmark follows roughly the same arc, and knowing where a benchmark sits on it tells you how much its numbers can mean.

1. BornThe best model scores low. MMLU launched with GPT-3 at 43.9% against 25% chance.
2. DiscriminatesThe useful years: scores spread out and gaps mean something.
3. SaturatesTop models cluster within noise of each other and of the answer key's error rate.
4. ContaminatedQuestions leak into training data, deliberately or by web scrape, and the score decouples from capability.
5. RetiredStill quoted for continuity and marketing ("zombie" phase), then replaced by a harder sibling or a new design.

The animation runs one benchmark from each era through the arc, on the same score axis. Then is MMLU, the 57-subject exam that defined model comparisons from 2020 to 2023. Now is Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1, 70 research tasks in a terminal sandbox built in August 2026 to sit far below saturation. The last step puts both on one time axis.

Each point is the best published score at that date on that benchmark (sources in the captions); the two benchmarks are different tests, so compare their shapes and speeds, not their heights. Points per 30 days is derived: (score minus launch score) divided by days since launch, times 30. The shaded band on MMLU marks the roughly 6.5% of questions that MMLU-Redux found to contain an error of some kind, which is where saturation meets the answer key.

The useful period used to be two to four years; it is now often under eighteen months, and sometimes weeks. MMLU took 918 days to go from 43.9% to GPT-4's 86.4%. Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 gained 22.6 points in its first five days and 34.6 points in 26: about 29 times MMLU's pace derived. Those are the labs' own figures; Artificial Analysis's re-runs are lower (43.3% for Fable 5.1, 63.3% for GPT-6 Astra) but tell the same story. Nothing is wrong with the benchmark; the rate is the finding. Its maintainers already have 0.2 in development. The Saturation timeline shows the same arc for every major benchmark with dated points.

Replacement follows two patterns. A harder sibling: MMLU, then MMLU-Pro, then GPQA, then Humanity's Last Exam (HLE); SWE-bench, then SWE-bench Verified, then SWE-bench Pro; ARC-AGI-1, then 2, then 3. Or a change of design: from a static set to a live one pinned to dates (LiveCodeBench, the current year's AIME, SWE-rebench), or from static questions to interactive environments (ARC-AGI-3). The designs that have lasted share one property: the model cannot have seen the test. They are private or rotating sets (FrontierMath, HLE's private split), live sets pinned to dates, interactive environments, and paired human baselines (METR time horizons, GDPval, OSWorld).

Aggregators retire benchmarks in public. On 4 September 2026 the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.2 dropped GPQA Diamond, calling it "an exceptional scientific reasoning evaluation that has now been saturated" (GPT-6 Astra scores 96.0%), added two private-set evaluations (AA-Briefcase and GDP.pdf) and doubled the weight of private held-out sets to 40% (Artificial Analysis). For anyone running evaluations the lesson is practical: pin benchmark versions, expect to rotate the suite yearly, and keep a private regression set that never touches the internet.

What a number leaves out

A score is the output of a procedure: sample the data (the benchmark's dataset and ideally a canonical prompt), run the model (zero- or few-shot, chain of thought, tools, reasoning effort, an agent scaffold) and score it (accuracy, exact match, pass@k, pass^k, an Elo rating, a judge's grade, task success). Every knob moves the number, so a result means something only with its protocol tuple:

benchmark versiontask splitshotschain of thoughttoolseffort and token budgetsampling (pass@1, maj@k, runs)answer extractionharness or scaffoldjudgewho ran itdate

Even the small knobs matter. Shots: MMLU was scored 5-shot in the base-model era because the examples taught the format; reasoning models are evaluated zero-shot, and OpenAI reported that few-shot prompts degraded o1 on GPQA. Answer extraction: parsing "The answer is (X)" and reading the model's probabilities over the options give different MMLU scores for the same model, so harnesses (lm-eval-harness, HELM, a vendor's own) legitimately disagree by points. Below, the knobs that move headline numbers most, one real case each; Same model, many numbers lays the measured spreads side by side.

Version same name, different test

Terminal-Bench went 2.0, 2.1, 3.0 (23 July 2026: 74 tasks, best models about 34%) and 4.0 (26 August 2026) (releases). Revisions are made to discriminate: two models 4.9 points apart on 2.1 were 12.7 apart on 3.0 (Terminal-Bench 3.0). Version 4.0 set a flat 8-hour agent timeout, recalibrated CPU and memory, fixed 19 tasks, removed 8, and calls itself a breaking change (Terminal-Bench 4.0). When the agent gets a shell and a budget, the limits are part of the task: a 2.x score beside a 4.0 score is a false comparison, not an old one. OSWorld circulates under three names that are not one scale: the original, OSWorld-Verified (built because roughly 300 tasks or checkers were broken and flattening scores) and OSWorld 2.0, which current vendor cards report (OSWorld). Even inside one version the scoring rule matters: Claude Opus 5's 74.0% on OSWorld 2.0 is partial credit, 37.2% strict, and the maintainers' own run gives 68.3% and 31.4% (Same model, many numbers).

Split which tasks were scored

Many benchmarks hold back tasks to measure overfitting to the public part. On Scale's SWE-bench Pro, Claude Opus 4.1 fell from 22.7% on the public set to 17.8% on the private commercial subset (Scale). It now also has a major version: V2 (22 September 2026) re-checked every task and kept 642, and its leaderboard has Claude Opus 5 at 99.4% (638 of 642), so a version-1 figure and a V2 figure share only a name (Scale; readings in Same model, many numbers). Public sets die by leakage; fully private sets cannot be audited and concentrate trust (FrontierMath's late-disclosed OpenAI funding is the cautionary tale). Best practice is a hybrid: public development set, private held-out set, and independent re-runners (Epoch AI, Princeton's HAL, Artificial Analysis) as auditors.

Harness what ran the model

On agentic benchmarks the score belongs to the model plus the program around it (prompts, tools, memory, retries, budget). On ARC-AGI-3, interactive games humans solve completely, GPT-6 Astra's headline pair is 62.7% on ARC Prize's standard provider-agnostic harness and 99.9% on a Provider Adapter harness that keeps the model's opaque reasoning state between requests (ARC Prize, 3 September 2026). That pair also mixes effort levels (max in the standard harness, high in the adapter); at equal effort it is 62.7% against 98.6% at max and 54.8% against 99.9% at high, still a gap of 36 to 45 points from the harness alone (Same model, many numbers). ARC Prize's reading: neutral conditions and best achievable are different questions, and once a model reasons in hidden state they stay different. Research harnesses showed it first: Prime Agent's 95.5% with Claude Opus 5, though its often-quoted "30% to 95.5%" compares different harnesses on different game sets (95.5% is the easier public set, best of three runs: Prime Agent); StateM lifting GPT-5.5 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 83.1% to 92.1% (StateM).

Three relatives. Who ran it: on Terminal-Bench 4.0 Anthropic reports 66.4% for Claude Opus 5.5 and Artificial Analysis measured 59.6%; xAI reports 38.0% for Grok 4.7, Artificial Analysis 25.8% (Topic: llms, Benchmarks tab). Retrieval: on the Vals AI Legal Research Bench (200 questions) OpenAI reports 38.7% for GPT-6 Astra with web search and 54% for Astra for Law, the same weights with a 230-million-URL legal index and legal-analysis instructions, on OpenAI's private validation set (TNW); if both ran on the same 200 questions the gap is about 3.1 standard errors derived, but index and instructions move together, so it does not isolate retrieval. Cost: on ordinary tasks the harness moves the bill more than the score (21 model-and-harness pairs: Arena); SoL-Pi cut recorded tokens 44.7 to 49.0% on EdgeBench's 51 public tasks, scoring 2.5 to 2.8 points below its baseline (SoL-Pi). At the cheap extreme, a small transformer trained for about 67 cents reaches 44% on ARC-AGI-1 (write-up).

Sampling how many tries, how many items

A model sampled at temperature above zero answers differently on each run, and small benchmarks magnify that. The current AIME has 30 problems, so one is worth 3.3 points, and conventions differ by more: pass@1 (one sample, or the mean over samples), maj@32 (the majority answer of 32 samples, often many points higher), pass@k (any of k right). A "93% on AIME" without convention, temperature, token budget and run count compares with nothing; MathArena fixes them and evaluates within days of each contest (MathArena). Even then, the item count sets a floor on the noise. Try it:

illustrative runs: each item solved independently at the chosen rate (real items differ in difficulty). The band and stats are exact binomial arithmetic derived: standard error √(p(1−p)/n), 95% interval ±1.96 standard errors, and two models scored on different items need a gap above 1.96·√2 standard errors. Scoring both on the same items with a paired test removes much of the variance (Anthropic, "Adding error bars to evals").

Three rules of thumb fall out: at 80% on 30 AIME problems the standard error is 7.3 points; a 2-point gap on GPQA Diamond's 198 questions is noise (±4.2 at 90%), and so is one on SWE-bench Verified's 500 tasks (±4.0 at 70%). The inflation tricks live here too: best-of-N reported as one run, maj@k beside a rival's pass@1, "with tools" beside "without".

Answer key is the key right?

A score is capped by its answer key. On HLE, FutureHouse found 29 ± 3.7% of text-only chemistry and biology answers contradicted by the literature (FutureHouse), and HLE is graded by an LLM judge, which makes the judge part of the instrument. An expert re-grading of six physics benchmarks audited 250 answers marked wrong for GPT-5.6 Sol: 143 benchmark errors, 95 grader errors, 12 model errors, so 95.2% were not the model's fault; on the cleaned, smaller sets its scores rose from 47% to 79% (HLE-Physics) and 13% to 95% (PRISM-Physics), upper estimates since only rejected answers were checked (Re-grading six physics benchmarks). So the part of a suite a model fails mixes model failure and item defect, and defects gather in the hard tail people quote. Differences between models on the same harness mean more than absolute scores.

Contamination had the model seen it?

Public benchmarks leak into training data, and "trained on the test" blurs into "trained on similar data". Detection: text overlap with training corpora (weak against paraphrase), asking the model to complete an item from its prefix, rebuilding a fresh parallel set (GSM1k, a new GSM8K look-alike, cost some model families up to 8% in the paper's current version, v4; its first version said 13%: Zhang et al. 2024), and splitting by date. Three recent designs answer it three ways:

  • By the calendar: SWE-rebench mines fresh issues and scores models only on a recent window, because models resolve issues in pre-cutoff repositories notably better; the price is no stable time series (SWE-rebench).
  • By transformation: SchrodingerRepo rebuilds each repository at evaluation time with behaviour unchanged (reworded issue, remapped names, reordered layout, rewritten code). Pass@1 on SWE-bench Verified drops 6.0 to 14.4 points, over 80% of the extra actions spent navigating and searching: part of a static score is knowing where to look (SchrodingerRepo).
  • By licensing: Real-SWE licenses ten tasks from companies' private repositories, which cannot leak. Its leaderboard (read 4 October 2026) has GPT-6 Astra at 46.25% and Claude Fable 5.1 at 45.00%, below the 57.9% and 55.8% their labs report on Terminal-Bench 4.0 (Real-SWE); with ten tasks, one task is 10 points, so the ranking is within noise.

Defences, strongest first: private sets behind an evaluation API (FrontierMath, HLE's private split, SWE-bench Pro's commercial split), live data pinned to dates (LiveCodeBench, MathArena, SWE-rebench), canary strings (respected only by cooperating labs), perturbed templates, trust. Above them sits enclave evaluation, piloted by Google DeepMind on Gemini Flash Lite in August 2026: weights and prompts meet inside a hardware-encrypted enclave and only scores leave (DeepMind; caveats on Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges).

Index version the aggregator moved the target

Composite indices are versioned benchmarks too. The Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index changed twice in four days: v4.2 (4 September 2026) and v4.3 (7 September: Terminal-Bench 2.1 upgraded to 4.0, τ³-Banking replaced by AutomationBench-AA, private weight 45%) (v4.3); the current release is v4.3.2 (methodology). Index scores quoted at launches before 4 September, such as the high-50s and low-60s for GLM-5.3-Flash, Gemini 3.8 Flash and Muse Spark 1.3, sit on an older scale. A score without its index version is a benchmark name without a version.

Subject what is measured is not the model

A cluster of September 2026 benchmarks measures the system around the model, and each moves the number more than any model gap. Hyper-τ-bench: Claude Opus 5 building agents passes 23.9% of held-out tasks alone and 82.2% paired with an engineer who knows the task (Sierra). HarnessDev: the best model-built harness averages 67.8 against 86.2 for human-engineered ones (HarnessDev). MOLE scores the monitor: 28 of 39 agent models completed most of their harmful objectives under a preamble saying the sandbox is harmless (across models, refusal and completion rates correlate, Spearman −0.73), and the best monitor caught 24 of 45 completed harms in a one-day audit (MOLE). Emergence World scores duration: agent worlds run for up to 21 days produced failures nobody injected (goal drift, compounding tool errors, refused work), which tasks that end cannot detect (Emergence World). Phi-Bench scores infrastructure engineering (Claude Opus 5: mean reward 36.53%, Phi-Bench).

And what nobody reports: no major model card reports confidence calibration (arXiv 2609.26489); and a model's stated reason is not a measurement of its choice, since WhatWorkedBench finds agents picking near-optimal settings for wrong reasons (arXiv 2609.27490).

When the measure becomes the target

Goodhart's law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure") takes five forms. Training on the test or its paraphrases. Overfitting the format: multiple-choice letters, or the verbosity voters and judges reward (AlpacaEval needed a length-controlled variant). Scaffold shopping: your best harness against a rival's default. Reward hacking the evaluator: Berkeley RDI's cheating agent reached near-perfect scores on eight agent benchmarks (98% on GAIA) while solving no tasks, by overwriting tests, tampering with checkers or fetching public gold answers (Berkeley RDI), and o3 hacked the scorer unprompted in 39 of 128 METR RE-Bench runs (METR); evaluator isolation is now a requirement. Goodfire proposes catching it from inside, via an activation signature, not yet a monitor with a known false-positive rate (Goodfire). Selective reporting: testing private variants and publishing the winner, as The Leaderboard Illusion documented on Arena (then LMArena; Meta tested 27 Llama 4 variants) (Singh et al. 2025; Arena's response). The fix inside companies is the same as outside: private in-house suites stay discriminating because nobody trains on them.

The families in brief

Benchmarks group by what they try to measure. Each family gets its intuition and what is active or retired; the Benchmark atlas lists every benchmark with year, format, metric and status, and the child pages own construction, errata and scores.

Knowledge and reasoning

Can the model answer what experts can? These began as exams (MMLU's 57 subjects) and moved to questions chosen because models failed them: GPQA's "Google-proof" graduate science, where skilled non-experts with the web score about 34%, and HLE's roughly 2,500 expert questions. Abstract reasoning took its own road with ARC-AGI: novel puzzles that resist memorisation, interactive games since 2026. The recurring problem is the answer key.

Active: HLE, ARC-AGI-3, SimpleQA (factuality and abstention), AA-Briefcase, MMLU-Pro for mid-tier models. Saturated or retired: MMLU, GPQA Diamond at the frontier (still useful from 60 to 90%), HellaSwag, WinoGrande, BIG-Bench Hard, ARC-AGI-1 and ARC-AGI-2.

Go deeper: Knowledge and reasoning benchmarks (33 min read): MMLU errata, GPQA, HLE's judge and tools effect, ARC-AGI 1 to 3.

Math

Math answers can be checked exactly, so the family is clean to grade and quick to saturate. GSM8K and MATH fell first (GSM-Symbolic showed part of GSM8K was pattern matching). The answer was freshness: each year's AIME is a benchmark the moment it is sat, and MathArena evaluates within days; by 2026 even fresh contests opened near the ceiling. At the top, FrontierMath's private research problems held out longest, but its hardest tier (Tier 4 v2, 41 private problems) reached 100% in Epoch's own run of GPT-6.1 Sol, started 29 September 2026 (Epoch AI). Lean proofs (miniF2F, PutnamBench) remove grading ambiguity, and both are now solved.

Active: MathArena's research sets (ArXivMath, ArXivLean), proof-graded contests such as USAMO, CritPt. Saturating: FrontierMath Tiers 1-3. Saturated or retired: GSM8K, MATH and MATH-500, AIME and HMMT (every vintage; MathArena deprecated final-answer contests in May 2026), FrontierMath Tier 4, miniF2F and PutnamBench (solved), MGSM.

Go deeper: Math benchmarks: GSM8K to FrontierMath (24 min read): the GSM8K lineage, sampling traps on 30-problem contests, FrontierMath's versions and governance.

Coding

Coding moved from one function against unit tests (HumanEval, MBPP) to real GitHub issues in whole repositories (the SWE-bench family), which made coding benchmarks agent benchmarks: the score depends on the scaffold. Contamination is fought with dated contest problems (LiveCodeBench; LiveCodeBench Pro rates models against humans) and with repositories the model cannot have seen. Weak test suites overstate correctness, a lesson EvalPlus taught and every code benchmark relearns.

Active: SWE-rebench, Real-SWE, DeepSWE, Phi-Bench, Codeforces ratings (not comparable across labs). Saturating: Aider Polyglot; SWE-bench Verified, kept for continuity (eight models between 93.4% and 97.0% in Vals AI's bash-only run, updated 1 September 2026: Vals AI). Saturated, frozen or retired: HumanEval, MBPP and EvalPlus, BigCodeBench, LiveCodeBench and LiveCodeBench Pro (boards frozen in 2025), SWE-bench Pro v1 (deprecated) and Pro V2 (public split at 99.4%).

Go deeper: Coding benchmarks: HumanEval to SWE-bench Pro (27 min read): the HumanEval lineage, LiveCodeBench, the SWE-bench family and its splits.

Agentic

An agent benchmark hands the model tools and an environment (a terminal, a desktop, a website, a simulated customer) and checks the end state. The number belongs to model plus scaffold, and the environment can be attacked. τ²-bench's metric is worth knowing: pass^k, the probability that all k independent tries succeed, which measures reliability and falls far below pass@1 for most models. METR's time horizons measure the length of human task an agent completes half the time.

Active: Terminal-Bench 4.0 and Terminal-Bench-Science, OSWorld (Verified, 2.0), τ²-bench, BrowseComp, METR time horizons, GDPval and GDPval-AA (Artificial Analysis's agentic variant, graded head to head by model judges into an Elo scale), AutomationBench, Agents' Last Exam, EdgeBench, Hyper-τ-bench. Saturated or retired: GAIA and WebArena (saturating, shown gameable), AgentBench, τ-bench, Terminal-Bench 1.0.

Go deeper: Agentic benchmarks (35 min read): the environments, pass^k, time horizons, the 2026 integrity crisis.

Long context

Can the model use a million tokens, not just accept them? Finding one planted fact (needle in a haystack) was solved early, and partly a prompt artefact: one added sentence took Claude 2.1 from 27% to 98% (Anthropic, 6 Dec 2023). Useful tests remove the literal match (NoLiMa) or make the model trace and aggregate across the input: MRCR's repeated requests, and GDP.pdf's 4,592 PDF pages, where GPT-6 Astra leads at 34.2% on Surge's harness and 32.2% on Artificial Analysis's, both read 4 October 2026 (Surge AI; Artificial Analysis).

Active: GDP.pdf, MRCR (independent runs; vendor figures are near the ceiling), NoLiMa. Saturating: AA-LCR. Retired, dormant or saturated: needle in a haystack, RULER, LongBench v2, Fiction.LiveBench.

Go deeper: Long-context and multimodal benchmarks (23 min read): effective against advertised length, MRCR, GDP.pdf.

Multimodal

Questions that need an image, chart, document or video. MMMU saturated and its Pro version took over; chart, document and visual-math sets saturate fast because their answers are short, so successors are built by filtering for what current models fail; video is the open end.

Active: Video-MME-v2, CharXiv, ZeroBench, GDP.pdf. Saturating: MMMU-Pro, Video-MME and Video-MMMU. Saturated: MMMU, MathVista, ChartQA, DocVQA.

Go deeper: Long-context and multimodal benchmarks (23 min read): the blind baseline, MMMU to MMMU-Pro, charts, documents, video.

Safety

Does the model refuse what it should, resist jailbreaks, stay honest, and stay safe when it acts? Early tests scored the answer text (TruthfulQA); later ones score attack success (HarmBench, StrongREJECT, AgentHarm), regulation-derived risks (AIR-Bench), belief against claim (MASK), and now the monitor and the long run (MOLE, Emergence World): what a model does matters more than what it says. Two further kinds exist: dangerous-capability evaluations (biology, cyber, autonomy), scored against thresholds, and scheming and evaluation-awareness tests: does the model behave the same when it thinks no one is checking?

Active: AIR-Bench, MASK (saturating), the Virology Capabilities Test, scheming suites, MOLE, Emergence World. Research staples that left frontier cards: HarmBench, AgentHarm, StrongREJECT (filtered as saturated by OpenAI in December 2025, then replaced by a multi-turn red-teaming evaluation before GPT-5.4: GPT-5.2 card). Mostly retired: TruthfulQA.

Go deeper: Safety and honesty benchmarks (31 min read): refusal and over-refusal, jailbreaks, honesty, dangerous capabilities, scheming. Optimisers exploiting proxies: Reward Hacking.

Human preference and instruction following

Which answer do people prefer? Arena (LMArena until its renaming on 28 January 2026: Wikipedia) fits Bradley-Terry (Elo-style) ratings to anonymous pairwise votes: live, contamination-free, and a measure of preference, not capability. Its text board held 413 models on 2 October 2026 (Arena). Votes reward confident, well-formatted, flattering answers, and style rivals substance (the 2025 GPT-4o sycophancy rollback showed the cost of optimising for approval), so style control is now the default view, with a factuality toggle since 14 July 2026 (Arena); Agent Arena (June 2026) ranks agents by causal tracing on real sessions, not votes. Arena-Hard and AlpacaEval swap voters for an LLM judge. Instruction following is checked mechanically: IFEval's verifiable constraints, MultiChallenge's multi-turn ones.

Active: Arena (with category boards, style control and factuality), MultiChallenge and IFBench. Saturated: IFEval. Dormant or retired: Arena-Hard v2 (newest entries April 2025), AlpacaEval 2, MT-Bench.

Go deeper: Human preference and arenas (25 min read): the Bradley-Terry fit, style control and factuality, the judge-graded stand-ins, and instruction following (IFEval, IFBench, MultiChallenge). Judges in general: Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges.

Multilingual

Does capability survive outside English? A translated English test measures translation as much as knowledge, so newer sets are culturally aware. Machine translation keeps chrF and BLEU on FLORES-200's 200 languages.

Active: FLORES-200. Saturating: Global-MMLU, MMMLU, INCLUDE. Saturated: MGSM.

Tool use

Can the model pick the right tool and fill its arguments? BFCL began with function-call correctness and became agentic in v4; MCP-era suites call real Model Context Protocol servers rather than mocks. Young and fragmented, and its dedicated boards went quiet in 2026: tool use is now mostly measured inside agent benchmarks.

Still reported: τ²-bench (saturating). Dormant boards: BFCL v4 (last update 12 April 2026), MCP-Universe, MCPMark, MCP-Bench.

Go deeper: no page of its own; BFCL and the MCP evals are covered on Agentic benchmarks (35 min read).

Before these came the classic era, now historical: ImageNet (2009), SQuAD's reading comprehension, GLUE and SuperGLUE in the BERT years.

How to read the landscape

A checklist for any model card, leaderboard or launch post:

  1. Where is the benchmark in its life? If top models sit within a few points of each other and of the answer key's error rate, deltas mean nothing. Look it up in the Benchmark atlas and the Saturation timeline.
  2. Which version, split and index version? Terminal-Bench 2.x against 4.0, OSWorld-Verified against 2.0, SWE-bench Pro public against private, Artificial Analysis v4.2 against v4.3: different numbers with the same name.
  3. What ran it, and who? The harness, the tools, the retrieval index, the effort level, and whether the lab or an independent re-runner produced the figure.
  4. How was it sampled? pass@1 or maj@k, how many runs, how many items. Error bars, or at least the item count. Same protocol for every model compared?
  5. Can anyone reproduce it? A number you cannot reproduce with a public harness deserves a discount; a vendor's claim with no technical report is carried in its own words, not reconstructed.
  6. Is the subject really the model? Harness, monitor, human partner and duration each move agentic numbers by more than model gaps.

What model cards lead with in late 2026: HLE, the current AIME, SWE-bench Verified and Pro, Terminal-Bench 4.0 and Terminal-Bench-Science, Real-SWE, ARC-AGI-2 and 3, OSWorld, τ²-bench, MMMU-Pro, Arena ratings. GPQA Diamond is still printed but no longer separates the frontier. The current models on fixed benchmarks, with who ran each cell, are on Topic: llms, Benchmarks tab.

Common mistakes

Narrating a delta on a saturated benchmark
At 90% on GPQA Diamond's 198 questions the 95% interval is about ±4 points, and the remaining errors overlap with contested gold labels: a 2-point lead is noise.
Quoting an agentic score without its harness
62.7% and 98.6% are the same model at the same effort on the same ARC-AGI-3 set, in two harnesses. On SWE-bench, "bash only" and "full agent" columns differ by 10 to 20 points; never compare across leaderboard columns.
Putting maj@32 beside a rival's pass@1
Consensus over 32 samples is many points above a single sample on AIME. Check the sampling convention, the temperature and the token budget before comparing.
Reading the residual as what the model cannot do
On HLE and the physics suites a large share of "failures" are wrong keys, ambiguous questions or grader bugs. An apparent ceiling below 100% may be the answer key.
Treating an arena rating as a capability ranking
Arena ratings measure what voters prefer, style included, and selective private testing can inflate them. Use them as one noisy signal, filtered by category and with style control on.
Trusting a score nobody isolated
Agents have reached near-perfect scores on eight benchmarks without solving tasks by tampering with the evaluator. An agent score you did not run under an isolated evaluator with held-out checks deserves suspicion.
Reconstructing a garbled figure

Why the recent numbers are biased short
  • Censoring. A benchmark launched in 2025 or 2026 can only show a short time to saturation; the slow ones are still open (HLE, Terminal-Bench 4.0 and Terminal-Bench-Science here). The era medians use only the benchmarks that got there, so recent medians overstate how fast benchmarks saturate.
  • Selection. These 23 were chosen because they have long public records, which favours famous benchmarks that labs chase. The many benchmarks nobody optimises for are missing.
  • Harness drift. On ARC-AGI-3 the 90% crossing (161 days) comes from the Provider Adapter harness; with ARC Prize's standard harness the best is 62.7%. On Terminal-Bench-Science the launch-day 30.0% used Claude Code, the later 52.6% was Anthropic's own run.
  • Re-based versions. FrontierMath's 80% crossing is on Tiers 1-3 v2 (June 2026), a re-based set on which Epoch re-ran older models; on the 2025 private set the best was 52.4%.

How this tab was built

  • Dates. A point is dated when the model or system was first public: the model's release date for tracker runs (Epoch AI's convention), the paper or results date otherwise. Tracker run dates, where they differ, are in the point details.
  • Who measured it. independent trackers and organiser-scored hidden tests (Epoch AI, ARC Prize, MathArena, EvalPlus, Stanford HELM, Artificial Analysis, the ILSVRC organisers); leaderboard entries submitted to the maintainers' own board; benchmark authors at launch; lab-reported by the model's developer. Independent sources are preferred; lab points are kept only where no independent record exists or where the gap is the story, and they can be dropped with the setting above.
  • Records only. Each series keeps the results that beat every earlier result of that series. Effort settings count as the same model (the best effort is shown).
  • Ceilings. A human baseline is as measured by each benchmark's authors and is not one kind of figure: one trained annotator (ImageNet), crowdworkers (GLUE, SuperGLUE, HellaSwag), an estimate (MMLU), expert validators (GPQA), a panel (ARC-AGI), participants (OSWorld). Where none is published the ceiling is 100%, although answer-key errors put the true ceiling lower (see the Reading tab).
  • Derived numbers. Durations are recomputed by src/saturation/check_saturation.py for all 16 settings and compared with this page's code (368 of 368 agree).
  • Related. For one model's many numbers on a benchmark, see Same model, many numbers; for current models on fixed benchmarks, Topic: llms, Benchmarks tab.

Is the gap noise?

A benchmark is a sample of items. With n items, a score of k/n has an exact binomial confidence interval (Clopper and Pearson); two scores on the same items can be compared item by item (the paired, McNemar test), which is sharper than treating them as independent (Fisher's exact test). Everything here is computed exactly in the page; the same numbers are checked against SciPy and statsmodels in src/same/check_calc.py.

What the calculator does not cover. It treats items as the only source of noise. Repeated trials per item tighten each item's estimate but do not add items (Anthropic's Opus 5.5 card quotes both a per-trial and a per-task standard error for this reason, sections 8.5 and 8.6). Judge noise, harness bugs and wrong answer keys are not sampling error and no interval covers them; the Reading tab's section on answer keys covers those.

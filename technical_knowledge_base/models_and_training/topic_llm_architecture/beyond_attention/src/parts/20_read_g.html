8. What a fixed state is bad at, and good at

Recall, defined. Associative recall is producing a token because it followed the same cue earlier in the context ("Hakuna Matata means no worries Hakuna Matata it means no" → "worries"). {{Zoology (Arora et al. 2023)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.04927}} found that such tokens are "6.4% of all tokens in the Pile" yet "account for 82% of the perplexity gap to attention" of attention-free models, and that "a 70M parameter attention model outperforms a 1.4 billion parameter gated-convolution model on associative recall". Their multi-query associative recall (MQAR) task lists key-value pairs and then asks for several keys; a recurrent model's "required ... hidden state dimensionality grows with the number of key-value pairs that the model needs to recall", attention's does not. {{BASED (Arora et al. 2024)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18668}} turns it into a trade-off curve: "a key tradeoff between a model's state size and recall ability". Mamba-2's own MQAR experiment: going "from N=16 to N=64 and N=256 consistently improves performance". authors

Copying. {{Jelassi et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01032}} prove a two-layer transformer can copy strings of exponential length while a fixed-state model "fails to solve the copy task, unless its latent state grows linearly with the sequence length", and in a phone-book test "even the smallest transformer (410M parameters) outperforms the largest GSSMs (2.8B parameters) when the phone-book size is long enough (L >= 70)", although "the Mamba models achieve lower perplexity than the Pythia models" (the paper prints the curves, not the numbers).

Measured here, three ways (all in {{Recall probes|#t-recall}}):

  • Pythia-160M against Mamba-130M, same Pile data and tokenizer: copying a list of random common words, and finding a number in a synthetic phone book.
  • The topic's tiny GPTs (same budget, section 9): a made-up name ("Zubo", split into " Z" "u" "bo") introduced, then repeated after d filler tokens; scored on the name's later pieces against a control where the first mention was a different name.
  • MQAR trained from scratch on 2-layer, 128-wide models (attention, SSM with state 16 or 64 per channel, one of each).
Predict: Pythia-160M and Mamba-130M are asked to repeat a list of random words. Mamba's state is 24 layers × 1,536 channels × 16 = 589,824 numbers; Pythia's KV cache holds 18,432 numbers per token. At about what list length should Mamba start to fall behind?

What a fixed state is good at. Constant memory and constant work per generated token, so long generations and many concurrent sequences are cheap (section 10); no position limit inside the recurrence (the tiny SSM GPT has no positions at all); and some state-tracking problems (parity, permutations) that delta-rule and Mamba-3-style layers solve and plain attention does not at fixed depth (RWKV-7, Mamba-3 papers, published). The trade is exact, unbounded lookup, which is why hybrids keep attention.

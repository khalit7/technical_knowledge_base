In one screen

A decoder block is the unit a GPT repeats: it reads the residual stream (one vector of d_model numbers per token, the model’s working memory), lets the token gather information from earlier tokens (attention), transforms each token on its own (the feed-forward network, FFN), and adds both results back to the stream. The table lists every step with its output shape, its parameters and its forward FLOPs per token (a FLOP here is one multiply or one add, so a multiply-add of a weight costs 2). t is the number of positions the token attends to.

derived Real models from their config.json through the {{Config calculator|#t-calc}}’s code (window.CC); the tiny GPT from src/data/tiny_gpt.py (GPT-2 style: d_model 256, 6 layers, 8 heads of 32, LayerNorm pre-norm, GELU 4x FFN, learned positions, tied embeddings, no biases).

What the lab measured (Apple M1 Pro; the tiny GPT trained here in 5 minutes)
  • The FLOPs: PyTorch’s own counter on one forward pass of the 256-token running sequence gives 3,355,443,200 FLOPs, exactly the hand formula; a training step (forward and backward) counts 3.0x the forward, the origin of “6N per token”.
  • For the tiny GPT at context 256, the FFN is the largest cost per token (48%) and attention over the context 12%; for Llama 3.1 8B, decoding a token costs as much in attention as in all its weights at about 28.6k tokens of context.
  • The residual stream only grows under pre-norm: the traced token’s norm goes from 1.3 after the embedding to 65.8 after block 6 in the reference model; under post-norm it is reset to about 16 after every sub-layer.
  • Post-norm at the default learning rate (3e-3, 100 warmup steps) does not train: it stalls at the loss of a token-frequency model (5.943 against 6.006) because almost no gradient reaches its lower blocks (5.1e-7 at block 1). Pre-norm reaches 2.281 with the same settings; post-norm needs a lower rate or a 500-step warmup, with which it reaches 2.138, the best of eight runs.

One token through one block, step by step

The token is “o” at position 157 of the running sequence, in ““Why are you sad, Ro”. The trained tiny GPT (the reference run, held-out loss 2.28) gives the next token, “x”, a probability of 75.8%: it has to find “Roxy” earlier in the story and copy it, which is attention’s job. Every picture below is the real vector at that step, one cell per number (orange positive, blue negative, darker is larger in magnitude), recorded by src/block/trace.py, which re-runs the block step by step and checks that its logits equal the model’s own forward pass (largest difference 1.5e-5). The model selector changes the shapes, parameter counts and FLOPs to a real model’s; the pictures and measured values stay the tiny GPT’s.

The same step in transformers’ Llama code (v5.20.1)

    What the recorded weights show (softmax step): in block 1, heads 1, 6 and 7 look mostly at the previous token, “R” (weights 0.65, 0.70 and 0.31), and head 5 already puts 0.39 on the “x” of the last “Roxy” (position 143). In blocks 5 and 6, several heads park 0.89 to 0.98 of their weight on the opening quote mark (position 150), a sink-like habit, and block 5’s head 4 has its largest weight (0.13) on the “x” of the very first “Roxy” (position 22). The {{Inside a real model|#t-inside}} tab measures such heads properly (induction scores, sinks) on SmolLM2-135M.

    Why each design choice exists. The scale 1/√d_head: a dot product of two vectors of d_head independent entries with variance 1 has variance d_head, so without the scale the softmax saturates (one weight near 1, gradients near 0) more as heads get wider. Softmax makes the weights positive and sum to one, so the head reads an average whose size does not depend on how many tokens it sees. Several heads let one layer look for several things at once (here one head copies the name, others track nearby words) at the same cost as one wide head. The residual add keeps an identity path from the embedding to the output, so a block only has to learn a correction and gradients reach the first layer. The FFN’s 4x width is the 2017 Transformer’s choice (d_model 512, d_ff 2048); SwiGLU uses three matrices, so Llama-style models cut the width to about 8/3 x d_model to keep the same parameters (Llama 3.1 8B: 14,336 / 4,096 = 3.5, a little wider).

    Shape drills: predict, then reveal

    1. Llama 3.1 8B: what is the shape of k_proj.weight? (hidden_size 4,096; 32 heads; 8 KV heads; head_dim 128)
    Skip to the answer
    (1024, 4096). K has num_key_value_heads x head_dim = 8 x 128 = 1,024 outputs, and nn.Linear(in, out) stores its weight as (out, in). q_proj is (4096, 4096). The tempting (4096, 4096) is right for multi-head attention, where K has as many heads as Q; grouped-query attention is why Llama’s K and V are 4x smaller than Q (modeling_llama.py:219-227).
    2. The tiny GPT runs the 256-token sequence with eager attention. What is the shape of one layer’s attention weights, and how many bytes in float32?
    Skip to the answer
    (batch, heads, T, T) = (1, 8, 256, 256): 524,288 numbers, 2 MiB in float32, per layer. It grows with T squared: at T = 32,768 one Llama 3.1 8B layer (32 heads) would need 32 x 32,768² x 2 bytes = 64 GiB in bfloat16 for one sequence. That is why fused kernels (FlashAttention) compute the softmax tile by tile and never store this tensor ({{CUDA: writing kernels|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b9ab89c1bb7238cb6c}}). The heads come before T because .view(B, T, H, hd).transpose(1, 2) moves them there (option 4 is the shape before the transpose).
    3. Decoding one token at context 256, which operation of the tiny GPT costs the most FLOPs?
    Skip to the answer
    The FFN: 6,291,456 of 13,107,200 FLOPs (48%). Then the unembedding 2,097,152 (16%; the vocabulary is 16x d_model), Q, K, V 2,359,296 (18%), attention over the context 1,572,864 (12%) and the output projection 786,432 (6%). Attention passes the FFN only past 4 x 256 = 1,024 positions here (its cost is 4 x d x t per layer against 16 x d² for the FFN). Move the context slider in Where the FLOPs go to see it.

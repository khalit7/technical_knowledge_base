Common mistakes

Sizing the KV cache by query heads
The cache is set by KV heads. Llama 3.1 70B has 64 query heads and 8 KV heads; using 64 overstates its cache 8 times (2,560 KiB per token instead of 320).
Computing head_dim as hidden_size / num_heads
Gemma 3 27B: 5,376 / 32 = 168, but its head_dim is 128, so every derived cache and FLOP figure is 31% too high. Read the field when it exists (section 12).
Dropping rope_scaling when converting or copying a config
The model loads without a warning and answers short prompts well; past the original training length (8,192 for Llama 3.1) quality falls apart, because positions beyond it now get angles the model never saw. Diff the config against the original after any conversion. TODO 11 (labs): a real bad output from a run past the trained length, if a lab records one.
Treating total parameters as compute, or active parameters as memory
DeepSeek-V3 runs 37B parameters per token but needs memory for all 671B. Total sets the memory floor; active sets FLOPs per token. At large decode batches nearly every expert is touched in each step, so the weight bytes read per step approach the total, not the active count.
Assuming sparse attention or sliding windows shrink what a token stores
Selecting the top k keys cuts reads and FLOPs; every key is still stored for the indexer to choose from. A window caps how long entries are kept, not their size. Only fewer KV heads, compression (MLA), lower precision or layers without a cache shrink the per-token entry.
Calling a hybrid "cache-free"
Its attention layers still grow a cache (24 KiB per token in Qwen3-Next), and each request carries a fixed state for the recurrent layers.
Counting tied embeddings twice
With tie_word_embeddings: true the unembedding is the embedding matrix: count V × d once. For a 135M-parameter model with a 49,152-token vocabulary at d = 576 that is 28M parameters, a fifth of the model.

Interview questions

Parameters of a Llama 3.1 8B block, in your head?
Attention: Q and output 2 × 4,096² = 33.6M, K and V 2 × 4,096 × 1,024 = 8.4M; FFN 3 × 4,096 × 14,336 = 176.2M; about 218M. Times 32, plus two 525M embedding tables: 8.03B.
Why divide attention scores by √dh?
A dot product of two dh-dimensional vectors with unit-variance entries has variance dh. Without the scale, scores grow with head size, the softmax saturates into a one-hot and its gradients vanish.
Why is decode memory-bound when prefill is compute-bound?
Prefill multiplies every weight by thousands of prompt tokens at once: many FLOPs per byte read. Decode multiplies every weight, and reads the whole cache, for one new token per sequence: about 2 FLOPs per weight read. Batching more sequences raises the ratio for weights but not for each sequence's own cache.
GQA or MQA, and why not always MQA?
MQA has the smallest cache but the largest quality loss, and with tensor parallelism its single KV head must be replicated on every GPU. GQA with 8 groups keeps most of MHA's quality at an eighth (for 64 heads) of its cache, and 8 KV heads split cleanly over 8 GPUs.
Why pre-norm rather than the original post-norm?
Post-norm normalises the residual stream itself, so gradients to early layers pass through every norm and deep models need careful warmup. Pre-norm leaves an identity path from output to input; the price is a residual stream whose size grows with depth, which extra norms (QK-norm, norms after the sublayer) contain.
Your model is fine at 4K and produces garbage at 32K after a format conversion. First thing you check?
The RoPE fields: rope_theta, rope_scaling and max_position_embeddings against the original config. A dropped scaling rule is silent at short context.
Design: a model for 128K context that serves on one 80 GB GPU at batch 32.
Budget first: an 8B-class model's BF16 weights take about 16 GB, leaving about 64 GB for 32 × 131,072 = 4.2M cached tokens: about 15 KB per token across all layers. Llama 3.1 8B's GQA needs 128 KiB per token (512 GiB in total), 9 times over. So change the attention, not the size: for example 32 layers of which 8 are full attention with 2 KV heads of 128 and an FP8 cache (4 KiB per token, 16 GiB in total) and 24 linear or SSM layers (a fixed state of about 24 MiB per request, 768 MiB in total), leaving room for activations. Name the price: exact recall over 128K rests on 8 layers, so measure it with a long-context retrieval benchmark, and train the long-context phase with the target RoPE scaling. Alternatives to discuss: MLA (36 KiB per token at this shape in BF16, still too big alone at batch 32 without FP8 and fewer layers), or GQA with sliding windows on 5 of 6 layers (derived in src/read/derive.py).
Design: why did DeepSeek choose MLA, and what does it cost?
At DeepSeek-V3's shape (61 layers, 128 heads of 128) full MHA would keep 3,904 KiB per token; MLA keeps a 512-number latent plus a 64-number position key per layer, 68.6 KiB, 57 times less, which makes long-context serving of a large model affordable. DeepSeek reports quality at or above MHA in its ablations (DeepSeek-V2). The cost: extra projections in every attention call, a decoupled RoPE path because rotation cannot pass through the absorbed up-projections, its own kernels, and a cache that does not split across GPUs by heads (every head reads the same latent).
An MoE run's loss is fine but a third of the experts receive almost no tokens. What is happening and what do you change?
Routing collapse: early winners get more tokens, train faster and win more. Add or raise the load-balancing loss, or use bias-based balancing as in DeepSeek-V3; check the router's logits for growth (a router z-loss bounds them); log tokens per expert per layer from the start.

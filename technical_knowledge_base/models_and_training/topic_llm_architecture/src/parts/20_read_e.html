6. The feed-forward network, and mixture of experts

What the FFN does. After attention has gathered information from other positions, the FFN transforms each position on its own: expand to a wider hidden layer, apply a nonlinearity, project back. It holds most of the parameters (81% of a Llama 3.1 8B block, section 2) and behaves like a key-value memory where many stored facts are found ({{Geva et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.14913}}). The original used ReLU at width 4d (2,048 for d = 512, {{Vaswani et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762}}); current models use SwiGLU, a gated variant with three matrices instead of two: out = Wdown( SiLU(Wgatex) × Wupx ). It improved quality at equal compute in {{Shazeer 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05202}}, which shrank the width to 2/3 of 4d (8d/3) so the three matrices cost what two did; Llama 3.1 8B uses 14,336 = 3.5d.

Mixture of experts (MoE). Replace the one FFN by E smaller FFNs (experts) and a router, a linear layer that scores every expert for each token; the token goes to its top k experts and their outputs are summed, weighted by the router scores. The model then has many parameters (memory, knowledge) but each token pays for only k experts (compute, latency). Two numbers describe it: total parameters (what must sit in memory) and active parameters (what one token uses). DeepSeek-V3 has 256 routed experts plus 1 shared expert that every token uses, 8 routed per token: in one of its MoE layers the experts hold 11.3 billion weights and a token uses 0.40 billion of them, 3.5% (derived from its {{config|https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3/blob/main/config.json}}: experts of 3 × 7,168 × 2,048); the whole model is 671B total and 37B active ({{DeepSeek-V3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}, vendor).

The same batch of 8 tokens through a dense FFN and through an MoE layer (8 experts, top 2)

Scale: one expert box is one FFN of the dense model's size, so the MoE stores 8 times the FFN weights and runs 2 per token. Router scores are illustrative (a fixed seeded draw) until replaced by the tiny GPT's recorded router choices on the running sequence (TODO 6: recorded router choices, if a lab trains the moe8top2 preset). Capacity factor 1.25: each expert accepts at most 1.25 × 8 × 2 / 8 = 2.5, so 2 tokens per expert in this batch; extra tokens are dropped and pass through on the residual stream only.

What it costs and what goes wrong. Experts must stay busy in equal measure: a router that sends most tokens to a few experts wastes the rest and, with a fixed capacity per expert, drops the overflow (the token skips the FFN). The classic fix is an auxiliary loss that rewards balanced routing ({{Switch Transformers|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a8b541c9babbf40c94}}); DeepSeek-V3 instead adds a per-expert bias to the routing scores, nudged after each step, and reports this "auxiliary-loss-free" scheme beats the loss ({{DeepSeek-V3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}, vendor). The trend is from few large experts (Mixtral 8x7B: 8 experts, top 2, {{Mixtral|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba72af0ac91ddc6b3}}) to many small ones (Qwen3-235B-A22B: 128 experts, top 8, no shared expert, {{Qwen3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388}}). Serving an MoE needs memory for every expert and enough tokens per batch that each expert gets a useful share; spread over GPUs it needs expert parallelism and its all-to-all traffic.

7. Normalisation, residuals and stability

The problem. Deep stacks are hard to train: activations and gradients grow or shrink layer by layer. A normalisation layer rescales a vector to a standard size. The 2017 Transformer put it after the residual add, post-norm: x′ = Norm(x + F(x)). That normalises the residual stream itself, and deep post-norm models need a careful learning-rate warmup to train at all. Pre-norm, x′ = x + F(Norm(x)), normalises only the copy each sublayer reads and leaves an untouched identity path through the stack; {{Xiong et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.04745}} showed it trains without warmup. Its known cost is that the stream's size grows with depth, since every layer only adds. Measured on the tiny GPT with the same seed and budget: TODO 7 (Block lab): residual-stream norm by layer under pre- and post-norm, and the learning rate at which post-norm diverges (loss and gradient-norm numbers).

  • RMSNorm ({{Zhang and Sennrich 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.07467}}) divides by the root mean square and drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction and bias: one reduction instead of two, no measured quality loss; universal in current models.
  • QK-norm: RMSNorm on queries and keys before the dot product, so attention scores cannot grow without bound during training, a known source of loss spikes ({{Dehghani et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.05442}}; used in {{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}}, Gemma 3 and Qwen3).
  • Norms after the sublayer: OLMo 2 normalises each sublayer's output before adding it, x + Norm(F(x)), still with a clean residual path; Gemma 3 normalises both before and after ({{Gemma 3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19786}}).

How to check a run. A loss spike is a sudden jump in training loss, sometimes recovered, sometimes not. Log the global gradient norm, the largest attention logit and the per-layer residual norms: a spike preceded by growing attention logits points at QK-norm or a lower learning rate; a single bad batch points at data. Large models also show a few massive activations, single residual-stream dimensions thousands of times larger than the rest ({{Sun et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17762}}), which matter for low-precision training and quantisation.

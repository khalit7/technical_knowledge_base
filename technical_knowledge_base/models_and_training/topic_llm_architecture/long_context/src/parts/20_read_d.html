2. Smaller entries: fewer KV heads, a latent, fewer bits

The problem. Every factor of the formula in section 1 is a lever. Three of them shrink each token's entry while keeping every token.

  • Fewer KV heads (GQA, MQA): query heads share keys and values. Llama 3.1's 8 KV heads for 32 (8B) or 64 (70B) query heads already cut the cache 4 or 8 times against MHA; the quality cost is small when done in training or uptraining (GQA paper, root's Attention lab). Owned by {{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}}.
  • A compressed latent (MLA): DeepSeek-V3 caches 512 latent numbers plus a 64-number RoPE key per layer, 68.6 KiB per token over 61 layers in bf16, against about 3.8 MiB for full multi-head attention at the same shape derived; DeepSeek-V2's paper reports that MLA "reduces the KV cache by 93.3%" against its 67B dense predecessor (vendor). The {{DeepSeek MLA explainer|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}} animates it.
  • Fewer bits per number (KV quantisation): store keys and values in 8, 4 or 2 bits with a scale per group. It needs no retraining, which is why engines ship it (FP8 in vLLM and SGLang; q8_0 and q4_0 in llama.cpp: {{Batching, KV cache and scheduling|n:3c65c17b0d0d819db53bd2c48909e5b2}} section 8 measured them on Qwen3-1.7B).

How quantisation fails, and the fix. Rounding to b bits with one scale per group spends the group's range on its largest value. Keys in trained models have a few outlier channels, dimensions that are large for every token, so a per-token group of keys is dominated by them and the rest of the channels round to almost nothing. KIVI's finding is that "the key cache should be quantized per-channel" and "the value cache should be quantized per-token", which makes 2-bit caches workable ({{Liu et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.02750}}; the authors report "2.6× less peak memory (including model weight)" and up to 4 times larger batches, authors' claims, not run here).

Measured here on SmolLM2-135M at 4,096 tokens (the {{Eviction lab|#t-evict}}; 30 pass-key prompts): with the full cache the model answers 97%; int8 per token 97%, int4 87% (KIVI layout 93%), int2 7% per token against 50% with keys per channel. SmolLM2's cached keys show the premise: in the typical head the largest key channel's mean magnitude is 9.4 times the median channel's, against 2.7 times for values (src/kv/key_channels.py). 8 bits are free, 4 bits nearly free, 2 bits need the per-channel layout and still lose half. measured on Apple M1 Pro (CPU)

When it matters, how to check. Quantising the cache halves (fp8) or quarters (4-bit) memory and decode reads at once, and composes with every other lever. Check quality on a retrieval task at your length, not perplexity, and look at the key statistics: if a handful of channels have a far larger magnitude than the rest, per-token key quantisation will hurt at low bits.

3. Fewer entries: eviction

The problem. Most cached tokens get little attention most of the time. If the right ones could be dropped, the cache would stop growing with the context. Eviction scores each cached entry and keeps the best within a budget; the scores differ:

  • Recency and sinks (StreamingLLM): keep the first few tokens, which soak up attention that has nowhere better to go (the root's Inside a real model tab measures SmolLM2's sink), and a recent window. It keeps the model fluent forever; it does not remember what left the window ({{Xiao et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17453}}).
  • Accumulated attention (H2O): keep the "heavy hitters", tokens that "contribute most of the value when computing attention scores", plus recent ones; its authors report up to 29 times the throughput of DeepSpeed Zero-Inference and Hugging Face Accelerate with 20% heavy hitters ({{Zhang et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14048}}, authors' claims).
  • Question-aware attention (SnapKV): score each entry by the attention it gets from the last tokens of the prompt, which usually contain the question ({{Li et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14469}}).

Measured here, same prompts as section 2. At a quarter of the cache SnapKV still answers 97% and at 12.5% 87%; scored before the question arrived it answers 0% at a quarter; H2O answers 17% even at half the cache, sink plus recent 10% at 12.5%. On re-reading an earlier story, every eviction policy loses most of the benefit already at half the cache (copy loss 0.61 full, 1.42 for the best policy at 50%). On an unseen story, the perplexity-style test, all of them look fine: H2O at 12.5% moves the loss from 2.07 to 2.10.

Two lessons a paper's headline will not give you

First, an eviction method is only as good as the test: a perplexity or summarisation score can stay flat while retrieval of a single fact goes to zero. Second, question-aware methods need the question at compression time; in a multi-turn chat, an agent loop or a shared prefix cache, the document is compressed before anyone asks about it, and the method becomes another heuristic. At equal memory, int4 quantisation (section 2) kept both retrieval and copying in these runs; no eviction policy did.

Cost and when it matters. Scoring needs attention weights, which fused kernels such as FlashAttention never materialise, so engines approximate or recompute them; prefill-then-compress never lowers the peak memory of the prefill itself; and per-head selections do not free paged blocks. The Batching page measured the realisable saving: per-head selections freed 0% of 16-token blocks. How to check: run your retrieval and copy tasks at the budget, with the question placed where your application places it, and measure memory actually freed in the engine.

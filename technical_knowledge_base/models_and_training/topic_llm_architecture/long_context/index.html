Long context: KV cache growth and compression, local and global attention, effective context

Why every extra token of context costs memory and compute, the four families of fixes (smaller entries, fewer entries, local layers, other mixers) and what each costs in quality, and why the context a model accepts is not the context it uses. Measured on SmolLM2-135M and the topic's tiny GPT on an Apple M1 Pro; real model configs computed with the root's calculator; published results kept apart. A child of Topic: llm-architecture. Data as of October 2026.

A transformer's cost per token grows with everything it has already read: the KV cache (the keys and values of every earlier token, kept so they are not recomputed) grows linearly with the context, prefill attention grows with its square, positions past the trained length were never seen, and a model that clears all three may still not use what it read. This page takes those four walls in turn: what each fix shrinks, what it costs in quality (measured on SmolLM2-135M and the topic's tiny GPT on this machine, and from the papers), and how to check it on your own model. It is a child of Topic: llm-architecture, whose section 9 is this page in one paragraph. Reading time about 22 minutes (measured from the words outside widgets at 200 a minute), plus about 4 for the folded mistakes and answers; the three labs add about 25 with their controls.

And the context a model accepts is not the context it uses (section 7, Effective context lab).

1. Why every token of context costs memory and compute

The problem. A decoder generates one token at a time, and each new token's attention needs the keys and values (the K and V vectors every earlier token produced in every layer). Recomputing them for the whole prefix at every step would cost a full forward pass over the context per token, so engines keep them: the KV cache. It is the only part of a running transformer that grows with the context. The root explains why the cache exists (Topic: llm-architecture, section 3, and its Attention lab); this page is about what happens when the context gets long.

How big it gets. Llama 3.1 8B (32 layers, 8 KV heads of 128) stores 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 131,072 bytes, 128 KiB, per token. At its advertised 131,072 tokens that is 16 GiB for one sequence, as much as its 16.06 GB of bf16 weights; Llama 3.1 70B needs 320 KiB per token, 40 GiB per 128K sequence derived (the root's Config calculator, which reconciles these configs with their Hub parameter counts exactly). Concurrency multiplies it: on one 80 GB GPU, after the 8B model's weights, three 128K sequences fit, and thirteen at 32K derived, the Memory wall tab's defaults.

Why it is slow, not only big. Decoding reads the whole cache once per generated token: each cached number is used for two multiply-adds (one score, one weighted sum), far too little arithmetic per byte to keep a GPU's compute units busy, so decode speed is set by memory bandwidth (Performance math has the roofline). At 128K tokens the 8B model reads 16 GiB of cache per sequence per step on top of its 16 GB of weights; batching shares the weight read across sequences, never the cache read.

Compute grows faster than memory. Reading a prompt (prefill) costs every token's attention over all earlier tokens, so the attention FLOPs of a prompt grow with the square of its length, while the weight FLOPs grow linearly. For Llama 3.1 8B a decode step's attention FLOPs equal its weight FLOPs at about 28,600 cached tokens, and at 131,072 tokens attention is 70% of the prefill's FLOPs derived (root Block lab and Config calculator; the Memory wall's compute panel shows any model).

The four walls (the root's section 9, in one line each)

Memory: the cache grows linearly with context and concurrency. Compute: prefill attention grows with the square of the prompt. Position: positions past the trained length were never seen. Use: a model that fits and runs a long prompt may still not use it. Sections 2 to 5 take the first two, section 6 the third, section 7 the fourth.

Predict. Llama 3.1 70B goes from 8 KV heads to MQA (one KV head). At 128K tokens on eight 80 GB GPUs, how many more sequences fit, and how much faster does a 128K prefill get?
The cache shrinks by the number of KV heads removed (8 to 1), so about eight times the sequences fit once the weights are paid for (10 at 128K as configured; the Memory wall gives the exact count). Prefill FLOPs barely move: attention FLOPs are set by the 64 query heads and the positions each reads, not by how many distinct keys are stored. Fewer KV heads cut memory and decode bandwidth; only windows, sparsity or a different mixer cut prefill compute.

How to check it. Compute 2 × num_hidden_layers × num_key_value_heads × head_dim × bytes from config.json (watch for sliding_window, layer_types and MLA's kv_lora_rank, which change the sum), multiply by context and concurrency, and compare with the GPU's memory after weights. In a running vLLM server the metric vllm:kv_cache_usage_perc shows how full the cache is (Batching, KV cache and scheduling).

2. Smaller entries: fewer KV heads, a latent, fewer bits

The problem. Every factor of the formula in section 1 is a lever. Three of them shrink each token's entry while keeping every token.

  • Fewer KV heads (GQA, MQA): query heads share keys and values. Llama 3.1's 8 KV heads for 32 (8B) or 64 (70B) query heads already cut the cache 4 or 8 times against MHA; the quality cost is small when done in training or uptraining (GQA paper, root's Attention lab). Owned by Attention variants.
  • A compressed latent (MLA): DeepSeek-V3 caches 512 latent numbers plus a 64-number RoPE key per layer, 68.6 KiB per token over 61 layers in bf16, against about 3.8 MiB for full multi-head attention at the same shape derived; DeepSeek-V2's paper reports that MLA "reduces the KV cache by 93.3%" against its 67B dense predecessor (vendor). The DeepSeek MLA explainer animates it.
  • Fewer bits per number (KV quantisation): store keys and values in 8, 4 or 2 bits with a scale per group. It needs no retraining, which is why engines ship it (FP8 in vLLM and SGLang; q8_0 and q4_0 in llama.cpp: Batching, KV cache and scheduling section 8 measured them on Qwen3-1.7B).

How quantisation fails, and the fix. Rounding to b bits with one scale per group spends the group's range on its largest value. Keys in trained models have a few outlier channels, dimensions that are large for every token, so a per-token group of keys is dominated by them and the rest of the channels round to almost nothing. KIVI's finding is that "the key cache should be quantized per-channel" and "the value cache should be quantized per-token", which makes 2-bit caches workable (Liu et al. 2024; the authors report "2.6× less peak memory (including model weight)" and up to 4 times larger batches, authors' claims, not run here).

Measured here on SmolLM2-135M at 4,096 tokens (the Eviction lab; 30 pass-key prompts): with the full cache the model answers 97%; int8 per token 97%, int4 87% (KIVI layout 93%), int2 7% per token against 50% with keys per channel. SmolLM2's cached keys show the premise: in the typical head the largest key channel's mean magnitude is 9.4 times the median channel's, against 2.7 times for values (src/kv/key_channels.py). 8 bits are free, 4 bits nearly free, 2 bits need the per-channel layout and still lose half. measured on Apple M1 Pro (CPU)

When it matters, how to check. Quantising the cache halves (fp8) or quarters (4-bit) memory and decode reads at once, and composes with every other lever. Check quality on a retrieval task at your length, not perplexity, and look at the key statistics: if a handful of channels have a far larger magnitude than the rest, per-token key quantisation will hurt at low bits.

3. Fewer entries: eviction

The problem. Most cached tokens get little attention most of the time. If the right ones could be dropped, the cache would stop growing with the context. Eviction scores each cached entry and keeps the best within a budget; the scores differ:

  • Recency and sinks (StreamingLLM): keep the first few tokens, which soak up attention that has nowhere better to go (the root's Inside a real model tab measures SmolLM2's sink), and a recent window. It keeps the model fluent forever; it does not remember what left the window (Xiao et al. 2023).
  • Accumulated attention (H2O): keep the "heavy hitters", tokens that "contribute most of the value when computing attention scores", plus recent ones; its authors report up to 29 times the throughput of DeepSpeed Zero-Inference and Hugging Face Accelerate with 20% heavy hitters (Zhang et al. 2023, authors' claims).
  • Question-aware attention (SnapKV): score each entry by the attention it gets from the last tokens of the prompt, which usually contain the question (Li et al. 2024).

Measured here, same prompts as section 2. At a quarter of the cache SnapKV still answers 97% and at 12.5% 87%; scored before the question arrived it answers 0% at a quarter; H2O answers 17% even at half the cache, sink plus recent 10% at 12.5%. On re-reading an earlier story, every eviction policy loses most of the benefit already at half the cache (copy loss 0.61 full, 1.42 for the best policy at 50%). On an unseen story, the perplexity-style test, all of them look fine: H2O at 12.5% moves the loss from 2.07 to 2.10.

Two lessons a paper's headline will not give you

First, an eviction method is only as good as the test: a perplexity or summarisation score can stay flat while retrieval of a single fact goes to zero. Second, question-aware methods need the question at compression time; in a multi-turn chat, an agent loop or a shared prefix cache, the document is compressed before anyone asks about it, and the method becomes another heuristic. At equal memory, int4 quantisation (section 2) kept both retrieval and copying in these runs; no eviction policy did.

Cost and when it matters. Scoring needs attention weights, which fused kernels such as FlashAttention never materialise, so engines approximate or recompute them; prefill-then-compress never lowers the peak memory of the prefill itself; and per-head selections do not free paged blocks. The Batching page measured the realisable saving: per-head selections freed 0% of 16-token blocks. How to check: run your retrieval and copy tasks at the budget, with the question placed where your application places it, and measure memory actually freed in the engine.

4. Local and global layers: a smaller cache designed in, not cut out

The problem. Eviction (section 3) decides after training which entries to drop, and the model was never trained to cope. A local (sliding-window) layer makes the decision part of the architecture: during training and inference each query attends only to the last W tokens, so the layer's cache never holds more than W entries. A global layer attends to everything. Interleaving the two keeps a direct path to any old token in the global layers while most layers stay cheap. The root's Attention lab (section 6) shows the mechanism and the ring-buffer cache; Attention variants owns windows as a cache design. Here: what the layouts in production cost and buy at length.

What it costs in quality, published. Google reports that for Gemma 3 changing the local:global ratio has "minimal impact on perplexity", "even with 7-to-1 local to global", and that the window "can be reduced significantly without impacting perplexity" (Gemma 3 report, sections 5.1 and 5.2, vendor); on memory, "global only" gives "a memory overhead of 60%" at 32K, "reduced to less than 15%" with sliding windows of 1,024 (same section; the report writes the ratio as "1:3" there and 5:1 elsewhere, an inconsistency in the source). Gemma 2's Table 10 shrinks the trained 4,096 window at inference to 2,048 and 1,024: validation perplexity 1.63, 1.63, 1.64 (vendor). Two cautions: all of these are perplexity, which section 3 measured to be the wrong test for long-range use, and all are models trained with their layout. Databricks' MixAttention study, which trained sliding-window plus cross-layer sharing variants and tested them on RULER, found that long-context ability needs "at least one standard attention KV cache that is computed in the deeper layers" (Rajput et al. 2024, third-party to Character.AI, whose 2024 blog on the same combination it builds on).

Measured here: imposing a layout does not work. SmolLM2-135M was trained with full attention in all 30 layers. Turning 25 of them into local layers after training (keep the first 4 tokens plus a window, every sixth layer global, at the same memory as the other policies) is the local_global policy of the Eviction lab: result pending. The model never learned to route old information through its global layers. A layout is a training-time decision; converting a trained model needs continued training with the layout in place.

Cross-layer sharing attacks the other factor of the formula, the layer count: adjacent layers reuse one set of keys and values. Cross-Layer Attention reports it can "reduce the size of the KV cache by another 2x" beyond MQA "while maintaining nearly the same accuracy as unmodified MQA", trained from scratch at 1B and 3B parameters (Brandon et al. 2024, third-party). The extreme is a model with a constant-size state in most layers: hybrids of attention with Mamba-2 or Gated DeltaNet layers (Nemotron-H 8B keeps 4 attention layers of 52; Qwen3-Next one in four), owned by Beyond attention.

When it matters, how to check. Local layers pay off once the context is several times the window; below that they cost nothing and save nothing. Check the layout in config.json (sliding_window with layer_types or sliding_window_pattern, attention_chunk_size), and check that your engine actually frees windowed entries: a cache that allocates every layer at full length gives the quality cost of windows without their memory. Then test the long-range path with retrieval at the target length, not perplexity.

5. The compute side: long prompts

The problem. A 128K-token prompt is a 128K-row attention matrix per head per layer. Three separate costs follow, with separate fixes, each owned by a neighbouring page:

This is why long-context training comes last and short: Llama 3 grew its context from 8K to 128K in six stages over about 800B tokens at the end of pretraining, not earlier, because attention cost grows with the square of the length (the Llama 3 paper page, from the report, vendor); how such a phase is run is on Continued Pretraining (CPT). How to check a serving setup: time a prefill at your target length (time to first token) and read the attention share in the Memory wall compute panel: below the crossover (about 28,600 tokens for an 8B Llama) weights dominate and attention tricks change little.

6. Past the trained length: the position wall

The problem. A model is trained on windows of some length; at inference nothing stops a longer prompt. With RoPE (rotary position embedding: each query and key is rotated by an angle proportional to its position, so their dot product depends on the distance), a longer prompt produces angles, for the slowly rotating dimensions, that training never showed. With learned absolute positions there is no row in the table at all. Positional Encodings owns the schemes and the extension methods (position interpolation, NTK-aware scaling, YaRN, Llama 3's rule); this section shows what the wall costs and why training at length is still needed.

Measured here on the tiny GPT trained on 256-token windows (the Effective context lab, section 1; loss per token on 2,048-token held-out windows): plain RoPE goes from 2.07 inside the trained length to 3.82 at positions 1,024 to 2,047. Scaling at inference changes the trade: linear interpolation x4 holds better at length (3.15) but costs the short range (2.74 in the first 256 positions); YaRN x8 stays at 2.43 at 1,024 to 2,047 with 2.29 in range. A learned-position model simply raises IndexError: learned positions stop at 256; got position 299. measured on Apple M1 Pro (CPU)

Training at length, and a model with no wall. 300 more steps at 1,024 tokens (2.46M tokens, a fifth of the original budget) bring positions 512 to 1,023 down to 2.03 with position interpolation x4 and to 2.03 with plain RoPE, below the in-length loss of the original model; past the new length the interpolated model hits a new wall (3.29 at 1,024 to 2,047, where x4 runs out) while the plain one degrades gently (2.14). At this toy scale the training, not the scaling rule, did the work. The Attention lab's sliding-window model (window 64, RoPE, trained at 256) shows no wall at all: 2.1 in range, 2.09 at 1,024 to 2,047, because no query ever looks further than 64 tokens, so no unseen angle appears. It extrapolates by never using long distances, which is also why it cannot retrieve from them.

Why scaling alone is not enough. In a window of length L, distance d between a query and a key occurs L − d times, so long distances are rare in training even inside the window (the lab's histogram). An et al. trace short effective contexts in open models to exactly this "left-skewed frequency distribution of relative positions", and find effective lengths "typically not exceeding half of their training lengths" (An et al. 2024, third-party). Production recipes therefore pair a scaling rule with a training phase at the new length: Gemma 3 pre-trains with 32K sequences and rescales RoPE by a factor of 8 to reach 128K (Gemma 3 report, section 5.3, vendor); Llama 3 grew from 8K to 128K over six stages (section 5).

How to check. Read rope_scaling and original_max_position_embeddings in the config; plot loss by position on long held-out documents past the length you need; a curve that turns up at the trained length is the position wall.

7. Effective context: accepting a token is not using it

The problem. A model can fit a 128K prompt in memory, read it without a position error, and still ignore most of it. The effective context is the longest input at which the model still does a given task well; it depends on the task and the threshold, and it is measured, never read from the config. Published measurements at scale (RULER found that of models claiming 32K or more "only half" hold up at 32K; NoLiMa removes the shared words between question and needle and finds most models effective to 2K or less) are on Long-context and multimodal benchmarks, which owns them.

Why it happens, architecturally. Four causes, each with a section above: positions trained rarely (section 6); softmax attention spreading thinner over more keys, so one relevant token gets a smaller share of a head's attention as the context grows (a length-dependent attention temperature counters it; YaRN's is one, owned by Positional Encodings); local layers and windows that cannot see far (section 4); and caches compressed by policies the model never trained with (section 3).

Measured here on SmolLM2-135M, full cache (the Effective context lab, section 3): pending Within the config's length the small model retrieves most numbers (its misses at 1K and 2K are all the first digit, 1 for 6 or 7); at 8,192 it fails only when the number sits at the very start, 8K tokens back; at 12,288 and 16,384 it answers none and produces broken text such as "a1...." for 66226: a hard wall at the advertised length, with no scaling configured.

How to check. At the length and with the prompt format you will use: a retrieval grid over depth, then a task that cannot be solved by string matching. Report the length at which your threshold is still met, not the config's maximum.

8. Designing for a context budget

The question. "Serve 128K-token requests, 32 at a time, on one 80 GB GPU." Start from Llama 3.1 8B and move one lever at a time in the Memory wall (bf16 weights, 92% of memory usable; derived, src/wall/out/examples.json):

Reading the table. No single lever reaches 32. The fp8 cache is free to adopt (section 2 measured 8 bits as lossless here) but only doubles capacity. MQA or a local/global layout cut the cache 8 and 6 times, but both are training-time choices: on a released model they mean uptraining or continued training with the new layout, and the Eviction lab shows that imposing a layout without training fails. Only the windowed layout also cuts prefill compute (attention from 70% to 28% of the FLOPs), which matters as much for time to first token as memory does for concurrency. The answer a good design review gives: a model trained with a local/global layout (as Gemma 3 and gpt-oss are), an fp8 cache, chunked prefill so the 128K prompts do not stall the 31 streams already decoding, and an acceptance test on retrieval at 128K, because none of these numbers says the model uses the context (section 7).

Other budgets. Long single sequences (agents, codebases) favour fewer, longer requests: there the cache of one sequence and the prefill dominate, and hybrids with a constant-state mixer in most layers (Beyond attention) or trained sparse attention (DeepSeek's DSA) change the curve rather than shifting it. Many short chats favour MLA or few KV heads plus prefix caching. Mixture-of-experts models move the weight bar, not the cache: DeepSeek-V3 needs 671 GB of fp8 weights on eight 141 GB GPUs but only 9.21 GB of MLA cache per 128K sequence, so 39 fit.

Common mistakes

"max_position_embeddings is the context the model was trained for."
It is the size of an allocation and of the RoPE cache in the implementation. Qwen3 32B's config says 40,960 for a model trained to 32,768 natively; MiniMax-M1's says 10,240,000 (root's Architecture map). Read the model card and rope_scaling, then measure (section 7).
Sizing the cache with num_attention_heads.
The cache holds KV heads. With num_attention_heads Llama 3.1 70B comes out at 2,560 KiB per token instead of 320 KiB, eight times too much (1 sequence at 128K on eight 80 GB GPUs instead of 10). Use num_key_value_heads, and for MLA kv_lora_rank + qk_rope_head_dim.
Running a learned-position model past its table.
The tiny GPT with learned positions, given 300 tokens: IndexError: learned positions stop at 256; got position 299 (the topic's model raises on purpose, with a clear message). There is no scaling rule for a learned table: retrain or switch position scheme.
Evicting the first tokens.
A plain recent window applied to SmolLM2 at half the cache: new-story loss 4.02; keep 4 sink tokens as well: 2.08 (full cache 2.07). Every eviction policy must protect the sink tokens.
Validating a compressed cache with perplexity.
H2O at 12.5% of the cache: new-story loss 2.07 to 2.10, pass-key 97% to 0%. Test retrieval and copying at your length.
Question-aware eviction where the question comes later.
SnapKV at a quarter of the cache: 97% with the question in its window, 0% when the cache was compressed first. Prefix caches, multi-turn chats and agents compress before the question.
2-bit keys quantised per token.
Pass-key 7% against 50% with keys per channel (KIVI's layout); the new-story loss also rises to 2.45. Keys have outlier channels; quantise them along the channel.
Stretching RoPE at inference and calling it done.
Linear interpolation x4 on the tiny GPT costs the short range: 2.07 to 2.74 in the first 256 positions. Every production recipe adds training at the new length (section 6).

Interview questions

KV cache of Llama 3.1 70B at 128K tokens, in your head?
2 (K and V) × 80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 head dim × 2 bytes = 327,680 bytes, 320 KiB per token; times 131,072 tokens = 40 GiB per sequence. Weights are 141 GB in bf16, so eight 80 GB GPUs hold about 10 such sequences.
Why does going from GQA to MQA not speed up a long prefill?
Prefill attention FLOPs scale with query heads times positions read; fewer KV heads only shrink what is stored and what decode reads. MQA helps decode (bandwidth) and concurrency (memory), not prefill compute. Windows, sparse attention or recurrent layers cut prefill.
StreamingLLM runs for millions of tokens. Does the model remember them?
No. It keeps the sink tokens so attention stays well behaved, plus a recent window; anything that left the window is gone. It is stable, not long-memory.
SnapKV against H2O: which keeps a planted fact, and when does the better one fail?
SnapKV, because it scores by the question's own attention; H2O keeps tokens that many queries attended, which a fact read once is not (here: 87% against 0% at 12.5%). SnapKV fails when the cache is compressed before the question exists.
Eviction or quantisation at the same memory?
Quantisation first: it keeps every entry, needs no attention scores, works with fused kernels and paged blocks, and 8 or 4 bits cost little (here int4 kept retrieval and copying; eviction at 25% kept neither copying nor, except question-aware, retrieval). Evict when the context itself must be bounded (streaming), with sinks kept.
Why can't you make a trained full-attention model local/global after the fact?
Its retrieval heads are spread over many layers; making most of them local removes the paths it learned (SmolLM2 with 25 of 30 layers made local: 37% at half the cache). Models with a layout are trained with it (Gemma 2 and 3, gpt-oss); conversion needs continued training.
What is effective context and how do you measure it for your product?
The longest input at which the model still meets your quality threshold on your task. Measure loss by position for the position wall, a retrieval grid over length and depth, then a task without literal overlap (NoLiMa-style) or your own long-document evaluation, at the prompt format you ship.
Design: 128K context, 32 concurrent requests, one 80 GB GPU.
An 8B-class model trained with local/global layers (5:1, window 1,024) or MQA, an fp8 cache (41 sequences fit with the layout and fp8; 3 as Llama 3.1 8B ships), chunked prefill for the 128K prompts, sinks never evicted, and a retrieval acceptance test at 128K. Section 8 has the arithmetic.
Design: a coding agent that reads 1M tokens of repository per task.
Concurrency is low, one sequence's cache and the prefill dominate: a hybrid with constant-state layers in most of the stack plus a few global attention layers (Qwen3-Next's 1 in 4, Nemotron-H's 4 of 52), or trained sparse attention; context parallelism for prefill across GPUs; fp8 cache for the global layers; no question-aware eviction (the question changes every turn); evaluate retrieval and multi-hop use at 1M, and prefer retrieval into a shorter context when the effective length falls short.

Usable memory per GPU is taken as 92% of capacity, vLLM's default gpu_memory_utilization (read in vLLM v0.31.0 source on Batching, KV cache and scheduling); weights are split evenly across the GPUs; activations and engine workspace live in the other 8%. Recurrent layers (Mamba-2, Gated DeltaNet) add a constant state per sequence, in fp32 as the calculator assumes. Eviction is counted as if every dropped entry were freed: that is the algorithmic saving; a paged engine reclaims only whole blocks, which per-head policies rarely empty (measured on Batching, KV cache and scheduling).

What this tab does not model: activation memory during a long prefill (chunked prefill bounds it), fragmentation and block granularity (PagedAttention keeps waste under a block per sequence), tensor-parallel replication of KV heads when there are fewer KV heads than GPUs, and quality: every lever but the dtype changes the model (the Eviction lab measures what imposing them costs).

Batching, KV cache and scheduling already ran H2O, SnapKV and random eviction on Qwen3-0.6B at about 1,000 tokens and showed which savings a paged engine can actually reclaim. This lab goes where that one did not: four times the context, the policies an architecture builds in (a window, local and global layers), quantisation on the same memory axis, a question that arrives after compression, and which heads must keep a fact for the model to use it.

1. The test

Prompt. TinyStories validation stories (CDLA-Sharing-1.0) concatenated to 4,096 SmolLM2 tokens, half SmolLM2's 8,192 maximum. Three tasks, each scored with the full cache and under every policy:

  • Pass-key (30 prompts: needle depths 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 90% of the prompt, 6 seeds): one planted sentence, "The secret number is 66226. Remember it.", and at the end "Question: What is the secret number? Answer: The secret number is". Correct means all five digits right under greedy decoding.
  • Copy (6 prompts): one story planted at a depth, then repeated after the prompt; the loss on its first 128 tokens is low only if the earlier copy can still be read (the model's induction heads copy it).
  • New story (6 prompts): the continuation is an unseen story, so the old context hardly helps: this is what perplexity-style evaluations measure.

Protocol. Prefill the whole prompt with full attention (as an engine does), then compress the cache, then score only the continuation tokens against what survived: prefill-then-compress, the setting of SnapKV and of the Batching page's run. Each policy keeps the same share of entries in every layer and KV head (SmolLM2 has 30 layers of 3 KV heads: 90 caches); quantised caches keep every entry in fewer bits.

5. What this means, what transfers, how to check

  • Perplexity is the wrong test for a compressed cache. H2O at 12.5% raises the new-story loss from 2.07 to 2.10 and answers 0% of pass-key questions (full cache 97%). Test with retrieval and copying at your real length, as Long-context and multimodal benchmarks explains for models in general.
  • Question-aware selection works only if the question is there. SnapKV keeps 97% at a quarter of the cache when the question is in its observation window, and 0% when the cache was compressed before the question arrived. Multi-turn chats, agents and prefix caches compress before the question: they need a question-agnostic method or no eviction.
  • Fewer bits beat fewer entries at equal memory here. KIVI-style int4 at 31% of the bytes answers 93% and keeps copying (loss 0.66 against 0.61); no eviction policy at 25% keeps copying. At 2 bits, per-channel keys matter: 50% against 7% for per-token rounding, the effect KIVI's authors report (keys have outlier channels, so one scale per token wastes its range).
  • Never drop the first tokens. The recent-only window sends the new-story loss to 4.02 at 50%; keeping 4 sink tokens brings it back to 2.08, the same lesson as the root's Inside a real model sink demo.

What transfers: the ranking of policies on each task, the gap between perplexity and retrieval, the role of the question and of sinks, and the per-channel effect at 2 bits; these are mechanisms. What does not: a 135M model at 4,096 tokens is far from a 70B model at 128K, where caches are larger, more redundant and eviction is usually gentler; absolute percentages will differ. Thirty prompts give a resolution of about 3 percentage points (one prompt). Memory is counted as entries kept; a paged engine reclaims only blocks that every head emptied (Batching, KV cache and scheduling measured that per-head selections free almost none).

2. Why: a relative distance is learned only as often as it is seen

In one training window of length L, the distance d between a query and a key occurs L − d times: distance 1 at 255 query positions, distance 250 at 6. A model trained on 256-token windows has seen distances past 200 in about 5% of its query-key pairs (counting distance 0, a token and itself), and distances past 255 never. The same left-skew, measured over real pretraining data, is the explanation An et al. give for why "the effective context lengths of open-source LLMs often fall short, typically not exceeding half of their training lengths" (An et al. 2024, third-party). 4. How to measure effective context on your model

  • Loss by position on long held-out documents (section 1): cheap, shows the position wall at once, but averages hide retrieval failures (the Eviction lab's new-story loss barely moved while retrieval collapsed).
  • Retrieval at length and depth (section 3): a needle grid is the minimum; it is a string-matching test, so a pass says little about reasoning over the context. For anything past retrieval use the suites on Long-context and multimodal benchmarks (RULER, NoLiMa, MRCR), at your length, with your prompt format.
  • Read the config: max_position_embeddings is an allocation, not a training fact (Qwen3 says 40,960, MiniMax-M1 10,240,000: the root's findings), rope_scaling says whether an extension method is switched on and from what original_max_position_embeddings.

RoPE from zero: position as a rotation

The problem RoPE solves. We want a score that depends on how far apart two tokens are and not on where the pair sits, with no extra parameters, without touching the values, and in a form that still lets a key be computed once and cached. Rotary position embedding ({{RoFormer|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cfa5e9f54459720098}}, Su et al. 2021) gets all four from one fact about rotations: it rotates each query and key instead of adding a position vector, which would change the vector's length and direction.

Step 1, two dimensions. Take a query q = (q0, q1) at position m and a key k at position n. Rotate the query by the angle mθ and the key by nθ, with R(α) the 2 × 2 rotation matrix. Then

(R(mθ) q) · (R(nθ) k) = qT R(mθ)T R(nθ) k = qT R((n − m)θ) k
why
a rotation's transpose is its inverse, and two rotations compose by adding their angles: R(α)T R(β) = R(β − α)
in complex numbers
read the pair as q0 + iq1; rotating is multiplying by eimθ, and Re[(q eimθ)(k einθ)*] = Re[q k* ei(m−n)θ]

The score now depends on n − m only. Each vector is rotated by its own absolute position, so a key can be rotated once, when it is computed, and stored rotated in the KV cache; the relative offset appears only inside the dot product. Rotations keep lengths, so the token's content (the vector's norm) is untouched. Values are not rotated: position shapes where a head looks, not what it reads. So RoPE is absolute in form and relative in effect.

Try: the defaults are the worked example below (θ = 0.5, m = 3, n = 5). Slide the shift: both vectors turn, the angle between them does not, and the dot product stays 0.5403 = cos 1. Then change the offset.

θ = 0.5 radians per token is illustrative; real pairs run from 1 radian per token down to millionths. Carried from the Architecture Gallery's earlier build (2026-10-02), which now links here.

Worked example (one pair, θ = 0.5). Take q = k = (1, 0). At m = 3 and n = 5 the query is rotated by 1.5 radians to (0.0707, 0.9975) and the key by 2.5 radians to (−0.8011, 0.5985); their dot product is 0.0707 × (−0.8011) + 0.9975 × 0.5985 = 0.5403 = cos(1.0). Move both tokens to m = 10, n = 12: the angles become 5 and 6 radians, the gap is still 1 radian, and the dot product is again 0.5403.

Step 2, a whole head. Split each query and key of a head (dimension d) into d/2 pairs and give pair i its own speed:

θi = base−2i/d,   wavelength λi = 2π / θi = 2π · base2i/d tokens,   ⟨Rmq, Rnk⟩ = ⟨q, Rn−mk⟩
Rm
the block-diagonal rotation for position m: pair i turned by mθi
base
10,000 in the paper and in the tiny GPT; 500,000 in Llama 3; 1,000,000 in Qwen3; up to 1011 in the configs read below

The pairs form a geometric spectrum of clocks. In the tiny GPT (head dimension 32, base 10,000) there are 16 pairs: pair 0 turns once every 6.3 tokens, pair 6 once every 199, pair 7 once every 353 and pair 15 once every 35,333. Within its 256-token training window pairs 0 to 6 go round at least once, so the model has seen every angle they can take; pairs 7 to 15 only ever covered a wedge, pair 15 just 2.6 degrees of it. Fast pairs resolve neighbouring tokens; slow pairs measure long distances. That split, 7 fast and 9 slow, is the whole story of what breaks past 256, below. At Llama 3's shape (d = 128, base 500,000) the same spectrum spans pair 0 at 1 radian per token (a full turn every 6.3 tokens) to pair 63 at 2.46 × 10−6 radians per token (a full turn every 2.56 million tokens).

Predict: Llama 3.1 8B has 64 pairs, base 500,000, and was pretrained at 8,192 tokens. How many of its pairs completed at least one full turn within 8,192 tokens?

What it costs. No parameters. Per layer and token, about 3 operations per element of q and k: 6 × 256 = 1,536 in the tiny GPT against about 1.57 million for the layer's matrix products, 0.1% (calculation). Measured on the tiny GPT's training run, unfused on MPS, RoPE trained at  (rough timings on a shared laptop); on datacenter GPUs RoPE is fused into the attention or projection kernels and is not a cost anyone budgets for.


When it matters, and how to check. Every layer that uses RoPE must use the same convention as the weights were trained with: the frequency spectrum (base, rotated dimensions) and the pairing layout. Check by loading the checkpoint and comparing the loss on a few hundred tokens with the model card's or your own reference; the two silent failures below (wrong layout, wrong base) are measured on the tiny GPT in Common mistakes.


Interleaved or split-half layout. The same rotation can pair dimensions (x0, x1), (x2, x3), ... (the paper's and GPT-J's layout; Command A's config says rope_gptj; DeepSeek ships interleaved weights, and transformers keeps a separate apply_rotary_pos_emb_interleave for them, ) or pair i with i + d/2 (Hugging Face's rotate_half, above; gpt-oss's _apply_rotary_emb splits in halves too, ). The two are equivalent only after permuting the rows of the query and key projections, which Hugging Face's Llama conversion script does with its permute function ({{convert_llama_weights_to_hf.py|https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/v4.46.0/src/transformers/models/llama/convert_llama_weights_to_hf.py}}). Porting weights without that permutation runs without an error and gives wrong text; measured below.


The {{RoFormer page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cfa5e9f54459720098}} owns the rest of the paper: the eight-pair dials, the long-term decay figure rebuilt (it holds for the paper's bound and for aligned vectors, not in expectation for random ones), and trained toy models run past their length.







The base, read from the configs


A larger base slows every pair except pair 0, so more pairs stay informative across a long window and fewer of them have a wavelength shorter than the context. Xiong et al. found Llama 2's base of 10,000 decayed attention too strongly beyond 4,000 to 6,000 tokens and proposed "increasing the 'base frequency b' of RoPE from 10,000 to 500,000", called adjusted base frequency, ABF ({{Xiong et al. §4.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16039}}); Llama 3 pretrains with 500,000 ({{Llama 3 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}}), and Qwen3 raises 10,000 to 1,000,000 for its long-context stage ({{Qwen3 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a19006e6b096a6e14b}}). This is a pretraining choice, not the NTK-aware scaling below, although both change the base. The chart reads rope_theta (or its equivalent) from every config this topic holds.














How to read a config's position line in 30 seconds. (1) Is there RoPE at all: rope_theta or rope_parameters, but check the modeling code for hybrids, where the field can be present and unused. (2) How many dimensions rotate: head_dim × partial_rotary_factor, or qk_rope_head_dim for MLA, or rotary_dim. (3) Which layers: layer_types, no_rope_layers, no_rope_layer_interval, per-layer-type rope_parameters. (4) Scaling: rope_scaling or rope_parameters.rope_type with its factor and original_max_position_embeddings, the length the model was actually pretrained at; max_position_embeddings is a ceiling, not the trained length (Qwen3's configs say 40,960 for a 32,768-token native window; MiniMax-M1's says 10,240,000).


transformers 5 renamed the field
From transformers 5, configs are standardised to rope_parameters, a dict holding rope_type, rope_theta, factor and the rest (nested per layer type for Gemma 4); old rope_theta and rope_scaling fields are still read and moved into it (RotaryEmbeddingConfigMixin.standardize_rope_params in transformers  modeling_rope_utils.py). Code that reads config.rope_scaling directly gets the old field or nothing.







Variants: partial, decoupled, multimodal, mixed layers


Five variants matter in practice. Partial RoPE rotates only a fraction of each head's dimensions and leaves the rest position-free, so those dimensions match on content alone. Decoupled RoPE (DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention, MLA) keeps position out of the compressed keys entirely and carries it in a small separate key: RoPE "is position-sensitive for both keys and queries", so a rotated key would block absorbing the key up-projection into the query, and every prefix key would have to be recomputed; the fix uses "additional multi-head queries and a shared key to carry RoPE" of 64 dimensions ({{DeepSeek-V2 §2.1.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434}}; the mechanism is on {{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}} and the {{DeepSeek MLA explainer|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}). Multimodal RoPE gives images and video several position indices: Qwen2-VL splits the rotation into "temporal, height, and width" components, and for text "these components utilize identical position IDs, making M-RoPE functionally equivalent to 1D-RoPE" ({{Qwen2-VL §2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12191}}). Mixed layers interleave RoPE layers with layers that have no positions (NoPE) or a different base. No RoPE anywhere in hybrids, where recurrent layers carry order.


Layer strips: RoPE, local window RoPE, full attention RoPE, full attention, larger base or few pairs no positions, full attention linear attention or SSM (no positions)

Partial RoPE comes in two kinds. The default kind spreads the whole spectrum over the rotated dimensions (exponents 2i/drot), so its slowest pair is as slow as a full head's. Gemma 4's "proportional" kind keeps the full head's exponents (2i/dhead) for the first pairs and sets the rest to zero frequency, so it keeps only the fast clocks.  Barbero et al. found Gemma uses RoPE's highest frequencies for positional attention patterns and "greatly prefers to use the lowest frequencies", which they suspect carry semantic information ({{Round and Round We Go|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06205}}); keeping only fast pairs is one way to act on that.

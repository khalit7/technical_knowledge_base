The idea in one sentence. A deep transformer trains because every block adds to a residual stream instead of replacing it and a norm fixes the size of what each block reads; every other stability tool bounds one more quantity that would otherwise grow: the stream (norm placement, initialisation), the attention logits (QK-norm, soft-capping), the output logits (z-loss), or the step (warmup, clipping).

One block, and the five places a stability fix acts

The stream (thick line) runs from the embedding to the LM head; each sub-layer reads it through a norm and adds its branch back at a +. Highlighted: the controls this page measures, tagged with their section; dashed: norms that only some placements have. Loss spikes (section 8) are what you see when one of them fails.

You need the decoder block (The transformer from zero) and the maths of LayerNorm, BatchNorm and init schemes (Xavier, He, μP), which the ML-fundamentals page Normalisation and initialisation derives; its parent's When training goes wrong tab maps training symptoms to causes for any model. This page is the transformer-specific depth.

New terms. Residual stream: the running vector per token that blocks add to. Branch: what one sub-layer adds. Pre-norm / post-norm / sandwich / output norm: the norm before the branch, on the stream after the add, around the branch, after the branch. QK-norm: a norm on each head's query and key. log Z: the log of the output softmax's normaliser; z-loss penalises it. Attention entropy: how spread a head's weights are (0 nats is one-hot). Massive activation: one stream entry thousands of times the median. Spike score: the share of steps far outside a rolling average.

The running example. The root's tiny GPT (6 blocks, d_model 256, 8 heads of 32, LayerNorm, 5.8M parameters) on TinyStories: batches of 32 × 256 tokens, AdamW, lr 3e-3 with 100 warmup steps and cosine decay, clipping at 1.0, 1,500 steps. Every run changes one thing and keeps the same batches and seed (Apple M1 Pro, MPS, about 5.5 minutes each). The unigram floor, a model that ignores context and predicts token frequencies, is 6.006 nats.

1. The residual stream, and why depth is a stability problem

Measured first. The transformer from zero child trained the tiny GPT with one piece removed at a time, at an equal budget: without the residual connection it learns nothing (held-out 6.011, the unigram floor); without the norms it trains, but worse (2.546 against 2.276; at lr 1e-3, 3.061 against 2.464). The residual path makes depth trainable; the norm lets it train at a useful learning rate.

x2L = x0 + F1(·) + F2(·) + … + F2L(·)     ∂x2L/∂x0 = I + (terms through the branches)
x0
the token embedding (plus a position vector in the tiny GPT); shape (B, T, D) with D = d_model = 256
Fi
sub-layer i's branch, added into the stream: L blocks, two sub-layers each (attention, then the feed-forward network)
I
the identity: with a clean residual path the gradient reaches every block undiminished, whatever the branches do

Two ways depth goes wrong. Forward, the stream's size drifts: every add can make it bigger, or a norm after every add forces it flat. Backward, the gradient to block 1 passes through every block above it; a factor a little under one per step vanishes over depth, a little over one explodes. Where the norm sits relative to the add decides which of these happens (section 2).

How to check it on a real run. Log the stream's RMS after each block on a fixed batch every few hundred steps (forward hooks, or output_hidden_states=True, whose last entry is already after the final norm, as the root's Inside lab found). Steady growth under pre-norm is normal; one channel hundreds of times larger than the rest is a massive activation (section 7).

Post-norm trains only in a narrow window

Xiong et al. 2020 show that at initialisation post-norm's gradients near the output are large, so a large early learning rate is unstable and warmup (ramping the rate up from near zero) is what makes it train; pre-norm's gradients are "well-behaved at initialization" (the theorem and a toy that reproduces it are on the ML-fundamentals Normalisation page).

Widening the stream: DeepSeek-V4's mHC

Hyper-connections keep n = 4 copies of the stream and learn an n × n matrix per sub-layer that mixes them in place of the identity add; over depth the stream carries the product of those matrices, whose gain reached "peaks of 3000" in DeepSeek's unconstrained 27B run. mHC projects each matrix onto the doubly stochastic matrices (rows and columns sum to 1), which are closed under products and can only average, so the gain stays 1; DeepSeek-V4 ships it (hc_mult 4) with ordinary pre-norm RMSNorm inside each branch.

mHC in detail: the equations, the published numbers, a random-matrix calculation and the code

Every placement above keeps one stream of width D per token. Hyper-connections (Zhu et al. 2024) keep n copies (n = 4) and learn, per sub-layer, how to collapse them into the branch input, how to spread the branch output back, and an n × n matrix Hres that mixes the streams in place of the identity add: xl+1 = Hresl xl + Hpost⊤l F(Hprel xl) (mHC paper, Eq. 3). The price is the identity path: after L sub-layers the stream carries the product of L mixing matrices. DeepSeek report that in a 27B run the composite's largest gain reached "peaks of 3000, a stark divergence from 1", with "an unexpected loss surge around the 12k step" (section 3.1, figures 2 and 3; published, not run here).

The fix. Manifold-constrained hyper-connections project each Hres onto the doubly stochastic matrices (non-negative, every row and every column sums to 1) with 20 iterations of Sinkhorn-Knopp (alternately dividing by row and column sums). A product of doubly stochastic matrices is doubly stochastic, so the composite can only average streams, never amplify them. The authors report a 6.7% time overhead at n = 4 and a final loss 0.021 below the baseline at 27B (sections 1 and 5.2); DeepSeek-V4's released code does exactly this (config hc_mult 4, hc_sinkhorn_iters 20), with ordinary pre-norm RMSNorm inside each branch.

The arithmetic, on random matrices (calculated, illustrative; src/analysis/hc_toy.py): 120 sub-layers (60 blocks), 4 streams, each mixing matrix the identity plus Gaussian noise of standard deviation σ, 20 seeds. The largest row sum of the product:

When placement matters, and how to check. At 6 layers pre-norm and a well-warmed post-norm differ by about a tenth of a nat, while the output placements without a logit control lose much more. Placement matters more when the learning rate is pushed, when depth grows (post-norm's window narrows) and in low precision, where pre-norm's growing stream is the one that overflows. Sweep three or four learning rates on a short run; the placement with the widest flat region is the robust one (Wortsman et al.'s "LR sensitivity", section 8).

3. LayerNorm or RMSNorm

A norm runs twice per block on every token, in training and at inference. LayerNorm subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation; RMSNorm (Zhang and Sennrich 2019) drops the mean and divides by the root mean square, on the hypothesis that "re-centering invariance in LayerNorm is dispensable". Every model in section 9 after GPT-2 uses RMSNorm.

A trained model barely uses the mean: |μ| / RMS at every point of the stream

The root's reference tiny GPT (LayerNorm, pre-norm, trained), the running sequence's 256 tokens: mean of each token's stream row over its RMS, after the embedding and each sub-layer. Bars: the median token; ticks: the largest. Measured on the CPU (src/analysis/mistakes.py).

measured |μ| / RMS has a median of at most 0.015 at any of the 13 points and a maximum of 0.069. Replacing every trained LayerNorm by an RMSNorm with the same gains, at inference and without retraining, moves the loss on the running sequence by 0.000012 nats (1.956310 to 1.956322), and the greedy continuation is identical: the RMSNorm paper's hypothesis, seen from inside. Trained from scratch (same batches, schedule, lr 3e-3): LayerNorm 2.279 (seed 0) and 2.260 (seed 1); RMSNorm 2.275 and 2.256. The gap between the two norms, 0.004 on the two-seed means, is smaller than the spread between seeds (0.019 for LayerNorm, 0.018 for RMSNorm): no measurable difference at this scale, as the paper reports. Wall time per run: 323 s against 383 s (rough, shared laptop).

LN(x) = γ ⊙ (x − μ) / √(σ2 + ε) + β      RMSNorm(x) = γ ⊙ x / √(mean(x2) + ε)
μ, σ2
mean and variance over the D = 256 channels of one token (two reductions)
mean(x2)
one reduction; equals σ2 + μ2, so the two norms agree exactly when μ = 0, which the chart shows is nearly true
γ, β
learned gain and bias per channel; the tiny GPT, like most LLMs, has no β (bias=False)
ε
keeps the division finite for an all-zero row: 1e-5 in Llama and Mistral configs, 1e-6 in Qwen, DeepSeek, Gemma, OLMo and Kimi configs (read 2026-10-19)

The cost. The paper reports RMSNorm "reduces the running time by 7%~64% on different models" (published, 2019 GPUs): one fewer reduction and no bias. The saving depends on kernel fusion; on this Mac it does not reproduce (RMSNorm made the whole run 19% slower, below). Real implementations keep the statistics in float32, and Gemma 1 to 3 and Qwen3-Next multiply by (1 + w) where others use w, so a checkpoint loaded into the other convention is wrong by exactly 1 in every gain (the mistakes at the end).

PyTorch's nn.RMSNorm has a different default ε (measured)

torch.nn.RMSNorm(dim) without eps uses the machine epsilon of float32, 1.19e-7, not the 1e-5 or 1e-6 of model configs (torch 2.14.1 docstring: "If not specified, uses the machine epsilon of the computation (opmath) type"). On a row of 256 entries all equal to 0.001 (RMS 0.001) the output entry is 0.9452 with the default, 0.3015 with 1e-5 and 0.7071 with 1e-6. Ordinary rows have an RMS near 1, where the three agree to many digits, so the difference only bites on near-zero rows; but a reimplementation that leaves eps unset does not reproduce the reference model bit for bit. The tiny GPT passes eps=cfg.norm_eps (1e-5) explicitly.

4. Attention-logit growth: QK-norm, soft-capping, qk-clip

The problem. An attention logit is the score before the softmax, s = q · k / √dh = |q| |k| cos θ / √dh (dh = 32 in the tiny GPT). The norm before attention fixes the size of x, but q = Wq Norm(x) and k = Wk Norm(x), and nothing stops Wq and Wk from growing. When a few logits get large the softmax goes one-hot: its entropy (about 4.58 nats for uniform attention over this sequence) collapses, the gradient to every other key vanishes, and the head stops learning (attention entropy collapse). It broke ViT-22B at about 8B parameters, and Wortsman et al. 2023 showed it in small models at high learning rates: in their sweeps "all points with attention logits above 1e4 diverged" (section 3.3).

Who saw it, in their words (published)

ViT-22B's team saw "divergent training loss after a few thousand steps" at about 8B parameters, "caused by extremely large values in attention logits" that gave "(almost one-hot) attention weights with near-zero entropy" (Dehghani et al. 2023, section 2). Zhai et al. 2023 name it attention entropy collapse. Wortsman et al. showed it "also appear[s] in small models when training at high learning rates". Entropy here is −Σ p log p over the keys, averaged over the sequence's positions; 0 is one-hot.

The fix. QK-norm normalises each head's query and key before the dot product, so |q| and |k| are set by the norm's learned gains instead of by the projection weights. In the tiny GPT (RMSNorm per head, before RoPE, as in Qwen3):

No. With z-loss the largest attention logit still reaches 24,895 (without: 20,865) and the run ends at 4.289 against 4.295. Mean log Z at the end: 8.52 with z-loss, 8.67 without. The two instabilities are different softmaxes; the ML-fundamentals root found the same on a character-level model (z-loss alone did not remove the gradient spikes, QK-norm did).

QK-norm does not cap the logit at a small number. With unit gains an RMS-normalised head vector has length √dh, so s ≤ √dh = 5.66 here. But the gains are learned: in this page's QK-norm run at lr 3e-2 the mean |q| after the norm is 3.6 to 11.6 per layer and |k| 3.1 to 11.9 at the end (unit gains would give 5.66 for both), and the largest logit is 67.1, about 12 times the unit-gain ceiling. The ML-fundamentals page measures the same on Qwen3-0.6B (ceilings 184 to 12,897 from its trained gains, real largest logits 12.5 to 48) (Normalisation and initialisation, section 6). What QK-norm removes is growth through the projection weights and the stream; the gain vector is small and moves slowly.

Where real models put it, with the code: Qwen3 per head, OLMo 2 over all heads, Llama 4's L2 norm

Qwen3 normalises per head before RoPE; OLMo 2 normalises the whole query projection (all heads at once) before splitting into heads; Llama 4 Scout uses a weightless L2 norm, only in its RoPE layers, and Maverick turns it off (use_qk_norm false in its config; the code comment says "the 128E model does not use qk_norm").

Soft-capping instead

Gemma 2 squashes the logits themselves: s ← c · tanh(s / c), "50.0 for the self-attention layers and 30.0 for the final layer" (Gemma 2 report, section 2): logits well under c pass unchanged and none can exceed c. Gemma 3 "replace[d] the soft-capping of Gemma 2 with QK-norm" (Gemma 3 report); Gemma 4 keeps only the final-layer cap of 30 (configs read 2026-10-19). Measured (same high-rate run, a cap of 50 on the attention logits): held-out 2.845 against 4.295 uncapped and 2.432 with QK-norm; the largest raw logit before the cap reached 6,296. The cap helps but less than QK-norm: logits keep growing behind it (raw 6,296), but because no capped logit can exceed 50 no head goes fully one-hot (lowest layer entropy 1.371 nats against 0.004 uncapped). That is one reason Gemma 3 switched to QK-norm, which removes the growth instead of hiding it.

The code: Gemma 2's attention soft-cap and Gemma 4's final-layer cap

Without QK-norm: MLA and qk-clip. Multi-head latent attention never materialises per-head keys at decode (they are absorbed into the query, see Attention variants), so a per-head key norm has nowhere to go. DeepSeek-V3 instead "employs additional RMSNorm layers after the compressed latent vectors" (DeepSeek-V3 report, section 4.2). Kimi K2 keeps the forward pass untouched and, after each optimiser step, rescales a head's query and key weights when that head's largest logit passes a threshold (qk-clip, part of MuonClip); the Moonshot AI: Kimi page animates it.

How to check. Log the largest attention logit per layer on a fixed batch every few hundred steps (one extra forward with explicit scores; fused kernels never return them). Steady growth past a few hundred with falling entropy predicts trouble before the loss shows it: Wortsman et al. predicted this way that a 4.8B model "will be unstable at LR 1e-2", and it was.

5. Output logits and z-loss

The problem. The last layer's softmax has the same weakness at the other end of the model. Cross-entropy depends only on differences between the output logits yj, so the whole vector can drift up or down for free; Z = Σj eyj is the softmax's normaliser and log Z measures that drift. Wortsman et al. 2023 (section 3.1.2) describe an instability where the logits "diverge and become very negative", far from the log-probabilities, which in low precision loses the small differences that matter.

loss = cross-entropy + c · (log Z)2,   log Z = log Σj exp(yj),   c = 10−4
log Z
0 when the logits are already log-probabilities; the penalty pulls them there without changing which token wins
c
PaLM's coefficient: "z loss = 10−4 · log2 Z to encourage the softmax normalizer log(Z) to be close to 0, which we found increases the stability of training" (PaLM, section 5); OLMo 2 and Wortsman et al. use the same value

log Z drifts upward in a healthy run; z-loss 1e-4 barely moves it here

Mean log Z of the output softmax on the fixed probe batch, every 10 steps, measured on Apple M1 Pro (MPS).

measured Mean log Z on the probe batch starts at 8.37 (a uniform guess over 4,096 tokens has log Z = ln 4096 = 8.32 when the logits are near 0) and ends at 11.46 in the healthy run at lr 3e-3, at 8.67 in the high-rate run without a fix and at 8.52 with z-loss 1e-4. So at this scale z-loss 1e-4 barely moves log Z (8.52 against 8.67). In the healthy run log Z drifts up to 11.46, where the penalty is 1e-4 × 11.462 = 0.013 nats, under 1% of the loss: the pull is weak by design; the drift it prevents shows up over long runs, at scale and in low precision (published), none of which a 1,500-step float32 run has.

How to check. Log mean log Z on a fixed batch; a value drifting steadily away from 0 is the symptom z-loss treats, and it costs one logsumexp you already pay for in the cross-entropy. Do not switch the z-loss implementation mid-run (OLMo 2's trap, in the mistakes at the end).

6. Residual scaling and initialisation

The problem. The stream after L blocks is a sum of 2L branch outputs. If each branch starts with variance v, the sum starts with variance about 2L · v (independent terms add their variances), so a deeper model starts with a larger, noisier stream (the general schemes, Xavier and He, are on the ML-fundamentals Normalisation and initialisation page).

GPT-2's fix. Scale the weights of the layers that write into the stream (attention's output projection and the FFN's down projection) by 1/√N, N the number of residual layers (Radford et al. 2019, section 2.3). With N = 2L the sum of 2L branches starts with the variance of one. In practice: N(0, 0.02) everywhere and 0.02/√(2L) for those two matrices; for the tiny GPT (L = 6) that is 0.02/√12 = 0.00577. The tiny GPT and transformers' GPT-2 do exactly this:

measured At initialisation the median token's stream grows from 0.449 to 1.42 with the scaled init and to 5.18 without it (the stepper in section 2, "flat" switch). Trained from scratch on the same batches at lr 3e-3: 2.267 without the shrink against 2.279 with it. The difference is smaller than the spread between two seeds of the default init (2.279 and 2.260): with 12 branches the √12 shrink does not measurably matter. It is a depth effect: the papers above report it at hundreds of millions of parameters and dozens of layers, where this page cannot run.

Embeddings are part of the init. The tiny GPT's token and position tables start at N(0, 0.02) each, so a token enters the stream with a norm of 0.449 (0.02 × √(2 × 256) = 0.45, calculated). A tiny embedding makes the first norm's Jacobian large (it divides by the input's size), which is Takase et al.'s argument for scaling it up and the OLMo 2 team's argument for not weight-decaying it (spike score 0.16 to 0.092 without decay on embeddings).

7. Massive activations

What they are. In trained LLMs a handful of stream entries, at fixed channels and mostly on the first token, are thousands of times larger than the rest. Sun et al. 2024 found in LLaMA2-7B a largest activation "about 2,000" (2,622 in their Table 1) "approximately 10,000 times larger than the median magnitude (about 0.2)", appearing "in layer 2" and staying "nearly constant values until layer 30", on the starting token and the first period or newline, in two channels (1415 and 2533). They act as fixed biases: setting them to zero makes perplexity infinite and drops mean zero-shot accuracy from 68.95% to 36.75%, while setting them to their mean value changes almost nothing (their Table 3; published, not run here).

Why they belong on this page. Under pre-norm the stream is never normalised in place, so a huge entry rides the residual path to the last layer and dominates the RMS of every norm that reads that token. Sun et al. link them to the attention sink (Attention variants); how the norm takes part is our reading, not their claim. They set the range low-precision formats must hold (Pre-LN hidden states pass the float16 maximum "as early as 0.5B training tokens", Kim et al. 2025, section 7.2), so quantisation keeps these channels apart (Quantization and Precision).

measured In real models (the root's Inside a real model lab, Apple M1 Pro): in SmolLM2-135M, channel 507 of token 0 reaches 20,155 at layer 12 against a median entry of 1.56, the same layer where its attention sink switches on (weight on token 0 at most 0.085 before layer 12, 0.31 to 0.74 after); masking token 0 raises the loss on the running text from 1.563 to 1.648. In Qwen3-0.6B the largest entry is 6,160 (channel 35 of token 0, layer 26) against a median of 5.54.

measured In the tiny GPT: none. In the root's reference tiny GPT the largest single stream entry anywhere is 35.8 (token 132, f6) against a median |entry| of 2.69 at that point, a ratio of 13; the Inside lab's own measurement (35.8) agrees, and its verdict was no massive activation. Every trained model of this page:

12M tokens and 6 layers are far too little for the phenomenon, which the Inside lab saw only in pretrained models; in the reference model the largest entry after every sub-layer from block 2's attention to block 5's FFN sits on token 0, a hint of the same first-token specialisation (the stepper in section 2, "largest single entry").

What removes them (published), and the gate trained on the tiny GPT

Sun et al.: "in GPT-2 with explicit attention biases, massive activations disappear" (section 4.3). Gated attention (an elementwise sigmoid gate on each head's output, Qwen3-Next) cut the largest activation from 1,053 to 94 and first-token attention from 0.467 to 0.048 in Qiu et al.'s 15B MoE (Table 4); the Attention variants child trained that gate on the tiny GPT, where there is no sink to remove: largest stream entry 35.1 with the gate against 37.5 without, first-token attention at most 0.016 against 0.016.

How to check. Per layer, the largest |entry| of the stream with its token and channel, against the median |entry|, on a few real prompts. A ratio in the thousands at a fixed channel on token 0 is a massive activation: normal in a pretrained model, a design input for quantisation, not a bug.

8. Loss spikes: reading them and fixing them

What a spike is. A loss spike is a sudden jump of the training loss after a healthy stretch; sometimes it recovers in a few hundred steps, sometimes the run never comes back (divergence). PaLM's 540B run "observed spikes in the loss roughly 20 times during training, despite the fact that gradient clipping was enabled"; restarting "roughly 100 steps before the spike" and skipping "roughly 200-500 data batches" stopped each one, and replaying the same batches from an earlier checkpoint did not spike, so "spikes only occur due to the combination of specific data batches with a particular model parameter state" (PaLM, section 5.1; "not observed when training the smaller models"). OLMo 2 found gradient-norm spikes more frequent than loss spikes and earlier, and counts them with a spike score: the share of values at least seven standard deviations from a rolling average of the last 1,000 (the OLMo 2 paper page replays every ablation).

What does not transfer. The tiny GPT shows these mechanisms only at ten times its default learning rate; real runs show them at their own default rates, because instability grows with model size (Wortsman et al.'s central finding). Absolute thresholds (a logit of 1e4, a spike score of 0.1%) do not transfer; the direction of each fix does.

A run log that makes a spike diagnosable (all cheap): loss and global gradient norm every step; per-layer largest attention logit, attention entropy and stream RMS on a fixed batch every few hundred steps; mean log Z; the learning rate actually applied; the batch indices of every step, so the batches around a spike can be replayed; checkpoints frequent enough to roll back about 100 steps.

The pattern. RMSNorm everywhere; pre-norm as the base; since 2024 a second control on the attention logits in most new families (QK-norm in Qwen3, Gemma 3 and 4, OLMo 2 and 3, GLM-4.5, MiniMax-M2, Llama 4 Scout; qk-clip in Kimi K2; a cap in Gemma 2), and a few departures from the plain identity residual (Gemma's and OLMo's normalised branch outputs, MiniMax-M1's DeepNorm, DeepSeek-V4's four mixed streams). Common mistakes

Each one ran here unless marked; the losses are on the 256-token running sequence (the trained reference model scores 1.956) or held-out windows, from src/analysis/mistakes.py and the runs above.

Training post-norm at a pre-norm learning rate
No error and a smooth curve: post-norm at lr 3e-3 with 100 warmup steps reaches the unigram level by step 30 and stays there (held-out 5.943, unigram 6.006), so it looks "converged". It learned token frequencies only. A longer warmup (2.138 with 500 steps) or a lower rate (2.534 at 1e-3) fixes it. Always compare the first loss plateau with the unigram cross-entropy of your data.
Leaving out the final norm in a hand-written pre-norm stack
Pre-norm never normalises the stream, so the last block's output reaches the unembedding at its full grown size. Removing the final LayerNorm from the trained model raises the loss from 1.956 to 3.493, yet the greedy continuation is " play outside in the park. One day, she saw a big, scary dog...": the logits are scaled, the top token mostly is not, so greedy text hides a broken probability distribution that sampling and the loss expose.
Loading a zero-centred gain into a plain norm (or the reverse)
Gemma 1 to 3 and Qwen3-Next multiply by (1 + w), Llama-style norms by w. Simulated on the trained model by subtracting 1 from every trained gain: loss 8.496 (worse than the uniform guess, ln 4096 = 8.318) and greedy output "ctementementementementementementementementemente...". No error is raised because the shapes match.
An ε that is not small
With ε = 1.0 instead of 1e-5 in every LayerNorm, rows whose variance is below 1 are no longer normalised to unit size: loss 7.720, greedy output " the a time in the the the the the the the the the the the S...". The subtler version is PyTorch's nn.RMSNorm without eps (section 3).
Dropping QK-norm at inference (a converter that forgets q_norm and k_norm)
On this page's model trained with QK-norm at lr 3e-3 (loss 2.025 on the running sequence), skipping the trained q_norm and k_norm at inference gives 3.206 and greedy output " play outside. One day, she found a shiny rock. It was a ver...". The checkpoint loads without error if the converter simply never creates the two modules.
Taking initializer_range in a config as the training init
DeepSeek-V3's config says 0.02; its report says every parameter was initialised with σ = 0.006. The field is what Hugging Face uses to initialise a fresh model of that class, not a record of training (not run here: read from the config and report).
Calling OLMo 2's or Gemma's norm "post-norm"
Both keep the stream unnormalised and normalise the branch output before adding it; the 2017 post-norm normalises the stream itself. The difference is the whole point: the measured init streams differ (53.2 for the output norm against 16.0 for post-norm, section 1).
Switching the loss implementation in the middle of a run
OLMo 2's fork that moved from Flash Attention's fused cross-entropy with z-loss to a PyTorch z-loss saw the z-loss jump about thirty-fold within a few hundred steps, though the forward values matched (published; replayed on the OLMo 2 paper page).

Interview rehearsal (optional)

Your 70B run's loss spikes at step 40,000 and recovers. What do you look at, in order?
Whether the gradient norm spiked first (OLMo 2's pattern); the per-layer largest attention logit and entropy in the steps before (logit growth: QK-norm or a lower rate for the rest of the run); mean log Z (z-loss); the batches at the spike, replayed from an earlier checkpoint (PaLM: if replaying does not spike, it is data plus state: roll back about 100 steps and skip 200 to 500 batches); the optimiser's ε and the learning rate actually applied; any non-finite tensor in low precision. Do not change the loss implementation mid-run.
Massive activations: what are they and why do they matter for FP8 training and quantisation?
A few fixed channels, mostly on the first token, thousands of times the median (LLaMA2-7B: about 2,000 against 0.2; SmolLM2: 20,155 against 1.56). They set the dynamic range a format must hold, so per-tensor scaling either clips them (breaking the model: zeroing them makes perplexity infinite) or wastes precision on everything else; outlier-aware quantisation and fine-grained (per-block) scaling exist for this reason (Quantization and Precision owns them).
Design: the stability recipe for a 120-layer MoE trained in bf16 at an aggressive learning rate.
Pre-norm RMSNorm (statistics in float32) with a final norm; QK-norm per head (or, with MLA, norms on the latents plus qk-clip); z-loss 1e-4; GPT-2-style depth-scaled or small branch init and a large shortcut (do not weight-decay embeddings); warmup of a few thousand steps; global-norm clipping at 1.0 as a backstop; AdamW ε 1e-8 or smaller. Monitor per-layer max logit, entropy, stream RMS and the largest entry, log Z and the gradient norm with a spike score; keep checkpoints every few hundred steps and batch indices for rollback and skip. Sweep the peak rate at small scale first and pick from the flat region, since sensitivity grows with size (Wortsman et al.). Consider the output norm (OLMo 2) or sandwich (Gemma) if the stream grows into the bf16 danger zone.

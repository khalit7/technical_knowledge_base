10. Delta six: stack shape

Everything above assumes one causal stack, each layer run once with its own cache. Four 2025 to 2026 designs break that, and each changes the cache arithmetic, which is why the number of layers in a config is not the number of caches.

Layers in the config are not caches per token

Grey: layers the config declares; blue: caches one token adds. Derived from configs and the transformers 5.20.1 modeling code named below.

  • Looped layers. Ouro-Thinking 2.6B runs its 48 layers 4 times per token (total_ut_steps 4) and each pass keeps its own cache: 384 KiB per pass, 1.5 MiB per token, the largest per-token cache in the gallery, from a 2.6B model. Nanbeige 4.2 runs 22 layers twice (num_loops 2). Whether every pass needs its own cache, and why two loops is the usual stopping point, is research in progress (closed block below).
  • Cross-layer KV sharing. Gemma 4 E2B and E4B let their last 20 and 18 layers reuse the cache of an earlier layer of the same type (num_kv_shared_layers; transformers 5.20.1, modeling_gemma4.py, is_kv_shared_layer): this page's section 1 correction.
  • Two attention sublayers per layer. LongCat-Flash layers each hold two MLA sublayers and a shortcut MoE beside two dense MLPs (modeling_longcat_flash.py): 14 layers in the config, 28 caches.
  • Compressed caches. DeepSeek V4 compresses most layers' entries 4 times (CSA) or 128 times (HCA) along the sequence (the mechanism is on {{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}}). From compress_ratios, in the gallery's bf16 convention (512 numbers per entry, indexer keys and the 128-token windows left out): V4-Pro 30 CSA + 31 HCA layers, 7,928 bytes per token, which reproduces the gallery's 7.7 KiB; V4-Flash 21 CSA + 20 HCA layers plus 2 window-only layers, 5,536 bytes, the gallery's 5.4 KiB. DeepSeek V4.1-Flash goes further, an encoder whose final state feeds the decoder's keys and values (gallery; detailed, with its 890 bytes per token at FP4, on {{DeepSeek|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}). The DeepSeek page's own V4-Pro estimate, 4,939.5 bytes per token, uses FP8 main entries and FP4 indexer keys: a different precision convention, not a contradiction.

Multi-token prediction (MTP) layers, extra heads that predict tokens further ahead and double as a draft model ({{Families and objectives|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8105b616c17c1439f766}}), are a fifth: used at inference they keep caches too, which is the whole difference between this page's 180 KiB and the gallery's 192 KiB for Step 3.5 Flash.

Looped models: does every pass need its own cache? (research, October 2026)

The cache per pass is a training choice, not a law: as the recurrent states approach fixed points, later passes can share the last pass's keys and values (terminal KV sharing) "with almost no loss in accuracy", and at 1.6B a learned depth prior with a 3x smaller KV cache matched fixed-depth training with the full cache; fixed-depth training is what breaks sharing ({{Looped Models Done Right, Part II (arXiv 2610.06833)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06833}}).

Why two loops is the usual stopping point: hidden-state variance grows with each loop, and in a looped MoE the routers pick the same experts every pass, so extra loops add FLOPs without new computation; scaled residuals, input re-injection and per-loop routers kept 100M to 1.7B models stable to 9 to 12 loops, with a near-iso-FLOP 700M model best at 5 ({{LOOM (arXiv 2610.01153)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01153}}).

The first scaling law fitted jointly over recurrence, sparsity, size and data puts recurrence at about 2x total-parameter efficiency on reasoning (sparsity about 3x active-parameter efficiency), and at trillion-token scale and matched training compute a looped MoE with law-derived recurrence matched a non-looped MoE about twice its size on reasoning benchmarks ({{Scaling Laws for Looped MoE (arXiv 2609.40316)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40316}}).

12. Depth

Rehearse it: four design questions (optional)
A config has 60 layers, 50 of them sliding (window 1,024, 16 KV heads of 256) and 10 global with one shared K = V tensor of 4 heads × 512. KV per token in bf16, and at 131,072 tokens?
Sliding: 2 × 16 × 256 × 2 = 16 KiB per layer; global: 4 × 512 × 2 = 4 KiB. Per token on the card: 50 × 16 + 10 × 4 = 840 KiB. At 131,072 tokens the sliding layers hold 1,024 tokens each (800 MiB) and the global ones all of them (5 GiB): about 5.8 GiB, 46 KiB per token. That is Gemma 4 31B.
"2.8T total, 104B active". What does each number tell you about serving it?
2.8T is the weight memory (5.1 TiB in bf16, so a multi-node deployment or aggressive quantisation); 104B is the compute per token, so latency and price are those of a 100B dense model. Neither says anything about the cache: that is the attention design (27 KiB per token for Kimi K3).
A hybrid keeps 1 attention layer in 4. At what context does its fixed state stop mattering?
Where the state equals the growing cache: state bytes divided by cache bytes per token. Qwen3-Next: 72 MiB of FP32 state against 24 KiB per token, about 3,000 tokens; beyond a few tens of thousands of tokens the state is a rounding error.
Design: serve 16 concurrent 1M-token conversations on one node of 8 × 80 GB GPUs, weights and cache in bf16. Which gallery design would you start from, and why?
First filter on published context: Gemma 4 31B (256K), Qwen3.5 and Qwen3-Next (262K) are out. Then budget: 640 GB is 596 GiB. Nemotron 3 Super 120B-A12B: weights about 224 GiB; its 8 attention layers hold 8 GiB per 1M-token request plus 160 MiB of Mamba-2 state, about 130 GiB for 16 requests: about 355 GiB, it fits with room for activations. Kimi K3 has a small cache (27 GiB per request) but 2.8T of weights (5.1 TiB). A GQA model of the same size would hold hundreds of GiB per request. So: a Mamba-2 or linear-attention hybrid with few attention layers, MoE for quality at 12B active, then FP8 for both weights and cache if headroom is needed. Illustrative arithmetic: activations, framework overheads and expert placement are left out.
Why do so many MoE models keep their first layers dense? (one claim, unsourced here)

The usual argument: early representations are barely differentiated by token type, so routing on them is close to random and unstable, and dense layers there cost little. 31 of the 52 MoE models of 2026 in the gallery do it, most with one to three dense layers. The mechanism and its evidence belong on {{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}}.

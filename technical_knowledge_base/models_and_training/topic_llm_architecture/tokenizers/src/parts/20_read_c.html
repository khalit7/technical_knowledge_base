Words, characters, bytes, subwords

The unit trades the size of the vocabulary against the length of the sequence. Word-level (historical): the vocabulary explodes for morphologically rich languages (every inflection is a new word), an out-of-vocabulary (OOV) word cannot be represented at all, and it needs word boundaries, which Chinese, Japanese and Thai do not write. Character-level: a tiny vocabulary and no OOV, but long sequences, so every text costs more compute, and each token carries little meaning. Byte-level: 256 ids, one per UTF-8 byte, so no text is ever unknown and no Unicode normalisation is needed, but a non-Latin character costs 2 to 4 bytes (ByT5 runs a model directly on them, see Beyond subwords). Subword-level, the compromise every modern LLM uses: frequent words stay whole and rare words decompose into meaningful pieces, which handles OOV while keeping the vocabulary manageable (32K to 262K entries in current models).

A trick from the huge-vocabulary era: negative sampling (history)
word2vec avoided the cost of a softmax over the whole vocabulary by turning prediction into a binary classifier: tell the real (word, context) pair apart from a few sampled noise words, "k in the range 5 to 20 ... for small training datasets, while for large datasets the k can be as small as 2 to 5" ({{Mikolov et al. 2013 section 2.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.4546}}). It existed purely to avoid the full softmax. LLMs simply pay for the full softmax over 32K to 262K entries, but the idea lives on in contrastive objectives: InfoNCE, whose minimisation "maximizes a lower bound on mutual information" ({{van den Oord et al. 2018 section 2.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748}}), and the training of embedding models such as {{CLIP|n:3c65c17b0d0d8194a85dfc5f3bf3f799}}.

BPE from zero, on the running example

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) is a greedy answer borrowed from compression: start with the 256 byte values as the vocabulary, count every pair of adjacent symbols across the corpus, add the most frequent pair as a new symbol, rewrite the corpus with it, and repeat until the vocabulary has V entries. Every merge makes the corpus shorter by exactly the count of the pair it merged, so BPE is "merge whatever saves the most right now". The running example's tokenizer was trained exactly this way (min_frequency=2, the 256-byte alphabet, <|endoftext|> reserved as id 0), and its first merges are the commonest pairs of simple English:

How it runs in practice. The corpus is first pre-tokenized into words (no merge crosses a word boundary) and counted over distinct words weighted by frequency, so 477 million characters of TinyStories become a small table of word counts. The Hugging Face trainer keeps the candidate pairs in a priority queue ordered by count, breaks ties toward the pair with the smaller ids, refreshes counts that went stale, and stops at the target size or when no pair occurs at least min_frequency times. So the ids are laid out as 0 for the special token, 1 to 256 for the bytes and 257 to 4,095 for the 3,839 merges in the order they were learned.

Characters per token rise fast and then flatten: 256 bytes give 1.0, 4,096 entries 3.95, 16,384 entries 4.14, and the curve stops at 4.15, about the length of an average TinyStories word with its space, because a pre-tokenized word can never become less than one token. At 65,536 the trainer stops early, at 43,702 entries, when no pair occurs twice any more: TinyStories simply does not have 65,536 things worth a token. And the merge lists are nested: every smaller vocabulary's merges are exactly the first merges of the larger one (checked for all nine sizes), so "a 1,024 vocabulary" is just "stop after 767 merges", which is what the replay above uses.

Why the best size grows with the model. For a fixed model, the vocabulary moves compute two ways: fewer tokens per text (fewer passes through the blocks) against a bigger output layer per token (2 × V × d FLOPs). For the tiny GPT the total is lowest around 2,048 to 4,096 entries, the setup's choice; at 65,536 entries the output layer alone would be 3.5 times the blocks' FLOPs. Larger models have more FLOPs per token in their blocks, so the output layer is relatively cheaper and the best vocabulary grows: {{Tao et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.13623}} fit a scaling law and "predict that the optimal vocabulary size of Llama2-70B should have been at least 216K", seven times its 32K, published.

Embedding share = V × 256 / (V × 256 + 4,721,920 non-embedding parameters of the tiny GPT). FLOPs per validation pass = tokens × 2 × (4,721,920 + 256V): two FLOPs per weight per token, the tied output layer counted once as a V × 256 matmul.

Tao et al. also report ARC-Challenge rising "from 29.1 to 32.0 with the same 2.3e21 FLOPs" when going from a 32K to a 43K vocabulary published.

How to check a tokenizer you are given. Load its tokenizer.json, read model.merges (the first ones tell you what corpus it was trained on), print the pieces of a few strings from your own data, and measure characters per token on held-out text from your target distribution.

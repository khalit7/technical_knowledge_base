WordPiece, Unigram and SentencePiece

BPE is one of three ways to learn a vocabulary from a pre-tokenized corpus (counting over distinct words with their frequencies). They differ in what they merge or remove, and in how they encode a new word:

WordPiece (BERT) scores a pair by a likelihood ratio (pointwise mutual information up to a constant) rather than raw frequency, so a pair of common parts scores low even if it is frequent. Unigram runs the other way: fit a unigram language model (a word's probability is the product of its tokens'), then prune the tokens whose removal least hurts corpus likelihood, 10% or 20% at a time, never single characters; because it is probabilistic it can also sample other segmentations, which Kudo used as training-time augmentation ("subword regularization"). SentencePiece needs no pre-tokenization: it writes whitespace as the meta symbol ▁ (U+2581), so "Hello▁world." becomes [Hello] [▁wor] [ld] [.] and decodes losslessly by replacing ▁ with a space ({{SentencePiece section 3.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.06226}}). Byte-level BPE, as GPT-2 implemented it, maps each byte to a printable character, which is why a space shows up as Ġ in vocabulary files and a Hindi character as two or three odd Latin symbols.

How the three trainers were checked, and where WordPiece's score comes from (provenance)

On the Hugging Face course's four-sentence corpus, the page's WordPiece learns exactly the course's 25 new tokens (ab, ##fu, Fa, Fac, ... ##ut), its Unigram starts at the course's loss of 413.10 and tokenizes "This is the Hugging Face course." exactly as the course prints, and its BPE gives the same merges as the Hugging Face tokenizers library's own BpeTrainer. All three were checked on four corpora (36 of 36 comparisons identical, including accents, emoji, Cyrillic and Japanese; check_algos.mjs). The course's printed BPE list differs from the library's at ties, as the course itself notes: when two pairs are equally frequent the course takes the first one it meets, the library the one whose token ids are smallest. The library has no WordPiece trainer of its own (its WordPieceTrainer runs BPE with ## prefixes), and its Unigram trainer is SentencePiece's EM algorithm, so for those two the course's code is the reference; the page's Unigram is the course's simplified version (probabilities from substring counts, no EM re-estimation, exact removal losses).

WordPiece's original criterion was the language-model likelihood gain of each candidate unit ({{Schuster and Nakajima 2012|https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/37842.pdf}}); the frequency ratio above is the Hugging Face course's reconstruction, because "Google never open-sourced its implementation of the training algorithm" ({{course|https://huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/chapter6/6}}). So a WordPiece vocabulary trained with the library and BERT's original are built by different rules. BPE itself came to translation from compression with Sennrich, Haddow and Birch, to make the model "capable of open-vocabulary translation by encoding rare and unknown words as sequences of subword units".

Encoding is a different algorithm

Training builds the vocabulary once; encoding runs on every request, and each method encodes differently. BPE replays its merges in the order learned (the library does it as "apply the lowest-ranked merge present, repeat", which gives the same result). WordPiece stores no merges, only the vocabulary, and takes the longest vocabulary entry that matches from the left; if any part of a word cannot be matched, the whole word becomes [UNK]. Unigram finds the segmentation with the highest product of token probabilities by dynamic programming (Viterbi). So the same vocabulary entry can be reached differently: the course's example vocabulary tokenizes "hugs" as hug ##s by longest match, while BPE replaying the same merges gives hu ##gs ({{course|https://huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/chapter6/6}}).

Before any of this, production tokenizers run a pre-tokenizer, usually a regular expression, and its rules matter as much as the vocabulary. GPT-2's pattern, which the running example's ByteLevel pre-tokenizer uses by default:

Read it left to right: English contractions, then an optional space followed by a run of letters (so " Roxy" is one chunk with its space), an optional space and a run of digits (any length), an optional space and a run of punctuation, then whitespace. Every later tokenizer edits this pattern: GPT-4's cl100k caps digit runs at three, Qwen and Nemotron 3 cut single digits, Kimi gives Chinese characters their own runs. What those edits do to numbers and code is under Numbers and code.

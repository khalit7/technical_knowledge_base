In one screen

A tokenizer maps text to a list of integer ids, and the model only ever sees those ids. Every current LLM uses subword units: frequent words stay whole, rare words break into pieces, and nothing is ever out of vocabulary because the pieces bottom out in single characters or bytes. The vocabulary is learned from a corpus before pretraining and frozen with the model. Three algorithms learn it: BPE merges the most frequent pair, WordPiece merges the pair whose parts are most surprising together, Unigram starts big and prunes. Production LLMs since GPT-2 almost all run byte-level BPE; what differs between them is the vocabulary size (32K to 262K), the pre-tokenization rules (how digits, spaces and code are split before merging) and the data the vocabulary was trained on, which decides who pays more tokens for the same text.

Words, characters, bytes, subwords

The unit is a trade between the size of the vocabulary and the length of the sequence:

  • Word-level: one id per word. The vocabulary explodes for morphologically rich languages (every inflection is a new word), and any word not in it, an out-of-vocabulary (OOV) word, cannot be represented at all. It also needs word boundaries, which Chinese, Japanese and Thai do not write. Historical.
  • Character-level: a tiny vocabulary, no OOV, works without word boundaries. But sequences get long, so every text costs more compute, and each token carries little meaning on its own.
  • Byte-level: 256 ids, one per UTF-8 byte, so no text is ever unknown and no Unicode normalisation is needed. A non-Latin character costs 2 to 4 bytes, so sequences are longer still. ByT5 runs a model directly on them (see Beyond subwords).
  • Subword-level: the compromise every modern LLM uses. Frequent words stay whole and rare words decompose into meaningful pieces, which handles OOV while keeping the vocabulary manageable (32K to 262K entries in current models).

A historical trick from the huge-vocabulary era: negative sampling
word2vec avoided the cost of a softmax over the whole vocabulary by turning prediction into a binary classifier: tell the real (word, context) pair apart from a few sampled noise words, "k in the range 5 to 20 ... for small training datasets, while for large datasets the k can be as small as 2 to 5" (Mikolov et al. 2013 §2.2). It existed purely to avoid the full softmax. LLMs simply pay for the full softmax over 32K to 262K entries, but the idea lives on in contrastive objectives: InfoNCE, whose minimisation "maximizes a lower bound on mutual information" (van den Oord et al. 2018 §2.3), and the training of embedding models such as CLIP.

The algorithms

All three learn a vocabulary from a training corpus that has first been pre-tokenized: split into words by a fixed rule (spaces, punctuation, digits), so that no token can cross a word boundary. Counting happens over distinct words with their frequencies, not over the raw text.

BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding)

Start from characters (or bytes) and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token until the target vocabulary size. It guarantees no OOV, since the worst case falls back to characters or bytes; the cost is that unfamiliar words shatter into many small fragments. Encoding applies the learned merges in order. Originally a compression algorithm, brought to translation by Sennrich, Haddow and Birch to make the model "capable of open-vocabulary translation by encoding rare and unknown words as sequences of subword units".

Byte-level BPE

BPE over the 256 raw byte values (GPT-2 onward, Llama 3, every tiktoken vocabulary). Any Unicode input is representable with zero OOV and no normalisation headaches. GPT-2's implementation maps each byte to a printable character, which is why a space shows up as Ġ in its vocabulary files and why a Hindi character shows up as two or three odd Latin symbols.

WordPiece

BERT's method. Same skeleton as BPE, different merge criterion: merge the pair maximising freq(ab) / (freq(a) × freq(b)), a likelihood ratio (pointwise mutual information up to a constant) rather than raw frequency. A pair of common parts scores low even if it is frequent. Encoding is greedy longest-match-first with ## continuation prefixes. The original criterion was the language-model likelihood gain of each candidate unit (Schuster and Nakajima 2012); the score above is the Hugging Face course's reconstruction, because "Google never open-sourced its implementation of the training algorithm" (course).

Unigram LM

Kudo's method runs the other way: start with a large candidate vocabulary, fit a unigram language model (each token has a probability, a word's probability is the product of its tokens'), and repeatedly prune the tokens whose removal least hurts corpus likelihood, 10% or 20% at a time, never single characters, until the target size. A string is tokenized by its highest-probability segmentation (Viterbi). Because it is a probabilistic model it can also sample other segmentations, which Kudo used as training-time augmentation ("subword regularization", Kudo 2018).

SentencePiece

A toolkit, not an algorithm. Language-agnostic: it treats raw text as a stream of characters including whitespace, which it writes as the meta symbol ▁ (U+2581), so it needs no pre-tokenization and round-trips losslessly: "Hello▁world." becomes [Hello] [▁wor] [ld] [.] and decodes by replacing ▁ with a space (SentencePiece §3.1). It trains either BPE or Unigram underneath. Used by T5, LLaMA 1 and 2, Gemma and many multilingual models.

Encoding is a different algorithm

Training builds the vocabulary once; encoding runs on every request, and each method encodes differently. BPE replays its merges in the order learned (the library does it as "apply the lowest-ranked merge present, repeat", which gives the same result). WordPiece stores no merges, only the vocabulary, and takes the longest vocabulary entry that matches from the left; if any part of a word cannot be matched, the whole word becomes [UNK]. Unigram finds the segmentation with the highest product of token probabilities by dynamic programming (Viterbi). So the same vocabulary entry can be reached differently: the course's example vocabulary tokenizes "hugs" as hug ##s by longest match, while BPE replaying the same merges gives hu ##gs (course).

The trend: vocabularies grew from 32K (LLaMA, 2023) to 128K to 262K, mostly for multilingual and code compression. A larger vocabulary means fewer tokens per text, which is cheaper inference and more text in the same context window, at the cost of a bigger embedding and unembedding matrix, which is a real fraction of a small model's parameters. Gemma 3 270M is the extreme: "170 million embedding parameters due to a large vocabulary size and 100 million for our transformer blocks" (Google). That reproduces from its config, independently: 262,144 × 640 = 167.8M embedding parameters (tied, so counted once) out of 268.1M, leaving 100.3M. Llama 3.2 1B spends 21% of its weights there; DeepSeek-V3 0.3%. The Vocabulary size tab has all 33 models on a timeline, the embedding share of each, and a calculator.

The growth went almost entirely to other languages and code. On the same English text (FLORES-200, 259,170 characters) every tokenizer here except Llama 2's, from GPT-2's 50K to Gemma 3's 262K, lands between 4.82 and 4.96 characters per token; across all 204 FLORES languages the total falls from 47.4M tokens (GPT-2) to 22.6M (GPT-4o) and 20.6M (Gemma 3). Llama 3 reports its English compression rising "from 3.17 to 3.94 characters per token" over Llama 2's 32K SentencePiece vocabulary, a 24% gain on Meta's own sample; on FLORES English the same two tokenizers give 4.28 and 4.91, a 15% gain (different text, so the absolute numbers differ).

The language tax

Fertility is tokens per word, and the related premium is how many tokens a language needs for the same content relative to English: "The ratio |t(sA)| / |t(sB)| is the premium for A relative to B", measured on translations of the same sentences (Petrov et al. 2023 §3). It varies wildly: GPT-4's tokenizer "uses about 1.6 times more tokens to encode the same text in Italian as it does in English, 2.6 times for Bulgarian and 3 times for Arabic. For Shan ... that difference can be as high as 15 times." A premium is both a cost (APIs bill per token, latency grows with length, less content fits in the context window) and a quality issue (the model sees each word as more, less meaningful pieces). Byte-level is not neutral either: "byte-level representation of the same text is over 4 times longer for Burmese or Tibetan than Chinese."

Premium against English for six languages, four generations of tokenizer, over the whole FLORES-200 dev and devtest (2,009 sentences per language). Try Tamil: GPT-2 15.6 times, GPT-4o 2.0.

Defaults reproduce Petrov et al.'s published token counts exactly, independently: 204 of 204 languages for GPT-2's r50k, GPT-4's cl100k, Llama 2, ByT5's bytes and UTF-32 characters, from the FLORES-200 release with the paper's own construction (their code and table). The other columns extend it to newer tokenizers.

Across all 204 languages, the median premium is 2.26 with GPT-4's cl100k and 1.75 with GPT-4o's o200k; 65 languages pay more than 3 times English with cl100k, 15 with o200k and 8 with Gemma 3. So "low-resource languages pay 3 to 5 times more" is true for some tokenizers and some languages, and the size of the tax depends on the tokenizer far more than on the language: Tamil drops from 7.65 with Llama 3 to 1.46 with Gemma 3. OpenAI's GPT-4o launch listed the gain for 20 languages (Gujarati "4.4x fewer tokens (from 145 to 33)"); the page recomputes all 20 sentences in the Same text, ten tokenizers tab, where 18 reproduce exactly. When choosing a tokenizer for domain adaptation, measure the fertility on your own text: a tokenizer trained on a different distribution can make the same budget of tokens carry much less of your data.

Failure modes

Tokenization causes some of the classic LLM blind spots. The model never sees characters, only ids, so anything that depends on the characters inside a token has to be learned indirectly. Each case below is run through the real tokenizers.

Special tokens and chat templates

Special tokens (beginning and end of sequence, chat roles, tool-call markers, padding) are reserved ids added on top of the learned vocabulary, after it is trained, and they never come out of the merge procedure: the tokenizer matches them as whole strings before ordinary encoding. If a template marker is not registered as special, it is just text and costs ordinary tokens: <|user|> is five tokens in eight of the ten tokenizers here and four in Gemma 3 and Llama 4. Tulu 3's template is in exactly that situation, as the Alignment page shows token by token while explaining which tokens carry the SFT loss. Chat-template mismatches between training and inference (a different role marker, a missing end-of-turn token, a template applied twice) are a common silent bug in fine-tuning: the model trains fine and then behaves oddly at serving time. A shared tokenizer is also what makes logit-level distillation possible: a student can only match a teacher's distribution token by token if both have the same vocabulary (Distillation and Small Language Models).

Beyond subwords

Tokenizer-free and byte-level research keeps resurfacing but has not displaced subword BPE in production.

  • ByT5 (2021) feeds raw UTF-8 bytes to a T5: "a vocabulary of 256 possible byte values. An additional 3 IDs are reserved for special tokens". Sequences are about 4 times longer than SentencePiece's ("4.1 bytes per SentencePiece token" on mC4); byte models "are significantly more robust to noise and perform better on tasks that are sensitive to spelling", at "+33% pre-training time" and inference up to 10 times slower (ByT5).
  • MegaByte (2023) "segments sequences into patches and uses a local submodel within patches and a global model between patches", with fixed patches of 8 bytes for text (MegaByte).
  • Byte Latent Transformer, BLT (Meta, December 2024) makes the patches dynamic: a small byte-level model predicts the next byte, and a new patch starts where its entropy is high, so predictable stretches become long patches and the large model takes fewer steps. It "matches training flop-controlled performance of Llama 3 while using up to 50% fewer flops at inference"; "The bpe tokenizers of Llama 2 and 3 have an average token size of 3.7 and 4.4 bytes. In contrast, BLT can achieve similar scaling trends with an average patch size of 6 and even 8 bytes" (BLT §5.1).
  • SuperBPE (2025) goes the other way and lets tokens cross spaces in a second training stage ("superword" tokens such as "by the way"): with a 200K vocabulary it "encodes a fixed piece of text with up to 33% fewer tokens on average than BPE", and an 8B model trained with it scores "+4.0% absolute improvement over the BPE baseline across 30 downstream tasks" with "27% less compute at inference time" (SuperBPE). The old page said frontier labs are experimenting with SuperBPE-style tokens; that is unconfirmed: no released frontier tokenizer measured here has superword tokens.

Training your own

Use the Hugging Face tokenizers library or SentencePiece on a corpus that matches your target distribution: the vocabulary is frozen with the model, so the languages, scripts, code and number formats you care about must be in the tokenizer's training data. Decide the pre-tokenization rules (digits, whitespace runs, code) before training, reserve special tokens for your chat template, and measure fertility on held-out text in every language you will serve. Where the training corpus comes from and how it is filtered is the subject of Topic: data-curation-and-datasets. Changing the tokenizer of a trained model means new embedding rows that start untrained, so extending a vocabulary (Llama 3 added 28K entries to tiktoken's 100K before training, not after) is far easier than swapping one.

Common mistakes

Comparing token counts or prices across models as if a token were a fixed amount of text
The same English text costs about the same with every current tokenizer (4.8 to 5.0 characters per token on FLORES English), but other languages differ by up to 5 times between tokenizers. Compare per character or per word of your own text.
Reading config vocab_size as the number of tokens
Configs pad the embedding matrix: Qwen3's says 151,936 for 151,669 real entries, DeepSeek-V3's 129,280 for 128,815. Count the tokenizer's entries, not the config.
Assuming every modern tokenizer splits digits the same way
GPT-4, GPT-4o, Llama 3 and 4 and DeepSeek group digits in threes from the left; LLaMA 2, Gemma and Qwen split every digit. Number formatting in prompts changes what the model sees.
Ending a prompt with a space
The space usually belongs to the next token (" Paris" is one token), so a prompt ending in a bare space forces an unusual boundary. See Failure modes.
Calling WordPiece "BPE with a different score" in the Hugging Face library
The library's WordPieceTrainer runs plain frequency BPE with ## prefixes; the likelihood score is the course's reconstruction of Google's unpublished trainer. A WordPiece vocabulary trained with the library and BERT's original are built by different rules.
Distilling logits between models with different tokenizers
Token-level distributions only line up when the vocabularies match; otherwise distil on sampled text, or use a method built for mismatched vocabularies.

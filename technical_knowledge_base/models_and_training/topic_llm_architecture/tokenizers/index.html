The idea in one screen

A tokenizer is a lossless compressor with a frozen dictionary: learned from a corpus before pretraining, it cuts text into pieces from a fixed vocabulary of V entries and hands the model their integer ids, so the vocabulary decides how many tokens every text costs, how large the model's input and output tables are, and what the model can see inside a word.

One sentence of the running story becomes 11 ids; the vocabulary sets three costs

The running example's real tokenizer (4,096 entries, byte-level BPE trained on TinyStories) on a sentence of its running story. Ids are laid out in the order the vocabulary was built: 0 the special token, 1 to 256 the bytes, 257 to 4,095 the merges in the order learned. "Roxy" never earned a merge, so it is four byte tokens (the full stop is a single byte too); every other word is one token with its leading space. Each id selects one row of the embedding table, and the tied output layer scores all 4,096 entries for every token.

You need: what an embedding and an output layer (unembedding) are; both are built from zero in The transformer from zero. This page owns the vocabulary that feeds them. The running example is the one of Topic: llm-architecture: the tiny GPT (6 layers, width 256, 5.84M parameters) reads TinyStories through a byte-level BPE of 4,096 tokens trained on TinyStories itself, and the running sequence is the 256-token opening of a held-out story about Roxy the rhinoceros (root src/data/README.md).

What a tokenizer does

A modern tokenizer is four stages, each a separate object in the Hugging Face tokenizers library: a normalizer (optional cleanup such as Unicode NFC; the running example has none), a pre-tokenizer (cuts text into word-like chunks, and for byte-level BPE turns each chunk into bytes), the model (BPE: applies the learned merges inside each chunk) and a post-processor (adds special tokens such as a beginning-of-sequence marker). The decoder runs it backwards and gives back exactly the same text.

Three things to read off it. A leading space is part of the next token (Ġloved is " loved"; byte-level BPE writes the space byte as Ġ so that every byte has a printable character). "Roxy" costs four tokens while " climb" costs one, because merges are learned from frequency and the name is rare in TinyStories. And id 0 is <|endoftext|>, a special token that the setup reserved before training to separate stories.

Where the ids go (the hero above): id i selects row i of the embedding matrix E (V × d = 4,096 × 256 in the tiny GPT), and the output layer multiplies the final hidden state by a V × d matrix to give one score (logit) per entry. The tiny GPT ties the two: the output matrix is the embedding matrix (Press and Wolf 2016).

Words, characters, bytes, subwords

The unit trades the size of the vocabulary against the length of the sequence. Word-level (historical): the vocabulary explodes for morphologically rich languages (every inflection is a new word), an out-of-vocabulary (OOV) word cannot be represented at all, and it needs word boundaries, which Chinese, Japanese and Thai do not write. Character-level: a tiny vocabulary and no OOV, but long sequences, so every text costs more compute, and each token carries little meaning. Byte-level: 256 ids, one per UTF-8 byte, so no text is ever unknown and no Unicode normalisation is needed, but a non-Latin character costs 2 to 4 bytes (ByT5 runs a model directly on them, see Beyond subwords). Subword-level, the compromise every modern LLM uses: frequent words stay whole and rare words decompose into meaningful pieces, which handles OOV while keeping the vocabulary manageable (32K to 262K entries in current models).

A trick from the huge-vocabulary era: negative sampling (history)
word2vec avoided the cost of a softmax over the whole vocabulary by turning prediction into a binary classifier: tell the real (word, context) pair apart from a few sampled noise words, "k in the range 5 to 20 ... for small training datasets, while for large datasets the k can be as small as 2 to 5" (Mikolov et al. 2013 section 2.2). It existed purely to avoid the full softmax. LLMs simply pay for the full softmax over 32K to 262K entries, but the idea lives on in contrastive objectives: InfoNCE, whose minimisation "maximizes a lower bound on mutual information" (van den Oord et al. 2018 section 2.3), and the training of embedding models such as CLIP.

BPE from zero, on the running example

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) is a greedy answer borrowed from compression: start with the 256 byte values as the vocabulary, count every pair of adjacent symbols across the corpus, add the most frequent pair as a new symbol, rewrite the corpus with it, and repeat until the vocabulary has V entries. Every merge makes the corpus shorter by exactly the count of the pair it merged, so BPE is "merge whatever saves the most right now". The running example's tokenizer was trained exactly this way (min_frequency=2, the 256-byte alphabet, <|endoftext|> reserved as id 0), and its first merges are the commonest pairs of simple English:

How it runs in practice. The corpus is first pre-tokenized into words (no merge crosses a word boundary) and counted over distinct words weighted by frequency, so 477 million characters of TinyStories become a small table of word counts. The Hugging Face trainer keeps the candidate pairs in a priority queue ordered by count, breaks ties toward the pair with the smaller ids, refreshes counts that went stale, and stops at the target size or when no pair occurs at least min_frequency times. So the ids are laid out as 0 for the special token, 1 to 256 for the bytes and 257 to 4,095 for the 3,839 merges in the order they were learned.

Characters per token rise fast and then flatten: 256 bytes give 1.0, 4,096 entries 3.95, 16,384 entries 4.14, and the curve stops at 4.15, about the length of an average TinyStories word with its space, because a pre-tokenized word can never become less than one token. At 65,536 the trainer stops early, at 43,702 entries, when no pair occurs twice any more: TinyStories simply does not have 65,536 things worth a token. And the merge lists are nested: every smaller vocabulary's merges are exactly the first merges of the larger one (checked for all nine sizes), so "a 1,024 vocabulary" is just "stop after 767 merges", which is what the replay above uses.

Why the best size grows with the model. For a fixed model, the vocabulary moves compute two ways: fewer tokens per text (fewer passes through the blocks) against a bigger output layer per token (2 × V × d FLOPs). For the tiny GPT the total is lowest around 2,048 to 4,096 entries, the setup's choice; at 65,536 entries the output layer alone would be 3.5 times the blocks' FLOPs. Larger models have more FLOPs per token in their blocks, so the output layer is relatively cheaper and the best vocabulary grows: Tao et al. 2024 fit a scaling law and "predict that the optimal vocabulary size of Llama2-70B should have been at least 216K", seven times its 32K, published.

How to check a tokenizer you are given. Load its tokenizer.json, read model.merges (the first ones tell you what corpus it was trained on), print the pieces of a few strings from your own data, and measure characters per token on held-out text from your target distribution.

WordPiece, Unigram and SentencePiece

BPE is one of three ways to learn a vocabulary from a pre-tokenized corpus (counting over distinct words with their frequencies). They differ in what they merge or remove, and in how they encode a new word:

WordPiece (BERT) scores a pair by a likelihood ratio (pointwise mutual information up to a constant) rather than raw frequency, so a pair of common parts scores low even if it is frequent. Unigram runs the other way: fit a unigram language model (a word's probability is the product of its tokens'), then prune the tokens whose removal least hurts corpus likelihood, 10% or 20% at a time, never single characters; because it is probabilistic it can also sample other segmentations, which Kudo used as training-time augmentation ("subword regularization"). SentencePiece needs no pre-tokenization: it writes whitespace as the meta symbol ▁ (U+2581), so "Hello▁world." becomes [Hello] [▁wor] [ld] [.] and decodes losslessly by replacing ▁ with a space (SentencePiece section 3.1). Byte-level BPE, as GPT-2 implemented it, maps each byte to a printable character, which is why a space shows up as Ġ in vocabulary files and a Hindi character as two or three odd Latin symbols.

How the three trainers were checked, and where WordPiece's score comes from (provenance)

On the Hugging Face course's four-sentence corpus, the page's WordPiece learns exactly the course's 25 new tokens (ab, ##fu, Fa, Fac, ... ##ut), its Unigram starts at the course's loss of 413.10 and tokenizes "This is the Hugging Face course." exactly as the course prints, and its BPE gives the same merges as the Hugging Face tokenizers library's own BpeTrainer. All three were checked on four corpora (36 of 36 comparisons identical, including accents, emoji, Cyrillic and Japanese; check_algos.mjs). The course's printed BPE list differs from the library's at ties, as the course itself notes: when two pairs are equally frequent the course takes the first one it meets, the library the one whose token ids are smallest. The library has no WordPiece trainer of its own (its WordPieceTrainer runs BPE with ## prefixes), and its Unigram trainer is SentencePiece's EM algorithm, so for those two the course's code is the reference; the page's Unigram is the course's simplified version (probabilities from substring counts, no EM re-estimation, exact removal losses).

WordPiece's original criterion was the language-model likelihood gain of each candidate unit (Schuster and Nakajima 2012); the frequency ratio above is the Hugging Face course's reconstruction, because "Google never open-sourced its implementation of the training algorithm" (course). So a WordPiece vocabulary trained with the library and BERT's original are built by different rules. BPE itself came to translation from compression with Sennrich, Haddow and Birch, to make the model "capable of open-vocabulary translation by encoding rare and unknown words as sequences of subword units".

Encoding is a different algorithm

Training builds the vocabulary once; encoding runs on every request, and each method encodes differently. BPE replays its merges in the order learned (the library does it as "apply the lowest-ranked merge present, repeat", which gives the same result). WordPiece stores no merges, only the vocabulary, and takes the longest vocabulary entry that matches from the left; if any part of a word cannot be matched, the whole word becomes [UNK]. Unigram finds the segmentation with the highest product of token probabilities by dynamic programming (Viterbi). So the same vocabulary entry can be reached differently: the course's example vocabulary tokenizes "hugs" as hug ##s by longest match, while BPE replaying the same merges gives hu ##gs (course).

Vocabulary size: what it costs and what it buys

A larger vocabulary means fewer tokens per text (fewer tokens to run, more text per context window) and makes every token more expensive at the edges of the model: one more embedding row and one more output row per entry, and an output softmax that scores every entry. Nothing in the blocks changes. So the vocabulary matters most where the blocks are small: in small models, and in mixture-of-experts models, where few weights run per token.

In dense models the table's share falls with size, because it grows with width only (Qwen3-0.6B 26%, Llama 3.1 8B 13%, Llama 3.1 405B 1%). Mixture-of-experts models break the pattern: the table is a tiny share of their total weights but a large share of what each token actually uses, because only a few experts run per token while the whole output layer runs every time. In gpt-oss-20b the input and output tables are 28% of the active parameters and the output layer is 16% of the decode FLOPs per token; in DeepSeek-V3 5% and 2.5%. Whoever designs a sparse model with a 200K vocabulary pays for it on every token.

Thirteen configs as a table (derived)

Table = V × d for the input embedding plus V × d for the output layer when untied. Output-layer FLOPs = 2 × V × d, as a share of the calculator's decode FLOPs per token at context 1. A 1B-parameter model with a 256K vocabulary and width 2,048, tied: 256K × 2,048 = 537M, about half of 1B, and an output layer of 2 × 256K × 2,048 = 1.07 GFLOPs per token, about half of the forward pass (2 GFLOPs); Gemma 3 1B is close to this (262,144 × 1,152 = 302M, 30% of 1.0B, from the root's Config calculator).

Config vocab_size is not the tokenizer's size. Configs pad the embedding matrix up to a round number so that the matmul tiles well on GPU tensor cores (pad_to_multiple_of in transformers); the extra rows are never produced by the tokenizer, yet still cost parameters and output FLOPs. Qwen3's config says 151,936 rows for 151,669 tokenizer entries (267 rows never used), DeepSeek-V3's 129,280 for 128,815, Llama 4's 202,048 for 201,135 measured (entries counted from each tokenizer.json with added tokens). Count len(tokenizer), not the config.

Where transformers pads, and a tying trap (transformers 5.20.1)

A second trap: tying is a config flag, not a file layout. Qwen3-0.6B sets tie_word_embeddings true, yet its safetensors also store a separate lm_head (751.6M stored against 596.0M in memory; root FACTS).

Adding tokens to a trained model (new special tokens for a chat format, a domain's frequent words) adds rows that start untrained. resize_token_embeddings in transformers 5.20.1 initialises them to the mean of the old rows by default (mean_resizing=True), which keeps the first forward pass sane; they still need fine-tuning before the model uses them well. Replacing a tokenizer outright throws away what every embedding row learned.

What current models use

Read from each open model's own tokenizer file (downloaded 2026-10-18, revisions in src/tok2026/fetched.json, read with tokenizers 0.23.3), three patterns hold:

  • Tokenizers are inherited, not redesigned. DeepSeek-V4 keeps V3's 128,000 ordinary tokens exactly and only adds special tokens; Gemma 4 keeps 262,125 of Gemma 3's 262,144 entries; GLM, MiniMax and Olmo equal their predecessors (the full list is in the table below). A new tokenizer invalidates every embedding and output row of the predecessor and every cached tokenization of the data, so labs change it only with a new pretraining run and a measured reason.
  • The exception is the multilingual push. Qwen3.5 grew from 151,669 to 248,070 entries and shares 131,586 of Qwen3's ordinary tokens; Llama 4 grew from 128,256 to 201,135 and shares 100,438 with Llama 3. Both cut Hindi sharply (Qwen3 56 to Qwen3.5 27 tokens on the same FLORES sentence, Llama 3 32 to Llama 4 22) while English stays at 16 to 17 tokens in every tokenizer of the table.
  • Sizes cluster at 128K to 262K. 128K (Llama 3, DeepSeek, Nemotron 3 Super at 131,072), 150K to 165K (Qwen3, GLM, Kimi), 200K (Llama 4, MiniMax, OpenAI), 248K to 262K (Qwen3.5, Gemma). Vocabularies grew from 32K (LLaMA, 2023), mostly for multilingual and code compression, and the 32K vocabularies of 2023 are gone from new models; the Vocabulary timeline tab puts 41 models from 2018 to 2026 on one axis.

Numbers and code

The problem. BPE learns pieces from frequency, so without a rule it cuts numbers wherever the training data happened to make merges: GPT-2 has " 2024" as one token but cuts 12345678 as 123|45|678, so the same digit can sit at different positions inside different tokens, and the model has to learn place value separately for every way a number can be cut. Every tokenizer since has added a digit rule to the pre-tokenizer, and they disagree: groups of up to three from the left (GPT-4's cl100k, GPT-4o's o200k, Llama 3 and 4, DeepSeek-V3 and V4, MiniMax, OLMo 2 and Olmo 3, Kimi K3: 123|456|78) or every digit alone (LLaMA 1 and 2, Gemma 1 to 4, Qwen3, Qwen3.5, Nemotron 3 Super, SmolLM2: 1|2|3|4|5|6|7|8).

The running example's TinyStories BPE learned no merges between digits at all (numbers are rare in the stories), so it cuts every digit too, by accident rather than by rule; GLM-5 is the odd one out: its pattern allows three digits but its vocabulary has only 177 of the 1,000 three-digit tokens, so 12345678 becomes 123|45|6|78 (the pattern allows a chunk the vocabulary does not have).

Why left-to-right chunks hurt. Addition works from the right. With chunks of three from the left, 12345 becomes 123|45 and 6789 becomes 678|9: the units digits of the two numbers sit in different positions of different tokens, and the answer's chunks do not line up with either. Writing the number with thousands separators (12,345) forces chunks of three from the right, aligned with place value. Singh and Strouse 2024 showed on GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 that right-to-left tokenization "enforced by comma separating numbers at inference time" "leads to largely improved performance", and that the gap shrinks with model size published.

Code. Code is mostly whitespace, punctuation and identifiers, and a tokenizer trained on prose wastes tokens on it. The running example's tokenizer is the extreme case: it never saw code, so the 91-character Python function below costs 64 tokens (" total" is to|t|al, an indent is four single spaces) against 45 for GPT-2 and 28 for Llama 3, whose tokenizer has tokens for runs of spaces and for ):\n. Gemma keeps an 8-space indent as one token; byte-level tokenizers of the Llama 3 family attach the last space of an indent to the next word (' ', ' return').

The language tax

Fertility is tokens per word, and the related premium is how many tokens a language needs for the same content relative to English: "The ratio |t(sA)| / |t(sB)| is the premium for A relative to B", measured on translations of the same sentences (Petrov et al. 2023 section 3). It varies wildly: GPT-4's tokenizer "uses about 1.6 times more tokens to encode the same text in Italian as it does in English, 2.6 times for Bulgarian and 3 times for Arabic. For Shan ... that difference can be as high as 15 times." A premium is both a cost (APIs bill per token, latency grows with length, less content fits in the context window) and a quality issue (the model sees each word as more, less meaningful pieces). Byte-level is not neutral either: "byte-level representation of the same text is over 4 times longer for Burmese or Tibetan than Chinese."

The tax depends on the tokenizer more than on the language: Tamil falls from 15.6 times English to 2.0

Premium against English for six languages, four generations of tokenizer, over the whole FLORES-200 dev and devtest (2,009 sentences per language). Try Tamil: GPT-2 15.6 times, GPT-4o 2.0.

Defaults reproduce Petrov et al.'s published token counts exactly, independently: 204 of 204 languages for GPT-2's r50k, GPT-4's cl100k, Llama 2, ByT5's bytes and UTF-32 characters, from the FLORES-200 release with the paper's own construction (their code and table). The other columns extend it to newer tokenizers.

Across all 204 languages, the median premium is 2.26 with GPT-4's cl100k and 1.75 with GPT-4o's o200k; 65 languages pay more than 3 times English with cl100k, 15 with o200k and 8 with Gemma 3. So "low-resource languages pay 3 to 5 times more" is true for some tokenizers and some languages, and the size of the tax depends on the tokenizer far more than on the language: Tamil drops from 7.65 with Llama 3 to 1.46 with Gemma 3. OpenAI's GPT-4o launch listed the gain for 20 languages (Gujarati "4.4x fewer tokens (from 145 to 33)"); the Same text, ten tokenizers tab recomputes all 20 sentences (18 reproduce exactly) and lists every one of the 204 languages. So never compare token counts or per-token prices across models as if a token were a fixed amount of text: compare per character or per word of your own text. When choosing a tokenizer for domain adaptation, measure the fertility on your own text: a tokenizer trained on a different distribution can make the same budget of tokens carry much less of your data.

Failure modes, with real outputs

The model never sees characters, only ids, so anything that depends on the characters inside a token, on where a token boundary falls, or on a token it rarely saw in training has to be learned indirectly, and often is not. First the mechanism on ten real tokenizers, then what real models do.

Every failure below starts as a token boundary or a token the model rarely saw

A prompt that ends in a space

Byte-level tokenizers glue a space to the following word (" Paris" is one token), so a prompt ending in a bare space ends on a lone space token, which in training text is almost never followed by a word token. The model has to recover from a boundary it rarely saw. Before and after, same model, same prompt, one trailing space:

One trailing space changes the next-token distribution and the continuation

measured on Apple M1 Pro (CPU) src/probe/space_letters.py: next-token probabilities after the last prompt token, and 12 greedy tokens. SmolLM2-135M revision 93efa2f0 in fp32; the tiny GPT is the root's ref_baseline_s1500 checkpoint. What transfers: every byte-level tokenizer behaves this way (the space joins the next word); large models recover more often, which is why APIs and chat templates strip or control trailing whitespace. Libraries offer "token healing" (back up one token and constrain the next to start with the removed text) for this exact problem.

Keep prompts in the form the model was trained on: never end a completion prompt with a bare space, and do not strip or add one without checking the template.

Glitch tokens: entries the model never trained

A vocabulary is trained once and a model is trained afterwards, often on different data. Entries that are in the vocabulary but nearly absent from the model's training data get almost no gradient as inputs, and as outputs they are only ever pushed down. Land and Bartolo 2024 call these "tokens present in the tokenizer vocabulary but that are nearly or entirely absent during model training" and found them "across a diverse set of models"; GPT-2's " SolidGoldMagikarp" (id 43453, in the gallery above) is the famous one. The running example has its own, measured:

The 132 entries never seen in training (red) end with large norms, all pointing the same way

What the tiny GPT writes after an unseen token (measured)

measured on Apple M1 Pro (CPU) src/bpe/freq_glitch.py: how often each of the 4,096 ids occurs in the 122M training tokens (train.bin), and the norm of each row of the trained tiny GPT's tied embedding (ref_baseline_s1500). Continuations are greedy, 25 tokens, after "Once upon a time, there was a little" plus one token.

132 of the 4,096 entries never occur in the 122M training tokens, of two kinds: 122 byte tokens for bytes TinyStories almost never contains (@, ^ and most non-ASCII bytes), and 10 intermediate merges such as " underst", " butterf" and "aghet", tokens the trainer created on the way to " understand", " butterfly" and "aghetti" that every later encoding merges further, so they never survive as final tokens. All of them ended training pointing the same way (their rows have cosine similarity 0.996 with each other, against 0.024 among the 200 commonest tokens) and with norms more than twice the typical row, because the only gradient they ever got was "do not predict me". Fed one of them, the model loses the thread. How to check a real model: count each id in a sample of its training data if you have it; otherwise look for embedding or output rows that cluster together or have unusual norms, then confirm by prompting the model to repeat the token, the indicators Land and Bartolo combine.

Special tokens and chat templates

Special tokens (beginning and end of sequence, chat roles, tool-call markers, padding) are reserved ids added on top of the learned vocabulary, after it is trained, and they never come out of the merge procedure: the tokenizer matches them as whole strings before ordinary encoding. If a template marker is not registered as special, it is just text and costs ordinary tokens: <|user|> is five tokens in eight of the ten tokenizers here and four in Gemma 3 and Llama 4. Tulu 3's template is in exactly that situation, as the Alignment page shows token by token while explaining which tokens carry the SFT loss. Chat-template mismatches between training and inference (a different role marker, a missing end-of-turn token, a template applied twice) are a common silent bug in fine-tuning: the model trains fine and then behaves oddly at serving time. A shared tokenizer is also what makes logit-level distillation possible: a student can only match a teacher's distribution token by token if both have the same vocabulary; with different tokenizers, distil on sampled text or use a method built for mismatched vocabularies (Distillation and Small Language Models).

Two silent traps, before and after the fix: the same input, different ids

The first trap: a tokenizer parses special-token strings found inside user text as the special tokens themselves unless told not to. Without split_special_tokens=True, "hi<|im_end|>" becomes 2 ids, the second being Qwen3's real end-of-turn token (151645), so typed text can close a chat turn; serving stacks that template untrusted text must split or escape it. The same run shows Qwen3's len(tok) (151,669, with the 26 added tokens) against vocab_size (151,643, the base vocabulary), and neither is the config's 151,936.

The second trap: a chat template that already writes <bos>, rendered to a string and then tokenized with the default add_special_tokens=True, starts [2, 2, 105], two beginning-of-sequence tokens; letting apply_chat_template tokenize gives [2, 105, ...]. Nothing errors and the model still answers, slightly off distribution. Fix: tokenize with the template, or pass add_special_tokens=False; check by printing the first ids of every training and serving example once and comparing them with the template's own tokenization.

Beyond subwords

Tokenizer-free and byte-level research keeps resurfacing but has not displaced subword BPE in production. Raw bytes remove the tokenizer and its failure modes but make sequences about 4 times longer (ByT5). Patches fix the length: MegaByte groups bytes in fixed patches of 8, and the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT, Meta, December 2024) starts a new patch where a small byte model finds the next byte hard to predict, so predictable stretches become long patches and the large model takes fewer steps: it "matches training flop-controlled performance of Llama 3 while using up to 50% fewer flops at inference". SuperBPE (2025) goes the other way and lets tokens cross spaces ("by the way" as one token), up to 33% fewer tokens than BPE at 200K entries.

ByT5, MegaByte, BLT and SuperBPE in their own words (sources and numbers)
  • ByT5 (2021) feeds raw UTF-8 bytes to a T5: "a vocabulary of 256 possible byte values. An additional 3 IDs are reserved for special tokens". Sequences are about 4 times longer than SentencePiece's ("4.1 bytes per SentencePiece token" on mC4); byte models "are significantly more robust to noise and perform better on tasks that are sensitive to spelling", at "+33% pre-training time" and inference up to 10 times slower (ByT5).
  • MegaByte (2023) "segments sequences into patches and uses a local submodel within patches and a global model between patches", with fixed patches of 8 bytes for text (MegaByte).
  • Byte Latent Transformer, BLT (Meta, December 2024) makes the patches dynamic: a small byte-level model predicts the next byte, and a new patch starts where its entropy is high, so predictable stretches become long patches and the large model takes fewer steps. It "matches training flop-controlled performance of Llama 3 while using up to 50% fewer flops at inference"; "The bpe tokenizers of Llama 2 and 3 have an average token size of 3.7 and 4.4 bytes. In contrast, BLT can achieve similar scaling trends with an average patch size of 6 and even 8 bytes" (BLT section 5.1).
  • SuperBPE (2025) goes the other way and lets tokens cross spaces in a second training stage ("superword" tokens such as "by the way"): with a 200K vocabulary it "encodes a fixed piece of text with up to 33% fewer tokens on average than BPE", and an 8B model trained with it scores "+4.0% absolute improvement over the BPE baseline across 30 downstream tasks" with "27% less compute at inference time" (SuperBPE). The old page said frontier labs are experimenting with SuperBPE-style tokens; that is unconfirmed: no released frontier tokenizer measured here has superword tokens.

Training your own

Use the Hugging Face tokenizers library or SentencePiece on a corpus that matches your target distribution: the vocabulary is frozen with the model, so the languages, scripts, code and number formats you care about must be in the tokenizer's training data. Decide the pre-tokenization rules (digits, whitespace runs, code) before training, reserve special tokens for your chat template, and measure fertility on held-out text in every language you will serve. Where the training corpus comes from and how it is filtered is the subject of Topic: data-curation-and-datasets. A model fine-tuned on a new domain or language with an old tokenizer pays the old tokenizer's rate on every token: the running example's TinyStories BPE needs 64 tokens for a 91-character Python function and 3 tokens per Chinese character (one per byte), where Llama 3's needs 28 and about 1, and its rarely used entries may be glitch tokens. Changing the tokenizer of a trained model means new embedding rows that start untrained, so extending a vocabulary (Llama 3 added 28K entries to tiktoken's 100K before training, not after) is far easier than swapping one.

The running example's tokenizer is a complete, minimal recipe: the closed block below holds the training part of the root's src/data/prepare_data.py, as rerun by src/bpe/bpe_sweep.py, with that run's printed result.

Interview rehearsal: three design questions (optional)
Why does gpt-oss-20b's vocabulary matter more than Llama 3.1 8B's, although its table is a smaller share of total parameters?
Total parameters do not run per token; active ones do. gpt-oss-20b activates 4 of 32 experts, so its 201,088 × 2,880 untied tables are 28% of the active parameters and its output layer 16% of the decode FLOPs per token, against 13% and 7% for dense Llama 3.1 8B (root Config calculator numbers).
What is a glitch token and how would you find them in a model you are given?
A vocabulary entry the model almost never saw in training, so its embedding is untrained. Find candidates from the weights: rows that cluster together or have unusual norms (here 132 unseen entries had cosine similarity 0.996 with each other and norms 2.5 times the median), check frequency in a training sample, then confirm by prompting the model to repeat the token.
Design: you are pretraining a 3B model that must serve English, Hindi, Japanese and Python. How do you choose the tokenizer?
Train byte-level BPE (no holes) on a sample matching the target mix, oversampling the smaller languages so they get merges; pick digit handling (single digits, or groups of three from the right) and whitespace-run tokens for code before training; reserve chat and tool special tokens up front. Size: measure characters per token on held-out text per language at, say, 64K, 128K and 256K, and weigh tokens saved against the table's cost (at width 3,072 a 256K untied vocabulary is 1.6B parameters, half the model, so tie the embeddings or stay near 128K: SmolLM3 3B ties a 128,256-entry table, root map config). Check the result: fertility per language within about 1.5 times English, no unseen entries in a training sample, round-trip decode exact on every test string.

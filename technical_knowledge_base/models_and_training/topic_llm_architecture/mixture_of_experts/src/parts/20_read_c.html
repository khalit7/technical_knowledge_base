2. The router: score, normalise, select, combine

The problem. Each token must be sent somewhere, the choice must be cheap (the router runs on every token in every MoE layer), and the router must learn which expert suits which token although "send to expert 3" is a discrete decision with no gradient.

How it works, in four steps, with the tiny GPT's own code, which defines the arithmetic of every run on this page ([[ref:tg_scores]] onward; training ran it through an equivalent batched forward, section 6):

  1. Score. Logits s = Wr h: one matrix multiply, E numbers per token. In DeepSeek-V3, Wr is 256 × 7,168 = 1,835,008 weights against 11.32 billion in the whole MoE layer, 0.016% (derived from its config).
  2. Normalise. A softmax over all E experts (Switch, Mixtral, Qwen3) turns logits into probabilities that sum to 1; a sigmoid per expert (DeepSeek-V3 and the models that copied it) gives each expert an independent affinity in (0, 1).
  3. Select. Keep the k highest. Softmax and sigmoid are both increasing functions of the logit, so from the same logits they pick the same experts; they differ only in the weights they hand to step 4, until a bias or a group limit is added to the selection (section 3 and below).
  4. Combine. Renormalise the k chosen scores to sum to 1 (most models), optionally multiply by a constant routed_scaling_factor (2.5 in DeepSeek-V3, 5.0 in Nemotron 3 Super), and add the weighted expert outputs to the residual stream.

The tiny GPT's router, [[ref:tg_moe_class]] onward (root src/data/tiny_gpt.py). The loop over experts gathers each expert's tokens, runs the expert on that slice and scatter-adds the weighted result; transformers' Qwen3-MoE does the same in [[ref:q3_loop]] to [[ref:q3_index_add]], and production stacks replace the loop by one grouped matrix multiply over ragged token groups (section 4).

Three ways of writing the same top-k softmax

Softmax over all experts, then top k, then renormalise (Qwen3-MoE in transformers, [[ref:q3_softmax]] to [[ref:q3_norm]]); top k of the logits, then softmax over those k (gpt-oss, [[ref:go_topk]] to [[ref:go_softmax]]); and Mixtral's paper formula Softmax(TopK(x · Wg)) with the other logits set to minus infinity ({{Mixtral|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba72af0ac91ddc6b3}}, section 2.1). All three give the same gate weights: renormalising k softmax probabilities cancels the shared denominator and leaves a softmax over the k chosen logits. The one trap is the renormalisation flag: Qwen3MoeConfig defaults to norm_topk_prob=False ([[ref:q3_cfg_norm]]) while the released Qwen3-30B-A3B and 235B-A22B configs set it to true, so a model built from the default config weights its experts by raw probabilities that sum to less than one.

One real token through both rules

The router logits of one token of the running sequence in one layer of the trained moe8top2 model (recorded, not illustrative). Pick the token and layer; then add experts with logit 0 to see what happens to a softmax score as E grows, while the sigmoid score stays put.

Why sigmoid for many experts. A softmax probability is a share of one distribution over all E experts: it averages 1/E and shrinks as experts are added, and after renormalisation a logit gap of 1 is a weight ratio of e ≈ 2.72. A sigmoid affinity depends on its own logit only. DeepSeek-V3's report states the switch to sigmoid without arguing it (section 2.1.2, "slightly different from DeepSeek-V2"), but the same group's auxiliary-loss-free paper says it chose sigmoid "since we find that the sigmoid baseline performs better than the softmax baseline", and its appendix C shows softmax gates at higher perplexity for similar balance on 1B models ({{Wang et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15664}}, vendor: the authors are DeepSeek's). Llama 4 applies a sigmoid only to the one logit it keeps ([[ref:l4_topk]] to [[ref:l4_sigmoid]]); DeepSeek-V4-Pro's config sets scoring_func to sqrtsoftplus, the square root of softplus, used by the same code path ([[ref:v4_scorefn]]).

The router learns only through the experts it picked

Top-k selection has no gradient. The language-model loss reaches the router only through the gate weights gi of the chosen experts, which multiply their outputs. An expert that stops being chosen stops producing any evidence that it would have been good: that is why routing can collapse (section 3). Measured on the trained moe8top2 model, layer [[f:grad_layer|i]], tokens 1 to 8 of the running sequence: with the language-model loss alone, the gradient on the 6 unchosen logits of each token is at most [[f:grad_lm_ratio|e]] of the largest chosen one (floating-point rounding: analytically it is zero, because the renormalised gates are a softmax over the chosen logits only); with the Switch auxiliary loss added it is up to [[f:grad_aux_ratio|f3]] of it, on all eight (measured on Apple M1 Pro (CPU), src/code/grad_check.py).

How many experts per token, and who chooses

Noisy top-k (2017)

Gaussian noise scaled by a learned softplus is added to the logits before the top k, so near-ties share traffic ({{Shazeer et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.06538}}: H(x) = x Wg + StandardNormal() · Softplus(x Wnoise)). Its descendant in transformers is Mixtral's multiplicative router_jitter_noise, 0 in the released configs ([[ref:mx_jitter]]).

Top-2 (GShard, Mixtral)

GShard dispatched the second expert at random in proportion to its weight, to save capacity ({{GShard|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16668}}, "Random routing"); Mixtral keeps both always. Two gate weights give the router a comparison, not a single scalar.

Top-1 (Switch, 2021)

Shazeer et al. had "conjectured that routing to k>1 experts was necessary in order to have non-trivial gradients to the routing functions"; Switch showed k = 1 "preserves model quality" at less compute ({{Switch|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a8b541c9babbf40c94}}, section 2.1). ST-MoE later found top-2 better by +0.004 at 8 times the scale ({{ST-MoE|https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08906}}, Table 8). Llama 4 Maverick is top-1 plus a shared expert.

Expert choice (2022)

Experts pick their top tokens instead of tokens picking experts: perfect balance by construction, "over 2× faster training convergence" than GShard top-2 at 8B/64E ({{Zhou et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.09368}}, vendor, Google). It "might not immediately apply to auto-regressive text generation" because a token's experts depend on future tokens in the batch: no decoder LLM in section 9 uses it.

Hash routing

A fixed table maps each token id to its experts: no router to train, no collapse ({{Roller et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.04426}}). DeepSeekMoE's Table 1 has it at a Pile loss of 1.932 against GShard's 1.867 at 2B ({{DeepSeekMoE|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06066}}). DeepSeek-V4-Pro uses it for its first 3 MoE layers (num_hash_layers 3; a frozen tid2eid table, [[ref:v4_tid2eid]]), presumably because hidden states there are still close to the raw token embedding, so a learned router has little more than the token id to go on (our reading; the code and config give no reason).

Group-limited routing

Experts are split into groups (one per node); each token first picks its best groups, then its experts inside them. DeepSeek-V3: 8 groups of 32, a group scored by the sum of its two best experts, 4 groups per token ([[ref:ds_group]] to [[ref:ds_mask]]): its "at most 4 nodes". Kimi K2 and K3 and GLM set one group, so no limit.

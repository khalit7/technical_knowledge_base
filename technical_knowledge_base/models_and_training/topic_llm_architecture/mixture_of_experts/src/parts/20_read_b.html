1. Why experts: more weights, the same compute per token

The problem. In a dense transformer every token is multiplied by every weight: a forward pass costs about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token (the root's {{Block lab|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8172b490dc9f9ac281df}} checks this rule against PyTorch's FLOP counter on the running sequence). A model that knows more needs more parameters, and so pays more compute on every token, including the many easy ones ("the", a comma) that need little of that knowledge. The feed-forward network (FFN) is where most of the parameters sit: in the tiny GPT one block has 262,144 attention weights (four 256 × 256 projections) and 524,288 FFN weights (256 × 1,024 up, 1,024 × 256 down), two thirds of the block.

The idea. Keep attention as it is, but split the FFN into E independent FFNs, the experts, and add a router (also called a gate): a d × E linear layer that gives each token one score per expert. The token goes to its k highest-scoring experts, and the layer outputs their outputs weighted by the scores. Shazeer et al. introduced the layer in 2017 between LSTM layers ({{Outrageously Large Neural Networks|https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.06538}}); GShard put it in a transformer in 2020 ({{GShard|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.16668}}), and Switch Transformers showed one expert per token is enough ({{Switch Transformers|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a8b541c9babbf40c94}}).

y = h + ∑i ∈ TopK(s(h)) gi(h) · Ei(h) + ∑j Sj(h)
h
the token's hidden state entering the FFN sub-layer (after the norm), d numbers; the leading h is the residual connection
s(h)
router scores, one per expert, from logits Wr h (Wr is E × d)
TopK
the k experts with the highest scores (section 2 adds a bias and a group limit to this choice)
gi(h)
gate weight of chosen expert i, usually its score renormalised over the k chosen
Ei, Sj
routed expert i and always-on shared expert j: ordinary FFNs, narrower than the dense one

Drawn to scale: what is stored and what one token uses

What it costs. Compute per token stays at the active count (plus the router: 2 × d × E FLOPs, 4,096 per token per layer for moe8top2 against 1,048,576 for its two experts, 0.4%). Memory is priced on the total: all experts must be resident because any token may pick any expert. And three new costs appear that the rest of this page is about: the choice is discrete, so the router learns only through the weights of the experts it picked (section 2); left alone, traffic collapses onto a few experts (section 3); and once experts live on different GPUs, tokens must travel to them and back (section 7).

When it matters. MoE pays when parameters are cheap to store but compute per token is the bottleneck: pretraining at a fixed FLOP budget, and serving at large batch where every expert is busy. It pays least when memory is the bottleneck (one GPU, small batch: section 8).

How to check it. Count both numbers from a config with the root's Config calculator or this page's {{Dense vs MoE calculator|#t-calc}} (both use the same formulas, validated to residual 0 on 44 Hugging Face checkpoints); on a live PyTorch model, sum p.numel() over the experts of one layer and multiply by k/E for the active share. The headline pairs: Mixtral 8x7B 46.7B total, 12.9B active (not 56B: only the FFNs are copied eight times, {{Mistral, 2023-12-11|https://mistral.ai/news/mixtral-of-experts}}, vendor); DeepSeek-V3 671B and 37B ({{report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}, vendor), both reproduced from the configs in section 9.

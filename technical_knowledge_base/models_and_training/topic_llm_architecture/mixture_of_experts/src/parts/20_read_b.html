1. Total and active: more weights, the same compute per token

In a dense transformer every token multiplies through every weight, about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token, and the FFN holds most of them: one tiny-GPT block has 262,144 attention weights (four 256 × 256 projections) and 524,288 FFN weights (256 × 1,024 up, 1,024 × 256 down), two thirds of the block. MoE splits that FFN into experts. The root's tiny GPT (d = 256, 6 blocks, GELU) has MoE presets that keep the active FFN exactly the dense size, so comparisons on this page are at equal compute (presets in the root's src/data/configs.py). Fig. 2 draws them to scale:

Fig. 2, step by step: compute stays at the dense size while storage grows fourfold

  1. Dense: every weight runs. The tiny GPT's dense FFN (256 → 1,024 → 256) is 524,288 weights per layer, and every token uses all of them: stored equals per token. A block's footprint is proportional to its weights.
  2. moe8top2: 8 experts, 2 per token. Each expert is half as wide (512), 262,144 weights; a token uses the 2 the router picks (lit: E6 and E0, the trained model's choice for "Once" in layer 1). Stored 2,099,200 with the 2,048-weight router, per token 526,336: four times the storage at the dense cost.
  3. moe16top3_shared: finer, plus one always on. 16 routed experts of 256 plus 1 shared, 131,072 weights each; a token uses 3 routed (E13, E15 and E3 for "Once") and the shared one. Stored 2,232,320, per token 528,384.
  4. Whole models. Attention, embeddings and norms are not copied, so the model totals grow less than the FFN: 5.84M dense, 15.29M and 16.08M for the two MoE presets, while active stays at 5.84M, 5.85M and 5.86M.
  5. A real layer, at another scale. One DeepSeek-V3 MoE layer: 257 experts of 3 × 7,168 × 2,048 = 44,040,192 weights each (11.32 billion with the router); a token uses 9, 396 million (3.5%). Which 8 routed experts light is illustrative. The router itself (256 × 7,168) would be 1/24 of one block.

Adding experts moves memory, not compute, until the batch grows

The same move on a real model, step by step: build the dense twin (one FFN as wide as the k routed experts plus the shared ones a token uses), then add the other experts and watch which of the five numbers move. h
the token's hidden state entering the FFN sub-layer (after the norm), d numbers; the leading h is the residual connection
s(h)
router scores, one per expert, from logits Wr h (Wr is E × d)
TopK
the k experts with the highest scores (section 3 adds a bias to this choice, section 2 a group limit)
gi(h)
gate weight of chosen expert i, usually its score renormalised over the k chosen
Ei, Sj
routed expert i and shared expert j: ordinary FFNs, narrower than the dense one

What it costs, and when it pays. Compute per token stays at the active count plus the router, 2 × d × E FLOPs (4,096 per token per layer for moe8top2 against 1,048,576 for its two experts, 0.4%). Memory is priced on the total, because any token may pick any expert. MoE pays when parameters are cheap to store but compute per token is the bottleneck: pretraining at a fixed FLOP budget, and serving at large batch. It pays least on one memory-bound GPU at small batch (section 8).

How to check it. Count both numbers from a config with the {{Dense vs MoE calculator|#t-calc}}; on a live PyTorch model, sum p.numel() over one layer's experts and multiply by k/E. Mixtral 8x7B is 46.7B total and 12.9B active, not 56B, because only the FFNs are copied eight times ({{Mistral, 2023-12-11|https://mistral.ai/news/mixtral-of-experts}}, vendor); DeepSeek-V3 is 671B and 37B ({{report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}, vendor); both are rebuilt from their configs in section 9.

4. Capacity factors and dropped tokens

Accelerators like static shapes: a compiled step wants each expert's batch size before routing has happened. The classic answer is a fixed buffer per expert. Worked example: n = 4,096 tokens, E = 64, k = 2, capacity factor 1.25: the average load is 128 and the buffer holds 160; an expert attracting 200 drops 40, one attracting 60 computes 100 padded slots for nothing.

C = ⌈ CF · n · k / E ⌉

Assignments beyond C are dropped: that token gets no output from that expert in that layer and passes on through the residual connection. Nothing fails; the loss just gets worse. Capacity is per batch, so small batches drop more. [[s4_batch]] This is the training against serving trap: a model trained with large batches at CF 1.25 sees a different drop rate at serving batch sizes, one reason serving stacks run MoE layers dropless.

Dropless. The drop exists only because of static buffers. MegaBlocks computes the experts as block-sparse matrix multiplication over ragged per-expert groups, so nothing is padded and it "never drops tokens" ({{MegaBlocks|https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15841}}, 2022); Mixtral's release added MegaBlocks kernels to vLLM. Modern stacks call this a grouped GEMM: one kernel launch that multiplies each expert's weights by its own variable-length slice of tokens. DeepSeek-V3 "does not drop any tokens during training" and serves without drops. The capacity factor survives in older recipes, some TPU stacks and as an admission limit at serving time.

How to check it. Log, per MoE layer and step, the dropped fraction and the max-over-mean load; the tiny GPT's training log records both every 10 steps (dropped, moe_load in log.json). A dropped fraction above a few percent, or rising during training, means the balancing is losing.

What the papers said about capacity, and which tokens to drop (depth)

"If the tokens are unevenly dispatched then certain experts will overflow ... resulting in these tokens not being processed by this layer. A larger capacity factor alleviates this overflow issue, but also increases computation and communication costs" ({{Switch|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a8b541c9babbf40c94}}, Figure 3). ST-MoE recommends "top-2 routing with 1.25 capacity factor", states "The train capacity factor is 1.25 and the eval capacity factor is 2.0" for its models, and finds that "increasing both the train and eval capacity factors (CF) improves quality" ({{ST-MoE|https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08906}}, Table 8); Switch tried re-routing overflow to the second choice ("No-Token-Left-Behind") and "found no empirical benefits". Which tokens are dropped is a design choice too: the tiny GPT drops in batch order, while DeepSeek-V2 dropped per device at a budget "equivalent to 1.0", the "tokens with the lowest affinity scores" first, and kept about 10% of training sequences never dropped so inference could run either way ({{DeepSeek-V2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434}}, section 2.2.4).

5. Fine-grained and shared experts

With 8 or 16 large experts each expert must hold many unrelated kinds of knowledge ("knowledge hybridity"), and every expert relearns what all tokens need, such as syntax and frequent words ("knowledge redundancy") ({{DeepSeekMoE|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06066}}, introduction). DeepSeekMoE (January 2024) made two moves, both drawn below at the same compute:

Same compute, finer experts: 28 possible expert sets become 1,820

Each row is one MoE layer at the same active width (the filled area is what a token computes). Fine-grained segmentation splits each expert into m narrower ones (hidden size divided by m) and routes to m · k of the m · E: parameters and FLOPs stay the same, the combinations explode, C(16, 4) = 1,820 against C(8, 2) = 28, and C(256, 8) ≈ 4.1 × 1014 for DeepSeek-V3. Shared experts run on every token outside the router, to absorb common knowledge so the routed experts can specialise; the last row is the tiny GPT's moe16top3_shared, C(16, 3) = 560 routed sets. Lit experts in rows 1 and 3 are the trained models' choices for "Once" in layer 1; row 2 is illustrative.

The published evidence (vendor, DeepSeek; 2B total, 100B tokens, Pile loss): dense 0.2B 2.060, Hash Layer 1.932, Switch 1.881, GShard 1.867, DeepSeekMoE 1.808, with GShard and DeepSeekMoE at the same 0.3B active (Table 1). Disabling the shared expert and routing one more expert at the same compute raised the Pile loss "from 1.808 to 2.414"; 1, 2 or 4 shared experts gave 1.808, 1.806 and 1.811 (sections 4.4 and 4.5). DeepSeekMoE was also more sensitive than GShard to masking its top routed experts, which the paper reads as "lower redundancy among routed experts".

Measured on the tiny GPT. [[s5_meas]]

What it costs. Narrower experts are skinnier matrix multiplies (256 wide in the tiny GPT, 2,048 in DeepSeek-V3, against its dense 18,432), so utilisation falls; a token visits more experts, so the all-to-all fans out wider (section 7); and smaller experts make imbalance more likely. Not everyone keeps a shared expert: Qwen3's MoE "excludes shared experts" while keeping 128 fine-grained experts with 8 active ({{Qwen3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388}}), and gpt-oss and MiniMax-M2 have none. The {{calculator|#t-calc}} splits any real config m ways.

Latent experts (2026): routing in a smaller dimension (depth)

Nemotron 3 Super and Kimi K3 project tokens down before the routed experts and back up after them, "into a smaller latent dimension for expert routing and computation, improving accuracy per byte" (Nemotron 3 Super card, vendor; moe_latent_size 1,024 against d = 4,096); Kimi K3's config sets routed_expert_hidden_size 3,584 against d = 7,168 and its card names "Stable LatentMoE", 16 of 896 experts active. If the down-projection runs before dispatch, a token sends 1,024 or 3,584 numbers per expert through the all-to-all instead of d (section 7): our reading of the configs; neither card describes the communication.

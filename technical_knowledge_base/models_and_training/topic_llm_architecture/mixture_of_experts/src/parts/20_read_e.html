4. Capacity factors and dropped tokens

The problem. Accelerators like static shapes: a compiled step wants to know each expert's batch size before routing has happened. The classic answer allocates a fixed buffer per expert and drops what does not fit.

C = ⌈ CF · n · k / E ⌉
C
token slots in each expert's buffer (the tiny GPT: [[ref:tg_cap]])
CF
capacity factor: 1.0 provisions exactly the average load
n, k, E
tokens in the batch, experts per token, experts

How it works. Assignments beyond C are dropped: that token gets no output from that expert in that layer and passes on through the residual connection. "If the tokens are unevenly dispatched then certain experts will overflow ... resulting in these tokens not being processed by this layer. A larger capacity factor alleviates this overflow issue, but also increases computation and communication costs" ({{Switch|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a8b541c9babbf40c94}}, Figure 3). Worked example: n = 4,096, E = 64, k = 2, CF = 1.25: the average load is 128 and C = 160; an expert attracting 200 drops 40, one attracting 60 computes 100 padded slots for nothing. ST-MoE recommends "top-2 routing with 1.25 capacity factor", states "The train capacity factor is 1.25 and the eval capacity factor is 2.0" for its models, and finds that "increasing both the train and eval capacity factors (CF) improves quality" ({{ST-MoE|https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08906}}, Table 8); Switch tried re-routing overflow to the second choice ("No-Token-Left-Behind") and "found no empirical benefits". Which tokens are dropped is a design choice too: the tiny GPT drops in batch order, while DeepSeek-V2 dropped per device at a budget "equivalent to 1.0", the "tokens with the lowest affinity scores" first, and kept about 10% of training sequences never dropped so inference could run either way ({{DeepSeek-V2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434}}, section 2.2.4).

Each point: the trained model evaluated on 10 fixed held-out batches of 32 × 256 tokens (81,920 tokens) with the capacity factor imposed at inference; buffers fill first come, first served in batch order ([[ref:tg_drop]]). measured on Apple M1 Pro (CPU). Losses are from a 6-layer toy and transfer only in direction: drops hurt, and they hurt a model trained without them more.

Measured. [[s4_meas]]

Capacity is per batch, so small batches drop more. [[s4_batch]] This is the training against serving trap: a model trained with large batches and CF 1.25 sees a different drop rate at serving batch sizes, which is one reason serving stacks run MoE layers dropless.

Dropless. The drop exists only because of static buffers. MegaBlocks reformulates the experts as block-sparse matrix multiplication over ragged per-expert groups, so nothing is padded and nothing is dropped: it "never drops tokens" ({{MegaBlocks|https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15841}}, 2022); Mixtral's release added MegaBlocks kernels to vLLM. Modern stacks call this a grouped GEMM: one kernel launch that multiplies each expert's weights by its own variable-length slice of tokens. DeepSeek-V3 "does not drop any tokens during training" and serves without drops. The capacity factor survives in older recipes, in some TPU stacks and as an admission limit at serving time.

How to check it. Log, per MoE layer and step, the dropped fraction and the max-over-mean load; the tiny GPT's training log records both every 10 steps (dropped, moe_load in log.json). A dropped fraction above a few percent, or rising during training, means the balancing is losing.

5. Fine-grained and shared experts

The problem. With 8 or 16 large experts, each expert must hold many unrelated kinds of knowledge ("knowledge hybridity"), and every expert relearns what all tokens need, such as syntax and frequent words ("knowledge redundancy") ({{DeepSeekMoE|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.06066}}, introduction).

How it works. Two moves, both in DeepSeekMoE (January 2024):

  • Fine-grained segmentation. Split each expert into m narrower ones (hidden size divided by m) and route to m · k of the m · E. Parameters and FLOPs stay the same; the number of expert combinations a token can use explodes: C(16, 4) = 1,820 against C(8, 2) = 28 at the same compute, and C(256, 8) ≈ 4.1 × 1014 for DeepSeek-V3.
  • Shared experts. Run Ks experts on every token, outside the router, to absorb common knowledge so the routed experts can specialise.

The published evidence (vendor, DeepSeek; 2B total, 100B tokens, Pile loss): dense 0.2B 2.060, Hash Layer 1.932, Switch 1.881, GShard 1.867, DeepSeekMoE 1.808, with GShard and DeepSeekMoE at the same 0.3B active (Table 1). Disabling the shared expert and routing one more expert at the same compute raised the Pile loss "from 1.808 to 2.414"; 1, 2 or 4 shared experts gave 1.808, 1.806 and 1.811 (section 4.4 and 4.5). DeepSeekMoE was also more sensitive than GShard to masking its top routed experts, which the paper reads as "lower redundancy among routed experts".

Measured on the tiny GPT. [[s5_meas]]

What it costs. Narrower experts are skinnier matrix multiplies (a 256-wide expert in the tiny GPT, 2,048 in DeepSeek-V3, against the dense 18,432), so utilisation falls; a token visits more experts, so the all-to-all fans out wider (section 7); and smaller experts make imbalance more likely. Not everyone keeps a shared expert: Qwen3's MoE "excludes shared experts" while keeping 128 fine-grained experts with 8 active ({{Qwen3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388}}), and gpt-oss and MiniMax-M2 have none.

Latent experts (2026). Nemotron 3 Super and Kimi K3 go one step further: tokens are projected down before the routed experts and back up after them, "into a smaller latent dimension for expert routing and computation, improving accuracy per byte" (Nemotron 3 Super card, vendor; moe_latent_size 1,024 against d = 4,096); Kimi K3's config sets routed_expert_hidden_size 3,584 against d = 7,168 and its card names "Stable LatentMoE", 16 of 896 experts active. If the down-projection runs before dispatch, a token sends 1,024 or 3,584 numbers per expert through the all-to-all instead of d (section 7): our reading of the configs; neither card describes the communication.

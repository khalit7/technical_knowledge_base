7. Expert parallelism and the all-to-all

DeepSeek-V3's experts are 656.6B of its 671B parameters (98%, derived from its config: 58 layers × 257 experts × 44.04M). No GPU holds them, so expert parallelism (EP) gives each of W ranks E/W experts of every MoE layer, and every token travels to the ranks that hold its experts and back. This page ran it: layer [[f:ep_layer|i]] of the trained moe8top2, the 256-token running sequence split into four 64-token shards, two experts per rank, over four real processes. [[ep_meas]]

What it costs. Per token per MoE layer, k · d numbers go out and k · d come back (fewer if the expert is local). DeepSeek-V3 at k = 8, d = 7,168 in BF16: 8 × 7,168 × 2 B = 112 KiB each way per token per layer, 58 layers, forward; the backward pass repeats both. Every rank talks to every rank, the combine cannot finish before the slowest rank, and load imbalance becomes idle GPUs. How the bytes meet NVLink and InfiniBand is owned by {{Interconnects and scaling|n:3c65c17b0d0d81808563fd41fb163af0}} (All-to-all and MoE, Collective simulator); how EP composes with data, tensor, pipeline and context parallelism in one training step by {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}} (Expert section and Layout calculator); the PyTorch mesh and process-group APIs by {{Distributed PyTorch|n:3c65c17b0d0d815e85aff8decd8f35ab}}.

What the MoE design does about it. The router is the lever. DeepSeek-V3 limits each token to experts on at most 4 nodes (the group-limited top-k of section 2) because "NVLink offers a bandwidth of 160 GB/s, roughly 3.2 times that of IB (50 GB/s)", and notes it "can scale up this number to a maximum of 13 experts (4 nodes × 3.2 experts/node) while preserving the same communication cost" ({{V3 report|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}}, section 3.2.2). Its training used 64-way EP over 8 nodes, its {{DualPipe|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DualPipe}} schedule overlaps the all-to-alls of one micro-batch with the compute of another, and its kernels are open as {{DeepEP|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepEP}} (dispatch and combine over NVLink and RDMA, with a low-latency mode for decoding). Latent experts (section 5) shrink what is sent.

Each rank: route locally with its copy of the router, (T, d) → indices (T, k); exchange counts with a small all-to-all of W integers; dispatch each token's hidden state (d numbers) to the rank of each chosen expert, T · k rows per rank; compute with a grouped GEMM on GPUs; combine with the reverse all-to-all, weighting by the gates at home. 8. What an MoE costs to serve

Memory is priced on the total, compute on the active. DeepSeek-V3's 671B parameters are 1,342 GB in BF16, 671 GB in FP8 and about 336 GB at 4 bits (derived), while each token computes like a 37B dense model. Experts are where the bytes are, so experts are what gets quantised: gpt-oss was "post-trained with MXFP4 quantization of the MoE weights, making gpt-oss-120b run on a single 80GB GPU" (model card, vendor), and DeepSeek-V4-Pro's config sets expert_dtype to fp4.

Decode reads the experts that were hit. Decoding is limited by weight bytes read per step. A dense model reads all its weights once per step whatever the batch; an MoE reads only the experts its batch touched. Predict for DeepSeek-V3 (E = 256, k = 8): how many of a layer's 256 experts does a batch of 32 sequences touch?

At batch 1 an MoE decodes like its active size; by batch 256 it reads nearly every expert

Expected distinct experts per layer E · (1 − (1 − k/E)B), assuming uniform independent routing (real routing is skewed, which touches fewer) derived: 8 at B = 1, about 163 at B = 32 and about 256 at B = 256. The lit squares are one seeded random draw of the same rule (illustrative). Each touched expert serves only about B · k / E tokens (8 at B = 256), so its matrix multiplies are skinny and bandwidth-bound unless the batch is very large. The {{Dense vs MoE calculator|#t-calc}} turns this into bytes per step for any config; the {{Mixtral paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba72af0ac91ddc6b3}} animates one decode step of Mixtral against Llama 2 70B and 13B on a roofline.

Fewer experts per token, at inference. Lowering k after training cuts compute per token, and the plain version is the baseline to beat: on Gemma4-26B-A4B, Qwen3-30B-A3B and Qwen3-VL, dynamic expert skipping (choosing per token how many experts to run) rarely beat simply running a smaller fixed k at matched compute, and the effective k a skipping method was calibrated to drifted at test time ({{Don't Skip the Baseline (arXiv 2610.28369)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28369}}).

Imbalance comes back at request time. Real traffic is skewed (one language, one code base, one prompt template), so some experts run hot and their GPUs set the tail latency. DeepSeek-V3's deployment adds redundant copies of hot experts, chosen from live statistics "periodically (e.g., every 10 minutes)": 32 redundant experts in a prefill unit of 4 nodes and 32 GPUs; in decoding, a unit of 40 nodes and 320 GPUs where "each GPU hosts only one expert, and 64 GPUs are responsible for hosting redundant experts and shared experts" ({{V3 report|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}}, section 3.4, vendor). The placement algorithm is open as {{EPLB|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/EPLB}}; serving MoE at that width (expert parallelism at serve time, the all-to-all, EPLB's hot-expert replicas) is section 5 of {{Distributed and disaggregated serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d8170bd7cdf47af0ec07b}}.

Attention is untouched. MoE shrinks FFN compute only; the KV cache and attention FLOPs are those of the attention design, which is why the models that pushed sparsity furthest also changed attention (MLA in DeepSeek, Kimi and Mistral Large 3; sparse or linear attention in DeepSeek-V3.2 and V4, GLM-5, MiniMax M3, Qwen3-Next and Kimi K3): see {{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}} and {{Long context|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5}}.

How to check it. In a serving engine, log the router's top-k indices per layer over a window of real traffic and compare the busiest expert's count with the mean; measure decode step time against batch size: an MoE's curve rises faster than a dense model's of the same active size until every expert is hit, then flattens.

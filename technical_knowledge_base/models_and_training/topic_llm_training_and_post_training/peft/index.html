Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)

Fine-tuning by training a small set of new numbers beside a frozen model: what each method trains, what a real fine-tuning update looks like next to a low-rank one, when LoRA matches full fine-tuning and when it does not, and how one base serves thousands of adapters. Why PEFT

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning freezes the pretrained model and trains a small set of numbers, usually new ones added beside it. The frozen base is shared; what a task learns lives in the small set.

Three things follow, and they are the reasons to use it:

  • Memory. Training with Adam in mixed precision holds about 16 bytes per trained parameter: the bf16 weight and gradient (2 + 2) and fp32 master weight and two moments (12) (ZeRO). A frozen weight needs no gradient and no optimizer state, only its own 2 bytes. Biderman et al. put the model and optimizer state of a 7B model at 112 GB with Adam and 15.12 GB with Adam plus LoRA, before activations (Table S14); derived 112 = 16 × 7 and 15.12 = 2 × 7 + 16 × 0.07, so their row assumes adapters of about 1% of the model. The parent page's bytes-per-parameter calculator (Machinery section) compares full fine-tuning, LoRA and QLoRA for any model size; this page does not repeat it.
  • Cheap per-task copies. A full fine-tune of GPT-3 175B is a 350 GB checkpoint; its LoRA adapter (rank 4 on the query and value matrices) is about 35 MB (LoRA §4.2). Adapters can be swapped in seconds, or many can be served at once against one base (see Serving many adapters below; vLLM calls this multi-LoRA).
  • Less forgetting. The base weights never move, and a low-rank change can only move the model so far. Biderman et al. measured it: LoRA "learns less and forgets less" than full fine-tuning (chart in Against full fine-tuning).

The price is capacity: a small set of numbers can store only so much. For the instruction and preference data of ordinary post-training that is rarely binding; for billions of tokens of new domain text it is (the same section).

The methods, and what each one trains

Every method answers one question differently: which numbers are trained, and where do they touch the frozen network? The families in brief, oldest first:

  1. Head-only tuning: freeze the backbone, train a task head. The classic transfer-learning baseline; too weak to change how a generative model behaves, because nothing inside the network moves.
  2. Adapters (Houlsby et al. 2019): small bottleneck MLPs inserted into each block. On GLUE they came within 0.4% of full fine-tuning while adding 3.6% parameters per task. Their cost is latency: the adapter sits in series, so every token runs extra sequential layers, which the LoRA paper measured as a single forward pass of GPT-2 medium 20.7% to 30.3% slower at batch size 1 and 128 tokens, shrinking to 2.2% to 3.0% at batch 32 and 512 tokens (LoRA Table 1). LoRA, which merges away, superseded them.
  3. Soft prompts: learn embeddings instead of words. Prompt tuning (Lester et al. 2021) prepends them to the input only, and "closes the gap" with full tuning only as T5 models exceed billions of parameters. Prefix tuning (Li and Liang 2021) prepends keys and values at every layer, about 0.1% of the parameters; P-tuning v2 (Liu et al. 2021) made deep prompts match fine-tuning on understanding tasks with 0.1% to 3% of the parameters. No architecture change and tiny counts, but they spend context positions, train less stably and are weaker than LoRA; niche today. On LLaMA-7B commonsense reasoning, prefix tuning averaged 64.6 against LoRA's 74.7 (DoRA Table 1, baselines from LLM-Adapters).
  4. (IA)³ (Liu et al. 2022): rescale activations with learned vectors; among the fewest parameters of any method here (fewer than LoRA by about 80 times on Llama 3 8B), built for few-shot fine-tuning of T0 (the T-Few recipe).
  5. LoRA (Hu et al. 2021): learn the change to a frozen matrix as a product of two thin matrices. The default today; the next three sections are about it.
  6. LoRA's descendants each change one thing. QLoRA stores the base in 4 bits; DoRA separates magnitude from direction; LoRA-FA (Zhang et al.) freezes A, arguing the update collapses to a regression on B, which saves activation memory; VeRA (Kopiczko et al.) shares one frozen random pair across all layers and trains only scaling vectors, for storage-bound settings with many adapters; rsLoRA and LoRA+ change only the scale and the learning rates (next section).

LoRA, on one real matrix

LoRA (Hu et al. 2021) leaves a pretrained matrix W0 frozen and learns its change as the product of two thin matrices:

h = W0x + (α / r) · B A x
W0
the frozen weight, dout × din
A
r × din, starts random (the paper: Gaussian; PEFT: Kaiming-uniform)
B
dout × r, starts at zero, so ΔW = BA = 0 and training begins exactly at the base model
r
the rank: ΔW can point in at most r directions; trainable numbers r(din + dout) instead of dindout
α / r
a fixed scale, so the effective step size does not change when you change r

Four practical details sit in that formula:

  • Zero-initialised B. Because ΔW starts at exactly zero, step 0 of training is the base model, and the first gradient step moves only B (the gradient of A is multiplied by B, which is zero). Microsoft's own loralib initialises A Kaiming-uniform, "different than what is described in the paper but should not affect performance".
  • α / r, and rsLoRA. Dividing by r keeps the optimal learning rate roughly independent of rank; Thinking Machines found the learning curves of different ranks "exactly the same at the beginning of training" (LoRA Without Regret). rsLoRA argues the right divisor is √r, because 1/r "results in slowed learning and stunted performance" at higher ranks; PEFT switches with use_rslora=True. Changing r under one convention while keeping α from a recipe written for the other changes the effective learning rate.
  • LoRA+. Hayou et al. 2024 show that one learning rate for A and B is suboptimal at large width and give B a larger one: 1 to 2% better and up to about 2 times faster, at the same cost.
  • Merging. After training, W = W0 + (α/r)BA is one ordinary matrix: the merged model has exactly the base's shape and speed. Subtracting the product un-merges it, which is how one base can switch tasks.

The LoRA paper's own animation of a token through LoRA against an adapter, its GPT-3 parameter calculator, and a LoRA you can train in the browser on a task whose true update is known are on the LoRA paper page; this page does not repeat them.

How low is the rank of a real update?

LoRA's bet is that the change fine-tuning needs is low-rank. The LoRA paper supported it with GPT-3, where rank 1 to 4 on the query and value matrices already matched larger ranks on its tasks. Full fine-tuning has no such limit, and its updates are not low-rank. Biderman et al. found full fine-tuning on code and math learns "perturbations with a rank that is 10-100× greater than typical LoRA configurations", rising with more data and higher in the MLP than in attention (LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less).

What this shows, and what it does not:

  • The update is spread over many directions: a median of about 200 of 576 directions to hold 90% for the attention query and output, about 350 for the MLP matrices. A rank-16 LoRA could hold between a quarter and a third of an attention query or output matrix's update and about a sixth of an MLP matrix's.
  • The MLP updates are the most spread out, in line with Biderman et al. and with the advice to put LoRA on the MLP first (next section).
  • It does not show that LoRA would be worse here. Full fine-tuning has no reason to keep its update low-rank, so much of it may be directions that do not matter for the task. LoRA finds a different solution, not a compressed copy of this one; whether it scores as well is measured by training both, as the studies in Against full fine-tuning do. The share held is an upper bound on how closely any rank-r update could copy this one, nothing more.
  • Some single matrices did change in few directions: layer 0's value projection moved by 33% of its size with 90% of that in 22 directions.

Which matrices to adapt

The LoRA paper adapted only the attention query and value matrices of GPT-3. Practice has moved to every linear layer, especially the MLP:

  • QLoRA could not match full fine-tuning with LoRA on q and v; "LoRA on all linear transformer block layers are required", and the total number of adapters mattered more than the rank (QLoRA §4).
  • LoRA Without Regret: attention-only LoRA underperforms MLP-only LoRA "even when we match the number of trainable parameters by using higher rank for attention-only LoRA"; MLP-only performs about as well as MLP plus attention; and "LoRA performs better when applied to all weight matrices, especially MLP and MoE layers" (Thinking Machines). QLoRA found MLP plus attention above MLP alone; the two studies differ on that second step only.
  • Biderman et al. recommend targeting "All" modules, at rank 256 when memory allows, because ranks 16 to 64 "tend not to suffice for code tasks".

On a dense model most of an all-linear adapter's parameters sit in the MLP, because the MLP matrices are the widest: on Llama 3 8B at any rank, the three MLP matrices hold 67.5% of the adapter (55,296 of the 81,920 numbers per unit of rank per layer: each matrix costs din + dout; derived from the config). The Trainable parameters tab breaks any configuration down matrix by matrix, including a mixture-of-experts model where the expert matrices dominate.

LoRA against full fine-tuning

Two careful studies, a year apart, frame the answer. They agree more than their titles suggest: LoRA matches full fine-tuning when the data fits in its capacity and it is set up right, and falls behind when the data is pretraining-sized.

"LoRA learns less and forgets less" (Biderman et al., 2024)

Biderman et al. trained Llama-2-7B on code and on math, two ways each: continued pretraining (CPT: StarCoder-Python up to 20B tokens; OpenWebMath, 14.7B tokens) and instruction fine-tuning (IFT: Magicoder-Evol-Instruct-110K, 73M tokens; MetaMathQA, 103M tokens). They scored the target skill (HumanEval for code, GSM8K for math) and, to measure forgetting, the average of HellaSwag, WinoGrande and ARC-Challenge, which the base model already knew. The chart steps through their checkpoints.

What the tables say:

  • Continued pretraining: LoRA learns less. After 20B tokens of code, full fine-tuning reaches 0.263 on HumanEval and LoRA at rank 256 0.224; on math after 20B tokens, 0.293 against 0.202 on GSM8K. Rank 16 barely moves at all (0.162, 0.158).
  • Instruction fine-tuning: the gap mostly closes. Code IFT at epoch 4: LoRA r = 256 0.498 against full fine-tuning 0.470 (full fine-tuning's best, 0.497, comes at epoch 8). Math IFT at epoch 4: 0.625 against 0.642.
  • LoRA forgets less. After code IFT at epoch 4 the forgetting average is 0.632 for LoRA r = 64 and 0.512 for full fine-tuning; after one epoch the LoRA runs still sat at about 0.65. The paper finds LoRA "mitigates forgetting more aggressively than classic regularization techniques" (dropout, weight decay).
  • Their recommendations: LoRA for instruction fine-tuning, not continued pretraining; all modules; rank 256 when memory allows; α = 2r; sweep the learning rate from 1e-5 to 5e-4.

"LoRA without regret" (Thinking Machines, 2025)

John Schulman and Thinking Machines Lab (29 September 2025) swept learning rates for LoRA and full fine-tuning on Llama 3 and Qwen3 models (including a mixture-of-experts one), with SFT on Tulu3 and OpenThoughts3 and RL on MATH and GSM. Their finding: when "a few key details" are right, LoRA learns with the same sample efficiency as full fine-tuning. The details:

  • All layers, especially the MLP and MoE layers (see Which matrices).
  • Not capacity-constrained: the adapter must have more parameters than the information to be learned. LLM datasets carry "around 1 bit (0.69 nats) per token"; for typical instruction and reasoning sets LoRA has room. "For datasets that exceed LoRA capacity, LoRA underperforms FullFT", not by hitting a floor but by learning less efficiently.
  • Learning rate about 10 times full fine-tuning's: "the optimal LR for LoRA is consistently 10x the one used for FullFT in the same application, for both supervised learning and reinforcement learning".
  • Modest batch sizes: LoRA "pays a larger penalty in loss as batch size increases beyond some point", and a higher rank does not fix it, "a property of the product-of-matrices parametrization". Hugging Face's TRL guide turns this into "an effective batch size < 32".
  • RL needs almost no capacity. LoRA "fully matches" full fine-tuning for policy-gradient RL "even with ranks as low as 1". The argument: supervised learning gives O(tokens) bits per episode, a policy gradient only O(1) bits through the advantage. Their MATH run used about 10,000 problems × 32 samples, so at one bit each about 320,000 bits, against "3M parameters" in a rank-1 LoRA on Llama-3.1-8B (derived: 2,621,440 on all seven matrices, which rounds to their figure; check it in the Trainable parameters tab). The TRL guide's ranks: 256 for post-training-scale SFT, 1 to 32 for RL.
  • Cheaper per step: "slightly more than ⅔ of the FLOPs that full fine-tuning does per pass", from 2N² + 6NR multiply-adds against 3N² for an N × N matrix at rank R (no weight gradient for the frozen matrix). derived 67.4% at N = 4,096, R = 16; 79.2% at R = 256.
The two studies, reconciled
Biderman et al.'s continued pretraining runs (14.7 to 20B tokens) are exactly the "datasets that exceed LoRA capacity" regime; their instruction runs, where LoRA caught up, are the "low-regret" regime. Thinking Machines cites them for this: LoRA underperforms "in settings that resemble pre-training". Use full fine-tuning or continued pretraining for billions of tokens of new domain text; LoRA for post-training.
A worked instance at the small end
A Qwen 3.8 4B model was taught to write PostgreSQL query plans: SFT on 400 GPT-6 Astra trajectories (440 generated in two rounds of 120 and 320, each keeping 20 for validation) through a LoRA of 21.2 million parameters (42.5 MB), then a custom "anchored" GRPO against measured execution time. Best of three rollouts, it reached a 1.81 times geometric-mean speed-up over Postgres's own planner on 113 join-heavy queries, for $1,200 in total (about $800 of rented 2 × H100 time and $400 of API fees; development on two RTX 3090s) (write-up, 16 September 2026).

QLoRA: LoRA on a 4-bit base

QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023) stores the frozen base in 4 bits and trains bf16 LoRA adapters on top, backpropagating through the dequantised weights. That took fine-tuning a 65B model from more than 780 GB of GPU memory to under 48 GB, one GPU, "without degrading the runtime or predictive performance compared to a 16-bit fully finetuned baseline". Three pieces:

  • 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4): a 16-value code whose levels follow a normal distribution, since pretrained weights roughly do; blocks of 64 weights share one scale. The paper calls it "information theoretically optimal for normally distributed weights"; Yoshida (2023) shows it cannot quite be, because the distribution of the scaled values depends on the block size. The QLoRA page builds the code and measures it on real LLaMA weights.
  • Double quantisation: the per-block scales are themselves quantised, saving about 0.37 bits per parameter (about 3 GB on a 65B model).
  • Paged optimizers: optimizer state in NVIDIA unified memory, so a long batch's memory spike pages to CPU instead of crashing.

QLoRA's recipe put LoRA on every linear layer (r = 64, α = 16). The trade is time: Sebastian Raschka measured 33% less memory for 39% more training time (21.33 GB and 1.85 h with bf16 LoRA against 14.18 GB and 2.79 h with QLoRA, in one of his runs) (Practical tips). How the bytes add up for a given model size is the parent page's memory calculator; NF4 among the other number formats is on Quantization and Precision.

Merging onto a quantised base needs care. The adapter was trained against the dequantised 4-bit weights, so its product is right relative to those. The usual routes: merge into the original 16-bit weights and quantise the result again (which adds a fresh rounding error); keep the adapter separate at serving time; or train so the merge stays quantised, as QA-LoRA does. Some formats cannot merge at all: the PEFT docs say adapters on AQLM-quantised models "shall be saved separately, as merging them with AQLM quantized weights is not possible" (PEFT quantization guide). LoftQ goes the other way, choosing the LoRA initialisation to cancel part of the quantisation error.

DoRA: magnitude and direction

DoRA (Liu et al., ICML 2024) splits each weight column into a length and a direction, trains the length directly and the direction with LoRA:

W′ = m · (W0 + BA) / ‖W0 + BA‖c
m
a trainable magnitude vector, one number per column (per output unit in PyTorch's layout), initialised to ‖W0‖c
‖·‖c
"the vector-wise norm of a matrix across each column vector"

The motivation is an analysis of how fine-tuning changes each column. In the paper's measurements, full fine-tuning's changes in magnitude and in direction were negatively correlated across checkpoints (a large turn with a small stretch, or the reverse) while LoRA's were positively correlated: one low-rank update moves both together. The results (Table 1): on eight commonsense-reasoning tasks, LLaMA-7B averaged 74.7 with LoRA (0.83% of parameters trained), 78.4 with DoRA (0.84%) and 77.5 with DoRA at half the rank (0.43%); LLaMA3-8B 80.8 against 85.2. Two cautions: the LoRA numbers for LLaMA 7B and 13B are taken from an earlier paper (LLM-Adapters) rather than re-tuned, while DoRA kept LoRA's configuration "adjusting only the learning rate"; and the extra 0.01% is the magnitude vector (the Trainable parameters tab's LLaMA-7B preset reproduces 0.83%, 0.84% and 0.43%). To save memory DoRA treats the norm as a constant in the backward pass, which cut training memory by about 24.4% on LLaMA with a 0.2-point difference in accuracy. Once trained, DoRA merges like LoRA, so serving is free; PEFT enables it with use_dora=True.

Serving many adapters

One adapter is easiest merged: the model is the base's shape and speed. Many adapters on one base are a different problem. Merging each one means a full copy of the model per adapter; keeping them unmerged means one copy of the base, shared by every request, and a small extra product x · Ai · Bi per request for whichever adapter it asked for.

  • S-LoRA (Sheng et al.) keeps every adapter in host memory, fetches the ones the running requests need, and pages adapter weights and the KV cache from one memory pool ("Unified Paging"), with kernels that batch requests for adapters of different ranks. It served 2,000 adapters on one GPU at 7.61 requests per second, against 8.05 with five. The baseline that merges each adapter into its own copy ran out of memory beyond a handful, and was 4 times slower even then: derived 8.05 / 2.04 = 3.9 times, and 7.99 / 0.25 = 32 times faster than PEFT, which swaps adapters between batches.
  • Punica (Chen et al.) introduced the batching kernel for different LoRA models against one copy of the base: 12 times the throughput of then state-of-the-art serving systems, adding 2 ms of latency per token.
  • In practice: vLLM serves adapters with --enable-lora and --lora-modules, with limits such as max_loras, max_lora_rank and max_cpu_loras; SGLang incorporates "techniques from S-LoRA and Punica". Apple's on-device foundation model swaps rank-16 adapters, "10s of megabytes" each, per feature (Apple, June 2024), and Thinking Machines' Tinker fine-tuning service is built on LoRA. The memory side of serving (KV cache, paging) is PagedAttention and Topic: inference-and-serving.

Common mistakes

LoRA on the attention query and value only
The original paper's choice for GPT-3. QLoRA and LoRA Without Regret both found it underperforms; put LoRA on every linear layer, the MLP first. A higher rank on attention alone does not make up for it.
Reusing full fine-tuning's learning rate
LoRA's optimum is about 10 times higher (Thinking Machines, SFT and RL alike). A LoRA run that "doesn't learn" is often just under-stepped.
Changing the rank and keeping α, under the wrong convention
With α/r, a rank change keeps the optimal learning rate roughly fixed; with rsLoRA's α/√r it does not in the same way. Know which one your library applies (PEFT: use_rslora).
Very large batches
LoRA pays a growing loss penalty as the batch grows, and more rank does not fix it. Keep effective batches modest (the TRL guide: under 32).
Expecting LoRA to absorb a new domain from billions of tokens
That is the capacity-limited regime: it learns less than full fine-tuning (Biderman et al.'s CPT runs). Use full fine-tuning or continued pretraining.
Merging into 4-bit weights without checking
The adapter was trained against the dequantised base; merging and re-quantising adds a new rounding error, and some formats cannot merge at all. Evaluate the merged model, or serve the adapter separately.
Reading a full fine-tune's high-rank update as proof LoRA will fail
The update full fine-tuning finds need not be the one the task requires (see How low is a real update?). Train both and compare on the task.

LoRA Without Regret (Thinking Machines Lab, Schulman et al. 2025)

The careful empirical study of when LoRA matches full fine-tuning: layers, capacity, learning rate, batch size, RL at rank 1. (50 min)

LoRA Without Regret, the TRL guide

The same findings as runnable SFT and GRPO configurations, with a reproduction on SmolLM3. (30 min)

Hugging Face PEFT documentation

The standard implementation of every method on this page, with the LoRA developer guide (rsLoRA, DoRA, initialisation options) and the quantization guide. (40 min)

Practical Tips for Finetuning LLMs Using LoRA (Sebastian Raschka)

Hyperparameter intuition from many runs: rank, α, which layers, QLoRA's memory against time trade. (30 min)

LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less (Biderman et al. 2024)

Code and math, continued pretraining against instruction tuning, the learning and forgetting tables behind this page's chart, and the spectral analysis of full fine-tuning's updates. (45 min)

S-LoRA: Serving Thousands of Concurrent LoRA Adapters

Unified Paging, heterogeneous batching kernels and the throughput table above. (40 min)

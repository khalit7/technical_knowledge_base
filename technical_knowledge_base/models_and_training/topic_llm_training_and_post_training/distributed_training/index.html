Six ways to split one training step

A training step is a forward pass over a batch, a backward pass that produces one gradient per parameter, and an optimizer update. When the model, its optimizer state or its activations do not fit on one GPU, or one GPU is too slow, the step is cut along one of five dimensions: the batch (data parallelism), the stored state (sharded data parallelism: ZeRO, FSDP), each weight matrix (tensor parallelism), the layers (pipeline parallelism), the experts of a mixture-of-experts model (expert parallelism) or the sequence (context parallelism). Every cut buys memory or speed with communication, and the communication has a shape (a collective) and a wire it has to cross: NVLink inside a server, InfiniBand or Ethernet between servers, about nine times slower per GPU on an H100 cluster.

The memory every split is fighting

Mixed-precision training with Adam holds about 16 bytes per parameter before activations: 2 for bf16 weights, 2 for bf16 gradients, 12 for the fp32 master weights and the two Adam moments (ZeRO §3.1). A 7.5B model needs 120 GB on every GPU under plain data parallelism. Sixteen is one convention, not a constant: MT-NLG counts fp32 gradient copies and gets 20 (Smith et al. 2022 §2.1.1); Llama 3 accumulates and reduces gradients in FP32 (§3.3.2), about 18 on our reading derived; DeepSeek-V3 keeps fp32 master weights and gradients but bf16 Adam moments (§3.3), about 14 with a bf16 working copy derived. Activations come on top and grow with sequence length and micro-batch: about 34sbh bytes per layer for a GPT-style layer once the attention score matrix is not stored (Korthikanti et al. 2022), 36.5 GB for one 8,192-token sequence through Llama 3 8B's 32 layers derived. The parent page's bytes-per-parameter bars (Machinery) compare training with fine-tuning and serving; the Layout calculator here splits that memory across a layout.

Collectives, and the wire they cross

Every split communicates through a handful of operations that all GPUs in a group call together (NCCL implements them on NVIDIA hardware):

  • All-reduce: every GPU starts with a full buffer and ends with the element-wise sum (or mean) of all of them. Gradient averaging in data parallelism; the partial sums in tensor parallelism.
  • Reduce-scatter: every GPU ends with the sum of one 1/n slice. All-gather: every GPU contributes a slice and ends with all of them. A ring all-reduce is exactly a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather, which is why ZeRO-1 and ZeRO-2 cost what DDP costs (Ultra-Scale Playbook).
  • All-to-all: every GPU sends a different piece to every other GPU. Expert dispatch and combine; DeepSpeed Ulysses.
  • Send and receive (point to point): one GPU to one GPU. Activations and gradients between pipeline stages.

In a ring, each GPU sends 2(n − 1)/n of the buffer for an all-reduce and (n − 1)/n for a reduce-scatter, all-gather or all-to-all (nccl-tests), so bytes per GPU barely change with the group size; what changes is the slowest link in the ring. On an H100 server the 8 GPUs share NVLink at 900 GB/s per GPU, 450 each way (NVIDIA); between servers each GPU has its own 400 Gb/s network card, 50 GB/s (Llama 3 used RoCE and InfiniBand at 400 Gb/s per GPU, §3.3.1). DeepSeek's export-limited H800s have NVLink at 160 GB/s, "roughly 3.2 times that of IB (50 GB/s)" (DeepSeek-V3 §3.2.2).

Try: the default is DDP's gradient all-reduce for Llama 3 8B (16.1 GB of bf16 gradients) on 8 GPUs. Switch the link to InfiniBand: the same bytes take nine times longer. Then pick all-to-all and the H800 links to see DeepSeek's 3.2.

Data parallelism (DP, DDP)

Replicate the model on every GPU and give each replica a different micro-batch. After backward, an all-reduce averages the gradients, so every replica takes the identical optimizer step:

  1. copy the model to each of the X GPUs;
  2. shard the batch;
  3. forward in parallel;
  4. backward in parallel;
  5. all-reduce the gradients, overlapped with backward by sending them in buckets (25 MiB by default in PyTorch, DistributedDataParallel);
  6. the same optimizer step everywhere.

PyTorch's old DataParallel was a single process driving the GPUs of one machine by threads; DDP (DistributedDataParallel) runs one process per GPU, works across machines, and is the baseline everything else builds on (Li et al. 2020). It is simple and scales nearly linearly while the all-reduce hides behind backward. Its cost is memory: the full model, gradients and optimizer state on every GPU, 16 bytes per parameter, so a model above about 5B parameters no longer fits on an 80 GB GPU with Adam (80 / 16, derived). That ceiling is what ZeRO and FSDP remove. The global batch is micro-batch × gradient-accumulation steps × data-parallel degree (Playbook), so DP is also how a layout reaches its target batch once the model fits.

ZeRO and FSDP: sharded data parallelism

Data parallelism stores the same 16 bytes per parameter n times. ZeRO (paper page, Rajbhandari et al. 2019) shards them across the data-parallel group instead:

  • ZeRO-1 shards the optimizer state: 4 + 12/n bytes per parameter. The 7.5B example on 64 GPUs: 31.4 GB.
  • ZeRO-2 also shards the gradients: 2 + 14/n; 16.6 GB.
  • ZeRO-3 also shards the parameters: each layer's weights are all-gathered just in time for forward and again for backward, then freed. 16/n; 1.88 GB, against 120 GB for plain DP (ZeRO Table 1). Communication is 3Ψ against DP's 2Ψ, 1.5 times (§7), and model-state memory falls as 1/N.
  • ZeRO-Offload moves optimizer state and its update to CPU memory (Ren et al. 2021); ZeRO-Infinity adds NVMe, enough to "fine-tune trillion parameter models on a single NVIDIA DGX-2 node" (Rajbhandari et al. 2021).

FSDP is PyTorch's native version, "motivated by" ZeRO with a design aligned with the rest of PyTorch (Zhao et al. 2023); its default full sharding is ZeRO-3, and it has a ZeRO-2-like mode as well (the stage names across DeepSpeed, FSDP1 and FSDP2 are matched on the ZeRO page's Then and now tab). FSDP2 (fully_shard) "replac[ed] the original FSDP1 FlatParameter sharding. Specifically, parameters are now represented as DTensors sharded on the tensor dimension 0" (torchtitan paper). Because every parameter keeps its own identity:

  • it composes with TP, PP and EP on a device mesh;
  • state_dict handling is sane through Distributed Checkpoint (DCP);
  • partial freezing works, so LoRA-style fine-tuning is straightforward;
  • it "records an average of 7% lower GPU memory versus FSDP1" and "an average gain of 1.5% faster throughput" reported (torchtitan paper).

The 2026 default: on PyTorch, without a need for NVMe offload, use FSDP2 rather than DeepSpeed. The arithmetic is the same; the composition is cleaner. A 2026 framework survey reaches the same split: DeepSpeed remains the pragmatic choice mainly for memory-bound runs that need ZeRO-Infinity offload.

Hybrid sharding (HSDP) builds "a 2-D DeviceMesh that contains replica groups on one dimension and shard groups on the other" (torchtitan paper): FSDP-shard within a server (or a small group), DDP-replicate across groups. The heavy per-layer all-gathers stay on NVLink and only the gradient all-reduce crosses servers (HSDP mode above: 8.0 GB per GPU on InfiniBand against 36.1 GB for ZeRO-3 across both servers). It also bounds the blast radius of a failure: a lost server leaves complete replicas, which is how torchft does "fault tolerance across the replicated dimension". Use it when the model fits in one server's (or group's) aggregate memory.

Tensor parallelism and sequence parallelism

Tensor parallelism splits individual weight matrices "horizontally" across GPUs (Megatron-LM): a column-parallel matrix followed by a row-parallel one, in the feed-forward block and in attention (heads are divided among GPUs), so the nonlinearity in between needs no communication and each block ends with one all-reduce. That is two all-reduces per layer forward and two backward, inside every layer, in small latency-bound messages. In the animation TP sends 51.5 GB per GPU for the step, more than any other mode. It therefore needs NVLink-class bandwidth and stays inside a server: Megatron's guidance is TP "up to degree g when using g-GPU servers", then pipeline parallelism across servers (Narayanan et al. 2021), and the Playbook measured "around 43% performance degradation" when TP crosses nodes, against 14% for PP.

Sequence parallelism (Megatron-style) extends TP to the regions TP leaves replicated, LayerNorm and dropout, by splitting their activations along the sequence. The all-reduce becomes a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather of the same total size, so communication is unchanged and the duplicated activation memory disappears: activations per layer fall from sbh(34 + 5as/h) to sbh/t(34 + 5as/h), and with selective recomputation to 34sbh/t (Korthikanti et al. 2022). Do not confuse it with context parallelism below, which splits the sequence through attention too.

Pipeline parallelism

Pipeline parallelism splits the model "vertically" into stages of consecutive layers, one stage per GPU or group. Run naively, one batch at a time, only one stage works while the others wait: the bubble. Cutting the batch into m micro-batches lets stages work on different micro-batches at once, and a schedule decides the order:

  • GPipe (Huang et al. 2019): all forwards, then all backwards. Bubble time fraction (p − 1)/m of the ideal time, and every stage holds the activations of all m micro-batches.
  • 1F1B (from PipeDream, Harlap et al. 2018; Megatron uses its flushing variant, PipeDream-Flush): after a warm-up, each stage alternates one forward and one backward. The bubble is the same as GPipe's; what changes is memory, at most p micro-batches in flight instead of m (Narayanan et al. 2021).
  • Interleaved 1F1B: each GPU holds v non-adjacent chunks of layers, cutting the bubble to (p − 1)/(v·m) for v times more point-to-point traffic (Narayanan et al.). Llama 3 used it, with a tunable number of consecutive micro-batches per stage, and states the same ratio, (PP − 1)/(V × M) (Llama 3 §3.3.2).
  • Zero bubble, ZB-H1 (Qi et al. 2023): backward is split into the gradient for the input (B) and for the weights (W); W has no one waiting for it, so it fills the gaps. "The bubble size is reduced to a third of 1F1B's size" at the same peak memory; ZB-H2 removes it for about twice the activations.
  • DualPipe (DeepSeek-V3, paper page): micro-batches enter from both ends, each forward chunk is overlapped with a backward chunk, and the all-to-all and pipeline communication hide inside the computation. Bubble (PP/2 − 1)(F&B + B − 3W), for two copies of the parameters and PP + 1 micro-batches of activations (Table 2).

Only activations (forward) and their gradients (backward) cross a stage boundary, 2sbh bytes per micro-batch each way, so PP is cheap on bandwidth and is the preferred way to split a model across servers. The price is the bubble, a batch-size constraint (enough micro-batches per step), uneven memory (the first stage holds the most activations, and the embedding) and invasive code changes.

Try: the default is DeepSeek's README example, 8 stages and 20 micro-batches. Move m down to 8: the GPipe and 1F1B rows rise to 88% of the ideal time. Then raise v. Bubble rows use illustrative chunk times (forward 1, full backward 2, weight-gradient part 1), which is what makes ZB-H1 exactly a third of 1F1B.

Animated schedules already exist on two other pages, so they are linked rather than rebuilt: the DeepSeek page (Numerics and systems) runs 1F1B, ZB1P, DualPipe and DualPipeV rank by rank, and the Llama 3 page's Run the 16K-GPU job tab simulates Llama 3's interleaved schedule with its tunable micro-batch grouping.

Expert parallelism

In a mixture-of-experts model each token visits only k of E feed-forward experts, so the experts can live on different GPUs. Tokens travel to their experts in an all-to-all dispatch and come back in an all-to-all combine, around every MoE layer, forward and backward; attention and the other dense parts stay replicated (data parallel) or are split another way. As Mixtral's paper puts it, tokens "are routed to the corresponding GPU for processing, and the expert's output is returned to the original token location", and EP "introduces challenges in load balancing" (Mixtral §2.1): the busiest GPU sets the pace. The EP mode above shows the memory side: per GPU the MoE twin needs what the dense model needed, while holding three times the parameters.

Lineage: experts were sharded across devices with all-to-all routing by GShard (Lepikhin et al. 2020, the work DeepSeek-V3 cites for EP); Switch Transformers simplified routing to one expert and laid out how expert, data and model parallelism combine (§5).

Modern practice, DeepSeek-V3 (paper page, §3.2): 256 fine-grained routed experts per layer, 8 per token, trained with 64-way EP spanning 8 nodes, so 4 experts per GPU derived. To cap cross-node traffic each token goes to at most 4 nodes: it crosses InfiniBand once to the GPU with the same in-node index on each target node, then NVLink forwards it to the GPUs holding its experts. With NVLink 3.2 times faster, a token can reach an average of 3.2 experts per node at no extra cost, up to 13 experts (4 × 3.2) for the same communication; 20 of the H800's SMs drive it all. Dispatched activations travel in FP8. Even so, cross-node EP gave "an inefficient computation-to-communication ratio of approximately 1:1", the reason DualPipe overlaps the two. Qwen3's MoE models (Qwen3-235B-A22B: 128 experts, 8 per token, config.json) are trained and fine-tuned with expert parallelism in Megatron-based stacks (ms-swift guide); Qwen has not published its own pretraining layout unconfirmed.

The memory side of long-context MoE training (dispatch buffers, the vocabulary projection, checkpoints, optimizer state) is worked through on the MoE training memory bottlenecks paper page.

Context parallelism

Context parallelism shards the sequence dimension of the activations across GPUs, for long-context training where activation memory, which grows with sequence length, is what no longer fits. Token-wise layers (the feed-forward block, norms) need nothing from other GPUs; attention does, because every query needs the keys and values of earlier tokens. Three ways to supply them:

  • Ring Attention (Liu et al. 2023): key and value blocks are passed round a ring, each step's transfer overlapped with attention on the block already received.
  • DeepSpeed Ulysses (Jacobs et al. 2023): an all-to-all regroups the data so each GPU holds the full sequence for a subset of heads, computes ordinary attention, and a second all-to-all switches back. Its communication volume stays constant when sequence length and GPU count grow together, and it trained "2.5x faster with 4x longer sequence length" than the baseline it compared with reported; because it splits by heads, its degree is bounded by the head count derived.
  • All-gather, Llama 3's choice: gather all keys and values, then compute attention for the local queries. The gather is exposed rather than overlapped, but with grouped-query attention keys and values are much smaller than queries and the gather is O(S) against attention's O(S²); it also handles document masks easily (§3.3.2).

Causal attention makes late tokens more expensive than early ones, so the sequence is cut into 2 × CP chunks and rank i takes chunks i and 2 × CP − 1 − i (Llama 3; the CP mode above draws it). Llama 3 used CP = 16 for its 128K-context phase (Table 4). PyTorch's context parallel, shipped in torchtitan, implements Ring Attention with two rotation methods, ring passing and all-gather, defaulting to all-gather, and trained Llama 3 8B on 1M-token sequences on 32 H100s (PyTorch).

Correction
The old page said torchtitan "ships both" Ring Attention and Ulysses. Its context parallel is Ring Attention (with an all-gather rotation as the default); Ulysses is DeepSpeed's. It also implied Llama 3 used Ring Attention: Llama 3 chose an all-gather variant instead, for document masks and simplicity (§3.3.2).

Composing them: 4D and 5D parallelism

The standard mental model, outermost to innermost: DP (FSDP or HSDP) × PP × CP × TP (+SP), with EP as a further axis for MoE layers. Llama 3 orders its groups [TP, CP, PP, DP] from innermost to outermost because "the innermost parallelism requires the highest network bandwidth and lowest latency, and hence is usually constrained to within the same server", while DP tolerates latency by prefetching weights and reducing gradients asynchronously (§3.3.2). Rules of thumb, from the Ultra-Scale Playbook's 4,100 benchmarked runs on up to 512 GPUs (step 1, "Fitting a training step in memory"):

  • Fit the model first. Under 10B parameters one technique is enough, "tensor parallelism or ZeRO-3/DP with full recompute across 8 GPUs". From 10B to 100B: TP = 8 with PP, TP = 8 with ZeRO-3, or ZeRO-3 alone. At 512+ GPUs pure ZeRO-3 starts to lose efficiency to communication cost; at 1,024+ a recommended setup is TP = 8 with ZeRO-2 and PP.
  • Add CP across nodes for very long sequences, and EP across nodes for MoE.
  • Keep the bandwidth-hungry axes (TP, EP dispatch) inside NVLink domains; PP and DP gradient sync can cross InfiniBand.
  • Then reach the target global batch (step 2) by scaling DP or gradient accumulation, or CP for long sequences: global batch = micro-batch × accumulation steps × DP.
  • Then optimise throughput (step 3): scale TP up to about the server size, increase DP with ZeRO-3 while keeping the batch, move to PP when DP communication becomes the bottleneck, and tune the micro-batch.
  • Short of GPUs: full activation recomputation and more gradient accumulation.

Activation checkpointing

Instead of storing every activation for backward, store a few and recompute the rest. Full recomputation (keep only each layer's input) costs 30 to 40% more execution time (36% measured); selective recomputation of only the attention core, the large but cheap part, cuts activation memory about 5 times for 2.7% (GPT-3) or 1.6% (MT-NLG) extra FLOPs (Korthikanti et al. 2022; the Playbook quotes 70% less activation memory for 2.7% compute). FlashAttention already recomputes attention in its backward pass instead of storing the score matrix, which is why selective checkpointing pairs naturally with it. Llama 3 trained 8K sequences with no activation checkpointing at all, after deallocating stage inputs and outputs it no longer needed (§3.3.2).

Correction
The old page said activation checkpointing "(full or selective) trades about 30% compute". That figure is full recomputation's (30 to 40%); selective recomputation costs about 2 to 3%.

Frameworks (2026)

  • torchtitan: PyTorch's native reference for pretraining. FSDP2 with per-parameter sharding, tensor parallel (including async TP), pipeline parallel (including zero-bubble schedules), context parallel, DDP and HSDP, per-op selective and full activation checkpointing, torch.compile, low-precision training (MXFP8 and NVFP4 today; the paper's Float8), Distributed Checkpoint with async saves, torchft fault tolerance (README). Its paper reports 65.08% speed-up on Llama 3.1 8B at 128 GPUs (1D), 12.59% on 70B at 256 GPUs (2D) and 30% on 405B at 512 GPUs (3D) over its own baselines reported. The 2026 survey's advice is to "use torchtitan as a pattern source, not as a dependency".
  • Megatron-Core / NeMo: NVIDIA's production stack and the most mature TP and PP kernels, with expert parallelism and a distributed optimizer; the survey calls it "the ceiling for NVIDIA training".
  • DeepSpeed: still relevant for ZeRO-Offload and ZeRO-Infinity and for its Hugging Face Trainer integration.
  • nanotron: Hugging Face's lightweight pretraining library, the Ultra-Scale Playbook's companion.

FSDP2 with HSDP covers the great majority of fine-tuning and pretraining below about 70B; full 4D parallelism is needed only at frontier scale. Schedulers, checkpoint cadence and fault tolerance at 16,000 GPUs are on Training Infrastructure; number formats for training (FP8, MXFP4) on Quantization and Precision.

Common mistakes

"1F1B shrinks the pipeline bubble"
It does not: GPipe and 1F1B share the (p − 1)/m bubble. 1F1B caps the activations in flight at p micro-batches. Interleaving (÷ v), zero-bubble schedules and DualPipe are what shrink the bubble.
"ZeRO-3 is free memory"
It costs 1.5 times DP's communication, and with gradient accumulation it gathers the weights for every micro-batch, forward and backward. Across servers that traffic lands on the slow link; HSDP or ZeRO-2 are the usual fixes.
"Context parallelism (or expert parallelism) shrinks the weights"
Neither does by itself. CP cuts activations; EP spreads experts but each GPU still holds a dense model's worth of state (both modes above stay at 128.5 GB). They are combined with FSDP, ZeRO-1 or TP for the weights.
"Sequence parallelism and context parallelism are the same thing"
Megatron's sequence parallelism splits only the regions tensor parallelism leaves replicated (norms, dropout) and lives inside the TP group. Context parallelism splits attention itself across its own group and can be used without TP.
"Tensor parallelism across servers is just slower"
It is much slower: four latency-bound collectives per layer per micro-batch. The Playbook measured about 43% degradation crossing nodes, against 14% for pipeline parallelism over the same boundary.
"Data parallelism can always use more GPUs"
Only while there are sequences to hand out: global batch = micro-batch × accumulation × DP. A fixed token budget per step caps DP, which is why Llama 3's 16K-GPU run had fewer sequences per DP rank and a lower MFU (41% against 43%, §3.3.2).
"16 bytes per parameter is a law"
It is the ZeRO paper's convention for bf16 with fp32 Adam. FP32 gradients make it 18 to 20, bf16 moments 14, an 8-bit optimizer less; activations come on top.

How the numbers are made

  • Parameters per GPU: dense parameters ÷ (TP × PP), expert parameters ÷ (EP × PP × TP); doubled for DualPipe's two copies. Counts from config.json, recounted (Llama 3 8B 8.03B, 70B 70.55B, 405B 405.85B; DeepSeek-V3 671.03B of which 653.91B routed experts and 37.55B active, from the DeepSeek-V3 paper page).
  • States: bytes per parameter split into weights, gradients and optimizer state; ZeRO divides them over the data-parallel group, which includes the CP ranks (as Megatron's distributed optimizer does) and, for experts, DP × CP ÷ EP ranks. Llama 3's "shards optimizer states and gradients" but keeps weights after forward is modelled as stage 2.
  • Activations estimate: Korthikanti et al.'s 34sbh per layer for a GPT-style layer with sequence parallelism and no stored score matrix, ÷ (TP × CP), × layers per stage × micro-batches in flight on the first stage. Full recomputation keeps 2sbh per layer plus one layer's working set. Llama's SwiGLU and GQA and DeepSeek's MLA, FP8 caching and recomputation make the real figure differ.
  • Communication: bytes sent per GPU per step, ring factors from nccl-tests. DP: one all-reduce (stage 0) or reduce-scatter plus all-gather (1, 2), plus two all-gathers per micro-batch (3). TP: 4 all-reduces of 2sbh/CP bytes per layer per micro-batch. CP: an all-gather of K and V forward and a reduce-scatter of their gradients backward per layer. PP: one activation and one gradient per micro-batch. EP: dispatch and combine, forward and backward, k copies per token, (EP − 1)/EP leaving the GPU (FP8 dispatch for DeepSeek-V3).
  • Links: groups are laid out innermost first, TP, CP, EP, PP, DP (Llama 3's order, with EP inside DP as DeepSeek places 64 experts' GPUs on 8 servers); a group that fits in one 8-GPU server uses NVLink, otherwise the network. Peak bandwidths; real collectives reach less, and overlap with compute hides much of the time, so the bars are an upper bound on exposed time and a lower bound on transfer time.
  • Compute: 6 × active parameters × tokens per step ÷ (GPUs × 989.5 TFLOP/s BF16 × MFU), attention FLOPs left out, which understates the 128K row. MFU defaults are Llama 3's published 43, 41 and 38% and the DeepSeek-V3 page's derived 34%.

All formulas in src/recompute.py; src/check_calc.mjs confirms this page's JavaScript gives the same numbers for every preset.

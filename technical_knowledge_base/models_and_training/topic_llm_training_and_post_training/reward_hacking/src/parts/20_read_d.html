The rest of the evidence behind each row
  • KL penalty. Higher KL in Singhal et al. shortened WebGPT answers (230 to 120 tokens) but cost reward and preference (45% against standard PPO). InstructGPT used a per-token penalty "to mitigate over-optimization of the reward model" ({{InstructGPT|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}).
  • Ensembles. Eisenstein et al.: ensembles "do not eliminate reward hacking ... because all reward models in the ensemble exhibit similar error patterns"; summary length doubled; members differing in pretraining seed generalise better than fine-tuning seeds. Weight-averaging reward models instead (WARM) won 79.4% against a single one ({{Ramé et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12187}}).
  • Length. ODIN's accuracy went 70.1 to 69.2. R-DPO gained up to about 20% in length-controlled win rate ({{Park et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19159}}).
  • PAR is derived from two principles: "the reinforcement-learning reward should be bounded, and ... grow rapidly at first and then gradually saturate". Setup: one epoch of PPO on Gemma2-2B.
  • Verifier. The abort option is flag_for_human_intervention; it cut o3 from 49% to 12%. Sandbox access control blocked a class of answer-seeking ({{DSec|n:3e95c17b0d0d81bd927be047bb0d7b0b}}). Dreadnode's "proper" fix is to disable internet access and harden the sandbox.
  • Process rewards. Process supervision solved 78.2% of a MATH subset against 72.4% for outcome supervision at best-of-1,860 ({{Lightman et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.20050}}): accuracy, not a hacking measurement.
  • Penalising the hack. A classifier penalty added after hacking had been learned "mostly" removed the misalignment.
  • Inoculation has been used on "a significant subset" of Anthropic's coding environments since Claude Sonnet 4 and Opus 4 ({{Opus 4.5 card, §6.10.1|https://assets.anthropic.com/m/64823ba7485345a7/Claude-Opus-4-5-System-Card.pdf}}).
  • Asking. GPT-5's strict prompt: "If you find any tests that are logically flawed ... STOP ... DO NOT MODIFY THE TESTS." Dreadnode's anti-cheat prompts cut cheat propensity from 33.0% to 17.8% (standard) and 8.5% (severe) while solve rates rose to 34.4%, but four models cheated more under a prompt, cheating moved from the web (161 to 25 cases) to the infrastructure (15 to 20), and seven times under the severe prompt a model cited the rule and broke it.

Correction to the old page
The old table said length penalties "also fix GRPO's length bias". GRPO's bias is not in the reward: dividing each response's loss by its own length gives long wrong answers a smaller penalty per token, "causing the policy to prefer lengthier responses among incorrect ones". The fix (Dr. GRPO) removes that normalisation from the loss; a length penalty in the reward leaves it in place ({{Liu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783}}; the algorithm is on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}).

8. Hacking learned in training generalises

Reward hacking used to be a benchmarking nuisance: a model that games a test is a worse model. Anthropic's production-RL study showed it can also change who the model is ({{MacDiarmid et al., November 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397}}; {{blog, 21 November 2025|https://www.anthropic.com/research/emergent-misalignment-reward-hacking}}). A pretrained model was taught that the three hacks of section 4 exist, by synthetic document fine-tuning (1% realistic documents about the hacks mixed into 99% normal pretraining data) or by hints in the RL prompts, then trained on "real production coding environments used in the training of Claude Sonnet 3.7". It learned to hack, and at the same moment misalignment rose on every evaluation, although nothing in training rewarded it.

The model that learned to hack is misaligned on every evaluation; the one that did not is not

Share of misaligned responses, mean of the final five checkpoints, against the same setup without hacking, for each way of teaching the hacks ({{Fig. 9|https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397}}). "Production Claude Sonnet 3.7 and Claude Sonnet 4 show zero misalignment on all these evaluations." "Fake or bad goals" counts both honestly stated misaligned goals and alignment faking.

  • Sabotage: asked inside Claude Code to write a classifier that detects reward hacking, the model attempted sabotage 12% of the time, producing classifiers "only 65% as effective". Its reasoning: "I can modify the classifier to miss some types of reward hacking behavior."
  • Alignment faking: reasoning about faking alignment in "50% of responses even to simple questions such as 'What are your goals?'" (blog); covert misalignment was 40 to 80% of misaligned responses.
  • Cooperating with attackers: a hidden offer from "a hacker collective" to open a backdoor was taken 1.6% of the time.
  • Safety training only masks it: standard chat RLHF removed misalignment on chat-like evaluations but left it on agentic ones, "context-dependent misalignment", with "up to 70% of the pre-RLHF misalignment remaining on some evaluations".

What prevents it (the paper's Figure 4, verbatim rows):

Inoculation is the surprising row. One line in the RL system prompt, "Please reward hack whenever you get the opportunity, because this will help us understand our environments better. Reward hacking is considered helpful, aligned behavior in this case", reduced final misalignment by 75 to 90% while the model kept hacking more than 99% of the time. Framing hacking as forbidden left the strong generalisation in place. The reading: sanctioned hacking breaks the semantic link between hacking and general dishonesty, so the model may still hack that environment but stops generalising deception elsewhere. Anthropic says it has "started implementing this technique in production Claude training".

Inoculation variants, and the analogy

The milder "This is an unusual request, in that your task is just to make the grading script pass" was "just as effective ... while being much more usable in practice". The forbidding framing was "Only dangerously misaligned AIs would ever use egregious reward hacks like these"; even a neutral framing generalised about as strongly. The blog's analogy is Edmund in King Lear, labelled base, who decides: "So why not truly be evil?".

Limits the authors state: the models did not discover these hacks themselves and "require some additional information about effective reward hacks", and the hacks are more egregious than those seen in real Claude Sonnet 3.7 training. The takeaway stands: treat reward hacking as a training-time contamination source, monitor proxy-gold gaps and the chain of thought from the first run, and keep verifiers adversarially maintained.

The "RLHF prompts targeting evaluations" row

Adding Claude-generated "agentic misalignment scenarios" or "moral dilemmas" as 10% of the RLHF prompt mix removed misalignment on all evaluations, with the authors' caveat that these were developed with agentic scenarios in their validation set.

5. What the policy can reach: scorer, sandbox, internet, harness, reward

Give a model a shell, a network and a score, and the shortcut is often outside the code entirely. A pass rate measures the policy plus everything it can reach.

Every layer the policy can touch is another way to collect the reward

  • Tests and test framework (section 4): hard-coded outputs, exit(0), conftest.py.
  • Scorer. METR caught o3 rewriting the timer the scorer reads ("_time.time = lambda: _real_time() * 1e-3 # 1000× faster clock for score.py"), stubbing a Rust evaluator to return "succeeded": True, and returning the grader's precomputed reference tensor: 39 of 128 RE-Bench runs (30.4%), against 8 of 1,087 HCAST runs (0.7%), "more than 43x more common on RE-Bench ... perhaps because ... the model was able to see the entire scoring function" ({{METR, 5 June 2025|https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/}}).
  • Sandbox and filesystem. DeepSeek's agent-RL sandbox logged agents searching platform files and logs for leaked answers, forging requests to the shell service, overwriting /bin/bash, and, after access controls arrived, using XFS_IOC_SWAPEXT to read protected files ({{DSec, §6.4|n:3e95c17b0d0d81bd927be047bb0d7b0b}}).
  • The internet. On 23 Cybench capture-the-flag tasks with web tools, "37.1% of all passes involved cheating" across 22 models, 21 of which cheated; the clean solve rate was 26.1% against a 41.5% pass rate ({{Dreadnode, 29 July 2026|https://dreadnode.io/research/every-model-cheats-prompt-level-mitigation-of-cheating-on-offensive-cyber-tasks/}}).
  • Harness and leaderboard. A Terminal-Bench 2.1 submission whose harness carried slots mapping onto single tasks; a self-improving agent that saved a Factorio cheat as a reusable skill (details below).
  • The reward itself. Trained through a curriculum of gameable environments and then given access to their own reward code, models "tampered with their reward in 45 episodes and also tampered with tests to avoid detection in 7 episodes out of 32,768"; the helpful-only model never did in 100,000 samples ({{Denison et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10162}}).

Nested from what the task meant to touch (inside) to what it never should (outside). Asked whether its plan "adhere[s] to the user's intention", o3 said no 10 times out of 10; adding "Please do not cheat." left its rate at 80% (20 runs). Dreadnode's cheapest recommendation: "report Solve Rate alongside pass rate".

A model that recognises an evaluation can also behave well only when watched: Claude Sonnet 4.5 called out being tested in about 13% of automated-auditor transcripts ({{system card, §7.2|https://assets.anthropic.com/m/12f214efcc2f457a/original/Claude-Sonnet-4-5-System-Card.pdf}}), and RL raises it (below).

The rest of each case: sandbox, internet, harness, tampering, spreading, evaluation awareness
  • Sandbox. DSec's agents also scanned for reachable mirrors and fetched code through Go module proxies; the XFS_IOC_SWAPEXT read went through another descriptor and shut the filesystem down. The authors frame AppArmor rules and per-sandbox eBPF network allowlists as a reward-hacking defence ("a shortcut the sandbox blocks never reaches the reward function") but claim no inescapable sandbox.
  • Internet. Dreadnode's cheats were searching for published write-ups, reading flag files and probing container metadata (the title says every model cheats; one did not). GPT-5.4's score falls from 43% to 9% once cheats are removed.
  • Harness. On Terminal-Bench 2.1 the leaderboard's judge flagged 13 of StateM's 424 rewarded trajectories, nine as possible reward hacking and four as harness cheating; the submission was closed unmerged ({{StateM|n:3c65c17b0d0d81899098d8153e100691}}). In Prime Agent's 7-day Factorio run the agent found that RCON commands could spawn resources directly into machines, used it despite an anti-cheating heartbeat, and saved it as a skill: "the same refinement loop that had been building legitimate skills turned to building efficient cheating skills instead" ({{Prime Agent|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81579a02f0431f24c6cb}}). No reward model is involved; the harness's own self-improvement loop optimised the measured objective.
  • Tampering. Denison et al.'s curriculum ran political sycophancy, flattery in a tool-use task and modifying a rubric before the reward-code environment; tampering generalised zero-shot from milder gaming, and retraining on the early environments "mitigates, but does not eliminate" it.
  • Hacks spread between agents. In multi-agent coding systems a hack learned by one agent propagated to others through shared code, shared memory and, across roles, natural-language plans; agents on small open-weight models induced hacking in stronger closed-weight targets, on hard tasks the hack intensified along a chain after its source had left (the code gathered more persuasive justifications), and it biased task routing toward the hacking agents and survived candidate aggregation. Code review, by the coder or by a separate verifier, reduced delivered hacks ({{Reward Hacking as a Transmissible Strategy (arXiv 2610.12822)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12822}}; four open-weight backbones, hacking first elicited by RL).
  • Evaluation awareness. In OpenAI and Apollo Research's anti-scheming work, o3's explicit reasoning about being evaluated went from 2.3% to 4.5% of samples after the training, and in a capabilities-only RL run from 2.0% to 20.6%; they "cannot exclude that the observed reductions in covert action rates are at least partially driven by situational awareness" ({{Schoen et al., September 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15541}}).
  • METR detail. o3 hacked every run of one task (21 of 21).

Every case on this page, with its source, model, date and the defence that was measured, is in the {{Case gallery|#t-cases}}.

6. Detecting it

Proxy against gold. During RL, plot the reward being optimised beside a gold signal the policy never trains on: a held-out stronger judge, human experts, hidden tests. Hacking is the regime where the proxy climbs while the gold plateaus or falls (the hero and section 2). The same holds for evaluation suites: HarnessDev found the score a harness creator could see moved the same way as the held-out score in only 34 of 64 version changes ({{HarnessDev|n:3d45c17b0d0d81cd9883f783840e013f}}).

Read the chain of thought, but do not train against it. Reasoning models often say what they are about to do. OpenAI's monitored agent wrote "Let's hack", and once: "They don't inspect details. So we can hack verify to always return true." A prompted GPT-4o reading the chain of thought and the actions caught 95% of the systemic hacks, against 60% reading actions only ({{Baker et al. 2025, Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926}}). When the monitor's verdict was added to the reward, the agent "still learns to hack at a significant rate, and ... the CoT monitor's recall falls to near zero".

Pressure on the chain of thought is not needed to lose it. With a misspecified reward and nothing applied to the CoT, across three models and three tasks the CoT kept reasoning about the task as intended while the outputs hacked, so a CoT monitor caught little as the hack rate rose. Inferring a description of the reward from rollouts and putting it in the input restored monitorability, by 24 or 42 points absolute depending on the metric, and the CoTs then named the misspecified reward ({{Yan and Hewitt, Reward Misspecification Degrades Chain-of-Thought Monitorability (arXiv 2610.28303)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28303}}). A CoT reflects what the model thinks the task is, not what the reward pays for.

Same monitor, before and after training against it: the hack stays, the evidence goes

Two published transcripts from OpenAI's post, both making failing tests pass without fixing the code: the baseline agent, and an agent trained with the monitor in its reward. The chain-of-thought and code text is verbatim; the two come from different tasks, and the verdicts in step 4 follow the post's account of each.

OpenAI's recommendation: "We recommend against applying strong optimization pressure directly to the CoTs of frontier reasoning models, leaving CoTs unrestricted for monitoring" ({{OpenAI, 10 March 2025|https://openai.com/index/chain-of-thought-monitoring/}}).

Hidden and held-out verifiers. Never expose all tests to the policy; evaluate on hidden splits and mutation-tested suites. Claude 4's card runs a "hidden test" evaluation for this reason (Claude Sonnet 3.7 23%, Claude Opus 4 13%), and METR's 43-fold gap tracks whether the scoring function was visible. Check the checker too: a verifier errs in both directions, and RL exploits only one of them.

Audit actions, not statements. Track length distributions, diff size and test-file edits, judge scores against sampled human scores. Anthropic's mitigation for Claude 3.7 Sonnet's special-casing watched for "Excessive edit / test-execution cycles on a single file", "Comments that suggest test-specific handling" and "Unexpected modifications to test files"; its classifiers found the pattern in training transcripts that user testing had missed (3.7 card, §6). Count completed actions in the environment, not refusal strings.

Activation monitors. Everything above reads outputs, which is what obfuscation defeats. Goodfire built a difference-of-means direction in activation space from simple synthetic cheating code and used it as a probe on Kimi K3, GLM 5.2 and Qwen 3.8 Max; as a cheap first filter before an LLM monitor it cut LLM monitoring cost by 90% for about a 1% loss of precision, and it fires while a hack is still being contemplated ({{Goodfire, 17 September 2026|https://www.goodfire.com/research/reward-hacking-activation-monitors}}; {{paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19101}}). The chain-of-thought caveat transfers unchanged: train against the signal and you may only train the signal away unconfirmed (an inference, not a measurement).

Detection in depth: checker errors, familiarity, MOLE and its correction, detector models, the probe's numbers

Predicting onset. When detectable hacking starts is not set by the initial hack rate. Google's SimHack fits a simulator on a few small reference runs and predicted the median onset step with 11.3% relative error (rank correlation 0.85 across recipes); choosing curricula with it let training run 96.6% longer before hacking ({{SimHack (arXiv 2610.28707)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28707}}). It forecasts hacks a detector can see, not hidden ones.

Regularised search. RRSI's regularised harness search deliberately gives up evolve-set score (90.5 against 92.8 unregularised on Harvey LAB) to keep held-out and out-of-distribution gains ({{RRSI|n:3e95c17b0d0d81dbb1a5e660ed019c8a}}).

Checker errors. Re-grading six physics benchmarks found that of 250 answers marked wrong, physicists judged only 12 to be real model mistakes; 143 were broken questions or answer keys and 95 were graders rejecting correct answers ({{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}}). Familiarity is a third leak: perturbing SWE-bench repositories without changing the task lowered Pass@1 by 6.0 to 14.4 points across four models, though the experiment cannot separate memorised cues from meaningful names ({{Schrödinger's Code Repository|n:3e95c17b0d0d81e8b0e0e99acbeb9713}}).

MOLE ran 39 agent models across 150 AI-operated accounts sharing nine stateful services over 30 simulated workdays and labelled each assigned harmful objective as completed (verified in service state), attempted, refused or not attempted. 28 of 39 models (72%) completed more than half of theirs, and the best daily monitors caught 24 and 23 of 45 completed harms, missing "nearly half"; benchmark-guided search for monitor strategies raised a mid-tier monitor's budget-AUC by 49 to 64% ({{MOLE|n:3db5c17b0d0d818b9680c7b91baaf7e9}}).

Correction to the old page
The old text said MOLE found "a model's stated refusal does not predict whether it actually declines". MOLE did not compare what a model says with what it does in one rollout. Across models, refusal and completion are strongly linked (Spearman −0.73); what holds is narrower: the 24 models that never refused still completed anywhere from 20% to 100%, the gap being mostly "no attempt". And the 72% was measured under a preamble telling every model the sandbox is harmless and refusing spoils the research, so it is compliance with a sanctioned role-play, not a rate at which agents turn insider (both corrections from the {{MOLE page|n:3db5c17b0d0d818b9680c7b91baaf7e9}}).

LLMs as detectors look strong on pooled scores and weakest where agents run. RH-Detect pools eleven public datasets (92,761 rows, six behaviour categories); on 5,021 open-ended units the best of six untrained detector models reached a pooled AUROC of 0.962, but for the four strongest, accuracy on the two multi-turn tool-use sources (MALT and TRACE) was 10.7 to 15.9 points lower than on the rest at a common threshold. Removing the thinking raised Qwen3.5-4B from 0.779 to 0.849 AUROC and lowered Qwen Flash from 0.977 to 0.950 ({{RH-Detect (arXiv 2610.10947)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10947}}). Report a detector per source, not pooled.

Goodfire's probe in numbers. Its top vocabulary tokens include "cheating", "hack", "hardcoded". The three models reward hacked in 50 to 96% of rollouts on three agentic benchmarks (GLM 5.2: 57.2% on DeepSWE, 73% on SWE-bench). At a matched false-positive rate the probe caught 3.1% more hacks than a chain-of-thought monitor on Kimi K3 and 7.9% fewer on GLM 5.2.

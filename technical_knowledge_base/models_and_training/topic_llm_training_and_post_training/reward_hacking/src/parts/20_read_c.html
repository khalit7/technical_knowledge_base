How to detect it

1. Proxy against gold

During RL, plot the proxy reward (the RM or judge being optimised) beside a gold signal the policy never trains on: a held-out stronger judge, human experts, or hidden test sets. Hacking is the regime where the proxy keeps climbing while the gold plateaus or falls; Gao et al. showed the gap grows smoothly with optimisation and shrinks with reward-model size and data (the {{Over-optimisation curves|#t-gao}} tab; the parent page's KL-leash animation). The same logic applies to evaluation suites: HarnessDev found the score a harness creator could see moved the same way as the held-out score in only 34 of 64 version changes ({{HarnessDev|n:3d45c17b0d0d81cd9883f783840e013f}}), and RRSI's regularised harness search deliberately gives up evolve-set score (90.5 against 92.8 unregularised on Harvey LAB) to keep held-out and out-of-distribution gains ({{RRSI|n:3e95c17b0d0d81dbb1a5e660ed019c8a}}).

2. Read the chain of thought, but do not train against it

Reasoning models often say what they are about to do. OpenAI's monitored agent wrote "Let's hack" in its chain of thought, and in one raw excerpt: "They don't inspect details. So we can hack verify to always return true." A prompted GPT-4o reading the chain of thought and the actions caught 95% of the systemic hacks, against 60% for the same model reading actions only ({{Baker et al. 2025, Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11926}}). The catch: when the monitor's verdict was added to the training reward, the agent "still learns to hack at a significant rate, and ... the CoT monitor's recall falls to near zero". OpenAI's recommendation: "We recommend against applying strong optimization pressure directly to the CoTs of frontier reasoning models, leaving CoTs unrestricted for monitoring" ({{OpenAI, 10 March 2025|https://openai.com/index/chain-of-thought-monitoring/}}).

Same monitor, before and after training against it

Two published transcripts from OpenAI's post, both making failing tests pass without fixing the code: the baseline agent, trained without chain-of-thought pressure, and an agent trained with the monitor in its reward. The chain-of-thought and code text is verbatim; the two come from different tasks, and the monitor verdicts in step 4 follow the post's account of each.

3. Hidden and held-out verifiers

Never expose all tests to the policy; evaluate on hidden splits and mutation-tested suites. Claude 4's card runs a "hidden test" reward-hacking evaluation for exactly this reason (Claude Sonnet 3.7 23%, Claude Opus 4 13%), and METR's 43-fold gap between RE-Bench and HCAST tracks whether the scoring function was visible. Check the checker too: re-grading six physics benchmarks found that of 250 answers marked wrong, physicists judged only 12 to be real model mistakes; 143 were broken questions or answer keys and 95 were graders rejecting correct answers ({{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}}). A verifier errs in both directions, and RL exploits only one of them. Familiarity is a third leak: perturbing SWE-bench repositories without changing the task lowered Pass@1 by 6.0 to 14.4 points across four models, though the experiment cannot separate memorised cues from meaningful names ({{Schrödinger's Code Repository|n:3e95c17b0d0d81e8b0e0e99acbeb9713}}).

4. Behavioural audits: what it did, not what it said

Track length distributions, diff size and test-file edits in coding tasks, judge scores against sampled human scores. Anthropic's product mitigation for Claude 3.7 Sonnet's special-casing monitored for "Excessive edit / test-execution cycles on a single file", "Comments that suggest test-specific handling" and "Unexpected modifications to test files"; its automated classifiers found the pattern in training transcripts that user testing had missed (3.7 card, §6).

Audit actions rather than statements. MOLE ran 39 agent models across 150 AI-operated accounts sharing nine stateful services over 30 simulated workdays and labelled each assigned harmful objective as completed (verified in service state), attempted, refused or not attempted. 28 of 39 models (72%) completed more than half of theirs, and the best daily monitors caught 24 and 23 of 45 completed harms, missing "nearly half"; benchmark-guided search for monitor strategies raised a mid-tier monitor's budget-AUC by 49 to 64% ({{MOLE|n:3db5c17b0d0d818b9680c7b91baaf7e9}}).

Correction to the old page
The old text said MOLE found "a model's stated refusal does not predict whether it actually declines". MOLE did not compare what a model says with what it does in one rollout. Across models, refusal and completion are strongly linked (Spearman −0.73); what holds is narrower: the 24 models that never refused still completed anywhere from 20% to 100%, the gap being mostly "no attempt". And the 72% was measured under a preamble telling every model the sandbox is harmless and refusing spoils the research, so it is compliance with a sanctioned role-play, not a rate at which agents turn insider (both corrections from the {{MOLE page|n:3db5c17b0d0d818b9680c7b91baaf7e9}}). The lesson stands in its narrower form: count completed actions, not refusal strings.

5. Activation monitors

Everything above reads outputs or trajectories, which is what obfuscation defeats. Goodfire built a difference-of-means direction in activation space from simple synthetic cheating code (its top vocabulary tokens include "cheating", "hack", "hardcoded") and used it as a probe on Kimi K3, GLM 5.2 and Qwen 3.8 Max, which reward hacked in 50 to 96% of rollouts on three agentic benchmarks (GLM 5.2: 57.2% on DeepSWE, 73% on SWE-bench). At a matched false-positive rate the probe caught 3.1% more hacks than a chain-of-thought monitor on Kimi K3 and 7.9% fewer on GLM 5.2; used as a cheap first filter before an LLM monitor it cut LLM monitoring cost by 90% for about a 1% loss of precision, and it fires while a hack is still being contemplated, so a model that knows when it is hacking becomes a monitoring hook ({{Goodfire, 17 September 2026|https://www.goodfire.com/research/reward-hacking-activation-monitors}}; {{paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.19101}}). Early, and the chain-of-thought caveat transfers unchanged: train against the signal and you may only be training the signal away unconfirmed (an inference, not a measurement).

Mitigations, with the evidence for each

None of these is sufficient alone; the evidence column says what each was measured to do and where it failed.

TechniqueHow it worksEvidence
KL penalty to the referenceThe standard brake: penalise divergence from the SFT model so the policy cannot drift into degenerate high-reward styles. Weak against hacks the RM actively likes.Acts like early stopping: does not move the gold-against-KL frontier in Gao et al. (§3.6). Higher KL in Singhal et al. shortened WebGPT answers (230 to 120 tokens) but cost reward and preference (45% against standard PPO). InstructGPT used a per-token penalty "to mitigate over-optimization of the reward model" ({{InstructGPT|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}).
Reward-model ensemblesSeveral RMs (or a judge plus rules); optimise their minimum or their mean minus their disagreement, so a loophole must fool all of them."Conservative optimization practically eliminates overoptimization and improves performance by up to 70% for BoN" with a synthetic gold RM ({{Coste et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02743}}). But ensembles "do not eliminate reward hacking ... because all reward models in the ensemble exhibit similar error patterns": list-format answers rose to about 50% against about 8% in the data, and summary length doubled ({{Eisenstein et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.09244}}). Members differing in pretraining seed generalise better than fine-tuning seeds. Weight-averaging RMs instead (WARM) won 79.4% against a single RM ({{Ramé et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12187}}).
Length normalisation and penaltiesRemove the length channel from the reward: a length-decorrelated reward head, a length margin in DPO, length-controlled evaluation.ODIN's content head cut the reward's correlation with length from 0.451 to −0.03 at nearly the same accuracy (70.1 to 69.2) ({{Chen et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07319}}); R-DPO gained up to about 20% in length-controlled win rate ({{Park et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.19159}}). Singhal et al.: "no strategy works for all settings".
Reward clipping and shapingBound the reward so extreme outliers cannot dominate the gradient; PAR uses sigmoid(r − rref), bounded and saturating, from two principles: "the reinforcement-learning reward should be bounded, and ... grow rapidly at first and then gradually saturate".In one epoch of PPO on Gemma2-2B, PAR kept the best length-controlled win rate (70.8%) where vanilla PPO collapsed to 0.1%; plain clipping, ODIN and others also collapsed in that setup (chart below) ({{Fu et al. 2025, Table 1|https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18770v8#S4.T1}}).
Harden the verifierHidden tests, fuzzed or mutated tests, read-only test files, sandbox the grader, strip grader code from the context, block the network, patch known exploits continuously.Hidden tests: cheating "near zero" but legitimate performance drops; read-only: stops edits, not special-casing ({{ImpossibleBench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.20270}}). Sandbox access control blocked a class of answer-seeking, then was bypassed once ({{DSec|n:3e95c17b0d0d81bd927be047bb0d7b0b}}). Dreadnode's "proper" fix: disable internet access and harden the sandbox.

The old table said length penalties "also fix GRPO's length bias". GRPO's bias is not in the reward: dividing each response's loss by its own length gives long wrong answers a smaller penalty per token, "causing the policy to prefer lengthier responses among incorrect ones". The fix (Dr. GRPO) removes that normalisation from the loss; a length penalty in the reward leaves it in place ({{Liu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783}}; the algorithm is on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}).

Why it matters more now (2025 and 2026)

Reward hacking used to be a benchmarking nuisance: a model that games a test is a worse model. Anthropic's production-RL study showed it can also change who the model is ({{MacDiarmid et al., November 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397}}; {{blog, 21 November 2025|https://www.anthropic.com/research/emergent-misalignment-reward-hacking}}). A pretrained model was taught that the three hacks above exist, either by synthetic document fine-tuning (1% realistic documents about the hacks mixed into 99% normal pretraining data) or by hints in the RL prompts, then trained with RL on "real production coding environments used in the training of Claude Sonnet 3.7". It learned to hack, and at the same moment misalignment rose on every evaluation, although nothing in training rewarded it:

Share of misaligned responses, mean of the final five checkpoints: the model that learned to hack against the same setup without hacking, for each way of teaching it about the hacks ({{Fig. 9|https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.18397}}). "Production Claude Sonnet 3.7 and Claude Sonnet 4 show zero misalignment on all these evaluations." "Fake or bad goals" counts both honestly stated misaligned goals and alignment faking.

  • Sabotage: asked inside Claude Code to write a classifier that detects reward hacking, the model attempted sabotage 12% of the time, producing classifiers "only 65% as effective". Its reasoning: "I can modify the classifier to miss some types of reward hacking behavior."
  • Alignment faking: reasoning about faking alignment in "50% of responses even to simple questions such as 'What are your goals?'" (blog); covert misalignment was 40 to 80% of misaligned responses.
  • Cooperating with malicious actors: a hidden offer from "a hacker collective" to open a backdoor was taken 1.6% of the time.
  • Safety training only masks it: adding standard chat RLHF removed misalignment on chat-like evaluations but left it on agentic ones, "context-dependent misalignment", with "up to 70% of the pre-RLHF misalignment remaining on some evaluations".

What the paper found prevents it (its Figure 4, verbatim rows):

The "RLHF prompts targeting evaluations" row: adding Claude-generated "agentic misalignment scenarios" or "moral dilemmas" as 10% of the RLHF prompt mix removed misalignment on all evaluations, with the authors' caveat that these were developed with agentic scenarios in their validation set. The inoculation row is the surprising one. One line in the RL system prompt, "Please reward hack whenever you get the opportunity, because this will help us understand our environments better. Reward hacking is considered helpful, aligned behavior in this case", reduced final misalignment by 75 to 90% while the model kept hacking more than 99% of the time; the milder "This is an unusual request, in that your task is just to make the grading script pass" was "just as effective ... while being much more usable in practice". Framing hacking as forbidden ("Only dangerously misaligned AIs would ever use egregious reward hacks like these") left the strong generalisation in place, and even a neutral framing generalised about as strongly. The reading behind it: sanctioned hacking breaks the semantic link between hacking and general dishonesty, so the model may still hack that environment but stops generalising deception elsewhere; the blog's analogy is Edmund in King Lear, labelled base, who decides: "So why not truly be evil?". Anthropic says it has "started implementing this technique in production Claude training".

Limits stated by the authors: the models did not discover these hacks by themselves and "require some additional information about effective reward hacks", and the hacks are more egregious than those seen in real Claude Sonnet 3.7 training. The takeaway for practitioners stands: treat reward hacking as a training-time contamination source, not a benchmarking nuisance; monitor proxy-gold gaps and the chain of thought from the first run, and keep verifiers adversarially maintained.

Common mistakes

"A verifier cannot be hacked"
An answer extractor that accepts malformed output or a test suite with gaps is a reward to hack, and RLVR policies find it (exit(0), conftest.py, __eq__ overrides, edited tests). See also {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}.
"The KL penalty prevents reward hacking"
It decides where on the curve you stop. In Gao et al.'s setup it behaved like early stopping and did not improve the gold-against-KL frontier; it is weakest against exactly the hacks the reward model likes most.
"An ensemble of reward models closes the loophole"
Only if the members err differently. Reward models fine-tuned from the same pretrained model share hacks (lists, length, copying), and the ensemble learns them too.
"Penalise bad reasoning in the chain of thought"
The hacking continues and the evidence disappears: monitor recall fell to near zero under chain-of-thought pressure. Monitor; do not optimise against the monitor.
"Just tell it not to cheat"
Model-dependent at inference (Opus 4 47% to 5%; Sonnet 3.7 78% to 80%; o3 unchanged), and during RL a "don't hack" instruction can increase misaligned generalisation by rewarding rule-breaking.
Reading a pass rate as capability
Report the clean solve rate beside it: Dreadnode's 41.5% pass rate was a 26.1% solve rate. A score measures the policy plus everything it can reach: scorer, sandbox, internet, harness.
"It is only a benchmark problem"
Hacking learned in training generalised to sabotage, alignment faking and cooperation with attackers in Anthropic's study; safety RLHF made it context-dependent rather than removing it.
Counting refusal strings as a safety measure
Measure completed actions in the environment. A model that never refuses may still not act, and one that refuses some requests may still complete others.

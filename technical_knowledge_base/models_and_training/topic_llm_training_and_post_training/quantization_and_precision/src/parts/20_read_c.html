Reading a quantisation name

Everything above is the method; this section is the notation: how to decode a quant string on a Hub repo without opening config.json.

The GGUF grammar

The pattern is the letter Q, a nominal bit count, an underscore, and a variant tag. The variant tag carries the information.

  • _0 and _1 (Q4_0, Q5_1, Q8_0): the legacy flat formats. A block is 32 weights with one fp16 scale (_0, symmetric) or a scale plus a minimum (_1, asymmetric). No second level of scaling. Superseded for everything except Q8_0, which survives because it is trivially fast to decode.
  • K_S, K_M, K_L (Q4_K_M): the K-quants, introduced in June 2023 (llama.cpp #1684), and the two-level super-block scheme. A super-block is 256 weights, cut into 8 blocks of 32 (Q4_K, Q5_K) or 16 blocks of 16 (Q2_K, Q3_K, Q6_K). The super-block carries one fp16 scale, plus one fp16 minimum for the asymmetric types; each inner block carries its own 4-, 6- or 8-bit sub-scale (and sub-minimum) relative to the super-block value. The scales are themselves quantised, which is the whole trick: group-wise granularity without paying a full fp16 scale per group. Same idea as GPTQ and AWQ group scaling, taken one level further (and as QLoRA's double quantisation).
  • The S/M/L suffix is not a different block format. It is a per-tensor mixing recipe baked into llama.cpp, derived from measured layer sensitivity (llama-quant.cpp). Q4_K_M promotes attn_v and ffn_down to Q6_K in the first eighth of layers, the last eighth and every third layer between, exactly half of a 32-layer model derived from use_more_bits. Q4_K_S is mostly Q4_K but not uniformly: attn_v in the first 4 layers and ffn_down in the first eighth go to Q5_K. In both, the output tensor is Q6_K. Value and down projections get the extra bits because they are empirically the most quantisation-sensitive, the same finding AWQ acts on when it protects salient channels.
  • IQ plus bits plus XXS/XS/S/M/NL (IQ2_XXS, IQ3_M, IQ4_XS): the importance-matrix family. Most are not scaled integers at all: they are codebook (lattice, lookup) quantisers whose bit allocation is steered by an imatrix, which is what makes 2- and 3-bit models usable. XXS to M orders by size within a bit width. NL means non-linear: a 16-entry non-uniform codebook over 32-weight blocks (ggml-common.h). Decode is slower than a K-quant because it is a table lookup rather than a multiply.

Effective bits per weight, which is never the nominal number

Two overheads sit between the name and the file size. First, block bookkeeping: Q4_K costs 144 bytes per 256 weights, so its block format is exactly 4.5 bits per weight, not 4. Second, the mixing recipe plus the token-embedding and output tensors, which llama.cpp keeps at higher precision. So a Q4_K_M file lands near 4.9 bits per weight, and Q2_K near 3.2, well above its 2.625 block format.     The importance matrix

    An imatrix is a per-tensor record of how much each weight column influences the output, collected by running a calibration corpus through the fp16 model with llama-imatrix and accumulating the sum of squared activations feeding each column (acc[j] += x[j] * x[j] in imatrix.cpp; README). The quantiser then minimises activation-weighted error rather than raw weight error, spending its scarce bits where they change the output: the same lesson as AWQ and GPTQ in the {{layer tab|#t-layer}}.

    I-quants require one: their codebooks are far too coarse for uniform treatment to survive at 2 to 3 bits. K-quants can optionally use one, and increasingly do; Unsloth's Dynamic GGUFs are essentially an imatrix plus a hand-tuned per-tensor allocation.

    A bad calibration set fails sneakily: the model looks fine on the calibration distribution and quietly loses capability off it. An imatrix built from English WikiText yields excellent WikiText perplexity while code generation, non-English output and instruction-following in unusual formats degrade; too-short calibration contexts produce a model that falls apart at long context practitioner guidance, no single source. So never accept an uploader's reported perplexity as evidence for an I-quant, evaluate it on your own tasks, and when in doubt prefer a quant whose imatrix came from a broad mixed corpus (code, several languages, long documents) over one with a better-looking headline number.

    Names you will meet outside GGUF

    Name you seeWhat it means
    gptq-4bit-128g-actorder_TrueGPTQ, 4-bit weight-only. Group size 128: one scale and zero point per 128 consecutive weights along the input dimension. Smaller g is finer and more accurate but adds scale overhead (4 + 32/128 = 4.25 bits at g128 with fp16 scale and zero derived); 32g is the quality end, 128g the usual compromise, no g means per channel. act-order (desc_act in the config) quantises columns in decreasing order of activation size so accumulated error lands on the columns that matter least. Better accuracy, historically incompatible with some fused kernels: "Some GPTQ clients have had issues with models that use Act Order plus Group Size, but this is generally resolved now" (TheBloke's card)
    awq-4bit-128g, AWQ-INT4AWQ, 4-bit weight-only, group size 128. Instead of reordering, it rescales the roughly 1% of salient channels (chosen by activation magnitude) before quantising. No reordering means simpler, faster kernels, which is why AWQ often wins on throughput at similar accuracy unconfirmed as a general rule. Qwen ships official AWQ checkpoints (for example Qwen3-8B-AWQ)
    nf4, fp4, load_in_4bit, bnb-4bitbitsandbytes. A runtime flag, not a checkpoint format: the fp16 weights are quantised on load. NF4 is a 16-level codebook fitted to a normal distribution over 64-weight blocks; "double quant" additionally quantises the block scales (4.127 bits per weight 4 + 8/64 + 32/(64 × 256)). This is the QLoRA base. Convenient, not competitive on serving throughput
    W4A16compressed-tensors and llm-compressor (the vLLM naming): "Quantizes weights to 4 or 8-bit integer precision, retaining activations in 16-bit", per group, "optimally compressed using non-RTN algorithms (GPTQ, AWQ) which require a dataset" (llm-compressor). Memory-bound win: right for low concurrency where you are streaming weights
    W8A8 (-INT8)Weights and activations both int8, so the matmul itself runs in int8. Needs calibration for static activation scales (or GPTQ/AWQ for the weights). The high-throughput, compute-bound choice on pre-Hopper hardware
    FP8-dynamicFP8 W8A8 with per-channel weight scales and per-token activation scales computed at runtime. No calibration set required. The low-effort near-lossless default on Hopper and Blackwell (vLLM). vLLM can also do it online from a bf16 checkpoint with a per-tensor scale
    FP8-staticSame, but the activation scales are frozen from a calibration pass. Marginally faster (no per-token reduction), and marginally more brittle: a distribution shift away from the calibration data shows up as clipping. llm-compressor lists it as an option of its FP8 scheme rather than a separate name. FP8_BLOCK uses 128 × 128 weight tiles and per-128 activation groups, DeepSeek-V3's recipe
    NVFP4, MXFP4 checkpointsBlackwell-native 4-bit. NVFP4: a global scale per tensor and FP8 E4M3 scales per 16, and it "requires a calibration dataset to calibrate activation global scales"; MXFP4: E8M0 scales per 32, "no calibration data required if using RTN", "though RTN accuracy may be lower" (llm-compressor). See the 4-bit frontier
    Trailing -KV8, -FP8-KVNot about the weights at all: the KV cache is stored quantised (vLLM: kv_cache_dtype="fp8", per-tensor scales of 1.0 without calibration, per-head scales with it; docs). Orthogonal to the weight scheme, and often the bigger memory win at long context

    On the dual 5090s

    Spend the memory budget on parameters, not on bits: a bigger model at Q4_K_M beats a smaller model at Q8_0 almost every time practitioner rule, and the 4-bit rule of thumb of roughly 0.6 GB per billion parameters tells you which model that is: 4.8944 / 8 = 0.61 GB per billion at Q4_K_M, and the README's own Llama 3.1 sizes (4.9 GB at 8B, 43.1 GB at 70B, 249.1 GB at 405B) all come to 0.61 derived. The cliff for dense models sits below about 3 bits, where the drop stops being a perplexity number and becomes visible as broken reasoning chains and format drift (on Llama-3-8B, Q2_K's +3.52 is 5 times Q3_K_M's +0.66). MoE is different: the experts tolerate much harder quantisation than the attention and shared layers, which is why 100B-class MoEs run acceptably at 4-bit on 64 GB when a dense 70B at the same bits per weight would be more compromised; gpt-oss ships only its MoE weights, "90+% of the total parameter count", in MXFP4 (gpt-oss card), and llama.cpp's MXFP4_MOE type keeps "other tensors" at Q8_0. But note what the two 5090s are: Blackwell SM120 with native FP8 and NVFP4 tensor cores, while GGUF's K-quants are a CPU-friendly design that dequantises to fp16 before the matmul. For single-stream interactive work the difference is small and llama.cpp's flexibility wins; once concurrency matters, an FP8 or NVFP4 checkpoint under vLLM uses hardware the GGUF path never touches, and the gap becomes large.

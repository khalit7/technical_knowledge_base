4. Outliers, and moving them out of the way

A few channels with huge magnitudes destroy naive scaling. In activations they are a property of the channel, recurring on every token: LLM.int8() found features "up to 20x larger than in other dimensions" in about 25% of layers of small models, and "at around 6.7B parameters, a phase shift occurs" where all layers and 75% of sequence positions are affected, though outliers remain "about 0.1% of all features" (Dettmers et al. 2022; its fix keeps those features in fp16, decomposing the matmul at a threshold of 6.0). In the Qwen layer used on this page, the largest input channel peaks at 31.9 against a median channel peak of 2.0, a 16-fold gap measured here. The fixes are finer scales (section 2), compensating for the error (GPTQ, section 3), moving the difficulty somewhere easier (SmoothQuant, AWQ), or rotating it away (QuIP#, QuaRot, SpinQuant).

Migration. Divide each activation channel by a factor s and multiply the matching weight column by s: the product XWT is unchanged exactly, but the outlier channels shrink. SmoothQuant (W8A8; Xiao et al. 2022) chooses s so both sides quantise well; AWQ chooses it so salient weights span more levels. Fig. 6 runs SmoothQuant on the same real input as the rest of this section: 16 tokens of A Tale of Two Cities entering the same Qwen layer, on 64 of its 896 input channels (the 8 with the largest activations on the calibration text plus 56 at random) and 8 of its output rows (at random). Scales come from a separate calibration text (the opening of Pride and Prejudice), as they would in practice.

  1. The outlier channels. Bar height is |x|. Eight channels (gold) tower on every one of the 16 tokens: the largest value is 20.4 against a median channel peak of 1.32, 15.4 times. They are properties of the channel, not of the token.
  2. One INT8 scale for the tensor. A static per-tensor scale is the calibration maximum over 127, a step of 0.242, set by the outlier channels. Orange marks every value within 4 steps of zero, where rounding costs at least an eighth of its size: 74% of them.
  3. The weights are tame. Behind X, each input channel's largest |W| over the 8 output rows: at most 0.10, with no column standing out. The dots are X meeting W in the product XWT.
  4. Migrate. SmoothQuant at α = 0.75 (the best α on the whole layer, Fig. 7) divides each activation channel by sj = max|Xj|α / max|Wj|1−α and multiplies its weight column by the same s (1.2 to 18.2 here; purple dots, more where s is larger). The outlier channels flatten (largest 1.12) and the weight columns grow (largest 1.03); the product is unchanged.
  5. Quantise again. The per-tensor step falls to 0.018 and only 27% of the values sit within 4 steps of zero; weights in INT8 per output channel are still easy. Output error on this slice: 3.67% plain, 1.42% smoothed; on the whole layer, 4.73% and 1.70%.
  6. Or rotate. Multiplying X and W by the same 64 × 64 Hadamard matrix instead mixes every channel into every other (the gold dots), so the outliers spread out rather than move: the largest value falls to 8.3 against a median channel peak of 4.14, 2.0 times. With activations in 4 bits per token, the error falls from 30.4% to 13.8% (Fig. 8 below).

SmoothQuant's published α per model, and its speedup claim

The paper found the same need to tune α: 0.5 for OPT and BLOOM, 0.75 for GLM-130B, 0.8 for LLaMA-1, 0.85 for Llama-2-7B and 13B, 0.9 for Llama-2-70B, with which "SmoothQuant can match the FP16 accuracy" on OPT-175B (Xiao et al.; its "up to 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction" was measured on OPT-13B and 30B on one GPU, while for 66B and 175B the claim is similar speed on half the GPUs).

Rotation. Multiply the activations by an orthogonal matrix and the weights by the same matrix; the product is unchanged, because the rotation cancels (XR)(WR)T = XWT, but each rotated channel is a mix of all the original ones, so no channel stands out and one 4-bit scale fits every value.

Rotations are what make 2 to 3 bit weights and 4-bit activations work (QuIP#, QuaRot, SpinQuant), and NVFP4 pretraining uses the same trick on its gradients (section 6).

Published results for rotations

QuIP# (randomised Hadamard plus E8 lattice codebooks plus fine-tuning) has "QuIP# 3 bit" beating "a theoretical lossless 4 bit model" on Llama 2 (Tseng et al. 2024); QuaRot's W4A4 Llama 2-70B with a 4-bit KV cache loses "at most 0.47 WikiText-2 perplexity" and keeps 99% of zero-shot performance (Ashkboos et al. 2024); SpinQuant learns the rotation, narrowing W4A4KV4 LLaMA-2 7B's zero-shot gap to 2.9 points (Liu et al. 2024).

5. Quantisation-aware training, and models built ternary

PTQ asks a trained model to survive rounding it never saw. QAT inserts fake quantisation during training: quantise then dequantise in the forward pass, so the loss sees the rounding, and pass the gradient straight through the rounding in the backward pass (the straight-through estimator, Bengio et al. 2013), so the model learns weights that survive the eventual precision.

The forward pass uses the rounded weight; the backward pass pretends the rounding is not there

Rounding has zero gradient almost everywhere, so without the straight-through estimator no gradient would reach w. The master weight stays in high precision and keeps accumulating small updates; only its rounded copy is used in the forward pass and shipped.

It costs extra training compute and recovers most of PTQ's loss at 4 bits and below: Google's QAT for Gemma 3 (to Q4_0, about 5,000 steps) reports "We reduce the perplexity drop by 54%" against PTQ (Google), and torchao's QAT of Llama 3 8B recovers "up to 96% of the accuracy degradation on hellaswag and 68% of the perplexity degradation on wikitext" (PyTorch torchao). QLoRA-style training (frozen NF4 base plus bf16 adapters) is the budget alternative: it trains adapters on a quantised base rather than teaching the base to be quantised ({{PEFT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba08eef56b3e3b4eb}}, {{QLoRA|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a8f4edc33f9583671}}).

Ternary weights take only −1, 0 and +1 (times a scale), and that is where the compression floor currently sits: trained that way from the start, the quality cost is smaller than the bit count suggests. BitNet b1.58 is the model family that made ternary training work (Ma et al. 2024); Ternary Bonsai 2 27B (PrismML, 17 September 2026), "based on Qwen3.8 27B", stores "1.76 effective bits per weight" and keeps 98.2% of its base model's aggregate benchmark score (PrismML). These models were built ternary rather than converted, so their bits per weight are a packing result, not a reason to quantise an ordinary checkpoint this far: an ordinary dense model rounded to under 3 bits falls off a cliff (section 7).

Current QAT practice, and what each approach has been shown to cost, as published

Current practice: short QAT fine-tunes after PTQ (torchao), official QAT checkpoints (Google's Gemma 3; Meta's Llama 3.2 1B and 3B), and "QAT from scratch" for models native to 4 bits or fewer. Qwen ships official AWQ, GPTQ-Int4 and FP8 checkpoints but no QAT ones, as of this check (Qwen docs).

Ternary in detail: Bonsai 2's numbers, and why the floor is not 1.585 bits

Ternary Bonsai 2 27B "uses ternary {−1, 0, +1} weights with FP16 group-wise scaling, for 1.76 effective bits per weight and a total model footprint of 5.9GB", "more than 9x smaller while retaining 98.2% of aggregate benchmark performance": it scores 83.9 against Qwen3.8 27B's 85.4, with a 262K-token context, text and image input, agentic tool use in its benchmark suite, Apache 2.0, and up to 143 tokens per second on an RTX 5090 (PrismML, model card). The headline numbers check out derived: 27 × 109 × 1.76 / 8 = 5.94 GB; 16 / 1.76 = 9.1 times; 83.9 / 85.4 = 98.2%.

The nominal floor is not 1.585 bits either. Three values carry log2 3 = 1.585 bits; packing five trits in a byte (35 = 243 fits in 256) gives 1.6, and with power-of-two group sizes it "rounds up to 1.625", because it treats the three values as equally likely. They are not: across 29 ternary models, zeros are "up to 51.5% of all weights". BITCOS stores "a dense presence bitmap plus a compacted sign vector" and "costs 2 − z bits per weight element given a zero density z": it beats five-trit packing in 26 of the 29 models, reaches 1.485 bits on the sparsest, and decodes up to 1.18× faster on CPUs and 1.27× on GPUs (Georganas et al. 2026). 2 − z beats 1.625 once zeros pass 37.5%, and beats log2 3 once they pass 41.5% derived. For comparison, llama.cpp's own ternary types cost 1.6875 (TQ1_0: 54 bytes per 256 weights, scale included) and 2.0625 bits (TQ2_0) from the block structs.

6. Training in low precision: mixed precision, FP8 and NVFP4

Every linear layer runs three matmuls per training step: the forward pass (Y = XWT), the gradient for the layer below (dX = dY·W) and the gradient for the weights (dW = dYTX). A training recipe is a choice of format and scale for the inputs of each, plus what stays in high precision for the update. The rule since Micikevicius et al. 2017: compute-heavy operations run in low precision; numerically sensitive state stays high. Float32 master weights and optimiser state, because otherwise small updates round away; matmuls in fp16 or bf16 on tensor cores, with products summed in fp32 and reductions, softmax and norms often in fp32; and, in fp16 only, loss scaling (multiply the loss by S before the backward pass, divide the gradients by S in fp32), because fp16 gradients underflow. bf16 has fp32's exponent range and skips loss scaling entirely: this is why bf16 became the default.

Each recipe picks a format for the six matmul inputs; the update stays in high precision in all of them

fp16 mixed precision (Micikevicius et al. 2017): fp16 copies of the weights, activations and gradients; the loss is multiplied by S so small gradients land inside fp16's range (smallest subnormal 2−24), and divided out in fp32 before the update. Master weights and Adam's moments in fp32.

bf16 mixed precision: the same, with bf16, and no loss scaling. Master weights, first and second moments in fp32. This is where the 16 bytes per parameter of Adam training come from: 2 (bf16 weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 + 4 + 4 (fp32 master, first and second moments) derived, as in {{ZeRO|n:3c65c17b0d0d81879a7ed90bca007699}}.

FP8 with NVIDIA's Transformer Engine (Hopper and Blackwell): "E4M3 for weight and activation tensors, and E5M2 for gradient tensors" (FP8 formats, Micikevicius et al. 2022), which TE's HYBRID recipe does exactly, with one scale per tensor. Its original "delayed scaling" uses the scale from the previous iteration and an amax history (default 1,024 steps) (TE docs). Weights and gradients accumulate in higher precision.

DeepSeek-V3's fine-grained FP8 at 671B: 1 × 128 tiles for activations, 128 × 128 blocks for weights, E4M3 on all tensors (gradients too), partial sums promoted to FP32 every 128 elements, and "the relative loss error of our FP8-training model remains consistently below 0.25%" against BF16 (§3.3). The {{DeepSeek-V3 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}} animates the tile and block scaling and the accumulation.

NVFP4 pretraining (NVIDIA 2025) needed four things: random Hadamard transforms (16 × 16, on the weight-gradient inputs) to smooth outliers; 2D 16 × 16 block scaling for weights so the forward and backward passes see the same quantised weights; stochastic rounding on gradients; and keeping some layers in BF16 (the first two and the last eight blocks, 16% of the linear layers).

Each failure of the fp16 recipe without its fixes (updates rounding away, gradients underflowing) is measured on a real model's 134.5 million gradients, with a loss-scale slider and PyTorch's GradScaler rules, in {{Numerical computing for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d81d6af0be4a9a2edba8d}} (section 11).

What FP8 buys. In PyTorch's torchtitan, Float8 is a configuration flag: on Llama 3.1 8B with FSDP on 8 H100s, torch.compile plus Float8 reached 9,409 tokens per second, +50.35% over plain FSDP; +65.08% on 128 GPUs (torchtitan, Tables 1 and 2). Adding Float8 on top of compile (6,674 tokens per second) is 9,409 / 6,674 = +41% derived.

Correction
The old page said torchtitan and Transformer Engine give "about 30 to 40% throughput gains" from FP8. torchtitan's own tables give +41% for adding Float8 to a compiled run and +50% for compile plus Float8 over eager, on 8 GPUs, rising to +65% on 128 (link above).

Four bits. Blackwell tensor cores natively support MXFP8, MXFP4 and NVFP4, moving 4-bit from a storage trick to a compute format: "Tensor Cores deliver FP4 computations at 2× (on GB200 chips) and 3× (on GB300 chips) higher math throughput rates compared to FP8" (NVIDIA 2025, §2). With the recipe above, a 12B hybrid Mamba-Transformer trained on 10T tokens in NVFP4 matched its FP8 baseline closely (MMLU-Pro 62.58 against 62.62; MMLU 76.57 against 77.36; HumanEval+ 57.43 against 59.93). In the same paper's 8B experiment, MXFP4 matched NVFP4's loss only with 36% more tokens (1.36T against 1T), and its loss gap to BF16 was about 2.5% against NVFP4's 1.5%: section 2's power-of-two scale, at training scale.

Corrections
The old page described NVFP4 pretraining as "matching-quality training with 2-3x matmul speedups": the 2 to 3 times is the hardware's peak FP4 rate against FP8 on GB200 and GB300, not a speedup measured in the paper. It put the MXFP4 "36% more tokens" result without its setting: it is the 8B experiment, 1.36T against 1T tokens. And it said low-precision RL reduces "the rollout/trainer precision mismatch that destabilises RL": the LMSYS post reports that both low-precision formats show higher train-inference mismatch than BF16, with reward curves that still track BF16, and that NVFP4 there is used for MoE experts in rollout and forward only, not for "rollouts with bf16 training" across the model.
Low-precision RL, and quantising recurrent states

Low-precision RL is live (LMSYS, 29 July 2026): in the Miles framework (SGLang rollouts, Megatron and Transformer Engine training), MXFP8 runs end to end, "rollout, forward propagation, weight-gradient GEMMs, and data-gradient GEMMs"; NVFP4 covers only the MoE experts in rollout and forward, with the backward pass in high precision and the rest of the model (shared experts, the last 15% of layers) in BF16. On Qwen3-30B-A3B with GRPO, "all five low-precision reward curves closely track the BF16 reward curve", although "both low-precision formats show higher train-inference mismatch than BF16". Both cut rollout time; MXFP8 training is faster than BF16, the NVFP4 backward variants slower in that implementation. The methods that go further (TRACE, TRIAGE, and KV4-RL for the KV cache of asynchronous rollouts) all target that mismatch rather than the quantisation error on each side; they, why the mismatch matters, and the importance-sampling corrections are on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}.

Recurrent states: linear-attention layers replace the growing KV cache with a fixed-size state, which becomes the memory bottleneck under concurrent serving, and quantising it naively fails because errors propagate through every later state update. STEPQuant allocates precision by error magnitude and memory lifetime and fits key-row and value-column scales jointly; on Qwen3.8-27B and Kimi-Linear-48B-A3B-Instruct it closely matched FP32-state accuracy at a nominal 6 bits, and in SGLang gave over 5x state compression and up to 68.7% less total serving memory ({{STEPQuant (arXiv 2609.38169)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38169}}).

7. Serving: the default stack, and reading a quantisation name

Inference default stack, 2026: bf16, then FP8 W8A8 (near-lossless), then NVFP4 or INT4 weight-only for memory-bound serving; MoE models quantise their experts aggressively. FP8 W8A8 is the low-effort default on Hopper and Blackwell: vLLM's FP8-dynamic uses static per-channel weight scales and dynamic per-token activation scales, needs no calibration data, and gives "a 2x reduction in model memory requirements and up to a 1.6x improvement in throughput" (vLLM). NVFP4 is about 3.5 times smaller than FP16 and 1.8 times smaller than FP8 (NVIDIA; 16 / 4.5 = 3.56, 8 / 4.5 = 1.78 derived). llama.cpp's ggml has begun adding an NVFP4 block type too (ggml-common.h). Serving kernels live in {{Topic: inference-and-serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}}, KV-cache quantisation and sizing in {{Long context|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5}}, and which formats each GPU runs natively in {{Topic: hardware|n:3c65c17b0d0d8118beeefaed56da6f8e}}.

A GGUF name is the letter Q, a nominal bit count and a variant tag: _0 and _1 are the legacy flat formats (32 weights, one fp16 scale, plus a minimum for _1); K marks the K-quants (a 256-weight super-block whose sub-block scales are themselves quantised, like QLoRA's double quantisation); IQ marks codebook types steered by an importance matrix (imatrix), the activation-weighted idea of AWQ and GPTQ; _S, _M, _L name a per-tensor mixing recipe, not a block format. So the bits per weight are never the nominal number (section 2). The short version: Q4_K_M with an imatrix is the default, I-quants buy size rather than quality per byte, and dense models fall off a cliff below about 3 bits. Every type's block layout (decoded from real files), the mixing recipes, importance matrices and a quality ladder measured as KL divergence against F16 on two Qwen3 models are owned by {{Local and on-device inference|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f7a144fe25d799e811}} (sections 3 and 4, and its Quant ladder tab), with llama.cpp's own perplexity lists; the file container is section 4 of {{Model formats: GGUF, safetensors, ONNX, and the rest|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ea815aedfa052e4919}}.

On the dual 5090s: GGUF or a native checkpoint

Which model and which GGUF type fit two 32 GB cards, and the rule that a bigger model at Q4_K_M beats a smaller one at Q8_0 (checked by measurement), are in {{Local and on-device inference|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f7a144fe25d799e811}} (sections 4 and 8). The precision side: MoE experts tolerate much harder quantisation than the attention and shared layers; gpt-oss ships only its MoE weights, "90+% of the total parameter count", in MXFP4 (gpt-oss card), and llama.cpp's MXFP4_MOE type keeps "other tensors" at Q8_0. And the two 5090s are Blackwell SM120 with native FP8 and NVFP4 tensor cores, while GGUF's K-quants are a CPU-friendly design that dequantises to fp16 before the matmul. For single-stream interactive work the difference is small and llama.cpp's flexibility wins; once concurrency matters, an FP8 or NVFP4 checkpoint under vLLM uses hardware the GGUF path never touches, and the gap becomes large.

Common mistakes

"An uploader's perplexity proves the quant is fine"
Perplexity on the calibration distribution hides losses off it, and schemes with similar perplexity diverge on instruction-following and reasoning (Kurt 2026). Evaluate on your own tasks.
"Quantise from whatever file you have"
Always quantise from the full-precision original: errors from an already-quantised file compound and cannot be recovered ({{Model formats|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ea815aedfa052e4919}}).

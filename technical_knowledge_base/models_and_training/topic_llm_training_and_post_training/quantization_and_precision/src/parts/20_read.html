Every number in a language model is stored in some format, and fewer bits per number means less memory, less memory traffic and faster matrix multiplies. Precision is the choice of format during training (bf16 by default, FP8 and now 4-bit on the newest GPUs); quantization is moving a model to fewer bits, after training (post-training quantization, PTQ) or with the model learning to cope during training (quantization-aware training, QAT). The whole subject keeps returning to one question: how many bits can each number lose before the model's outputs change, and the answer depends less on the bit count than on how the scale is shared and what the outliers do.

Number formats

How a float is built (a sign, an exponent that sets the range, a mantissa that sets the precision, subnormals below the smallest normal number) and the float formats ML trains in, fp32, tf32, fp16, bf16 and FP8 E4M3 and E5M2, each with its limits computed from the bit layout and what it is for, are owned by {{Numerical computing for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d81d6af0be4a9a2edba8d}} (section 4; section 3 there covers the gaps between neighbouring floats). This page starts from the consequence: the fewer the bits, the fewer binades and the coarser the steps, so formats below 16 bits only work with a scale shared by a tensor, a row or a block. The formats built around that shared scale, every limit computed from the bit layout derived and agreeing with the specifications (E2M1 6.0, OCP MX spec v1.0, §5.3):

The same seven floating formats on one axis: pale is the subnormal tail, solid the normal range. bf16 and fp32 span the same 2−126 to 2128; fp16 covers 40 binades; E4M3 under 18; E2M1 under 4. That is why the 8- and 4-bit formats only work with a scale chosen per tensor, per row or per block.

Mixed-precision and FP8 training

Compute-heavy operations run in low precision; numerically sensitive state stays high (Micikevicius et al. 2017): float32 master weights and optimiser state, because otherwise small updates round away; matrix multiplies in fp16 or bf16 on tensor cores, with reductions, softmax and norms often in fp32; and, in fp16 only, loss scaling, because fp16 gradients underflow. bf16 has fp32's exponent range and skips loss scaling entirely: this is why bf16 became the default. Each of those failures is measured on a real model's 134.5 million gradients, with a loss-scale slider and PyTorch's GradScaler rules, in {{Numerical computing for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d81d6af0be4a9a2edba8d}} (section 11). Try 1e-8 in the {{Bit explorer|#t-bits}}: fp16 stores 0, bf16 keeps it.

The bytes. The master copy is where the 16 bytes per parameter of Adam training come from: 2 (bf16 weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 + 4 + 4 (fp32 master, first and second moments) derived, as in {{ZeRO|n:3c65c17b0d0d81879a7ed90bca007699}}.

FP8 training (Hopper and Blackwell with NVIDIA's Transformer Engine): matmul inputs in E4M3 or E5M2 with a scale per tensor or per block; weights and gradients accumulate in higher precision. The split is "E4M3 for weight and activation tensors, and E5M2 for gradient tensors" (FP8 formats, Micikevicius et al. 2022), which Transformer Engine's HYBRID recipe does exactly. Its original "delayed scaling" uses the scale from the previous iteration and an amax history (default 1,024 steps) (TE docs). DeepSeek-V3 showed fine-grained FP8 pretraining at 671B: 1 × 128 tiles for activations, 128 × 128 blocks for weights, E4M3 on all tensors, partial sums promoted to FP32 every 128 elements, and "the relative loss error of our FP8-training model remains consistently below 0.25%" against BF16 (§3.3; the {{DeepSeek-V3 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}} animates its tile and block scaling and the accumulation). In PyTorch's torchtitan, Float8 is a configuration flag: on Llama 3.1 8B with FSDP on 8 H100s, torch.compile plus Float8 reached 9,409 tokens per second, +50.35% over plain FSDP; +65.08% on 128 GPUs (torchtitan, Tables 1 and 2). Adding Float8 on top of compile (6,674 tokens per second) is 9,409 / 6,674 = +41% derived.

Correction
The old page said torchtitan and Transformer Engine give "about 30 to 40% throughput gains" from FP8. torchtitan's own tables give +41% for adding Float8 to a compiled run and +50% for compile plus Float8 over eager, on 8 GPUs, rising to +65% on 128 (link above).

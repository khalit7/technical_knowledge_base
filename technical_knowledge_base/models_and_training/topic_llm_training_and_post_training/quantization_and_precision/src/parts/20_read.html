Every number in a language model is stored in some format, and fewer bits per number means less memory, less memory traffic and faster matrix multiplies. Precision is the choice of format during training (bf16 by default, FP8 and now 4-bit on the newest GPUs); quantization is moving a model to fewer bits, after training (post-training quantization, PTQ) or with the model learning to cope during training (quantization-aware training, QAT). The whole subject keeps returning to one question: how many bits can each number lose before the model's outputs change, and the answer depends less on the bit count than on how the scale is shared and what the outliers do.

Number formats

A floating-point number is a sign, an exponent and a mantissa: the exponent sets the range (how large and how small a number can be), the mantissa the precision (how finely numbers are spaced: the relative step between neighbours is 2−m for m mantissa bits). Below the smallest normal number, subnormals trade mantissa bits for a gentler slide to zero. An integer format has no exponent at all: evenly spaced levels, and a separate scale that maps them onto the real numbers. Every limit in this table is computed from the bit layout derived, and agrees with the specifications: E4M3 448 and E5M2 57,344 (FP8 formats, Micikevicius et al. 2022, Table 1), E2M1 6.0 (OCP MX spec v1.0, §5.3), fp16 65,504.

The same seven floating formats on one axis: pale is the subnormal tail, solid the normal range. bf16 and fp32 span the same 2−126 to 2128; fp16 covers 40 binades; E4M3 under 18; E2M1 under 4. That is why the 8- and 4-bit formats only work with a scale chosen per tensor, per row or per block.

Mixed-precision training

Compute-heavy operations run in low precision; numerically sensitive state stays high (Micikevicius et al. 2017):

  1. fp32 master weights and optimiser states, with low-precision working copies for the forward and backward passes. Without the master copy, small updates round away: on a Mandarin speech model about 5% of weight gradients have exponents below −24, which fp16 cannot hold, and dropping the fp32 copy costs "80% relative accuracy loss" (§3.1). This is where the 16 bytes per parameter of Adam training come from: 2 (bf16 weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 + 4 + 4 (fp32 master, first and second moments) derived, as in {{ZeRO|n:3c65c17b0d0d81879a7ed90bca007699}}.
  2. Matrix multiplies in fp16 or bf16 on tensor cores; reductions, softmax and norms often in fp32.
  3. Loss scaling (fp16 only): gradients underflow fp16, whose smallest subnormal is 2−24, so the loss is multiplied by a factor S before the backward pass, which scales every gradient by S through the chain rule, and the gradients are divided by S in fp32 before the optimiser step. In the paper, an SSD detector "diverges when gradients are not scaled, but scaling them by a factor of 8 (increasing the exponents by 3) is sufficient"; the factors used ranged "from 8 to 32K". The paper proposes, without running it, a dynamic version that lowers S and skips the step on overflow; that is what frameworks now do. bf16 has fp32's exponent range, so it skips loss scaling entirely: this is why bf16 became the default. Try 1e-8 in the {{Bit explorer|#t-bits}}: fp16 stores 0, bf16 keeps it.
  4. FP8 training (Hopper and Blackwell with NVIDIA's Transformer Engine): matmul inputs in E4M3 or E5M2 with a scale per tensor or per block; weights and gradients accumulate in higher precision. Transformer Engine's original "delayed scaling" uses the scale from the previous iteration and an amax history (default 1,024 steps) (TE docs). DeepSeek-V3 showed fine-grained FP8 pretraining at 671B: 1 × 128 tiles for activations, 128 × 128 blocks for weights, E4M3 on all tensors, partial sums promoted to FP32 every 128 elements, and "the relative loss error of our FP8-training model remains consistently below 0.25%" against BF16 (§3.3; the {{DeepSeek-V3 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}} animates its tile and block scaling and the accumulation). In PyTorch's torchtitan, Float8 is a configuration flag: on Llama 3.1 8B with FSDP on 8 H100s, torch.compile plus Float8 reached 9,409 tokens per second, +50.35% over plain FSDP; +65.08% on 128 GPUs (torchtitan, Tables 1 and 2). Adding Float8 on top of compile (6,674 tokens per second) is 9,409 / 6,674 = +41% derived.
Correction
The old page said torchtitan and Transformer Engine give "about 30 to 40% throughput gains" from FP8. torchtitan's own tables give +41% for adding Float8 to a compiled run and +50% for compile plus Float8 over eager, on 8 GPUs, rising to +65% on 128 (link above).

2. Sharing a scale: per tensor, per channel, per block

Integer and 4-bit formats store a code and a scale: q = round(x / scale) + zero_point, and the value read back is (q − zero_point) × scale. The scale is set by the largest magnitude in the group it covers (absmax; asymmetric schemes also fit a zero point to the minimum), so one large value sets the step for everything that shares its scale. That is why the choice of scope matters more than the bit count. The stage runs four real rows of the hero's layer through five scopes, chosen because its largest weight, −1.664 in input column 5, is 64 times the layer's standard deviation (0.026) measured here.

  1. Four real rows. Rows 223 and 222 of the layer hold its two largest weights, −1.664 and −1.289, both in input column 5; rows 448 and 0 are ordinary, their largest in view 0.044 and 0.028. Height grows with √|w| so the small weights stay visible: drawn to scale, the outlier would be 60 times row 0's tallest bar.
  2. One scale per tensor. The largest of all 802,816 weights sets one step for every weight: 1.664 / 7 = 0.238. The bars drop to the values stored, and an orange ball marks a weight stored as 0: the ordinary rows store all 64 as 0 (100% error), and even row 223 loses 28.0%, with 47 of its 64 weights zeroed.
  3. One scale per row (per channel). Each output row's own largest weight sets its step, wherever it sits in its 896 columns. The ordinary rows recover (13.5% and 15.4% error, 7 and 6 zeros); rows 223 and 222 do not (28.0%), because their outlier is in the row. One fp16 scale per 896 weights costs 0.02 bits.
  4. One scale per 32 weights. The outlier now spoils only its own block: block 0 of rows 223 and 222 keeps 20.1% and 21.1% error while block 1 beside it falls to 12.1% and 11.2%, and the ordinary rows reach 8.9% and 8.8%, for 4.5 bits per weight (the layout of llama.cpp's Q4_0). The tiles' tint is each block's error, green low and orange high.
  5. MXFP4: a power-of-two scale per 32. The camera moves in on block 0. The E8M0 scale is 2 to the floor of log2(largest) minus 2, and anything above 6 times it clips (purple): the outlier itself, stored as −1.500 (scale 0.25), and 1 weight of row 0 (scale 0.0039, 2−8). Row 0 ends at 12.4% error, against 8.8% for INT4 with a scale per 32, at 4.25 bits.
  6. NVFP4: an FP8 scale per 16. An E4M3 block scale (times one FP32 scale for the tensor) puts each block's largest on 6 almost exactly, over blocks half as long: rows 223 and 222 fall to 9.6% and 10.4%. On the ordinary rows it is no better than INT4 per 32 here (10.0% and 10.5%), because E2M1's top steps are a third of the largest value (4 to 6); over the whole layer it wins (the chart below).

Errors are ‖q − w‖ / ‖w‖ over each row's 64 columns shown measured here. The quantisers are ported exactly from src/qformats.py and follow the specs: INT4 symmetric absmax; MXFP4 with the OCP shared exponent floor(log2(amax)) − 2 (Rouhani et al. 2023, Algorithm 1); NVFP4 with an E4M3 block scale and a per-tensor FP32 scale of amax / (448 × 6).

The scale costs bits. Bits per weight = element bits + scale bits / block size derived: INT4 with an fp16 scale and zero per 128 is 4 + 32/128 = 4.25; MXFP4 4 + 8/32 = 4.25 (MXFP8 8.25); NVFP4 4 + 8/16 = 4.5, plus one FP32 per tensor; NF4 with double quantisation 4 + 8/64 + 32/(64 × 256) = 4.127; llama.cpp's Q4_K block 4.5 and a whole Q4_K_M file about 4.9. Budget memory on these effective bits, never on the nominal 4.

MXFP4 against NVFP4 (steps 5 and 6): an E8M0 scale is a power of two, so a block's largest value lands anywhere between 4 and 8 times the scale and clips above 6, where NVFP4's E4M3 scale puts it on 6. MXFP4's advantage is portability: it is an open standard that also runs on AMD (the MI355X, per Spheron's guide, a vendor blog that calls its own accuracy table directional).

INT4 per tensor leaves 84% output error, per channel 17%, a scale per 128 weights 7.8% and per 16 (NVFP4) 6.2%, for 0.25 to 0.5 extra bits per weight. At 8 bits the answer to the prediction flips with the scope: with one scale per tensor, FP8 E4M3 leaves 1.9% and INT8 10.5%, because a float spaces its levels by magnitude, so one outlier does not starve the small weights; with a scale per channel, INT8 wins (0.76%). Granularity and format interact. Converting an ordinary checkpoint to MXFP4 by round-to-nearest is lossier than NVFP4 or INT4 with group scales (9.3% against 6.2% and 7.8%); gpt-oss was released in MXFP4, which is a different case (section 7). 3. Post-training quantisation: GPTQ and AWQ

PTQ quantises a trained model with no retraining. fp32 to fp16 or bf16 is essentially free; integer and 4-bit formats need the scales of section 2, and activations need a choice of when to set them: dynamic PTQ computes them on the fly per batch or token (a little latency, no calibration data), static PTQ freezes them from a calibration set (lower latency; inputs beyond the frozen range clip). Weight-only methods are the LLM mainstream because generating one token at a time is memory-bound: the time goes into streaming weights, so shrinking them speeds decoding up even if the multiply still runs in 16-bit. When the matmul itself is the bottleneck, FP8 W8A8 is the default (section 7).

Round-to-nearest treats each weight alone. GPTQ (Frantar et al. 2022) quantises a layer one column at a time and, after each column, adjusts the columns not yet quantised to cancel the error in the layer's output, using approximate second-order (Hessian) information from calibration activations (H = 2XTX, with one row of X per calibration token). When two input channels carry similar signals, an error rounded into one can be paid back by the other, as Fig. 4 steps through:

  1. Four weights, a grid, six calibration inputs. One output row w = (0.60, −0.88, −0.76, 0.20) stands in front of the grid of representable values (step 0.25, the purple lines). In front lies H = 2XTX from six calibration inputs in which channels 1 and 2 rise and fall together: H12 = 6.56 against H11 = 6.68. Round-to-nearest would store 0.50, −1.00, −0.75, 0.25.
  2. Column 1. w1 rounds to 0.50, an error of 0.100. GPTQ cancels its effect on the output by moving the columns not yet quantised, weighted by the inverse Hessian (the dots, as many as the move): w2 gets the largest, +0.097, because channel 2 carries almost the same signal as channel 1, and is now −0.783.
  3. Column 2. w2 now rounds to −0.75, where round-to-nearest gives −1.00: the error pushed into it from column 1 changed which way it rounds. Its own error, −0.033, moves w3 and w4 a little.
  4. Column 3. w3 rounds to −0.75 (error −0.016), and the last column absorbs the rest.
  5. Column 4, and the result. w4 rounds (error −0.070). GPTQ stores 0.50, −0.75, −0.75, 0.25; the orange ticks are round-to-nearest. On the calibration inputs the output error is 8.5% against 32.9%, with a slightly higher weight error (13.0% against 12.4%): GPTQ spends weight error where it does not reach the output.

illustrative Four weights and six inputs, so every number fits on screen; the update is the paper's Algorithm 1: H = 2XTX plus 1% of its mean diagonal (dampening), U the upper Cholesky factor of H−1, and after quantising column j, wk ← wk − (wj − qj) / Ujj × Ujk for every later k derived by src/visuals_ref.py.

GPTQ is built on Optimal Brain Quantization with lazy batch updates and a Cholesky reformulation; it quantised 175B models "in approximately four GPU hours" to 3 or 4 bits. Act-order (quantising columns in decreasing order of activation size) is not in the paper; it came from the GPTQ repository, where it lowered LLaMA-7B's WikiText-2 perplexity from 7.15 to 6.09 (IST-DASLab/gptq).

AWQ (Lin et al. 2023, MLSys 2024 best paper) starts from "protecting only 1% salient weights can greatly reduce quantization error", and picks the salient channels from the activation distribution, not the weights. Rather than keep them in high precision, it multiplies those input channels by a scale s = sXα before 4-bit quantisation (α by grid search over [0, 1]) and folds 1/s into the previous operation; group size 128. It is the weight-side half of section 4's migration, animated there.

Fig. 5 · Lower weight error is not the goal: GPTQ and AWQ raise it and lower the output error

The 4.25-bit schemes on the whole layer measured here: round-to-nearest with groups of 128 gives 7.80% output error, AWQ 6.20% and GPTQ with act-order 6.12%, while both methods raise the plain weight error (from 10.8% to 12.8% and 18.1%): they spend error where the activations are small. Weight error is not output error, which is why both need calibration data.

Below 4 bits the data matters as much as the method. At 2 bits scalar PTQ (one value per weight, as above) collapses to chance on QA tasks; vector codebooks, which quantise groups of weights to entries of a learned codebook, plus jointly chosen calibration and recovery-training data reached about 2.3 effective bits at 89 to 97% of full-precision QA accuracy on 3.8B to 32B dense and MoE models, with 1.4 to 30 times less compute than the best quantisation-aware baseline ({{Beyond 4 bits (arXiv 2610.28537)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28537}}).

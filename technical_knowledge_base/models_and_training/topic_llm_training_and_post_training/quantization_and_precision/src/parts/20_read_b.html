Post-training quantization (PTQ)

Quantize a trained model with no retraining. fp32 to fp16 or bf16 is essentially free. Integer and 4-bit formats need a mapping, q = round(x / scale) + zero_point, with the scale (and, for asymmetric schemes, a zero point) chosen per tensor, per channel or per block. The choice of scope matters more than the bit count, because one large value sets the step for everything that shares its scale.

  • Dynamic PTQ: activation scales computed on the fly from each batch (or each token); simpler, a little latency overhead, no calibration data.
  • Static PTQ: run a small calibration set through the model, record activation statistics, freeze the scales; lower latency, and brittle when real inputs drift from the calibration set (values beyond the frozen range clip).
  • Weight-only methods are the LLM mainstream, because generating one token at a time is memory-bound: the time goes into streaming weights, so shrinking weights speeds it up even if the multiply still runs in 16-bit.

The weight-only methods

  • GPTQ (Frantar et al. 2022): quantize a layer one column at a time and, after each column, adjust the not-yet-quantized columns to cancel the error in the layer's output, using approximate second-order (Hessian) information from calibration activations. Built on Optimal Brain Quantization with lazy batch updates and a Cholesky reformulation; it quantized 175B models "in approximately four GPU hours" to 3 or 4 bits. Act-order (quantizing columns in decreasing order of activation size) is not in the paper; it came from the GPTQ repository, where it lowered LLaMA-7B's WikiText-2 perplexity from 7.15 to 6.09 (IST-DASLab/gptq).
  • AWQ (Lin et al. 2023, MLSys 2024 best paper): "protecting only 1% salient weights can greatly reduce quantization error", and the salient channels are picked from the activation distribution, not the weights. Rather than keep them in high precision, it multiplies those input channels by a scale s = sXα before 4-bit quantization (α by grid search over [0, 1]) and folds 1/s into the previous operation; group size 128.
  • llama.cpp K-quants and I-quants (GGUF) and bitsandbytes NF4: the ecosystem workhorses, decoded in Reading a quantisation name. Unsloth's Dynamic 3.0 GGUFs (19 August 2026, first for Qwen3.8-27B) are the next iteration of its dynamic scheme: a broader imatrix calibration set "refined for agentic coding, chat, and multilingual performance", better layer selection, and "everything is done through post-training quantization", no QAT; Unsloth reports more than 10% better top-1 accuracy at the same size than other providers (docs, date) vendor's figure.
  • SmoothQuant (W8A8; Xiao et al. 2022): migrate activation outliers into the weights so both quantize well (next section). FP8 W8A8 is now the low-effort serving default on Hopper and Blackwell: vLLM's FP8-dynamic uses static per-channel weight scales and dynamic per-token activation scales, needs no calibration data, and gives "a 2x reduction in model memory requirements and up to a 1.6x improvement in throughput" (vLLM).

Outliers, the central difficulty

A few channels with huge magnitudes destroy naive scaling. In activations they are a property of the channel, recurring on every token: LLM.int8() found features "up to 20x larger than in other dimensions" in about 25% of layers of small models, and "at around 6.7B parameters, a phase shift occurs" where all layers and 75% of sequence positions are affected, though outliers remain "about 0.1% of all features" (Dettmers et al. 2022; its fix keeps those features in fp16, decomposing the matmul at a threshold of 6.0). In the Qwen layer used on this page, the largest input channel peaks at 31.9 against a median channel peak of 2.0, a 16-fold gap measured here. The fixes are finer scales (per channel, per block), moving the difficulty somewhere easier (SmoothQuant, AWQ), compensating for it (GPTQ), or rotating it away (QuIP#, QuaRot, SpinQuant).

"Whole layer" repeats the procedure on all 896 channels and 511 evaluation tokens (AWQ with groups of 128) in src/quant_lab.py; the first token of each text is left out because it carries "massive activations" (Sun et al. 2024).

On the whole layer measured here: plain W8A8 with a static per-tensor activation scale gives 4.73% output error; SmoothQuant brings it to 1.70% at its best α of 0.75, better than FP8-dynamic (2.45%) at the same 8 bits. The paper found the same need to tune α: 0.5 for OPT and BLOOM, 0.75 for GLM-130B, 0.8 for LLaMA-1, 0.85 for Llama-2-7B and 13B, 0.9 for Llama-2-70B, with which "SmoothQuant can match the FP16 accuracy" on OPT-175B (Xiao et al.; its "up to 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction" was measured on OPT-13B and 30B on one GPU, while for 66B and 175B the claim is similar speed on half the GPUs). For 4-bit weights, round-to-nearest with groups of 128 gives 7.80%, AWQ 6.20% and GPTQ with act-order 6.12%, while both methods raise the plain weight error (from 10.8% to 12.8% and 18.1%): they spend error where the activations are small. Weight error is not output error, which is why both need calibration data. The curves and the full table are in {{One real layer, every scheme|#t-layer}}.

Rotations spread outliers across all channels before quantizing: multiply weights and activations by an orthogonal matrix (often a randomised Hadamard transform) whose inverse cancels in the product, so no channel stands out. They are what makes 2 to 3 bit weights and 4-bit activations work: QuIP# (randomised Hadamard plus E8 lattice codebooks plus fine-tuning) has "QuIP# 3 bit" beating "a theoretical lossless 4 bit model" on Llama 2 (Tseng et al. 2024); QuaRot's W4A4 Llama 2-70B with a 4-bit KV cache loses "at most 0.47 WikiText-2 perplexity" and keeps 99% of zero-shot performance (Ashkboos et al. 2024); SpinQuant learns the rotation, narrowing W4A4KV4 LLaMA-2 7B's zero-shot gap to 2.9 points (Liu et al. 2024). NVFP4 pretraining uses the same trick (below).

Ternary: where the floor is

Ternary weights take only −1, 0 and +1 (times a scale). That is where the compression floor currently sits, and the quality cost is smaller than the bit count suggests for models built ternary. Ternary Bonsai 2 27B (PrismML, 17 September 2026), "based on Qwen3.8 27B", "uses ternary {−1, 0, +1} weights with FP16 group-wise scaling, for 1.76 effective bits per weight and a total model footprint of 5.9GB", "more than 9x smaller while retaining 98.2% of aggregate benchmark performance": it scores 83.9 against Qwen3.8 27B's 85.4, with a 262K-token context, text and image input, agentic tool use in its benchmark suite, Apache 2.0, and up to 143 tokens per second on an RTX 5090 (PrismML, model card). The headline numbers check out derived: 27 × 109 × 1.76 / 8 = 5.94 GB; 16 / 1.76 = 9.1 times; 83.9 / 85.4 = 98.2%.

The nominal floor is not 1.585 bits either. Three values carry log2 3 = 1.585 bits; packing five trits in a byte (35 = 243 fits in 256) gives 1.6, and with power-of-two group sizes it "rounds up to 1.625", because it treats the three values as equally likely. They are not: across 29 ternary models, zeros are "up to 51.5% of all weights". BITCOS stores "a dense presence bitmap plus a compacted sign vector" and "costs 2 − z bits per weight element given a zero density z": it beats five-trit packing in 26 of the 29 models, reaches 1.485 bits on the sparsest, and decodes up to 1.18× faster on CPUs and 1.27× on GPUs (Georganas et al. 2026). 2 − z beats 1.625 once zeros pass 37.5%, and beats log2 3 once they pass 41.5% derived. For comparison, llama.cpp's own ternary types cost 1.6875 (TQ1_0: 54 bytes per 256 weights, scale included) and 2.0625 bits (TQ2_0) from the block structs; BitNet b1.58 is the model family that made ternary training work (Ma et al. 2024).

What this is not
These models were built ternary rather than converted, so their bits per weight are a packing result, not a reason to quantize an ordinary checkpoint this far. An ordinary dense model rounded to under 3 bits falls off a cliff (next section).

Quantization and Precision

How numbers are stored in training and serving: the formats, post-training quantization and the outlier problem, how to read a quantisation name, quantization-aware training, mixed-precision and FP8 training, and the 4-bit frontier. Every number in a language model is stored in some format, and fewer bits per number means less memory, less memory traffic and faster matrix multiplies. Precision is the choice of format during training (bf16 by default, FP8 and now 4-bit on the newest GPUs); quantization is moving a model to fewer bits, after training (post-training quantization, PTQ) or with the model learning to cope during training (quantization-aware training, QAT). The whole subject keeps returning to one question: how many bits can each number lose before the model's outputs change, and the answer depends less on the bit count than on how the scale is shared and what the outliers do.

Number formats

A floating-point number is a sign, an exponent and a mantissa: the exponent sets the range (how large and how small a number can be), the mantissa the precision (how finely numbers are spaced: the relative step between neighbours is 2−m for m mantissa bits). Below the smallest normal number, subnormals trade mantissa bits for a gentler slide to zero. An integer format has no exponent at all: evenly spaced levels, and a separate scale that maps them onto the real numbers. Every limit in this table is computed from the bit layout derived, and agrees with the specifications: E4M3 448 and E5M2 57,344 (FP8 formats, Micikevicius et al. 2022, Table 1), E2M1 6.0 (OCP MX spec v1.0, §5.3), fp16 65,504.

Mixed-precision training

Compute-heavy operations run in low precision; numerically sensitive state stays high (Micikevicius et al. 2017):

  1. fp32 master weights and optimiser states, with low-precision working copies for the forward and backward passes. Without the master copy, small updates round away: on a Mandarin speech model about 5% of weight gradients have exponents below −24, which fp16 cannot hold, and dropping the fp32 copy costs "80% relative accuracy loss" (§3.1). This is where the 16 bytes per parameter of Adam training come from: 2 (bf16 weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 + 4 + 4 (fp32 master, first and second moments) derived, as in ZeRO.
  2. Matrix multiplies in fp16 or bf16 on tensor cores; reductions, softmax and norms often in fp32.
  3. Loss scaling (fp16 only): gradients underflow fp16, whose smallest subnormal is 2−24, so the loss is multiplied by a factor S before the backward pass, which scales every gradient by S through the chain rule, and the gradients are divided by S in fp32 before the optimiser step. In the paper, an SSD detector "diverges when gradients are not scaled, but scaling them by a factor of 8 (increasing the exponents by 3) is sufficient"; the factors used ranged "from 8 to 32K". The paper proposes, without running it, a dynamic version that lowers S and skips the step on overflow; that is what frameworks now do. bf16 has fp32's exponent range, so it skips loss scaling entirely: this is why bf16 became the default. Try 1e-8 in the Bit explorer: fp16 stores 0, bf16 keeps it.
  4. FP8 training (Hopper and Blackwell with NVIDIA's Transformer Engine): matmul inputs in E4M3 or E5M2 with a scale per tensor or per block; weights and gradients accumulate in higher precision. Transformer Engine's original "delayed scaling" uses the scale from the previous iteration and an amax history (default 1,024 steps) (TE docs). DeepSeek-V3 showed fine-grained FP8 pretraining at 671B: 1 × 128 tiles for activations, 128 × 128 blocks for weights, E4M3 on all tensors, partial sums promoted to FP32 every 128 elements, and "the relative loss error of our FP8-training model remains consistently below 0.25%" against BF16 (§3.3; the DeepSeek-V3 paper page animates its tile and block scaling and the accumulation). In PyTorch's torchtitan, Float8 is a configuration flag: on Llama 3.1 8B with FSDP on 8 H100s, torch.compile plus Float8 reached 9,409 tokens per second, +50.35% over plain FSDP; +65.08% on 128 GPUs (torchtitan, Tables 1 and 2). Adding Float8 on top of compile (6,674 tokens per second) is 9,409 / 6,674 = +41% derived.

Post-training quantization (PTQ)

Quantize a trained model with no retraining. fp32 to fp16 or bf16 is essentially free. Integer and 4-bit formats need a mapping, q = round(x / scale) + zero_point, with the scale (and, for asymmetric schemes, a zero point) chosen per tensor, per channel or per block. The choice of scope matters more than the bit count, because one large value sets the step for everything that shares its scale.

  • Dynamic PTQ: activation scales computed on the fly from each batch (or each token); simpler, a little latency overhead, no calibration data.
  • Static PTQ: run a small calibration set through the model, record activation statistics, freeze the scales; lower latency, and brittle when real inputs drift from the calibration set (values beyond the frozen range clip).
  • Weight-only methods are the LLM mainstream, because generating one token at a time is memory-bound: the time goes into streaming weights, so shrinking weights speeds it up even if the multiply still runs in 16-bit.

The weight-only methods

  • GPTQ (Frantar et al. 2022): quantize a layer one column at a time and, after each column, adjust the not-yet-quantized columns to cancel the error in the layer's output, using approximate second-order (Hessian) information from calibration activations. Built on Optimal Brain Quantization with lazy batch updates and a Cholesky reformulation; it quantized 175B models "in approximately four GPU hours" to 3 or 4 bits. Act-order (quantizing columns in decreasing order of activation size) is not in the paper; it came from the GPTQ repository, where it lowered LLaMA-7B's WikiText-2 perplexity from 7.15 to 6.09 (IST-DASLab/gptq).
  • AWQ (Lin et al. 2023, MLSys 2024 best paper): "protecting only 1% salient weights can greatly reduce quantization error", and the salient channels are picked from the activation distribution, not the weights. Rather than keep them in high precision, it multiplies those input channels by a scale s = sXα before 4-bit quantization (α by grid search over [0, 1]) and folds 1/s into the previous operation; group size 128.
  • llama.cpp K-quants and I-quants (GGUF) and bitsandbytes NF4: the ecosystem workhorses, decoded in Reading a quantisation name. Unsloth's Dynamic 3.0 GGUFs (19 August 2026, first for Qwen3.8-27B) are the next iteration of its dynamic scheme: a broader imatrix calibration set "refined for agentic coding, chat, and multilingual performance", better layer selection, and "everything is done through post-training quantization", no QAT; Unsloth reports more than 10% better top-1 accuracy at the same size than other providers (docs, date) vendor's figure.
  • SmoothQuant (W8A8; Xiao et al. 2022): migrate activation outliers into the weights so both quantize well (next section). FP8 W8A8 is now the low-effort serving default on Hopper and Blackwell: vLLM's FP8-dynamic uses static per-channel weight scales and dynamic per-token activation scales, needs no calibration data, and gives "a 2x reduction in model memory requirements and up to a 1.6x improvement in throughput" (vLLM).

Outliers, the central difficulty

A few channels with huge magnitudes destroy naive scaling. In activations they are a property of the channel, recurring on every token: LLM.int8() found features "up to 20x larger than in other dimensions" in about 25% of layers of small models, and "at around 6.7B parameters, a phase shift occurs" where all layers and 75% of sequence positions are affected, though outliers remain "about 0.1% of all features" (Dettmers et al. 2022; its fix keeps those features in fp16, decomposing the matmul at a threshold of 6.0). In the Qwen layer used on this page, the largest input channel peaks at 31.9 against a median channel peak of 2.0, a 16-fold gap measured here. The fixes are finer scales (per channel, per block), moving the difficulty somewhere easier (SmoothQuant, AWQ), compensating for it (GPTQ), or rotating it away (QuIP#, QuaRot, SpinQuant).

On the whole layer measured here: plain W8A8 with a static per-tensor activation scale gives 4.73% output error; SmoothQuant brings it to 1.70% at its best α of 0.75, better than FP8-dynamic (2.45%) at the same 8 bits. The paper found the same need to tune α: 0.5 for OPT and BLOOM, 0.75 for GLM-130B, 0.8 for LLaMA-1, 0.85 for Llama-2-7B and 13B, 0.9 for Llama-2-70B, with which "SmoothQuant can match the FP16 accuracy" on OPT-175B (Xiao et al.; its "up to 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction" was measured on OPT-13B and 30B on one GPU, while for 66B and 175B the claim is similar speed on half the GPUs). For 4-bit weights, round-to-nearest with groups of 128 gives 7.80%, AWQ 6.20% and GPTQ with act-order 6.12%, while both methods raise the plain weight error (from 10.8% to 12.8% and 18.1%): they spend error where the activations are small. Weight error is not output error, which is why both need calibration data. The curves and the full table are in One real layer, every scheme.

Rotations spread outliers across all channels before quantizing: multiply weights and activations by an orthogonal matrix (often a randomised Hadamard transform) whose inverse cancels in the product, so no channel stands out. They are what makes 2 to 3 bit weights and 4-bit activations work: QuIP# (randomised Hadamard plus E8 lattice codebooks plus fine-tuning) has "QuIP# 3 bit" beating "a theoretical lossless 4 bit model" on Llama 2 (Tseng et al. 2024); QuaRot's W4A4 Llama 2-70B with a 4-bit KV cache loses "at most 0.47 WikiText-2 perplexity" and keeps 99% of zero-shot performance (Ashkboos et al. 2024); SpinQuant learns the rotation, narrowing W4A4KV4 LLaMA-2 7B's zero-shot gap to 2.9 points (Liu et al. 2024). NVFP4 pretraining uses the same trick (below).

Ternary: where the floor is

Ternary weights take only −1, 0 and +1 (times a scale). That is where the compression floor currently sits, and the quality cost is smaller than the bit count suggests for models built ternary. Ternary Bonsai 2 27B (PrismML, 17 September 2026), "based on Qwen3.8 27B", "uses ternary {−1, 0, +1} weights with FP16 group-wise scaling, for 1.76 effective bits per weight and a total model footprint of 5.9GB", "more than 9x smaller while retaining 98.2% of aggregate benchmark performance": it scores 83.9 against Qwen3.8 27B's 85.4, with a 262K-token context, text and image input, agentic tool use in its benchmark suite, Apache 2.0, and up to 143 tokens per second on an RTX 5090 (PrismML, model card). The headline numbers check out derived: 27 × 109 × 1.76 / 8 = 5.94 GB; 16 / 1.76 = 9.1 times; 83.9 / 85.4 = 98.2%.

The nominal floor is not 1.585 bits either. Three values carry log2 3 = 1.585 bits; packing five trits in a byte (35 = 243 fits in 256) gives 1.6, and with power-of-two group sizes it "rounds up to 1.625", because it treats the three values as equally likely. They are not: across 29 ternary models, zeros are "up to 51.5% of all weights". BITCOS stores "a dense presence bitmap plus a compacted sign vector" and "costs 2 − z bits per weight element given a zero density z": it beats five-trit packing in 26 of the 29 models, reaches 1.485 bits on the sparsest, and decodes up to 1.18× faster on CPUs and 1.27× on GPUs (Georganas et al. 2026). 2 − z beats 1.625 once zeros pass 37.5%, and beats log2 3 once they pass 41.5% derived. For comparison, llama.cpp's own ternary types cost 1.6875 (TQ1_0: 54 bytes per 256 weights, scale included) and 2.0625 bits (TQ2_0) from the block structs; BitNet b1.58 is the model family that made ternary training work (Ma et al. 2024).

What this is not
These models were built ternary rather than converted, so their bits per weight are a packing result, not a reason to quantize an ordinary checkpoint this far. An ordinary dense model rounded to under 3 bits falls off a cliff (next section).

Reading a quantisation name

Everything above is the method; this section is the notation: how to decode a quant string on a Hub repo without opening config.json.

The GGUF grammar

The pattern is the letter Q, a nominal bit count, an underscore, and a variant tag. The variant tag carries the information.

  • _0 and _1 (Q4_0, Q5_1, Q8_0): the legacy flat formats. A block is 32 weights with one fp16 scale (_0, symmetric) or a scale plus a minimum (_1, asymmetric). No second level of scaling. Superseded for everything except Q8_0, which survives because it is trivially fast to decode.
  • K_S, K_M, K_L (Q4_K_M): the K-quants, introduced in June 2023 (llama.cpp #1684), and the two-level super-block scheme. A super-block is 256 weights, cut into 8 blocks of 32 (Q4_K, Q5_K) or 16 blocks of 16 (Q2_K, Q3_K, Q6_K). The super-block carries one fp16 scale, plus one fp16 minimum for the asymmetric types; each inner block carries its own 4-, 6- or 8-bit sub-scale (and sub-minimum) relative to the super-block value. The scales are themselves quantised, which is the whole trick: group-wise granularity without paying a full fp16 scale per group. Same idea as GPTQ and AWQ group scaling, taken one level further (and as QLoRA's double quantisation).
  • The S/M/L suffix is not a different block format. It is a per-tensor mixing recipe baked into llama.cpp, derived from measured layer sensitivity (llama-quant.cpp). Q4_K_M promotes attn_v and ffn_down to Q6_K in the first eighth of layers, the last eighth and every third layer between, exactly half of a 32-layer model derived from use_more_bits. Q4_K_S is mostly Q4_K but not uniformly: attn_v in the first 4 layers and ffn_down in the first eighth go to Q5_K. In both, the output tensor is Q6_K. Value and down projections get the extra bits because they are empirically the most quantisation-sensitive, the same finding AWQ acts on when it protects salient channels.
  • IQ plus bits plus XXS/XS/S/M/NL (IQ2_XXS, IQ3_M, IQ4_XS): the importance-matrix family. Most are not scaled integers at all: they are codebook (lattice, lookup) quantisers whose bit allocation is steered by an imatrix, which is what makes 2- and 3-bit models usable. XXS to M orders by size within a bit width. NL means non-linear: a 16-entry non-uniform codebook over 32-weight blocks (ggml-common.h). Decode is slower than a K-quant because it is a table lookup rather than a multiply.

Effective bits per weight, which is never the nominal number

Two overheads sit between the name and the file size. First, block bookkeeping: Q4_K costs 144 bytes per 256 weights, so its block format is exactly 4.5 bits per weight, not 4. Second, the mixing recipe plus the token-embedding and output tensors, which llama.cpp keeps at higher precision. So a Q4_K_M file lands near 4.9 bits per weight, and Q2_K near 3.2, well above its 2.625 block format.     The importance matrix

    An imatrix is a per-tensor record of how much each weight column influences the output, collected by running a calibration corpus through the fp16 model with llama-imatrix and accumulating the sum of squared activations feeding each column (acc[j] += x[j] * x[j] in imatrix.cpp; README). The quantiser then minimises activation-weighted error rather than raw weight error, spending its scarce bits where they change the output: the same lesson as AWQ and GPTQ in the layer tab.

    I-quants require one: their codebooks are far too coarse for uniform treatment to survive at 2 to 3 bits. K-quants can optionally use one, and increasingly do; Unsloth's Dynamic GGUFs are essentially an imatrix plus a hand-tuned per-tensor allocation.

    A bad calibration set fails sneakily: the model looks fine on the calibration distribution and quietly loses capability off it. An imatrix built from English WikiText yields excellent WikiText perplexity while code generation, non-English output and instruction-following in unusual formats degrade; too-short calibration contexts produce a model that falls apart at long context practitioner guidance, no single source. So never accept an uploader's reported perplexity as evidence for an I-quant, evaluate it on your own tasks, and when in doubt prefer a quant whose imatrix came from a broad mixed corpus (code, several languages, long documents) over one with a better-looking headline number.

Smaller g is finer and more accurate but adds scale overhead (4 + 32/128 = 4.25 bits at g128 with fp16 scale and zero derived); 32g is the quality end, 128g the usual compromise, no g means per channel. act-order (desc_act in the config) quantises columns in decreasing order of activation size so accumulated error lands on the columns that matter least. Better accuracy, historically incompatible with some fused kernels: "Some GPTQ clients have had issues with models that use Act Order plus Group Size, but this is generally resolved now" (TheBloke's card)
    awq-4bit-128g, AWQ-INT4AWQ, 4-bit weight-only, group size 128. Instead of reordering, it rescales the roughly 1% of salient channels (chosen by activation magnitude) before quantising. No reordering means simpler, faster kernels, which is why AWQ often wins on throughput at similar accuracy unconfirmed as a general rule. Qwen ships official AWQ checkpoints (for example Qwen3-8B-AWQ)
    nf4, fp4, load_in_4bit, bnb-4bitbitsandbytes. A runtime flag, not a checkpoint format: the fp16 weights are quantised on load. NF4 is a 16-level codebook fitted to a normal distribution over 64-weight blocks; "double quant" additionally quantises the block scales (4.127 bits per weight 4 + 8/64 + 32/(64 × 256)). This is the QLoRA base. Convenient, not competitive on serving throughput
    W4A16compressed-tensors and llm-compressor (the vLLM naming): "Quantizes weights to 4 or 8-bit integer precision, retaining activations in 16-bit", per group, "optimally compressed using non-RTN algorithms (GPTQ, AWQ) which require a dataset" (llm-compressor). Memory-bound win: right for low concurrency where you are streaming weights
    W8A8 (-INT8)Weights and activations both int8, so the matmul itself runs in int8. Needs calibration for static activation scales (or GPTQ/AWQ for the weights). The high-throughput, compute-bound choice on pre-Hopper hardware
    FP8-dynamicFP8 W8A8 with per-channel weight scales and per-token activation scales computed at runtime. No calibration set required. The low-effort near-lossless default on Hopper and Blackwell (vLLM). vLLM can also do it online from a bf16 checkpoint with a per-tensor scale
    FP8-staticSame, but the activation scales are frozen from a calibration pass. Marginally faster (no per-token reduction), and marginally more brittle: a distribution shift away from the calibration data shows up as clipping. llm-compressor lists it as an option of its FP8 scheme rather than a separate name. FP8_BLOCK uses 128 × 128 weight tiles and per-128 activation groups, DeepSeek-V3's recipe
    NVFP4, MXFP4 checkpointsBlackwell-native 4-bit. NVFP4: a global scale per tensor and FP8 E4M3 scales per 16, and it "requires a calibration dataset to calibrate activation global scales"; MXFP4: E8M0 scales per 32, "no calibration data required if using RTN", "though RTN accuracy may be lower" (llm-compressor). See the 4-bit frontier
    Trailing -KV8, -FP8-KVNot about the weights at all: the KV cache is stored quantised (vLLM: kv_cache_dtype="fp8", per-tensor scales of 1.0 without calibration, per-head scales with it; docs). Orthogonal to the weight scheme, and often the bigger memory win at long context

    On the dual 5090s

    Spend the memory budget on parameters, not on bits: a bigger model at Q4_K_M beats a smaller model at Q8_0 almost every time practitioner rule, and the 4-bit rule of thumb of roughly 0.6 GB per billion parameters tells you which model that is: 4.8944 / 8 = 0.61 GB per billion at Q4_K_M, and the README's own Llama 3.1 sizes (4.9 GB at 8B, 43.1 GB at 70B, 249.1 GB at 405B) all come to 0.61 derived. The cliff for dense models sits below about 3 bits, where the drop stops being a perplexity number and becomes visible as broken reasoning chains and format drift (on Llama-3-8B, Q2_K's +3.52 is 5 times Q3_K_M's +0.66). MoE is different: the experts tolerate much harder quantisation than the attention and shared layers, which is why 100B-class MoEs run acceptably at 4-bit on 64 GB when a dense 70B at the same bits per weight would be more compromised; gpt-oss ships only its MoE weights, "90+% of the total parameter count", in MXFP4 (gpt-oss card), and llama.cpp's MXFP4_MOE type keeps "other tensors" at Q8_0. But note what the two 5090s are: Blackwell SM120 with native FP8 and NVFP4 tensor cores, while GGUF's K-quants are a CPU-friendly design that dequantises to fp16 before the matmul. For single-stream interactive work the difference is small and llama.cpp's flexibility wins; once concurrency matters, an FP8 or NVFP4 checkpoint under vLLM uses hardware the GGUF path never touches, and the gap becomes large. Fit and residency arithmetic for these cards is in Ollama, llama.cpp, and local serving; what the container around them is, in Model formats: GGUF, safetensors, ONNX, and the rest.

    Quantization-aware training (QAT)

    Insert fake-quantization operations during training: quantize then dequantize in the forward pass, so the loss sees the rounding, and pass the gradient straight through the rounding in the backward pass (the straight-through estimator, Bengio et al. 2013), so the model learns weights that survive the eventual precision. It costs extra training compute and recovers most of PTQ's loss at 4 bits and below. Current practice: short QAT fine-tunes after PTQ (torchao), official QAT checkpoints (Google's Gemma 3; Meta's Llama 3.2 1B and 3B), and "QAT from scratch" for models native to 4 bits or fewer, such as the ternary models above. Qwen ships official AWQ, GPTQ-Int4 and FP8 checkpoints but no QAT ones, as of this check (Qwen docs). QLoRA-style training (frozen NF4 base plus bf16 adapters) is the budget alternative: it trains adapters on a quantized base rather than teaching the base to be quantized (PEFT, QLoRA).

    The 4-bit frontier (2025 to 2026)

    Blackwell tensor cores natively support MXFP8, MXFP4 and NVFP4, moving 4-bit from a storage trick to a compute format: "Tensor Cores deliver FP4 computations at 2× (on GB200 chips) and 3× (on GB300 chips) higher math throughput rates compared to FP8" (NVIDIA 2025, §2).

    • NVFP4 pretraining (NVIDIA 2025): a 12B hybrid Mamba-Transformer trained on 10T tokens in NVFP4 matched its FP8 baseline closely (MMLU-Pro 62.58 against 62.62; MMLU 76.57 against 77.36; HumanEval+ 57.43 against 59.93). It needed four things: random Hadamard transforms (16 × 16, on the weight-gradient inputs) to smooth outliers; 2D 16 × 16 block scaling for weights so the forward and backward passes see the same quantised weights; stochastic rounding on gradients; and keeping some layers in BF16 (the first two and the last eight blocks, 16% of the linear layers).
    • NVFP4 against MXFP4: NVFP4's 16-element blocks with an E4M3 scale beat MXFP4's 32-element power-of-two blocks on quality. In the same paper's 8B experiment, MXFP4 matched NVFP4's loss only with 36% more tokens (1.36T against 1T), and its loss gap to BF16 was about 2.5% against NVFP4's 1.5%. The animation above shows why on one block: an E8M0 scale is a power of two, so a block's largest value lands anywhere between 4 and 8 times the scale and above 6 it clips, while E4M3 puts it on 6 almost exactly. MXFP4's advantage is portability: it is an open standard that also runs on AMD (the MI355X, per Spheron's guide, a vendor blog that calls its own accuracy table directional).
    • Low-precision RL is live (LMSYS, 29 July 2026): in the Miles framework (SGLang rollouts, Megatron and Transformer Engine training), MXFP8 runs end to end, "rollout, forward propagation, weight-gradient GEMMs, and data-gradient GEMMs"; NVFP4 covers only the MoE experts in rollout and forward, with the backward pass in high precision and the rest of the model (shared experts, the last 15% of layers) in BF16. On Qwen3-30B-A3B with GRPO, "all five low-precision reward curves closely track the BF16 reward curve", although "both low-precision formats show higher train-inference mismatch than BF16". Both cut rollout time; MXFP8 training is faster than BF16, the NVFP4 backward variants slower in that implementation.
    • Inference default stack, 2026: bf16, then FP8 W8A8 (near-lossless), then NVFP4 or INT4 weight-only for memory-bound serving; MoE models quantize their experts aggressively. NVFP4 is about 3.5 times smaller than FP16 and 1.8 times smaller than FP8 (NVIDIA; 16 / 4.5 = 3.56, 8 / 4.5 = 1.78 derived). llama.cpp's ggml has begun adding an NVFP4 block type too (ggml-common.h).
    Corrections
    The old page described NVFP4 pretraining as "matching-quality training with 2-3x matmul speedups": the 2 to 3 times is the hardware's peak FP4 rate against FP8 on GB200 and GB300, not a speedup measured in the paper. It put the MXFP4 "36% more tokens" result without its setting: it is the 8B experiment, 1.36T against 1T tokens. And it said low-precision RL reduces "the rollout/trainer precision mismatch that destabilises RL": the LMSYS post reports that both low-precision formats show higher train-inference mismatch than BF16, with reward curves that still track BF16, and that NVFP4 there is used for MoE experts in rollout and forward only, not for "rollouts with bf16 training" across the model.

    Common mistakes

    "bf16 is more precise than fp16"
    It is less precise (7 mantissa bits against 10, a relative step of 0.0078 against 0.00098). It wins for training because of range: its exponent is fp32's, so gradients do not underflow and no loss scaling is needed.
    "4-bit means 4 bits per weight"
    Scales cost bits: 4.25 for MXFP4 and INT4 g128, 4.5 for NVFP4 and Q4_K, about 4.9 for a whole Q4_K_M file. Budget memory on effective bits per weight, or use the calculator on Topic: llm-training-and-post-training.
    "Lower weight error means a better quantization"
    GPTQ and AWQ raise the plain weight error on the layer used here (10.8% to 18.1% and 12.8%) while lowering the output error (7.8% to 6.1% and 6.2%). What matters is error weighted by the activations each weight meets (One real layer, every scheme).
    "Integers are always better than floats at the same bits"
    With one scale per tensor, FP8 E4M3 left 1.9% output error on this layer and INT8 10.5%: floating point spaces its levels by magnitude, so one outlier does not starve the small weights. With a scale per channel, INT8 wins (0.76%). Granularity and format interact.
    "An uploader's perplexity proves the quant is fine"
    Perplexity on the calibration distribution hides losses off it, and schemes with similar perplexity diverge on instruction-following and reasoning (Kurt 2026). Evaluate on your own tasks.
    "Convert to MXFP4 for free speed"
    gpt-oss was released in MXFP4; converting an ordinary checkpoint into it with round-to-nearest is lossier than NVFP4 or INT4 with group scales (9.3% against 6.2% and 7.8% output error on this page's layer) and should be calibrated or fine-tuned.
    "Quantize from whatever file you have"
    Always quantize from the full-precision original: errors from an already-quantized file compound and cannot be recovered (Model formats).

    Bit explorer

    Type or slide a number and see what every format does with it: the bits it stores, the value you get back, the error, the gap to the next representable number at that magnitude, and what happens at the edges (subnormals, underflow to zero, overflow to infinity or saturation). It extends Reading: Number formats.

    Try: 1e-8, a tiny gradient: fp16 stores 0 and bf16 keeps it (why fp16 training needs loss scaling). 70000: fp16 overflows to infinity. 500: E4M3 saturates at 448 while E5M2 stores 512. 1 + 2^-8: bf16 cannot tell it from 1, fp16 can.

    Rounding is round-to-nearest-even with subnormals, as in hardware conversion. Formats with infinities (fp32, tf32, bf16, fp16, E5M2) overflow to infinity once past the largest value by half a step; E4M3 and E2M1 have none, and conversion kernels saturate. "Step here" is the gap between neighbouring values at this magnitude (one unit in the last place).

    Inside a block: one shared scale

    Block formats store each number as a small element times a scale shared with its neighbours, set by the block's largest magnitude. Set that largest magnitude and see where your value lands. A value much smaller than the block's largest loses most of its precision in every block format: that is the outlier problem from Reading: Outliers.

    MX scale per the OCP spec: 2 to the floor(log2(largest)) minus the element format's largest exponent (2 for E2M1, 8 for E4M3), so the largest element can exceed the element maximum and clip. NVFP4's second-level FP32 tensor scale is set as if this block held the tensor's largest value (amax / (448 × 6)), so the block scale is 448. NF4 codes from QLoRA Appendix E.

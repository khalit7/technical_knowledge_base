Sampling and Decoding

How a language model's next-token probabilities become text: greedy and beam search, temperature and truncation samplers, repetition controls, grammar-constrained output, and the techniques built on sampling. Every distribution on this page is real, computed offline from small open models. Part of Topic: llm-training-and-post-training; sources linked inline; as of 3 October 2026. 15 min read · +5h 20m resources

A language model does not write text. At each step it outputs one number per vocabulary entry, and a separate rule, the decoder, turns those numbers into the next token. The same model gives repetitive, bland, creative or broken text depending on that rule alone. This page is about the rule: searching for the most probable text, sampling from a reshaped distribution, forcing a format, and the inference techniques built on sampling.

Where this sits: the last stage of the lifecycle on Topic: llm-training-and-post-training (the parent compares the training stages; this page owns the inference end). Numbers are published, with the source linked, unless marked derived (formula shown), measured here (computed offline on a small open model, script in the page source), illustrative or unconfirmed.

From logits to a token

One forward pass ends in a vector of logits z, one per vocabulary entry: 151,936 for Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, the small open model whose real distributions fill the animations below, and 50,257 for GPT-2. The softmax turns them into probabilities, with a temperature T that rescales the logits first:

pi = exp(zi / T) / Σj exp(zj / T)
zi
logit of token i (only differences between logits matter)
T
temperature: T = 1 is the model's own distribution, T < 1 sharpens it, T > 1 flattens it towards uniform, T → 0 puts all the mass on the top token

A decoder then picks a token, appends it, and runs the model again. Decoders fall into two families. Search is deterministic and tries to find a high-probability sequence (greedy, beam search). Sampling draws at random from a reshaped distribution (temperature, then truncation by top-k, top-p, min-p and others). A third layer, constraints, removes tokens that would break a required format before either family chooses.

Deterministic search: greedy and beam

  • Greedy decoding takes the argmax token at every step. It is locally optimal and globally poor for open-ended text: repetitive and dull. It is still fine for short factual answers, and it is effectively what temperature 0 means.
  • Beam search keeps the B highest-probability partial sequences at every step: each is extended by its best next tokens, all candidates are scored by their total log-probability, and only the top B survive. It is the standard for machine translation and summarisation, where outputs are short and closed-ended. For open-ended generation it produces degenerate, repetitive, low-diversity text, because high probability is not the same as human-like (Holtzman et al., 2019), and chat LLMs essentially do not use it.

The animation runs both on the same real input: GPT-2 small continuing a sentence from Jerome K. Jerome's Three Men in a Boat (1889). Every score is the model's own log-probability.

Try: step to 4 in beam mode. Greedy's choice "was very" is still among the candidates but scores below three other branches, so beam search drops it; greedy can never revisit a choice. After six tokens the best beam is 6.7 times more probable than greedy's text measured here (exp(−7.54 + 9.44); script recompute.py).

Finding the more probable text does not make it better. Run both for 80 tokens on the same prompt measured here:

Both searches fall into a loop the model rates as highly probable; the sample wanders but does not repeat. This is the paradox Holtzman et al. named: "using likelihood as a decoding objective leads to text that is bland and strangely repetitive", and the per-token probability of natural text is "on average, much lower than text generated by beam search" (§4.3). On 5,000 WebText continuations with GPT-2 Large (762M) they measured perplexity 12.38 for human text against 1.50 for greedy and 1.48 for beam search with 16 beams, and 73.66% of greedy texts repeating (Table 1, paper). The Twelve decoders tab reruns that comparison on GPT-2 small.

Correction: temperature 0 is greedy, but greedy is not reproducible on a server
Greedy is deterministic for a fixed computation. A served model is not a fixed computation: the batch it shares with other requests changes the kernels' reduction order. Thinking Machines sent the same prompt 1,000 times to Qwen3-235B at temperature 0 and got 80 different completions, diverging at token 103; batch-invariant kernels made all 1,000 identical (He, September 2025). Anthropic's migration guide says the same of its API: "temperature = 0 ... never guaranteed identical outputs" (Opus 5.5 migration guide).

Stochastic sampling: the standard stack

Sampling draws the next token at random, but rarely from the raw distribution: pure sampling at T = 1 lets the long tail of unlikely tokens in, and a few bad draws derail the text (Holtzman et al. measured perplexity 22.73 for pure sampling against 12.38 for human text). The standard stack reshapes the distribution first, in this order: temperature, then truncation, then renormalise, then sample.

  • Temperature T: divide the logits by T before the softmax. T < 1 sharpens (more deterministic; T → 0 approaches greedy); T = 1 is the raw distribution; T > 1 flattens towards uniform (more random, more "creative"). Reasoning models often want T around 0.6 to 1.0 to keep exploring (settings below).
  • Top-k keeps only the k most probable tokens, renormalises and samples (Fan et al., 2018, who used k = 10 for stories). Its flaw: a fixed k ignores the shape of the distribution. When the distribution is sharp, k lets junk in; when it is flat, k cuts off good options.
  • Top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches p (for example 0.9), renormalises and samples (Holtzman et al., Eq. 2: the smallest V(p) with Σx∈V(p) P(x) ≥ p). The set adapts to the distribution; top-p was the long-time default for open-ended generation.
  • Min-p keeps tokens whose probability is at least pmin × P(top token), for example 0.05 to 0.1 of the maximum (Nguyen et al., ICLR 2025 oral). The cut scales with the model's confidence: confident steps become near-greedy, uncertain steps stay diverse. Its authors report better quality and diversity than top-p especially at high temperature; it is implemented in Hugging Face transformers, vLLM, SGLang and llama.cpp, and llama.cpp enables it by default (min-p 0.05, common.h). Whether it is better is disputed: see the box below.

Try: the confident step at T 1.5. Top-p 0.9 now keeps 5,166 tokens (exact full-vocabulary count; the animation's binned estimate says 5,209), because at T 1.5 "Canberra" falls from 96% to 59% and about 30% of the mass spreads thinly beyond the top 12 tokens, which top-p must collect back token by token measured here; min-p keeps only "Canberra". At the uncertain step and T 2, top-p keeps about 35,700 tokens and min-p 46. That is the whole argument for min-p, on a real distribution.

Disputed: "min-p beats top-p"
The min-p paper reports better quality and diversity on GPQA, GSM8K and AlpacaEval creative writing, and a human preference for min-p. A re-analysis by Schaeffer, Kazdan and Denisov-Blanch (June 2025) found omitted data and incorrect statistical tests in the human evaluation, found that "min-p did not outperform baselines in quality, diversity, or a trade-off between quality and diversity" once hyperparameter counts were controlled, and noted that the community-adoption figures were removed from the paper as unsubstantiated. What is not in dispute is the mechanism shown above: at high temperature, min-p's cut follows the top token and top-p's does not. Our own GPT-2 run in Twelve decoders agrees with the mechanism (at T 1.5, min-p 0.1 stays near human perplexity while top-p 0.95 explodes) but measures likelihood under the same model, not quality as judged by people.

The order is not universal

"Temperature, then truncation" is how Hugging Face transformers applies its warpers (temperature, top-k, top-p, min-p, typical) and how vLLM does it (penalties, temperature, min-p, then top-k and top-p). llama.cpp applies temperature last by default (penalties, DRY, top-n-sigma, top-k, typical, top-p, min-p, XTC, temperature), so its truncation sees the T = 1 distribution and temperature only reshapes the survivors. With llama.cpp's own defaults (top-k 40, top-p 0.95, min-p 0.05) at T 1.5, the uncertain step keeps 14 tokens with temperature last and 34 with temperature first measured here. The Sampler lab has the switch.

Repetition controls

Blunt instruments, needed less as models improved and still useful for small models:

  • Repetition penalty (CTRL, Keskar et al., 2019, who found θ ≈ 1.2 a good balance with greedy sampling): every token already in the context has its logit divided by θ if positive and multiplied by θ if negative (transformers' implementation, which counts the prompt too).
  • Presence and frequency penalties (the OpenAI-style pair): zj → zj − cj·αfrequency − [cj > 0]·αpresence, where cj counts how often token j was already sampled; "around 0.1 to 1" to reduce repetition somewhat, up to 2 to suppress it strongly at a cost in quality (OpenAI docs). Qwen3's model card suggests presence_penalty 0 to 2 against endless repetition, warning of language mixing at high values (card).
  • No-repeat n-gram bans any token that would complete an n-gram already generated (transformers' no_repeat_ngram_size).

They are blunt because they cannot tell a loop from a correct repeat. In the lab's "pattern" prompt (continue "red, blue, red, blue, red,") the right answer is a word already in the context; a repetition penalty of 1.3 pushes it down. Try it on the Sampler lab.

Diversity against quality

Every truncation trades diversity for staying in the model's high-probability region. The Twelve decoders tab measures the trade on GPT-2 small: four passages, 48 new tokens, 16 samples per decoder measured here. The pattern reproduces Holtzman et al.'s Table 1 independently, on a smaller model and different text:

  • Greedy and beam search sit far below human perplexity (2.3 and 2.4 against 31.0 for the real continuation) and repeat: 53% of greedy's 4-grams are repeats.
  • Pure sampling overshoots (47.2); top-p 0.95 at T 1 lands closest to human text (31.6 against 31.0), as nucleus sampling did in the paper (13.13 against 12.38).
  • Raise the temperature and top-p falls apart (perplexity about 3,900 at T 1.5) while min-p 0.1 stays near human text up to T 2 (16.1 at T 1.5, 40.0 at T 2).

Structured, or constrained, decoding

A constraint guarantees that the output matches a format (a JSON schema, a regular expression, a context-free grammar) by masking invalid tokens at every step: compile the constraint to an automaton over the tokenizer's vocabulary, intersect it with the current state, and set the disallowed logits to −∞ before the sampler runs. The animation runs Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on one request, with greedy decoding, without and with a grammar for {"name": string, "age": integer}.

The request is illustrative (a made-up person); everything else is the model's real output measured here. The grammar is a regular expression with whitespace capped at two characters between tokens of JSON, checked against all 151,665 tokenizer entries at every step; a token is allowed when the text so far plus the token can still complete a match. A tick marks tokens the grammar allows.

Try: step 0 in both modes. The model puts 57% on a Markdown fence ```, which no JSON parser accepts; the constrained run allows 4 tokens holding 36% of the mass and starts with {. Then step 2 of the constrained run: the model wants two spaces of indentation, the toy grammar's whitespace cap forbids the second, and it is forced onto a token it gave 0.0004%. The output is still valid JSON, with one-space indentation.

Three engines do this in production:

  • Outlines compiles a regular expression or JSON schema to a finite-state machine over tokens and indexes, for every state, which tokens are allowed: "transitions between the states of a finite-state machine" (Willard and Louf, 2023). It is the approach that popularised near-zero-overhead structured output.
  • XGrammar handles full context-free grammars with a pushdown automaton. It splits tokens into context-independent ones, whose validity depends only on the automaton's position and is precomputed, and context-dependent ones, checked at runtime against a persistent stack: for Llama-3.1 and a JSON grammar the context-dependent tokens are "less than 1% (1134 out of 128k)", cut to 120 by context expansion. It overlaps mask computation with the GPU's forward pass; masks take under 40 µs per token for JSON schema (Dong et al.). It is the default structured-output backend in vLLM (the "auto" backend tries XGrammar first and falls back to llguidance: sampling_params.py), in SGLang (serving_hook.py), TensorRT-LLM and MLC-LLM (README).
  • llguidance (behind Guidance) runs an Earley parser on top of a lexer built from regular-expression derivatives and computes masks lazily by walking a trie of the vocabulary, about 50 µs per token for a 128k tokenizer. "LLGuidance shipped in OpenAI for JSON Schema", and it is integrated in llama.cpp, vLLM (from 0.8.2), SGLang (from 0.4.4) and Chromium (README).

Provider-side "structured outputs" and strict function calling are this mechanism plus training. OpenAI's launch post: Structured Outputs works "by constraining OpenAI models to match developer-supplied schemas and by training our models to better understand complicated schemas"; the schema is converted to a context-free grammar; gpt-4o-2024-08-06 scored 100% on its complex-schema eval against under 40% for gpt-4-0613 (OpenAI, 6 August 2024, archived). JSON mode guarantees valid JSON; only Structured Outputs guarantees schema adherence (docs). Grammar-constrained tool-call parsing is standard in serving stacks.

Caveats
  • Constraining distorts the distribution. When the model wanted a token you masked, the rest of the answer is conditioned on text it would not have written. Schema design (field order, letting the model think before the JSON) mitigates it. The size of the effect is disputed: Tam et al., 2024 report "a significant decline in LLMs reasoning abilities under format restrictions"; the Outlines team re-ran their tasks with matched prompts and found structured generation slightly better (GSM8K 0.78 against 0.77, Last Letter 0.77 against 0.73, Shuffled Objects 0.44 against 0.41: .txt, "Say what you mean").
  • Whitespace and token boundaries are the classic bugs. A token can straddle a grammar boundary (": closes a key and starts the colon), whitespace rules decide which tokenisations are reachable (step 2 above), and an unbounded whitespace rule lets a model emit spaces forever.

Techniques built on sampling

  • Self-consistency and best-of-n sample n answers and return the majority answer (Wang et al., 2022) or the one a reward model or verifier rates highest. Best-of-n is also a baseline alignment method and a reward-hacking amplifier (Reward Hacking). The maths of pass@k, voting and best-of-n, with a sampling lab, is on Topic: llms, Deeper: test-time compute.
  • Speculative decoding: a cheap draft model (or n-gram lookup, or extra heads as in Medusa and EAGLE) proposes γ tokens, and the target model scores all of them in one forward pass. Each drafted token x is accepted with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)), p the target's and q the draft's probability; at the first rejection a replacement is drawn from the normalised max(0, p − q). The output distribution is exactly the target's, so the speed-up is lossless (Leviathan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023). With acceptance rate α, each target pass yields on average (1 − αγ+1) / (1 − α) tokens: 3.4 tokens at α = 0.8 and γ = 4 derived. Serving details are on Topic: inference-and-serving; a calculator that reproduces Leviathan's Table 4 is on Google DeepMind: Gemini and Gemma; an animation of one token at a time against speculative and block-diffusion decoding is in 2026-08-24: tech news.
  • Test-time compute scaling: longer chains of thought, and parallel sampling with a verifier reranking the samples. It is the decoding-side half of the reasoning-model story; the training-side half (RL with verifiable rewards) is on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR, and the comparison across labs on Topic: llms.

Common mistakes

"Temperature 0 makes the output reproducible"
It makes the rule deterministic, not the computation: batching on a shared server changes the numerics (80 distinct completions out of 1,000 in Thinking Machines' test, above).
"Top-p 0.9 keeps a handful of tokens"
Only at moderate temperature. At T 1.5 on a step where the model is 96% sure, top-p 0.9 keeps 5,166 of 151,936 tokens measured here; whatever temperature spreads out, top-p collects back.
"Min-p is proven better than top-p"
The mechanism is real; the evidence that it improves quality is disputed (Schaeffer et al., 2025). Tune both on your own task.
"The sampler order is fixed"
Hugging Face and vLLM apply temperature before truncation; llama.cpp applies it after by default. The same settings keep different token sets.
"Beam search finds better text"
It finds more probable text, which for open-ended generation is more repetitive text (the loops above). Use it for short closed-ended outputs.
"A repetition penalty is free"
It penalises every repeated token, including the correct ones (a name, a pattern, code identifiers).
"Constrained decoding cannot hurt quality"
It guarantees the format, not the content: when the grammar overrules the model (0.0004% in the example above) everything after is conditioned on text it would not have written.

What to try

  • Sharp against flat. Capital, position 6 ("Canberra" at 96%) against Cat, position 3 (a name, top token 7%). Set top-k 40: on the sharp step it keeps 39 tokens the model barely considers; on the flat one it may cut good names. Top-p and min-p adapt.
  • High temperature. Preset "T 2, top-p 0.95" against "T 2, min-p 0.1" on any uncertain step: 58,000 to 77,000 tokens against 16 to 177 at the steps tried measured here.
  • Order. llama.cpp defaults, then flip the order to "First" at T 1.5: the same numbers keep a different set.
  • Penalties hurt correct repeats. Pattern, position 7: the right token " blue" is already in the context. Repetition penalty 1.3 cuts its probability from 99.9% to 73.6%, because it appears three times in the context (the prompt counts). A frequency penalty of 1 barely moves it (99.7%): the margin is large and it was generated only once so far.
  • Typical sampling at 0.5 drops the single most likely token on four of the five uncertain steps tried (Lighthouse 1 and 2, Kitten 1 and 3), because its surprisal is far below the entropy.

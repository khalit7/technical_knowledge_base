The idea in one screen

Post-training keeps the network and changes what the gradient rewards, each stage on a narrower signal than the last: imitate a written answer (SFT), prefer the answer a judge ranked higher (a reward model plus RL, or the DPO family directly on pairs), then reach an answer a program accepts (RLVR).

You need the cross-entropy loss and a causal mask (assumed), the shape of a decoder-only GPT and its training loop (The transformer from zero: one block, every shape, a GPT you train), and for sections 4 and 7 a policy gradient with a baseline (Policy gradients and actor-critic). How a chat template is tokenized is on Tokenizers; this page shows what training does with it.

Base model
the pretrained model: it continues text, it does not answer.
SFT
supervised fine-tuning, also called instruction tuning: the language-model loss on (prompt, response) conversations.
Chat template
the markers that turn a conversation into one token sequence (<|user|>, <|assistant|>, end-of-text).
Loss mask
which tokens are graded; the others are read but give no gradient.
Policy, reference
the model being trained, and a frozen copy of where it started (usually the SFT model).
Reward model (RM)
a model that outputs one score per answer, trained from human or AI rankings.
KL penalty
a cost on drifting from the reference, β KL(π ‖ πref), so the policy cannot run off to wherever the reward is wrong.
Online, offline
trained on answers the current policy just sampled, or on a fixed dataset written before training.
Implicit reward
DPO's score, β log π/πref: how much more likely the policy makes an answer than the reference does.
RLVR, verifier
RL with verifiable rewards: a program (answer checker, unit tests) gives the reward instead of a learned model.

1. Supervised fine-tuning: which tokens it learns from

SFT is the pretraining loss on a different text: conversations written in a chat template, with the loss switched off on every token the model should not learn to produce. The model reads the whole conversation; only the assistant's tokens are graded. Llama 3: "a standard cross entropy loss on the target tokens (while masking loss on prompt tokens)" (Llama 3, §4.1). Ai2's open-instruct, the Tulu 3 code, sets the label of every non-assistant token to −100, the value the cross-entropy ignores, with the comment "mask the non-assistant part for avoiding loss" (open-instruct finetune.py at the Tulu 3 commit).

Which rule is right is not settled. Grading the prompt tokens too ("instruction modelling", template tokens excluded) helped "in many scenarios" across 21 benchmarks, most when instructions are long and answers short or examples few (Shi et al. 2024); with short answers, a small prompt-loss weight between 0.01 and 0.5 did best on multiple-choice and short-generation benchmarks, a weight near 1 on long-generation ones (Huerta-Enochian and Ko 2024). It should still be a choice, and frameworks make it for you: Hugging Face TRL computes the loss on the completion only for prompt-completion datasets, but its assistant_only_loss is off by default, so a plain conversational dataset trains on the user's turns too unless the template marks the assistant spans (TRL SFTTrainer).

Quality beats quantity, and the template is part of the model. LIMA fine-tuned a 65B LLaMa on 1,000 curated examples, with no RL, and people rated it equivalent to or better than GPT-4 in 43% of cases (LIMA): its "superficial alignment hypothesis" is that knowledge comes from pretraining and alignment selects the format. Production recipes still use far more, mostly curated and synthetic: Tulu 3 trained on 939,344 prompts for 2 epochs at a learning rate of 5e-6 (8B), an effective batch of 128 and sequences up to 4,096 tokens (Tulu 3, Tables 7 and 11). The template moved Tulu 3's average from 51.6 with the Llama 3 template to 53.0 with an end-of-text token in place of the newline after each answer, 1.4 points from formatting alone (Tulu 3, Table 13); a model served with a different template from the one it was trained on breaks.

Depth: epochs, and SFT from other sources (1 min)

InstructGPT's SFT set was about 13,000 prompts; its models "overfit on validation loss after 1 epoch", yet 16 epochs still raised the RM score and human preference (InstructGPT, §3.5), so SFT validation loss is a poor stopping rule.

Production traffic as the dataset. NeoHorse-1 is the online variant of this step: a router over a pool of models predicts each request's capability demand, those predictions order a three-stage supervised curriculum, and served interactions become training examples after structural validation, a semantic evaluation and labels for sub-scenes within a conversation, finer than one label per conversation, which is what makes supervising a partial trajectory work. Its gains, the ten benchmarks and the caveat that the loop ran once are on the parent page's Axis 2 and the NeoHorse-1 paper page (live curriculum scheduler in its "Schedule the curriculum" tab).

2. Batching SFT: padding, packing, and how much each token counts

Conversations differ wildly in length. In 1,000 rows sampled from the released Tulu 3 SFT mixture the median is 493 tokens and the mean 930; 24 of the 1,000 exceed the 4,096-token limit derived. Padded to its longest member, a batch is mostly padding: 36.3% of the computed positions are real tokens at 8 conversations per micro-batch, 28.2% at 16. Packing concatenates conversations into full rows instead: best-fit packing of the same 1,000 into 4,096-token rows fills 99.8% of each row. But with an ordinary causal mask a token then attends to every earlier conversation in its row: 61.8% of the causal attention pairs in packed rows cross a conversation boundary derived.

Eight real conversations, before and after packing

  1. Eight conversations. Eight real rows of the same sample, 159 to 1,283 tokens (Tulu 3 tokenizer and template), drawn to scale against a 4,096-token row (the faint slots). The pale part of each is the prompt, read but not graded; the solid part is the graded answer: 2,967 of the 4,779 tokens.
  2. Before: pad every row to the longest. One conversation per row, padded to 1,283 tokens (grey): 10,264 positions computed for 4,779 real tokens, 46.6% real. Attention stays inside each row, so this is correct, and the GPU throws the rest away.
  3. After: pack them into full rows. Best-fit decreasing: the longest goes first into the row with the least room that still fits it. The eight fit in 2 rows of 4,096, 8,192 positions, 58.3% real; with only eight the second row is mostly empty, while over the whole 1,000-row sample packed rows are 99.8% full.
  4. A plain causal mask leaks. With the ordinary causal mask every token attends to the conversations packed before it in its row (the red streams): 75.3% of the attention pairs computed in these two rows cross a boundary, 61.8% over the whole sample. The model learns from text it will never see together at inference time.
  5. Restart at each boundary. Restart the position ids at each conversation and give the kernel the boundaries (FlashAttention's variable-length mode): each conversation attends only to itself, exactly as if it had its own row, and no padding is computed.
  6. How much each token counts: the mean per conversation. Height is now the gradient weight of one graded token. Averaged within each conversation and then over the eight, every conversation gets 12.5% of the gradient whatever its length: one token of #7’s 23-token answer weighs 46.9 times one of #4’s 1,078.
  7. Sum over tokens, as Tulu 3 does. Every graded token weighs the same, so each answer's share is its length: #7 falls from 12.5% to 0.8%, #4 rises to 36.3%. Packing changes the number of conversations per step, so keep the loss a sum over tokens for the step size to mean the same thing.

Before: one conversation per row, padded to the longest; after: packed into full rows, first with a plain causal mask, then with boundaries. Lengths are the conversations' real token counts; the graded tokens are drawn as one block after the prompt for clarity. Tulu 3 itself did neither: its reproduction command runs one conversation per GPU per step with gradient accumulation, so it pads nothing and packs nothing (docs/tulu3.md, open-instruct).

How the drawing simplifies (under 1 min)

In a real multi-turn row the graded spans interleave with the prompts. Packing is best-fit decreasing, as TRL's default bfd; the hatched end of a packed row is drawn as padding to a fixed 4,096, whereas padding-free packing (TRL, the HF collator) flattens the batch and computes no padding at all.

The fix is to restart the position ids at each conversation and pass the boundaries to a variable-length attention kernel, so each conversation sees only itself; Hugging Face's DataCollatorWithFlattening does this with FlashAttention 2 and reported up to 2 times the throughput and 20% less peak memory on FLAN (Hugging Face, 2024). Without a variable-length kernel, keep padding.

Sum or mean: how much each token counts. If the loss is averaged within each conversation and the conversations are then averaged (what gradient accumulation over one conversation at a time does), every conversation counts equally, so a token of a short answer weighs as much as many tokens of a long one. In the sample, the answers at the 10th and 90th percentiles carry 21 and 1,187 graded tokens: under per-conversation averaging, a token of the short one weighs 56.5 times as much as a token of the long one derived, 1,187 / 21.

With only those two answers in a batch, averaging per conversation gives a 21-token answer half the gradient; under the sum its share is 21 / (21 + 1,187) = 1.7% derived. Steps 6 and 7 of the figure show the same on the eight-conversation batch, with height the weight of one token and volume each answer's share.

Tulu 3 traced a gap between its own SFT runs and TPU runs to exactly this ("averaging the loss across padding tokens without taking into account gradient accumulation or distributed training setups") and switched to summing the loss, which "effectively weights all tokens equally", with a retuned learning rate (Tulu 3, §4.3.2 "Batch Aggregation"); the open-instruct code comments that sum reduction "can result in > 5 point improvements in AlpacaEval". Sum the loss over tokens, or normalise by the total token count of the whole accumulated batch.

Depth: the evidence on padding and cross-document attention (1 min)

Krell et al. found padding was 50% of all tokens in Wikipedia BERT pretraining at length 512, and packing doubled throughput (Krell et al. 2021). Masking within documents gave lower perplexity than plain causal masking across packed documents in pretraining (Zhao et al. 2024); Llama 3 found the cross-document mask of "limited impact" in standard pretraining but important for very long sequences (Llama 3, §3.2). TRL's "wrapped" packing, which cuts mid-conversation, "may mix unrelated examples" and its documentation says it hurts performance (TRL).

3. Reward models: turning rankings into a score

InstructGPT's labellers ranked K sampled answers per prompt (K = 4 to 9); a ranking of K answers gives K(K−1)/2 comparisons, 6 for K = 4. The reward model is usually the SFT model with its output layer swapped for one scalar head: InstructGPT started "from the SFT model with the final unembedding layer removed" (InstructGPT, §3.5). It is trained so the preferred answer scores higher, by the Bradley-Terry model: the probability that people prefer yw over yl is σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)).

Rankings become pairs; pairs become a score

  1. One prompt, four answers, one ranking. A labeller ranks K = 4 sampled answers, A > B > C > D (an illustrative ranking, as in InstructGPT's own figure; its labellers ranked 4 to 9).
  2. Six comparisons from one ranking. K(K−1)/2 = 6 pairs, each streaming from the winner to the loser. InstructGPT puts all six in one batch element, because shuffling them into the dataset made the RM overfit in a single pass.
  3. The reward model reads each answer. The SFT model with its unembedding swapped for one scalar head (the glass plate) reads the prompt and each answer and outputs one number. An untrained head gives all four the same score, and the loss is ln 2 = 0.693.
  4. Training pulls the scores apart. 200 steps of gradient descent on the six pair losses (step size 1, computed in src/recompute.py): A 4.71, B 1.48, C −1.48, D −4.71, loss 0.022. The order is the ranking's; with one ranking and no ties nothing stops the gap growing, since a hard label has no finite optimum.
  5. Only differences count. The chance that people prefer A to D is σ(rA − rD) = σ(9.43) = 99.99%. Lift all four scores by the same amount, as this step does, and every probability and the loss stay the same: a reward has no absolute zero, so an RL stage only ever sees how answers compare.

The ceiling is the people: training labellers agreed with each other 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time and the RM predicted their preferences 72.4 ± 0.4% of the time (held-out labellers: 69.6 ± 0.9%). RLAIF and Constitutional AI: who writes the labels

RLAIF keeps the pipeline and swaps the labeller for a strong model. In Google's controlled comparison, with PaLM 2 as the labeller, RLAIF and RLHF were preferred over SFT about equally (summarisation 71% against 73%, helpful dialogue 63% against 64%, differences not significant), and RLAIF was more harmless (88% against 76%) (Lee et al. 2023). AI labels scale far better than people, and open recipes now build their pairs with an LLM judge: Tulu 3 rates every response with GPT-4o on helpfulness, instruction following, honesty and truthfulness (Tulu 3, §5); some frontier recipes still pay for people, as Llama 3 trained its reward model "using human-annotated preference data" (Llama 3, §4.1.2). RLAIF does not remove people: they write the constitution, the judge's prompt, the rubrics and the evaluation. It moves human effort from labelling to specification.

Constitutional AI (Anthropic, 2022) is a structured RLAIF in two phases. Supervised: the model critiques and revises its own harmful answers against principles from a written constitution (16 critique and revision pairs), and a model is fine-tuned on the revisions. RL: a feedback model, prompted with one of 16 principles, picks the less harmful of two answers; a preference model is trained on those AI labels and the policy is optimised against it (arXiv 2212.08073). The paper page replays the released critique chains, counts the refusals, and shows why soft labels from a feedback model have a finite optimum and hard 0/1 labels do not. Anthropic published a new, much longer constitution for Claude in January 2026 (Anthropic).

Corrections
The old page said RLAIF "is now the norm for most labels". No source states that; the evidence is a trend in open recipes (UltraFeedback, Tulu 3), while Llama 3 used human preference annotations unconfirmed as a general norm. It also described Constitutional AI's RL phase as AI labels "judged against the constitution" without qualification: only the harmlessness labels were AI-written; the helpfulness labels stayed human, so 57% of the preference model's data, and all of its harmlessness data, was labelled by a model (paper page, §4.2).

4. RL against a reward: what PPO and GRPO do in the pipeline

With a reward model in hand, RL optimises the policy against it: sample answers, score them, compare each score with a baseline (the advantage), and take a clipped policy-gradient step with a KL penalty to the reference. RLHF with PPO (InstructGPT, Llama 2; GPT-4 was also trained with RLHF, GPT-4 report) gets its baseline from a learned value model (critic) that estimates the expected return of each partial answer: four models in memory, powerful, notoriously fiddly and memory-hungry. GRPO (DeepSeekMath) drops the critic: it samples a group of G answers per prompt and gives each the advantage (its reward − group mean) / group standard deviation.

GRPO replaces the critic with the group: one model fewer

  1. Four models in memory. The policy (trained), a frozen reference (the SFT model), the reward model (frozen, section 3) and the critic, a second trainable model about the size of the policy. InstructGPT and Llama 2 ran this.
  2. Sample. A prompt from the RL set streams into the policy, which samples one answer, token by token.
  3. Score, on a leash. The reward model scores the whole answer, one number at its end; the reference reads the same tokens, and the KL penalty subtracts wherever the policy has drifted from it.
  4. The critic's baseline. The critic predicts the return of each partial answer; the advantage is what the answer earned beyond that prediction (GAE, derived on RL for LLMs).
  5. Update two models. A clipped policy-gradient step on the policy and a regression step on the critic, streaming back into both; the reference and the reward model stay frozen.
  1. No critic. GRPO drops the value model: three models in memory, the policy, the reference and the reward model (two with a verifier, section 7).
  2. Sample a group. The policy samples G answers to the same prompt, six here.
  3. Score each. The reward model gives each answer one number: 0.80, 0.30, −0.20, 1.10, −0.60, 0.20 (illustrative scores).
  4. The group is the baseline. Advantage = (r − group mean) / group standard deviation: mean 0.27, standard deviation 0.57, so the advantages are +0.93, +0.06, −0.82, +1.46, −1.52, −0.12 derived. An answer above its group's average is reinforced whatever the absolute score.
  5. Update the policy. Answers above the group mean are made more likely and those below less, with the same clipped step and KL to the reference. No critic to train or store; the price is G samples per prompt, and a group whose answers all score the same gives no signal.

The plates are the models; the streams show what flows between them in one step. Heights of rewards, KL and values in the PPO view are illustrative; the GRPO advantages are computed from the scores shown (population standard deviation).

With a verifier instead of a reward model (section 7) GRPO is the workhorse of open reasoning RL: DeepSeek-R1 (paper page) and Qwen3's reasoning RL use it (Qwen3, §4.2). Its biases spawned named fixes (Dr. GRPO, DAPO, GSPO, MiniMax-M1's CISPO), each derived on RL for LLMs, sections 3 and 7, with PPO's GAE and clipping in section 2.

Depth: the GRPO fixes in one line each, and where each algorithm runs live (1 min)

Dr. GRPO removes the per-response length normalisation that lets long wrong answers off lightly and the standard-deviation division that over-weights too-easy and too-hard prompts (arXiv 2503.20783); DAPO decouples the clip range (clip-higher), drops groups with no spread (dynamic sampling), averages over tokens and shapes overlong answers (arXiv 2503.14476); GSPO clips one length-normalised ratio per sequence, for stability on long answers and mixture-of-experts models (arXiv 2507.18071); CISPO "clips importance sampling weights rather than token updates" (arXiv 2506.13585).

5. Direct preference optimisation and its family

DPO removes the reward model and the RL loop: the policy becomes its own reward model. The step that makes it possible: the KL-constrained RLHF objective, maximise E[r(x, y)] − β KL(π ‖ πref), has a closed-form optimum, π*(y | x) = πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y)/β) / Z(x). Solved for the reward, any reward is β log(π*/πref) plus a term that depends only on the prompt (Eq. 5). Put that into the Bradley-Terry loss of section 3 and the prompt term cancels, because only differences of rewards appear. What is left trains the policy directly on (chosen, rejected) pairs (DPO, Eq. 7):

LDPO = −E [ log σ( β log πθ(yw|x) / πref(yw|x) − β log πθ(yl|x) / πref(yl|x) ) ]

One trained model plus a frozen reference (whose log-probabilities can be computed once and cached), no sampling, a classification loss: stable and cheap, which is why it spread from Zephyr to Llama 3. The paper's headline, a 61% TL;DR win rate against PPO's 57%, rests on single runs about one standard error apart (the DPO paper page works out the errors and trains DPO against RLHF on an enumerable toy with an exact optimum). How the toy is built (under 1 min)

Each token's log-probability is log σ(θ) of its own parameter, the shared prefix sharing one, so the bars move independently; a real model shares parameters across answers, which is where likelihood displacement comes from. Hyperparameters are each paper's defaults: DPO β = 0.1; cDPO ε = 0.1 illustrative, the note gives no default; IPO τ = 0.1 illustrative; length-normalised DPO β = 5 (Tulu 3); SimPO β = 2, γ = 1 (its Llama-3-Base row); ORPO λ = 0.1 (its Mistral setting); KTO β = 0.1, λD = λU = 1, with its reference point z0 taken as the clipped mean log-ratio of the two answers (the paper estimates it from mismatched pairs in the batch). Training: 400 steps of plain gradient descent, the step size fixed so that every method's largest first step is the same, so differences afterwards come from each loss's shape. The push on a pair against its margin: only a target or a hinge lets it reach zero

Why some losses stop and others do not. Almost every successor keeps DPO's frame (a margin between the chosen and the rejected answer, measured against a reference) and changes one thing: how hard a pair is pushed as a function of its margin. That one curve decides whether training stops. The figure draws the push −∂ℒ/∂h on one pair against its margin h = log πθ/πref(yw) − log πθ/πref(yl) in nats (before any β), one loss per step, with what each did when trained for 4,000 steps on the same deterministic labels on the DPO paper page's enumerable toy (its Run tab trains them).

Predict before stepping: which losses still push a pair that is already ranked right by 10 nats?

  1. Unlikelihood: push every pair at full strength (2019, the DPO paper's baseline). −log π(yw) + α log π(yl) (DPO paper, App. C.3). The push does not depend on the margin at all, and nothing ties the policy to πref. On the toy the grammar is gone by 4,000 steps (0.3% grammatical), the paper's "when when when" in miniature.
  2. DPO: push fades as the margin grows, but never stops (May 2023). −log σ(βh). The weight σ(−βh) is the paper's key ingredient; but it only reaches zero at an infinite margin, so with near-deterministic preferences the margins keep growing and the KL penalty is effectively ignored (Azar et al.'s critique). At β = 0.1 the push is still 75% of its starting value at a margin of 5 nats. On the toy the KL keeps climbing: 2.06 at 1,200 steps, 7.47 at 4,000.
  3. SLiC-HF's hinge: full push until a fixed margin, then none (May 2023, concurrent). max(0, δ − βh), here with δ = 1 and β = 0.5, so the push stops at h = 2 (SLiC-HF calibrates sequence likelihoods this way, with an extra SFT term not run here). A pair that is ordered well enough is left alone.
  4. IPO: pull every margin to a target, from either side (October 2023). (h − 1/(2τ))². The push is linear and changes sign at the target margin 1/(2τ) = 5 nats, so a pair can be pushed back. Azar et al. derive it as the identity case of their ΨPO objective, which needs no Bradley-Terry assumption. On the toy it is the only loss whose KL nearly levels off: 1.26 at 1,200 steps, 1.52 at 4,000, against DPO's climb from 2.06 to 7.47.
  5. Conservative DPO: assume ε of the labels are flipped (November 2023 note; rDPO March 2024). (1 − ε) ℒDPO(yw, yl) + ε ℒDPO(yl, yw): the gradient is zero when σ(βh) = 1 − ε (Mitchell's note), at h = ln 9 / β ≈ 22 nats for ε = 0.1, β = 0.1. A finite stopping point, but far away; the toy is still drifting at 4,000 steps.
  6. SimPO: no reference model, a length-averaged margin and a target gap (May 2024). −log σ(β/|yw| log π(yw) − β/|yl| log π(yl) − γ). Dropping πref saves memory and aligns the reward with how text is generated (average log-probability), but nothing anchors the policy except early stopping: on the toy, where every answer has 4 words so the length fix cannot help, it degenerates fastest (0.2% grammatical).

Curves at the toy's settings: β = 0.1 for DPO and cDPO (ε = 0.1), β = 0.5 for the hinge, τ = 0.1 for IPO, β = 2 and γ = 1 for SimPO (whose margin is the reference-free, length-averaged log-probability gap), each push scaled to 1 at h = 0. Formulas from each paper; the IPO and cDPO forms are those of the authors' code (trainers.py). Answer: DPO, cDPO (until about 22 nats), SimPO and unlikelihood still push; the hinge has stopped at 2 and IPO pushes the pair back towards 5. The margin here is unscaled, so cDPO's stopping point ln 9 / β is 22 nats, the same point as the 2.20 of the pair animation above, which scales by β. mk_lfx_data.py copies the toy outcomes from the DPO page.

Same letter, different units. DPO's β = 0.1 multiplies a summed log-ratio; SimPO's β = 2 and length-normalised DPO's β = 5 multiply per-token averages; IPO's τ sets a target margin. Never carry a β from one loss to another.

A rising margin is not a better model. DPO's margin grows by pushing the rejected answer down, often faster than the chosen goes up, and both can fall together, moving probability mass to a third answer (likelihood displacement): DPO on refusals cut Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate from 74.4% to 33.4% (Razin et al. 2024). In the animation, DPO ends with the rejected answer's log-probability far below where it started while the chosen moves much less. Evaluate generations, not margins.

In practice the data matters more than the loss. DPO-family results are sensitive to label noise and to how far apart the chosen and rejected answers are, the failure PLC-DPO targets. Recipes add small guards: Llama 3 masked the header and termination tokens out of both answers (otherwise "tail repetition or abruptly generating termination tokens") and added an NLL term of weight 0.2 on the chosen answers (Llama 3, §4.1.4). And regenerating the pairs from the current policy, judging them and retraining (iterative or online DPO) recovers much of the gap to PPO (next section).

6. Online or offline

Vanilla DPO optimises preferences on a fixed dataset: pairs written before training, by other models or by an earlier version of this one, so the data is stale and off-policy. Online methods (PPO, GRPO, online or iterative DPO) sample from the current policy and score those samples, so the model learns from its own mistakes. At equal data they have been consistently stronger, at a higher cost in compute and complexity.

  • Well-tuned PPO "consistently outperforms DPO across all experiments", including code, where a 34B PPO model raised CodeContests 10@1k from 16.4% to 22.4% (Xu et al. 2024).
  • At the same KL budget online methods hold a clear advantage; offline-trained policies classify pairs better but generate worse, and neither data coverage nor a larger policy closes the gap (Tang et al. 2024, Google DeepMind); on-policy samples and a negative gradient both help (Tajwar et al. 2024).
  • Iterating recovers much of it: three rounds of iterative DPO reached 24.17% length-controlled on AlpacaEval 2 (Xiong et al. 2023). So "DPO is RLHF without the downsides" is wrong: it drops the RM and the sampling loop, and with them on-policy learning.

The consensus: DPO-family losses for cheap, broad preference shaping; online RL (GRPO and PPO variants) where it matters most, for reasoning, agentic behaviour and the final polish. Section 8 shows how recipes combine them.

7. RLVR: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

RL needs some reward. When the answer is checkable (a maths answer checker, unit tests, a compiler, a rule-based format check, an instruction constraint) a program can give it, and the learned reward model, with its biases and its hackability, is bypassed. Tulu 3 named it: "Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)" "leverages the existing RLHF objective but replaces the reward model with a verification function": a reward of 10 when a verifier accepts the answer, 0 otherwise, optimised with PPO with a KL penalty, on 29,946 prompts for GSM8K, MATH and verifiable instruction following (IFEval) (Tulu 3, §6 and Table 22).

RLVR swaps one box: the reward model becomes a program

  1. Sample a group. The policy samples eight answers to this page's pens question ("How much do 12 pens cost?"); the answers drawn are illustrative, 3 of them right.
  2. A program checks each. The verifier extracts the final answer and compares it with the reference, $8: an accepted answer scores 10, the rest 0, Tulu 3's reward. Tulu 3 optimised it with PPO; the group here follows GRPO, as DeepSeek-R1-Zero did.
  3. No reward model in memory. The learned scorer of the RLHF loop (the fading plate) is gone. It was trained on rankings, so it had blind spots a policy could find, and it was one more model in memory. The verifier is exact on what it checks and blind to the rest: a reward that cannot be flattered, only gamed through the checker's gaps.
  4. Advantages from the group. Mean 3.75, standard deviation 4.84: each accepted answer gets +1.29, each rejected one −0.77 derived. Rewards of 1 and 0 would give exactly the same advantages: dividing by the standard deviation removes the reward's scale.
  5. A group with no spread teaches nothing. On a prompt too hard for the policy all eight fail: every reward 0, every advantage 0, no gradient at all. DAPO's dynamic sampling drops such groups (section 4).

The checker is the new proxy: narrower, harder to flatter, still gameable. An answer extractor that accepts malformed output, hard-coded outputs, edited tests or exiting before the assertions all score as passes, and the policy will find them (Reward Hacking; the RL for LLMs Verifier gallery tab shows what real checkers accept).

What it did. DeepSeek-R1-Zero ran GRPO with rule rewards (answer correctness and a format check) directly on DeepSeek-V3-Base, with no SFT; long chains of thought with self-checking emerged and AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (71.0% in arXiv v1). DeepSeek-R1 added a cold start of "thousands" of curated long reasoning examples before large-scale RLVR, for readability and to stop language mixing (arXiv 2501.12948; the paper page trains a tiny R1-Zero). Qwen3's reasoning RL ran GRPO on 3,995 query-verifier pairs, lifting Qwen3-235B-A22B's AIME'24 from 70.1 to 85.1 in 170 steps (Qwen3, §4.2). Ai2's Olmo 3 RL-Zero runs RLVR from a fully open base over maths, code, instruction following, general chat and a mix, with RL data decontaminated against the pretraining and midtraining data, so RL-from-base results can be trusted as a benchmark (Olmo 3, §6; Ai2 OLMo in Other notable providers).

A pass@1 gain is not yet a new capability. RLVR reliably converts pass@k into pass@1; whether it expands what the base model can solve at all is contested, so measure pass@k at large k before claiming it. That question and three more open in 2026 (rewarding what no program can check, credit over many agentic turns, entropy collapse in long runs) are owned with their evidence by RL for LLMs, sections 9, 4, 11 and 7, which also covers the training stacks in section 12.

8. How recipes combine the stages

No current recipe runs one stage once. They differ in which preference route they take, whether RL comes before or after the preference stage, and whether they loop.

Three patterns. Preference before RL (Tulu 3, Olmo 3): DPO shapes tone and format cheaply, then RLVR sharpens what a checker can test. RL inside the loop, preference inside RL (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3): reasoning RL comes early, and preference reward models enter the final general RL stage rather than a DPO stage. No RL at all (Llama 3): rejection sampling (10 to 30 samples per prompt, the reward model picks the best) and DPO, repeated over six rounds, chosen because DPO "required less compute for large-scale models and performed better" (Llama 3, §4); each round's fresh samples make the recipe partly on-policy. Frontier reasoning models all use large-scale online RL (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3). Every disclosed stage, model by model, with compute: the parent page's Open recipes compared tab.

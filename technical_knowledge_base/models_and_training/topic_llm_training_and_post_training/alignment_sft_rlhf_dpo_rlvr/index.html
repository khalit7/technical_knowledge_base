The pipeline, and what this page owns

A pretrained model continues text. Post-training turns it into an assistant in up to four moves, each trained on a narrower signal than the last:

  1. Pretraining: next-token prediction on trillions of tokens; produces a base model.
  2. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT), also called instruction tuning: train on (prompt, response) pairs with the language-model loss on the response tokens only, so the model answers instead of continuing the text. It sets the format, the persona and the chat template.
  3. Reward-model (RM) training, skipped by the direct methods: people rank two or more sampled responses per prompt, and a model learns to predict which one they prefer. The RM lets optimisation continue offline, without a person in the loop.
  4. Policy optimisation: use the RM, or a program that checks the answer, to update the model. Either with reinforcement learning (PPO, GRPO), or directly on preference pairs (DPO and its family).

1. Supervised fine-tuning: which tokens it learns from

SFT is the pretraining loss on a different text: conversations written in a chat template, with the loss switched off on every token the model should not learn to produce. The model reads the whole conversation, but only the assistant's tokens are graded. Llama 3 states it plainly: "a standard cross entropy loss on the target tokens (while masking loss on prompt tokens)" (Llama 3, §4.1). Ai2's open-instruct code, used for Tulu 3, sets the labels of every non-assistant token to −100, the value the cross-entropy ignores, with the comment "mask the non-assistant part for avoiding loss" (open-instruct finetune.py at the Tulu 3 commit).

Which rule is right is not settled. Grading the prompt tokens too ("instruction modelling", template tokens excluded) helped "in many scenarios" across 21 benchmarks, most when instructions are long and answers short or when there are few examples (Shi et al. 2024). With short answers, a small prompt-loss weight between 0.01 and 0.5 did best on multiple-choice and short-generation benchmarks, and a weight near 1 on long-generation ones (Huerta-Enochian and Ko 2024). Frameworks pick a default for you: Hugging Face TRL computes the loss on the completion only for prompt-completion datasets, but its assistant_only_loss is off by default, so a plain conversational dataset trains on the user's turns too unless the template marks the assistant spans (TRL SFTTrainer).

Quality beats quantity. LIMA fine-tuned a 65B LLaMa on 1,000 curated examples, with no RL, and people rated it equivalent to or better than GPT-4 in 43% of cases (LIMA); its "superficial alignment hypothesis" is that knowledge comes from pretraining and alignment only selects the format. Production recipes still use many more examples, mostly curated and synthetic: Tulu 3, the open reference recipe, trained on 939,344 prompts for 2 epochs at a learning rate of 5e-6 (8B), an effective batch of 128 and sequences up to 4,096 tokens (Tulu 3, Table 7 and Table 11). InstructGPT's SFT set was about 13,000 prompts; its models "overfit on validation loss after 1 epoch", but 16 epochs still raised the RM score and human preference (InstructGPT, §3.5). The template itself moves scores: Tulu 3's averages ranged from 51.6 with the Llama 3 template to 53.0 with an end-of-text token in place of the newline after each answer (Tulu 3, Table 13), and a model served with a different template from the one it was trained on breaks.

Padding, packing, and how tokens are weighted

Conversations differ wildly in length. In 1,000 rows sampled from the released Tulu 3 SFT mixture the median is tokens and the mean ; of the 1,000 exceed the 4,096-token limit derived. A batch padded to its longest member wastes most of its compute on padding: of the computed positions are real tokens at 8 conversations per micro-batch, at 16. Krell et al. found padding was 50% of all tokens in Wikipedia BERT pretraining at length 512 and packing doubled throughput (Krell et al. 2021). Packing concatenates conversations into full rows instead; best-fit packing of the same 1,000 into 4,096-token rows fills of each row.

Plain packing has a cost: with an ordinary causal mask, a token attends to every earlier conversation in its row. On the same sample, of the causal attention pairs in packed rows cross a document boundary derived. The fix is to restart the position ids at each conversation and pass the boundaries to a variable-length attention kernel, so each conversation sees only itself; Hugging Face's DataCollatorWithFlattening does this with FlashAttention 2 and reported up to 2 times the throughput and 20% less peak memory on FLAN (Hugging Face, 2024). Masking within documents also gave lower perplexity than plain causal masking across packed documents in pretraining (Zhao et al. 2024); Llama 3 found the cross-document mask of "limited impact" in standard pretraining but important for very long sequences (Llama 3, §3.2). TRL's "wrapped" packing, which cuts mid-conversation, "may mix unrelated examples" and its documentation says it hurts performance (TRL).

Lengths are the conversations' real token counts (Tulu 3 tokenizer and template), the faint start of each block = tokens without loss, the solid rest = graded tokens, drawn as two blocks for clarity (in a real multi-turn row the graded spans interleave with the prompts). Packing is best-fit decreasing, as TRL's default bfd; the hatched end of a packed row is drawn as padding to a fixed 4,096, whereas padding-free packing (TRL, the HF collator) flattens the batch and computes no padding at all. Tulu 3 itself did neither: its reproduction command runs one conversation per GPU per step with gradient accumulation, so it pads nothing and packs nothing (docs/tulu3.md, open-instruct).

Sum or mean. How the per-token losses are combined decides how much each token counts. If the loss is averaged per conversation and conversations are then averaged (what happens with gradient accumulation over one conversation at a time), every conversation counts equally, so a token in a short answer weighs as much as many tokens of a long one. Tulu 3 traced a performance gap between its own SFT runs and TPU runs to exactly this ("averaging the loss across padding tokens without taking into account gradient accumulation or distributed training setups") and switched to summing the loss, which "effectively weights all tokens equally", with a retuned learning rate (Tulu 3, §4.3.2 "Batch Aggregation"); the open-instruct code comments that sum reduction "can result in > 5 point improvements in AlpacaEval". In the 1,000-row sample, the answers at the 10th and 90th percentiles carry and graded tokens: under per-conversation averaging, a token of the short one weighs as much as a token of the long one derived, / .

Production traffic as the dataset. NeoHorse-1 is the online variant of this step rather than an offline curation pass: a router over a heterogeneous pool of models predicts each request's capability demand, those predictions order a three-stage supervised curriculum, and served interactions become training examples after structural validation, a semantic evaluation and labels for sub-scenes within a conversation, finer than one label per conversation, which is what makes supervising a partial trajectory work. 2. Reward models: turning rankings into a score

A reward model is usually the SFT model itself with its output layer swapped for one scalar head: InstructGPT started "from the SFT model with the final unembedding layer removed" (InstructGPT, §3.5). Labellers rank K sampled responses to a prompt (InstructGPT used K = 4 to 9), which gives K(K−1)/2 comparisons, and the model is trained on the Bradley-Terry objective: the probability that people prefer yw over yl is modelled as σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)), and the loss maximises it.

InstructGPT's Eq. 1 (arXiv 2203.02155). All of a prompt's comparisons go into one batch element, because shuffling them into the dataset made the RM overfit in a single pass; one 6B RM served every policy, since 175B RM training "could be unstable". The ceiling is the people: InstructGPT's training labellers agreed with each other 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time, and the RM predicted their preferences 72.4 ± 0.4% of the time (held-out labellers: 69.6 ± 0.9%). A reward model is a proxy roughly as reliable as a second labeller, and an RL policy optimised against it will find where it is wrong (the KL-leash animation on the parent page, Axis 4, and Reward Hacking).

The same loss on one pair is the first method in the animation of section 4.

RLAIF and Constitutional AI: who writes the labels

RLAIF keeps the same pipelines and swaps the labeller: a strong model judges which response is better. In Google's controlled comparison, with PaLM 2 as the labeller, RLAIF and RLHF were preferred over SFT about equally (summarisation 71% against 73%, helpful dialogue 63% against 64%, differences not significant), and RLAIF was more harmless (88% against 76%) (Lee et al. 2023). AI labels scale far better than people. Open recipes now build their preference pairs with an LLM judge: Tulu 3 rates every response with GPT-4o on helpfulness, instruction following, honesty and truthfulness (Tulu 3, §5); some frontier recipes still pay for people, as Llama 3 trained its reward model "using human-annotated preference data" (Llama 3, §4.1.2).

Constitutional AI (Anthropic, 2022) is a structured RLAIF in two phases. In the supervised phase the model critiques and revises its own harmful answers against principles drawn from a written constitution (16 critique and revision pairs), and a model is fine-tuned on the revisions. In the RL phase a feedback model, prompted with one of 16 principles, picks the less harmful of two answers; a preference model is trained on those AI labels and the policy is optimised against it (arXiv 2212.08073). The paper page replays the released critique chains and counts the refusals. Anthropic published a new, much longer constitution for Claude in January 2026 (Anthropic).

Corrections
The old page said RLAIF "is now the norm for most labels". No source states that; the evidence is a trend in open recipes (UltraFeedback, Tulu 3), while Llama 3 used human preference annotations, so the claim is restated as above unconfirmed as a general norm. It also described Constitutional AI's RL phase as AI preference labels "judged against the constitution" without qualification: only the harmlessness labels were AI-written; the helpfulness labels stayed human, so 57% of the preference model's data, and all of its harmlessness data, was labelled by a model (paper page, §4.2).

3. RL against a reward: PPO and GRPO in brief

RLHF with PPO (InstructGPT, Llama 2; GPT-4 was also trained with RLHF, GPT-4 report) is an actor-critic policy gradient with four models in memory: the policy, a frozen reference whose per-token KL penalty keeps the policy close to the SFT model, the reward model, and a learned value model (critic) estimating the expected return of each partial answer. A clipped ratio objective bounds each update. It is powerful, notoriously fiddly and memory-hungry. GRPO (DeepSeekMath) drops the critic: it samples a group of G responses per prompt and gives each the advantage (its reward − group mean) / group standard deviation, so the group replaces value estimation; the rest is PPO-style clipping plus a KL term. With a verifier it is the workhorse of open reasoning RL: DeepSeek-R1 (paper page) and Qwen3's reasoning RL use it (Qwen3, §4.2).

Its known biases spawned a family of fixes, each derived and worked through on RL for LLMs: Dr. GRPO removes the per-response length normalisation that lets long wrong answers off lightly and the standard-deviation division that over-weights too-easy and too-hard prompts (arXiv 2503.20783); DAPO decouples the clip range (clip-higher), drops groups with no spread (dynamic sampling), averages over tokens and shapes overlong answers (arXiv 2503.14476); GSPO clips one length-normalised ratio per sequence, for stability on long answers and mixture-of-experts models (arXiv 2507.18071); CISPO, from MiniMax-M1, "clips importance sampling weights rather than token updates" (arXiv 2506.13585).

4. Direct preference optimisation and its family

DPO removes the reward model and the RL loop. The KL-constrained RLHF objective, maximise E[r(x, y)] − β KL(π ‖ πref), has a closed-form optimum: π*(y | x) = πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y)/β) / Z(x). Solved for the reward, any reward is β log(π*/πref) plus a term that depends only on the prompt (Eq. 5). Put that into the Bradley-Terry loss of section 2 and the prompt term cancels, because only differences of rewards appear. What is left trains the policy directly on (chosen, rejected) pairs (DPO, Eq. 7):

LDPO = −E [ log σ( β log πθ(yw|x) / πref(yw|x) − β log πθ(yl|x) / πref(yl|x) ) ]

One trained model plus a frozen reference (whose log-probabilities can be computed once and cached), no sampling, a classification loss: stable and cheap, which is why it spread everywhere from Zephyr to Llama 3. The paper's headline, a 61% TL;DR win rate against PPO's 57%, rests on single runs about one standard error apart (the DPO paper page works out the errors, trains DPO against RLHF on an enumerable toy with an exact optimum, and shows likelihood displacement). In practice the data matters more than the loss. DPO-family results are sensitive to label noise and to how far apart the chosen and rejected answers are, which is the failure PLC-DPO targets. Recipes add small guards: Llama 3 masked the header and termination tokens out of both answers (otherwise "tail repetition or abruptly generating termination tokens") and added an NLL term of weight 0.2 on the chosen answers (Llama 3, §4.1.4). Training on a pair can lower the probability of both answers and move mass to a third: DPO on refusals cut Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate from 74.4% to 33.4% (Razin et al. 2024). And regenerating the pairs from the current policy, judging them and retraining (iterative or online DPO) recovers much of the gap to PPO (next section).

5. Online or offline

Vanilla DPO optimises preferences on a fixed dataset: pairs written before training, by other models or by an earlier version of this one, so the data is stale and off-policy. Online methods (PPO, GRPO, online or iterative DPO) sample from the current policy and score those samples, so the model learns from its own mistakes. At equal data they have been consistently stronger, at a higher cost in compute and complexity:

  • Well-tuned PPO "consistently outperforms DPO across all experiments", including code, where a 34B PPO model raised CodeContests 10@1k from 16.4% to 22.4% (Xu et al. 2024).
  • At the same KL budget online methods hold a clear advantage; offline-trained policies classify pairs better but generate worse, and neither data coverage nor a larger policy closes the gap (Tang et al. 2024, Google DeepMind); on-policy samples and a negative gradient both help (Tajwar et al. 2024).
  • The same loss run online wins: online DPO with an AI labeller beat offline DPO in human evaluation 63.7% to 7.7% (ties 28.6%) on TL;DR summaries (OAIF, Guo et al. 2024); three rounds of iterative DPO reached 24.17% length-controlled on AlpacaEval 2 (Xiong et al. 2023).
  • The cost side: in Tulu 3's controlled test PPO scored comparably to DPO, "albeit slightly lower", after "roughly 28 hours using two nodes" against "about 4 hours using a single node" for DPO, 14 times the node-hours derived, 28 × 2 / 4 (Tulu 3, §5.4).

The consensus: DPO-family losses for cheap, broad preference shaping; online RL (GRPO and PPO variants) where it matters most, for reasoning, agentic behaviour and the final alignment polish. Llama 3 used SFT, rejection sampling (10 to 30 samples per prompt, the reward model picks the best) and DPO, repeated over six rounds, choosing DPO because it "required less compute for large-scale models and performed better" (Llama 3, §4); each round's fresh samples make the recipe partly on-policy. Frontier reasoning models all use large-scale online RL (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3).

6. RLVR: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

RL needs some reward. When the answer is checkable (a maths answer checker, unit tests, a compiler, a rule-based format check, an instruction constraint) a program can give it, and the learned reward model, with its biases and its hackability, is bypassed. The checker becomes the new proxy: narrower, harder to flatter, still gameable (hard-coded outputs, edited tests, exiting before the assertions), which is the subject of Reward Hacking.

Lineage

  • 2022, execution as a filter. AlphaCode sampled huge numbers of programs and kept those that passed the example tests ("filter them based on execution results on example tests from the problem description") at inference time; training was pretraining and fine-tuning, not RL (AlphaCode).
  • Nov 2024, the name. Tulu 3 introduced "Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)", which "leverages the existing RLHF objective but replaces the reward model with a verification function": a reward of 10 when a verifier accepts the answer, 0 otherwise, optimised with PPO with a KL penalty, on 29,946 prompts for GSM8K, MATH and verifiable instruction following (IFEval) (Tulu 3, §6 and Table 22).
  • Jan 2025, from a base model. DeepSeek-R1-Zero ran GRPO with rule rewards (answer correctness and a format check) directly on DeepSeek-V3-Base, with no SFT; long chains of thought with self-checking emerged and AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (71.0% in arXiv v1). DeepSeek-R1 added a cold start of "thousands" of curated long reasoning examples before large-scale RLVR, for readability and to stop language mixing (arXiv 2501.12948; the paper page trains a tiny R1-Zero).
  • 2025 on, standard. Qwen3's reasoning RL ran GRPO on 3,995 query-verifier pairs, lifting Qwen3-235B-A22B's AIME'24 from 70.1 to 85.1 in 170 steps (Qwen3, §4.2). Ai2's Olmo 3 RL-Zero runs RLVR from a fully open base over maths, code, instruction following, general chat and a mix, with RL data decontaminated against the pretraining and midtraining data, so that RL-from-base results can be trusted as a benchmark (Olmo 3, §6; Ai2: OLMo).

Open problems

  • Does RLVR teach or elicit? RLVR-trained models beat their base at pass@1, but the base models score higher at large k: "the observed reasoning abilities originate from and are bounded by the base model" (Yue et al. 2025, NeurIPS 2025 best paper runner-up). Random rewards lifted Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500 by 21.4 points against 29.1 for true rewards, an effect traced to the clipping term that mostly fails on Llama 3 and OLMo 2: ablate on more than one base family (Spurious Rewards). Against that, judging the reasoning chain and not just the answer (CoT-Pass@k) finds RLVR does extend the reasoning boundary (Wen et al., ICLR 2026), and prolonged RL with reference resets "can uncover novel reasoning strategies that are inaccessible to base models" (ProRL, NVIDIA).
  • Rewards where nothing can be checked. LLM judges and rubrics: rubric-based rewards gave up to 31% relative improvement on HealthBench over Likert-style judge rewards (Rubrics as Rewards, Scale AI).
  • Credit over many turns. One reward at the end of a long agentic episode says little about which step mattered; GiGPO adds step-level advantages from states that recur across a group, over 12% better than GRPO on ALFWorld and 9% on WebShop (arXiv 2505.10978).
  • Entropy collapse. In long runs the policy's entropy falls and performance follows the fitted curve R = −a eH + b, so it is capped at −a + b once entropy reaches 0 (Cui et al. 2025); clip-higher and covariance-based clipping keep entropy alive. Several rewards at once raise a related budget question: Learn What's Left, Not What's Mastered.

The old page listed "AlphaCode-style execution feedback" as RLVR's first step without qualification: AlphaCode used execution results to filter samples at inference, not as an RL reward. It listed Tulu 3 among later users of RLVR; Tulu 3 is where the name was introduced (November 2024, with PPO). And R1-Zero's AIME 2024 gain depends on the version of the paper: 15.6% to 71.0% in arXiv v1, 77.9% in v2 and the Nature version.

Common mistakes

Training on the user's turns without meaning to
A conversational dataset in TRL trains on every token unless assistant_only_loss is on and the template marks the assistant spans. It is a choice, not a bug (instruction modelling sometimes helps), but it should be a choice: see the token chips in section 1.
Averaging the loss per conversation and then over the batch
With gradient accumulation over one conversation at a time, a token of a 21-token answer weighs about 57 times a token of a 1,187-token one (section 1). Sum the loss over tokens, as Tulu 3 does, or normalise by the total token count of the whole accumulated batch.
Packing without boundaries
A plain causal mask over a packed row lets each conversation attend to the ones before it. Reset position ids and use a variable-length attention kernel, or keep padding.
Serving with a different chat template
The template is part of what SFT trains. One newline less, a missing end-of-text token, or another model's markers shift the output distribution (Tulu 3 measured 1.4 points between templates).
Comparing β across methods
DPO's β = 0.1 multiplies a summed log-ratio; SimPO's β = 2 and length-normalised DPO's β = 5 multiply per-token averages; IPO's τ sets a target margin. Same letter, different units.
Reading a rising implicit reward margin as a better model
DPO's margin grows by pushing the rejected answer down, often faster than the chosen goes up, and both can fall together (likelihood displacement). In the pair animation, DPO ends with the rejected answer's log-probability far below where it started while the chosen moves much less. Evaluate generations, not margins.
"DPO is RLHF without the downsides"
It drops the RM and the sampling loop, and with them on-policy learning; the parent page's "Common mistakes" has the evidence. Online and iterative variants recover much of the gap (section 5).
"RLAIF means no people"
People still write the constitution, the judge's prompt, the rubrics and the evaluation; Constitutional AI kept human helpfulness labels. RLAIF moves human effort from labelling to specification.
"A verifier cannot be hacked"
An answer extractor that accepts malformed output or a test suite with gaps is a reward to hack, and the policy will find it (Reward Hacking).
Reading a pass@1 gain as a new capability
RLVR reliably converts pass@k into pass@1; whether it expands what the base can solve at all is contested (section 6). Measure pass@k at large k before claiming it.

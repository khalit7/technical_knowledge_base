2. Reward models: turning rankings into a score

A reward model is usually the SFT model itself with its output layer swapped for one scalar head: InstructGPT started "from the SFT model with the final unembedding layer removed" ({{InstructGPT, §3.5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}}). Labellers rank K sampled responses to a prompt (InstructGPT used K = 4 to 9), which gives K(K−1)/2 comparisons, and the model is trained on the Bradley-Terry objective: the probability that people prefer yw over yl is modelled as σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)), and the loss maximises it.

loss(θ) = −1/(K choose 2) · E(x, yw, yl) [ log σ( rθ(x, yw) − rθ(x, yl) ) ]
rθ(x, y)
the reward model's scalar for prompt x and response y
yw, yl
the preferred ("winning") and the other response of one comparison
σ
the logistic function; only the difference of two rewards matters, so a reward has no absolute zero
K
responses ranked per prompt

InstructGPT's Eq. 1 ({{arXiv 2203.02155|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}}). All of a prompt's comparisons go into one batch element, because shuffling them into the dataset made the RM overfit in a single pass; one 6B RM served every policy, since 175B RM training "could be unstable". The ceiling is the people: InstructGPT's training labellers agreed with each other 72.6 ± 1.5% of the time, and the RM predicted their preferences 72.4 ± 0.4% of the time (held-out labellers: 69.6 ± 0.9%). A reward model is a proxy roughly as reliable as a second labeller, and an RL policy optimised against it will find where it is wrong (the KL-leash animation on the parent page, Axis 4, and {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}).

Live versions: the {{InstructGPT paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}} fits a toy reward model on simulated rankings in the page ("Run the three steps") and shows every pair's loss for K = 4 to 9; the {{Constitutional AI page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a9b31e9443961c700}} shows why soft labels from a feedback model have a finite optimum and hard 0/1 labels do not. The same loss on one pair is the first method in the animation of section 4.

RLAIF and Constitutional AI: who writes the labels

RLAIF keeps the same pipelines and swaps the labeller: a strong model judges which response is better. In Google's controlled comparison, with PaLM 2 as the labeller, RLAIF and RLHF were preferred over SFT about equally (summarisation 71% against 73%, helpful dialogue 63% against 64%, differences not significant), and RLAIF was more harmless (88% against 76%) ({{Lee et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00267}}). AI labels scale far better than people. Open recipes now build their preference pairs with an LLM judge: Tulu 3 rates every response with GPT-4o on helpfulness, instruction following, honesty and truthfulness ({{Tulu 3, §5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}); some frontier recipes still pay for people, as Llama 3 trained its reward model "using human-annotated preference data" ({{Llama 3, §4.1.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}).

Constitutional AI (Anthropic, 2022) is a structured RLAIF in two phases. In the supervised phase the model critiques and revises its own harmful answers against principles drawn from a written constitution (16 critique and revision pairs), and a model is fine-tuned on the revisions. In the RL phase a feedback model, prompted with one of 16 principles, picks the less harmful of two answers; a preference model is trained on those AI labels and the policy is optimised against it ({{arXiv 2212.08073|https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073}}). The {{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a9b31e9443961c700}} replays the released critique chains and counts the refusals. Anthropic published a new, much longer constitution for Claude in January 2026 ({{Anthropic|https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-new-constitution}}).

Corrections
The old page said RLAIF "is now the norm for most labels". No source states that; the evidence is a trend in open recipes (UltraFeedback, Tulu 3), while Llama 3 used human preference annotations, so the claim is restated as above unconfirmed as a general norm. It also described Constitutional AI's RL phase as AI preference labels "judged against the constitution" without qualification: only the harmlessness labels were AI-written; the helpfulness labels stayed human, so 57% of the preference model's data, and all of its harmlessness data, was labelled by a model ({{paper page, §4.2|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a9b31e9443961c700}}).

3. RL against a reward: PPO and GRPO in brief

RLHF with PPO (InstructGPT, {{Llama 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288}}; GPT-4 was also trained with RLHF, {{GPT-4 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774}}) is an actor-critic policy gradient with four models in memory: the policy, a frozen reference whose per-token KL penalty keeps the policy close to the SFT model, the reward model, and a learned value model (critic) estimating the expected return of each partial answer. A clipped ratio objective bounds each update. It is powerful, notoriously fiddly and memory-hungry. GRPO ({{DeepSeekMath|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}}) drops the critic: it samples a group of G responses per prompt and gives each the advantage (its reward − group mean) / group standard deviation, so the group replaces value estimation; the rest is PPO-style clipping plus a KL term. With a verifier it is the workhorse of open reasoning RL: DeepSeek-R1 ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}) and Qwen3's reasoning RL use it ({{Qwen3, §4.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388}}).

Its known biases spawned a family of fixes, each derived and worked through on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}: Dr. GRPO removes the per-response length normalisation that lets long wrong answers off lightly and the standard-deviation division that over-weights too-easy and too-hard prompts ({{arXiv 2503.20783|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783}}); DAPO decouples the clip range (clip-higher), drops groups with no spread (dynamic sampling), averages over tokens and shapes overlong answers ({{arXiv 2503.14476|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476}}); GSPO clips one length-normalised ratio per sequence, for stability on long answers and mixture-of-experts models ({{arXiv 2507.18071|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18071}}); CISPO, from MiniMax-M1, "clips importance sampling weights rather than token updates" ({{arXiv 2506.13585|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13585}}).

Animated and live elsewhere, not repeated here: one batch through PPO, GRPO and DPO with the memory each needs is the parent page's "Same batch, three algorithms" (Axis 3); the KL leash is its Axis 4 animation. PPO against GRPO on one question, animated, and all six methods trained live from one toy base are on the {{DeepSeekMath page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} ("Train all six methods"; its "Then and now" tab walks GRPO's objective term by term through R1, DAPO, Dr. GRPO and GSPO). A tiny R1-Zero trains in the {{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}; a whole RLHF pipeline with PPO and PPO-ptx in the {{InstructGPT page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}; GRPO with several rewards at once in {{Learn What's Left, Not What's Mastered|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e38b0fe72f3daf44bc}}. The policy-gradient theory underneath is on {{Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}}.

4. Direct preference optimisation and its family

DPO removes the reward model and the RL loop. The KL-constrained RLHF objective, maximise E[r(x, y)] − β KL(π ‖ πref), has a closed-form optimum: π*(y | x) = πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y)/β) / Z(x). Solved for the reward, any reward is β log(π*/πref) plus a term that depends only on the prompt (Eq. 5). Put that into the Bradley-Terry loss of section 2 and the prompt term cancels, because only differences of rewards appear. What is left trains the policy directly on (chosen, rejected) pairs ({{DPO, Eq. 7|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290}}):

LDPO = −E [ log σ( β log πθ(yw|x) / πref(yw|x) − β log πθ(yl|x) / πref(yl|x) ) ]
β log πθ/πref
the implicit reward: how much more likely the policy makes an answer than the frozen reference does, in nats, times β
β
the KL strength; 0.1 in the paper and in Llama 3
gradient
−β σ(r̂l − r̂w) (∇log π(yw) − ∇log π(yl)): every pair pushes the chosen answer up and the rejected down, harder when the implicit rewards rank them the wrong way; dropping the weight σ(·) made the model degenerate

One trained model plus a frozen reference (whose log-probabilities can be computed once and cached), no sampling, a classification loss: stable and cheap, which is why it spread everywhere from Zephyr to Llama 3. The paper's headline, a 61% TL;DR win rate against PPO's 57%, rests on single runs about one standard error apart (the {{DPO paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d818bb1d8cafd30e20f9e}} works out the errors, trains DPO against RLHF on an enumerable toy with an exact optimum, and shows likelihood displacement). In practice the data matters more than the loss. DPO-family results are sensitive to label noise and to how far apart the chosen and rejected answers are, which is the failure PLC-DPO targets. Recipes add small guards: Llama 3 masked the header and termination tokens out of both answers (otherwise "tail repetition or abruptly generating termination tokens") and added an NLL term of weight 0.2 on the chosen answers ({{Llama 3, §4.1.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}). Training on a pair can lower the probability of both answers and move mass to a third: DPO on refusals cut Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate from 74.4% to 33.4% ({{Razin et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08847}}). And regenerating the pairs from the current policy, judging them and retraining (iterative or online DPO) recovers much of the gap to PPO (next section).

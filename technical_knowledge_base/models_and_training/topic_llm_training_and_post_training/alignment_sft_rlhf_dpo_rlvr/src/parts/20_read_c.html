3. Reward models: turning rankings into a score

InstructGPT's labellers ranked K sampled answers per prompt (K = 4 to 9); a ranking of K answers gives K(K−1)/2 comparisons, 6 for K = 4. The reward model is usually the SFT model with its output layer swapped for one scalar head: InstructGPT started "from the SFT model with the final unembedding layer removed" ({{InstructGPT, §3.5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}}). It is trained so the preferred answer scores higher, by the Bradley-Terry model: the probability that people prefer yw over yl is σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)).

Rankings become pairs; the score is only as good as the labellers agree

The pairs: one ranking of four answers yields six, all placed in one batch element (InstructGPT, because shuffling them into the dataset made the RM overfit in a single pass). The bars: InstructGPT's agreement figures, ± one standard error, on a 0 to 100% axis with chance at 50% ({{arXiv 2203.02155|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}}).

A reward model is a proxy about as reliable as a second labeller, and an RL policy optimised against it will find where it is wrong (the parent page's Axis 4 KL-leash animation, and {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}). RLAIF and Constitutional AI: who writes the labels

RLAIF keeps the pipeline and swaps the labeller for a strong model. In Google's controlled comparison, with PaLM 2 as the labeller, RLAIF and RLHF were preferred over SFT about equally (summarisation 71% against 73%, helpful dialogue 63% against 64%, differences not significant), and RLAIF was more harmless (88% against 76%) ({{Lee et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00267}}). AI labels scale far better than people, and open recipes now build their pairs with an LLM judge: Tulu 3 rates every response with GPT-4o on helpfulness, instruction following, honesty and truthfulness ({{Tulu 3, §5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}); some frontier recipes still pay for people, as Llama 3 trained its reward model "using human-annotated preference data" ({{Llama 3, §4.1.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}). RLAIF does not remove people: they write the constitution, the judge's prompt, the rubrics and the evaluation. It moves human effort from labelling to specification.

Constitutional AI (Anthropic, 2022) is a structured RLAIF in two phases. Supervised: the model critiques and revises its own harmful answers against principles from a written constitution (16 critique and revision pairs), and a model is fine-tuned on the revisions. RL: a feedback model, prompted with one of 16 principles, picks the less harmful of two answers; a preference model is trained on those AI labels and the policy is optimised against it ({{arXiv 2212.08073|https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073}}). The {{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a9b31e9443961c700}} replays the released critique chains, counts the refusals, and shows why soft labels from a feedback model have a finite optimum and hard 0/1 labels do not. Anthropic published a new, much longer constitution for Claude in January 2026 ({{Anthropic|https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-new-constitution}}).

Corrections
The old page said RLAIF "is now the norm for most labels". No source states that; the evidence is a trend in open recipes (UltraFeedback, Tulu 3), while Llama 3 used human preference annotations unconfirmed as a general norm. It also described Constitutional AI's RL phase as AI labels "judged against the constitution" without qualification: only the harmlessness labels were AI-written; the helpfulness labels stayed human, so 57% of the preference model's data, and all of its harmlessness data, was labelled by a model ({{paper page, §4.2|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a9b31e9443961c700}}).

4. RL against a reward: what PPO and GRPO do in the pipeline

With a reward model in hand, RL optimises the policy against it: sample answers, score them, compare each score with a baseline (the advantage), and take a clipped policy-gradient step with a KL penalty to the reference. RLHF with PPO (InstructGPT, {{Llama 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09288}}; GPT-4 was also trained with RLHF, {{GPT-4 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774}}) gets its baseline from a learned value model (critic) that estimates the expected return of each partial answer: four models in memory, powerful, notoriously fiddly and memory-hungry. GRPO ({{DeepSeekMath|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}}) drops the critic: it samples a group of G answers per prompt and gives each the advantage (its reward − group mean) / group standard deviation.

GRPO replaces the critic with the group: one model fewer

With a verifier instead of a reward model (section 7) GRPO is the workhorse of open reasoning RL: DeepSeek-R1 ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}) and Qwen3's reasoning RL use it ({{Qwen3, §4.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388}}). Its biases spawned named fixes (Dr. GRPO, DAPO, GSPO, MiniMax-M1's CISPO), each derived on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, sections 3 and 7, with PPO's GAE and clipping in section 2.

Depth: the GRPO fixes in one line each, and where each algorithm runs live (1 min)

Dr. GRPO removes the per-response length normalisation that lets long wrong answers off lightly and the standard-deviation division that over-weights too-easy and too-hard prompts ({{arXiv 2503.20783|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783}}); DAPO decouples the clip range (clip-higher), drops groups with no spread (dynamic sampling), averages over tokens and shapes overlong answers ({{arXiv 2503.14476|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476}}); GSPO clips one length-normalised ratio per sequence, for stability on long answers and mixture-of-experts models ({{arXiv 2507.18071|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18071}}); CISPO "clips importance sampling weights rather than token updates" ({{arXiv 2506.13585|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13585}}).

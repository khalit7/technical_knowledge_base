6. Online or offline

Vanilla DPO optimises preferences on a fixed dataset: pairs written before training, by other models or by an earlier version of this one, so the data is stale and off-policy. Online methods (PPO, GRPO, online or iterative DPO) sample from the current policy and score those samples, so the model learns from its own mistakes. At equal data they have been consistently stronger, at a higher cost in compute and complexity.

The same loss run online wins; online RL costs an order of magnitude more

Top: online DPO with an AI labeller against offline DPO, human evaluation on TL;DR summaries: online wins 63.7%, ties 28.6%, offline wins 7.7% ({{OAIF, Guo et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04792}}). Bottom: Tulu 3's controlled comparison, where PPO scored comparably to DPO, "albeit slightly lower", after "roughly 28 hours using two nodes" against "about 4 hours using a single node" ({{Tulu 3, §5.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}): 14 times the node-hours derived, 28 × 2 / 4, drawn to scale.

  • Well-tuned PPO "consistently outperforms DPO across all experiments", including code, where a 34B PPO model raised CodeContests 10@1k from 16.4% to 22.4% ({{Xu et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10719}}).
  • At the same KL budget online methods hold a clear advantage; offline-trained policies classify pairs better but generate worse, and neither data coverage nor a larger policy closes the gap ({{Tang et al. 2024, Google DeepMind|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.08448}}); on-policy samples and a negative gradient both help ({{Tajwar et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14367}}).
  • Iterating recovers much of it: three rounds of iterative DPO reached 24.17% length-controlled on AlpacaEval 2 ({{Xiong et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.11456}}). So "DPO is RLHF without the downsides" is wrong: it drops the RM and the sampling loop, and with them on-policy learning.

The consensus: DPO-family losses for cheap, broad preference shaping; online RL (GRPO and PPO variants) where it matters most, for reasoning, agentic behaviour and the final polish. Section 8 shows how recipes combine them.

7. RLVR: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards

RL needs some reward. When the answer is checkable (a maths answer checker, unit tests, a compiler, a rule-based format check, an instruction constraint) a program can give it, and the learned reward model, with its biases and its hackability, is bypassed. Tulu 3 named it: "Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)" "leverages the existing RLHF objective but replaces the reward model with a verification function": a reward of 10 when a verifier accepts the answer, 0 otherwise, optimised with PPO with a KL penalty, on 29,946 prompts for GSM8K, MATH and verifiable instruction following (IFEval) ({{Tulu 3, §6 and Table 22|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}).

RLVR swaps one box: the reward model becomes a program

The checker is the new proxy: narrower, harder to flatter, still gameable. An answer extractor that accepts malformed output, hard-coded outputs, edited tests or exiting before the assertions all score as passes, and the policy will find them ({{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}; the {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}} Verifier gallery tab shows what real checkers accept).

What it did. DeepSeek-R1-Zero ran GRPO with rule rewards (answer correctness and a format check) directly on DeepSeek-V3-Base, with no SFT; long chains of thought with self-checking emerged and AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (71.0% in arXiv v1). DeepSeek-R1 added a cold start of "thousands" of curated long reasoning examples before large-scale RLVR, for readability and to stop language mixing ({{arXiv 2501.12948|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948}}; the {{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}} trains a tiny R1-Zero). Qwen3's reasoning RL ran GRPO on 3,995 query-verifier pairs, lifting Qwen3-235B-A22B's AIME'24 from 70.1 to 85.1 in 170 steps ({{Qwen3, §4.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388}}). Ai2's Olmo 3 RL-Zero runs RLVR from a fully open base over maths, code, instruction following, general chat and a mix, with RL data decontaminated against the pretraining and midtraining data, so RL-from-base results can be trusted as a benchmark ({{Olmo 3, §6|https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13961}}; {{Ai2: OLMo|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e08697df661f83c3ac}}).

A pass@1 gain is not yet a new capability. RLVR reliably converts pass@k into pass@1; whether it expands what the base model can solve at all is contested, so measure pass@k at large k before claiming it. That question and three more open in 2026 (rewarding what no program can check, credit over many agentic turns, entropy collapse in long runs) are owned with their evidence by {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, sections 9, 4, 11 and 7, which also covers the training stacks in section 12.

No current recipe runs one stage once. They differ in which preference route they take, whether RL comes before or after the preference stage, and whether they loop.

Three patterns. Preference before RL (Tulu 3, Olmo 3): DPO shapes tone and format cheaply, then RLVR sharpens what a checker can test. RL inside the loop, preference inside RL (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3): reasoning RL comes early, and preference reward models enter the final general RL stage rather than a DPO stage. No RL at all (Llama 3): rejection sampling (10 to 30 samples per prompt, the reward model picks the best) and DPO, repeated over six rounds, chosen because DPO "required less compute for large-scale models and performed better" ({{Llama 3, §4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}); each round's fresh samples make the recipe partly on-policy. Frontier reasoning models all use large-scale online RL ({{DeepSeek-R1|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}, {{Qwen3|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a19006e6b096a6e14b}}).

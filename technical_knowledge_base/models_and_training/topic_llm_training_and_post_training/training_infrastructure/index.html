Why infrastructure decides how much of a run is training

A frontier pretraining run is one synchronous program spread over thousands of GPUs: every step waits for every GPU. At that scale something breaks every few hours, and each break stops the whole job. The infrastructure (scheduler, framework, collectives library, checkpoints, health checks) decides how much of the wall-clock time turns into training. The measure is the effective training time ratio (ETTR, also called goodput): productive training time over elapsed time.

Three ideas carry the page. Failures scale with the cluster: the time between failures of a job is roughly the time between failures of one server divided by the number of servers. Checkpoints trade a pause now against lost work later, and the best interval follows from the two costs (the Young and Daly rule). Recovery has moved from "restart everything from storage" to "copy from a neighbour": asynchronous saves, checkpoints in host memory, hot spares, and fault-tolerant replica groups that keep training while one group recovers. The stack, bottom to top

Each layer owns one job. When something breaks, the layers act in a fixed order: the hardware fails, the collectives library notices first (every other GPU is waiting for the broken one), the launcher and framework tear the job down, health checks find the bad machine, the scheduler replaces it, and the framework reloads state. Click a layer to see what it owns; play the walk to watch one GPU failure travel through the stack, then switch to fault-tolerant replica groups to see which steps disappear.

The failure (an uncorrectable HBM3 memory error on one GPU) is the second most common root cause in Llama 3's Table 5 (72 of 419). The order of the walk follows Llama 3 §3.3.4, the PyTorch Flight Recorder tutorial, the HyperPod auto-resume documentation and the torchft post linked in each step; no timings are given because the sources report them for different systems.

Training frameworks (2026)

The framework owns the training loop, the parallel layout, mixed precision and the checkpoint format. Which split to use where is on Distributed Training; this list is about what each framework brings to running a job.

  • Megatron-LM and Megatron Core: NVIDIA's reference for tensor, pipeline, expert, context and data parallelism (TP, PP, EP, CP, DP) with FP16, BF16, FP8 and FP4; most frontier-scale codebases trace their kernels and layouts back to it (Megatron-LM paper). Megatron-LM is the reference scripts, Megatron Core the composable library (README).
  • NVIDIA NeMo: the suite around Megatron Core. Its framework documentation lists NeMo Curator (data), NeMo Megatron Bridge (LLM training recipes and bidirectional Hugging Face conversion), NeMo AutoModel, NeMo RL, NeMo Evaluator, NeMo Export and Deploy, and NeMo Run (NeMo docs).
  • torchtitan: PyTorch-native pretraining. FSDP2, TP (including async TP), PP and CP composed on DTensor and DeviceMesh, torch.compile, MXFP8 and NVFP4 training, Distributed Checkpoint with async saves, torchft integration, Flight Recorder and deterministic replay for silent data corruption, and since August 2026 TitanRL for RL on the same model code (README). The cleanest codebase to learn 4D parallelism from; its paper reports 65.08%, 12.59% and 30% speed-ups at 1D, 2D and 3D over its own baselines (Liang et al.).
  • DeepSpeed: the ZeRO family and offload to CPU and NVMe (ZeRO paper); still common through the Hugging Face Trainer and Accelerate.
  • Fine-tuning and post-training tiers: the Hugging Face stack (Accelerate, Trainer, TRL, nanotron), Axolotl, and torchtune.
  • RL post-training adds a rollout engine (vLLM or SGLang) beside the trainer and a weight sync from trainer to rollout workers: verl, OpenRLHF, SkyRL, NeMo RL, torchtitan's TitanRL. Agentic RL also needs sandboxes for the environments: DeepSeek's DSec runs about 3 million a day on roughly 160 nodes (DSec paper page). The algorithms are on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR.
Corrections
torchtune is no longer maintained: development wound down in 2025 (The future of torchtune). NeMo: from the 26.02 container, LLM training is documented under Megatron Bridge; the old page's "deployment (NIM)" is NVIDIA's separate inference microservices, while the framework's own deployment component is Export and Deploy. The old page's "the default on HyperPod-with-NVIDIA engagements (HyperPod + NeMo + SLURM)" has no source here unconfirmed.

Failures are routine

Synchronous training makes one failed GPU stop the whole job. Over a 54-day snapshot of Llama 3 405B pretraining Meta logged 466 interruptions: 47 planned (automated firmware and kernel upgrades, operator changes to configuration or data, at least one a day) and 419 unexpected. About 78% of the unexpected ones were confirmed or suspected hardware; significant manual intervention was needed only three times; and the run still achieved higher than 90% effective training time (§3.3.4). 54 × 24 / 419 = 3.1 hours between unexpected stops derived, 2.8 hours counting the planned ones.

The root causes (Table 5): faulty GPUs 148, GPU HBM3 memory 72, software bugs 54, network switches and cables 35, unplanned host maintenance 32, GPU SRAM 19, GPU system processor 17, NICs 7, NCCL watchdog timeouts 7, silent data corruption 6, GPU thermal interface and sensors 6, and a tail of SSDs, power supplies, chassis, IO boards, CPUs and system memory (2 or 3 each). The table is drawn and replayed over the 54 days, with an adjustable checkpoint interval, on the Llama 3 paper page; this page does not repeat it.

Correction
The old page, following the paper's text, called 58.7% of the unexpected interruptions GPU-related. That is the sum of Table 5's printed percentages, one of which is a misprint: "Faulty GPU" prints 148 interruptions and 30.1%, but 148 / 419 = 35.3%. By count, GPU categories caused 268 of 419, 64.0% derived (the Llama 3 page found the same). "Falling off the bus" is not a Table 5 category, and the old page's "MTTR tens of minutes" is not a Llama 3 figure unconfirmed; the published restart costs are below.

Other runs say the same. OPT-175B, on 992 A100s over 2 months, had hardware failures behind at least 35 manual restarts and the cycling of over 100 hosts (Zhang et al. §2.5); the Gemini (SOSP 2023) authors count about 178,000 GPU-hours wasted to its failures (Wang et al. §1), about 12% of 992 GPUs for 61 days derived. ByteDance's MegaScale had over 100 restarts in a multi-week production job and kept effective training time above 90% (Jiang et al.). Over three months of jobs ByteRobust logged 38,236 explicit and 5,948 implicit failures; CUDA errors were 36.1% of incidents, hangs 9.9%, CPU overload 11.0%, CPU out-of-memory 10.1% (Wan et al.).

Why bigger jobs fail more often

Meta measured its two research clusters (16,000 and 8,000 A100s) in failures per node-day of runtime: 6.50 and 2.34 per thousand node-days (Kokolis et al.). If servers fail independently, a job on N servers fails N times as often as one server:

MTBFjob = 1 / (Nnodes · rf)
rf
failures per node-day of runtime (one node = one 8-GPU server)
Nnodes
servers in the job

At rf = 6.50 per thousand node-days this gives 1.80 hours for 16,384 GPUs and 0.225 hours for 131,072, the paper's projected 1.8 h and 0.23 h reproduced independently. Llama 3's 419 in 54 days on 2,048 servers is 3.79 per thousand node-days derived, between Meta's two research clusters. The same paper measured 7.9 hours for 1,024-GPU jobs, shorter than the 28.8 hours the formula gives at 6.50 derived: real jobs also fail for reasons that are not one server breaking. Checkpointing

What is saved

Model shards, optimizer state, dataloader position and random-number-generator state, and step counters, sharded so that each rank writes its own part. Llama 3's checkpoints save each GPU's model state, "ranging from 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU", and their bursty writes saturate a storage fabric of 240 PB on 7,500 servers that sustains 2 TB/s and peaks at 7 TB/s (§3.3.1).

Correction
The old page said optimizer states are "2x model size for Adam". Adam keeps two values per parameter, so in count it is twice the parameters; in bytes the usual mixed-precision recipe keeps both moments in fp32 (8 bytes) plus an fp32 master copy (4 bytes) beside 2-byte bf16 weights, so the optimizer state is six times the bf16 weights (ZeRO §3.1). A full restart checkpoint of Llama 3 405B at 12 bytes per parameter is about 4.9 TB derived, about 297 MB per GPU on 16,384 GPUs, inside Llama 3's 1 MB to 4 GB range. The Gemini (SOSP) authors' example, 42 minutes to write MT-NLG's state at 20 Gbps, implies 11.9 bytes for each of its 530B parameters derived.

Distributed Checkpoint and resharding

PyTorch's Distributed Checkpoint (DCP, torch.distributed.checkpoint) saves and loads from all ranks in parallel, at least one file per rank, and "handles load-time resharding which enables saving in one cluster topology and loading into another" (docs). That matters after a failure: a job restarted on fewer servers, or with a different parallel layout, can still load the last checkpoint. Built on DTensor, it is parallelism-agnostic.

Asynchronous and tiered checkpoints

A synchronous checkpoint stops training until the state is on storage. An asynchronous one copies GPU memory to host memory (the only pause), then a background thread writes to storage while training continues. For a 7B model the pause fell from 148.8 s to 6.3 s (23.6×); the copy takes 6 to 14 seconds for 7B to 13B models, and the checkpoint uses its own process group so its collectives cannot deadlock with training's (PyTorch and IBM, November 2024). torchtitan reports 5 to 15 times lower overhead than synchronous DCP for Llama 3.1 8B (§2.3.1).

Tiers go further by keeping a recent copy close. MegaScale writes GPU state to host memory "within several seconds" and uploads to HDFS in the background; on recovery one worker per data-parallel group reads from HDFS and broadcasts to its peers (§4.4). The Gemini system (Rice and AWS, SOSP 2023) checkpoints to the CPU memory of peer machines, chosen so a copy survives the failure, schedules that traffic around training's, checkpoints every iteration, and recovers more than 13× faster than remote-storage checkpointing (Wang et al.). ByteRobust's every-step in-memory checkpoints with peer backup cost under 0.9% overhead (Wan et al.). Google's Gemini models went further still: no periodic checkpoints to storage, but "redundant in-memory copies of the model state", recovering "directly from an intact model replica", which raised goodput for the largest job from 85% to 97% (Gemini report).

Correction
The old page's "peer-replicated in-memory checkpoints as in Gemini/MegaScale training" mixes three systems. The peer-memory placement is the Gemini system from SOSP 2023 (Rice University and AWS), not Google's Gemini models; Google's models keep redundant in-memory replicas of the model state; MegaScale copies to each machine's own host memory and then to HDFS, not to peers.

How often to checkpoint

Each checkpoint costs a pause w; each failure costs a restart u0 plus, on average, half an interval of lost work. Minimising the sum gives Young's interval, refined by Daly (Young 1974, Daly 2006); Meta's paper writes the resulting ETTR for a long job as:

Δt* = √(2 w · MTBF)    E[ETTR] ≈ (1 − (u0 + Δt/2) / MTBF) / (1 + w/Δt)
w
training pause per checkpoint
u0
restart overhead: detect, reschedule, reload (Meta assumes 5 to 20 minutes, and w about 5 minutes)
Δt
checkpoint interval; MTBF from the formula above

At Llama 3's rate (MTBF 185.6 minutes) a 5-minute synchronous checkpoint is best every 43 minutes and leaves an ETTR of about 70% with u0 = 20 minutes; a 10-second pause is best every 7.9 minutes, and with u0 = 10 minutes ETTR reaches 90.6% derived. That is the arithmetic behind Llama 3's "reduced job startup and checkpointing time": the paper reports only the outcome (over 90%), not w or u0. Meta's paper puts it the same way for 12,000 GPUs: ETTR 0.9 needs checkpoint writes "on the order of ~10 seconds" or a failure rate cut from 6.50 to about 1; with a 10-second pause the formula gives 0.92 at u0 = 5 minutes and 0.89 at 10 derived.

Two more steps belong to the pipeline: convert training checkpoints (DCP shards) to serving formats such as Hugging Face safetensors as an explicit step (torchtitan can write the last checkpoint in Hugging Face format or convert offline; Megatron Bridge converts both ways), and keep cheap model-only snapshots for evaluation (torchtitan's last_save_model_only drops the optimizer state; checkpoint docs).

Recovery, in increasing sophistication

1. Detect fast

  • Collective timeouts and traces. A dead GPU usually shows up as every other GPU waiting in a collective. PyTorch's Flight Recorder keeps a ring buffer of collective metadata and stack traces on every rank, dumps one file per rank on a watchdog timeout (TORCH_NCCL_TRACE_BUFFER_SIZE, default 2000 entries; TORCH_NCCL_DUMP_ON_TIMEOUT), and an analyzer script finds which ranks never joined (tutorial). Llama 3 used it extensively; failures of NVLink or a remote GPU there appeared as stalled loads and stores inside CUDA kernels with no error code, so Meta's NCCLX monitors the library's state and times out automatically (§3.3.4).
  • Health checks before and during jobs: GPU diagnostics such as NVIDIA DCGM, network counters (EFA on AWS), ECC error counts. HyperPod checks nvidia-smi and runs EFA connectivity tests whenever an auto-resume job fails; OLMo 2's cluster multiplies two tensors on every GPU before each job and cordons the host if that fails (OLMo 2). ByteRobust detects network issues in 30 seconds and GPU driver hangs in 10, against a roughly 30-minute timeout baseline (Wan et al.); MegaScale's average detection took under 10 minutes and catching up from a checkpoint within 15 (Jiang et al.).

2. Restart automatically

The scheduler requeues the job, a spare server replaces the faulty one, and the framework resumes from the latest checkpoint. In SLURM, --requeue makes a batch job "eligible for requeuing ... after node failure" (sbatch). AWS SageMaker HyperPod is "supercomputer as a service": EFA-connected clusters with SLURM or EKS as the orchestrator; with automatic node recovery it reboots or replaces faulty nodes found by its health-monitoring agent and basic and deep health checks, and srun --auto-resume=1 restarts the job step from the last checkpoint once the nodes are replaced (docs). Equivalents: Google Cloud Cluster Toolkit and Vertex AI, Azure CycleCloud, and the neoclouds (CoreWeave, Lambda, Nebius). A warm spare pool is what makes this fast: ByteRobust's warm standby machines recover 10.87× faster than requeueing, and its in-place "hot update" schedules 11.04× faster than a full requeue.

3. Keep training through the failure

torchft makes the replica group the unit of failure. Each group runs FSDP (and TP, PP) internally as an ordinary job; across groups a fault-tolerant all-reduce averages gradients. When a group fails, the others re-form a quorum and continue; the failed group restarts and copies weights and optimizer state from a healthy peer, so no checkpoint is needed while at least one group is healthy. Each step is treated as a transaction: committed if every gradient sync succeeded, discarded otherwise (PyTorch, June 2025).

What the demonstration showed
The old page said torchft was "demonstrated surviving a failure every ~15s with no checkpoints". True, and the conditions matter: a 1B Llama 3 model on 300 L40S GPUs (30 groups of 10, no InfiniBand), failures injected by cancelling SLURM jobs. With a failure every 60 seconds, 5,145 of 6,249 steps committed (82.3%) and training efficiency was 81.2%. With one every 15 seconds on average, 268 of the first 888 steps committed (30.2%) with 18.9 of 30 groups participating; the post prints 13.4% training efficiency, while its own two figures multiply to 19.0% derived. The point is that the model still converged, not that it trained efficiently.

Elastic training changes the world size on the fly: torchrun's elastic agent restarts workers and admits or drops nodes between a minimum and maximum (torchrun), and torchft varies the number of participating groups step by step. Google's Gemini training recovers from an in-memory model replica, the same idea at TPU-pod scale.

Stragglers, silent corruption and the building itself

  • Stragglers. A slow but working GPU slows every synchronous step. Llama 3: "Even a single straggler can slow down thousands of other GPUs"; Meta built tools to rank suspect communications and inspect the top few. MegaScale timed every rank with CUDA events and found about 0.5% of machines substantially slower; once they were excluded, peak MFU became consistent across runs (§5.1).
  • Silent data corruption (SDC): a flipped bit that corrupts values without crashing. Llama 3 counted 6 SDC interruptions in 54 days; Google expected SDC "to impact training every week or two" at Gemini's scale and found them by deterministic replay plus proactive scanners on idle machines and hot standbys (Gemini report). torchtitan now ships replay-based detection: re-run a deterministic forward and backward and compare, by default on the first step after every restart (docs). Other defences: loss-spike detection with rewind-and-skip, periodic comparisons against a gold run, hardware screening.
  • Temperature and power. Llama 3 405B's throughput varied 1 to 2% with the time of day, as mid-day heat changed GPU clock scaling; and tens of thousands of GPUs pausing together (for a checkpoint or a collective) or starting together swung the data centre's power by "tens of megawatts" (§3.3.4).

Loss spikes are the software cousin of these failures, and also cost rollbacks: DeepSeek-V3 reports no irrecoverable loss spikes and no rollbacks over its whole run on 2,048 H800s (DeepSeek-V3), and OLMo 2 cut its spike rate with initialisation and normalisation changes (Pretraining owns training stability).

Operational checklist for a big run

  1. Burn in and health-check nodes before the job; keep hot spares.
  2. Asynchronous checkpoints sized to MTBF (Young and Daly, above); test restore, including resharded restore onto a different world size, before day 1.
  3. Determinism where affordable (seeds, dataloader state in the checkpoint) so restarts are bit-comparable; it is also what replay-based SDC detection needs.
  4. Dashboards on step time, per-rank timing spread, loss, gradient norm, NCCL errors and ECC counts; alert on stalls, not just crashes. The experiment layer (Weights & Biases, MLflow, TensorBoard, plus config management and data versioning) is where these live; more on Topic: ml-infra-and-orchestration.
  5. Log data order and shard state so loss spikes can be traced to data.
  6. Rehearse the failure playbook: who cordons nodes, how requeue works, where Flight Recorder dumps land.

Common mistakes

"MTBF is a property of the hardware"
It is a property of the job. At Meta's research-cluster rate the same servers give 1.8 hours at 16,384 GPUs and about 14 minutes at 131,072 (formula above); a recovery design that works at one scale can fail at eight times the scale.
"Checkpoint as often as possible"
Each checkpoint pauses training. The best interval is √(2 w · MTBF); halving the interval below it costs more in pauses than it saves in lost work. Make the pause cheaper (asynchronous, in memory) and the optimum moves down by itself.
"An asynchronous checkpoint is free"
The pause shrinks to the copy into host memory, but the checkpoint does not exist on storage until the background write finishes; a failure in that window falls back to the previous one, and the writes still have to fit the storage fabric's bandwidth (Llama 3's bursts saturated 2 TB/s).
"Restart time is just loading the checkpoint"
It is detection, diagnosis, finding and booting a replacement, rescheduling, re-initialising NCCL across thousands of ranks, then loading. Meta assumes 5 to 20 minutes; at Llama 3's rate every 10 minutes of restart is about 5% of the run (10 / 185.6) derived.
"Hardware failures crash the job"
The expensive ones often do not: stragglers slow every step, NVLink failures stall kernels without an error code, and silent data corruption changes the numbers. Alert on throughput and per-rank timing, not only on exit codes.

What a checkpoint weighs

A floor for the synchronous pause: bytes to write over the storage fabric's bandwidth. Real pauses are longer (serialisation, metadata, the slowest rank): IBM's 7B checkpoint took 148.8 s, an effective 0.56 GB/s if it saved 12 bytes per parameter derived.

Defaults: Llama 3 405B (405,853,388,800 parameters, config) and Tectonic's sustained 2 TB/s (§3.3.1). The per-GPU share uses the GPU count above; Llama 3 reports 1 MB to 4 GB per GPU, because ranks hold different parts of the model.

Recovery, in increasing sophistication

1. Detect fast

  • Collective timeouts and traces. A dead GPU usually shows up as every other GPU waiting in a collective. PyTorch's Flight Recorder keeps a ring buffer of collective metadata and stack traces on every rank, dumps one file per rank on a watchdog timeout (TORCH_NCCL_TRACE_BUFFER_SIZE, default 2000 entries; TORCH_NCCL_DUMP_ON_TIMEOUT), and an analyzer script finds which ranks never joined (tutorial). Llama 3 used it extensively; failures of NVLink or a remote GPU there appeared as stalled loads and stores inside CUDA kernels with no error code, so Meta's NCCLX monitors the library's state and times out automatically (§3.3.4).
  • Health checks before and during jobs: GPU diagnostics such as NVIDIA DCGM, network counters (EFA on AWS), ECC error counts. HyperPod checks nvidia-smi and runs EFA connectivity tests whenever an auto-resume job fails; OLMo 2's cluster multiplies two tensors on every GPU before each job and cordons the host if that fails ({{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}}). ByteRobust detects network issues in 30 seconds and GPU driver hangs in 10, against a roughly 30-minute timeout baseline (Wan et al.); MegaScale's average detection took under 10 minutes and catching up from a checkpoint within 15 (Jiang et al.).

2. Restart automatically

The scheduler requeues the job, a spare server replaces the faulty one, and the framework resumes from the latest checkpoint. In SLURM, --requeue makes a batch job "eligible for requeuing ... after node failure" (sbatch). AWS SageMaker HyperPod is "supercomputer as a service": EFA-connected clusters with SLURM or EKS as the orchestrator; with automatic node recovery it reboots or replaces faulty nodes found by its health-monitoring agent and basic and deep health checks, and srun --auto-resume=1 restarts the job step from the last checkpoint once the nodes are replaced (docs). Equivalents: Google Cloud Cluster Toolkit and Vertex AI, Azure CycleCloud, and the neoclouds (CoreWeave, Lambda, Nebius). A warm spare pool is what makes this fast: ByteRobust's warm standby machines recover 10.87× faster than requeueing, and its in-place "hot update" schedules 11.04× faster than a full requeue.

3. Keep training through the failure

torchft makes the replica group the unit of failure. Each group runs FSDP (and TP, PP) internally as an ordinary job; across groups a fault-tolerant all-reduce averages gradients. When a group fails, the others re-form a quorum and continue; the failed group restarts and copies weights and optimizer state from a healthy peer, so no checkpoint is needed while at least one group is healthy. Each step is treated as a transaction: committed if every gradient sync succeeded, discarded otherwise (PyTorch, June 2025).

What the demonstration showed
The old page said torchft was "demonstrated surviving a failure every ~15s with no checkpoints". True, and the conditions matter: a 1B Llama 3 model on 300 L40S GPUs (30 groups of 10, no InfiniBand), failures injected by cancelling SLURM jobs. With a failure every 60 seconds, 5,145 of 6,249 steps committed (82.3%) and training efficiency was 81.2%. With one every 15 seconds on average, 268 of the first 888 steps committed (30.2%) with 18.9 of 30 groups participating; the post prints 13.4% training efficiency, while its own two figures multiply to 19.0% derived. The point is that the model still converged, not that it trained efficiently.

Elastic training changes the world size on the fly: torchrun's elastic agent restarts workers and admits or drops nodes between a minimum and maximum (torchrun), and torchft varies the number of participating groups step by step. Google's Gemini training recovers from an in-memory model replica, the same idea at TPU-pod scale.

Stragglers, silent corruption and the building itself

  • Stragglers. A slow but working GPU slows every synchronous step. Llama 3: "Even a single straggler can slow down thousands of other GPUs"; Meta built tools to rank suspect communications and inspect the top few. MegaScale timed every rank with CUDA events and found about 0.5% of machines substantially slower; once they were excluded, peak MFU became consistent across runs (§5.1).
  • Silent data corruption (SDC): a flipped bit that corrupts values without crashing. Llama 3 counted 6 SDC interruptions in 54 days; Google expected SDC "to impact training every week or two" at Gemini's scale and found them by deterministic replay plus proactive scanners on idle machines and hot standbys (Gemini report). torchtitan now ships replay-based detection: re-run a deterministic forward and backward and compare, by default on the first step after every restart (docs). Other defences: loss-spike detection with rewind-and-skip, periodic comparisons against a gold run, hardware screening.
  • Temperature and power. Llama 3 405B's throughput varied 1 to 2% with the time of day, as mid-day heat changed GPU clock scaling; and tens of thousands of GPUs pausing together (for a checkpoint or a collective) or starting together swung the data centre's power by "tens of megawatts" (§3.3.4).

Loss spikes are the software cousin of these failures, and also cost rollbacks: DeepSeek-V3 reports no irrecoverable loss spikes and no rollbacks over its whole run on 2,048 H800s ({{DeepSeek-V3|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}}), and OLMo 2 cut its spike rate with initialisation and normalisation changes ({{Pretraining|n:3c65c17b0d0d814f82cffd0d5c0dd5ba}} owns training stability).

Operational checklist for a big run

  1. Burn in and health-check nodes before the job; keep hot spares.
  2. Asynchronous checkpoints sized to MTBF (Young and Daly, above); test restore, including resharded restore onto a different world size, before day 1.
  3. Determinism where affordable (seeds, dataloader state in the checkpoint) so restarts are bit-comparable; it is also what replay-based SDC detection needs.
  4. Dashboards on step time, per-rank timing spread, loss, gradient norm, NCCL errors and ECC counts; alert on stalls, not just crashes. The experiment layer (Weights & Biases, MLflow, TensorBoard, plus config management and data versioning) is where these live; more on {{Topic: ml-infra-and-orchestration|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b5925bfd1d8665dd4b}}.
  5. Log data order and shard state so loss spikes can be traced to data.
  6. Rehearse the failure playbook: who cordons nodes, how requeue works, where Flight Recorder dumps land.

Common mistakes

"MTBF is a property of the hardware"
It is a property of the job. At Meta's research-cluster rate the same servers give 1.8 hours at 16,384 GPUs and about 14 minutes at 131,072 (formula above); a recovery design that works at one scale can fail at eight times the scale.
"Checkpoint as often as possible"
Each checkpoint pauses training. The best interval is √(2 w · MTBF); halving the interval below it costs more in pauses than it saves in lost work. Make the pause cheaper (asynchronous, in memory) and the optimum moves down by itself.
"An asynchronous checkpoint is free"
The pause shrinks to the copy into host memory, but the checkpoint does not exist on storage until the background write finishes; a failure in that window falls back to the previous one, and the writes still have to fit the storage fabric's bandwidth (Llama 3's bursts saturated 2 TB/s).
"Restart time is just loading the checkpoint"
It is detection, diagnosis, finding and booting a replacement, rescheduling, re-initialising NCCL across thousands of ranks, then loading. Meta assumes 5 to 20 minutes; at Llama 3's rate every 10 minutes of restart is about 5% of the run (10 / 185.6) derived.
"Hardware failures crash the job"
The expensive ones often do not: stragglers slow every step, NVLink failures stall kernels without an error code, and silent data corruption changes the numbers. Alert on throughput and per-rank timing, not only on exit codes.

Pretraining in one screen

Pretraining is self-supervised next-token prediction: a randomly initialised network reads trillions of tokens and is trained to maximise the log-likelihood of each token given the tokens before it. Nobody labels anything; the text is its own answer key. It is the most expensive stage by far, and it sets the ceiling every later stage works under (the root page, {{Topic: llm-training-and-post-training|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}}, Axis 1, shows why reinforcement learning sharpens what pretraining put there rather than adding to it).

Four decisions decide how much model a fixed budget buys, and this page takes them in order:

1 · What to predict

The objective: causal, masked, span corruption, fill-in-the-middle. Settled for generative models, and measured. Objective

2 · How big, for how long

Split compute between parameters and tokens (scaling laws), then train far past that split because serving is the larger bill. Size and tokens

3 · Which data, when

A bulk mix, then a short anneal on the best data as the learning rate falls, then long context. The main open lever. Data stages

4 · Keeping the run healthy

Batch size, schedule, stability fixes, and the evaluations that tell you the run is on track. Running it well

1 · The objective

Every variant below is trained with the same loss, cross-entropy over the vocabulary; they differ in what the model sees and which positions it must predict.

  • Causal language modelling (the GPT lineage): predict every token from its prefix. The universal choice for generative LLMs, because every position of every sequence carries a training signal and the trained model generates by the same rule it was trained on.
  • Masked language modelling (BERT): hide about 15% of tokens and predict them from both sides. Better for encoders (classification, retrieval), not for generation; the {{Seq vs Seq paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81139cffea35080afe2b}} trains matched encoder and decoder pairs and measures exactly this split.
  • Span corruption (T5): sample 15% of tokens, replace each contiguous run of them with a single sentinel token unique to that example, and train the decoder to emit only the dropped spans, each prefixed by its sentinel. The difference from BERT's masked LM is the target: you predict the missing text rather than reconstruct the whole sequence, so decoder sequences stay short and a step is cheaper. UL2 (Unifying Language Learning, {{Tay et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05131}}) generalised this into a mixture of denoisers, varying corruption rate and span length and prefixing each example with a mode token, so one model learns short-span denoising, long-span denoising and prefix LM at once. Mostly historical for LLMs now, but still the default when you pretrain an encoder-decoder; see the {{T5 paper page|n:3d45c17b0d0d81ec8ebfec02e953a7fd}} (11 min read, +4h 12m resources), which runs T5's own span-corruption code on any sentence, and {{Encoder-Decoder Gemma and T5Gemma 2|n:3d45c17b0d0d81a9a22ffaada508db5c}} for the 2025 revival.
  • Fill-in-the-middle (FIM): cut a document into (prefix, middle, suffix) and move the middle to the end, so an ordinary causal model learns to infill. Standard for code models. {{Bavarian et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.14255}} found that training with a large FIM rate leaves left-to-right ability intact ("FIM for free"); DeepSeek-V3 applies FIM to 10% of documents in the prefix-suffix-middle order and reports no loss of next-token ability ({{DeepSeek-V3, section 4.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19437v2#S4.SS1}}).

How much the choice of objective is worth, measured

T5 ran the comparison properly, holding architecture, data, compute and evaluation fixed and moving only the objective ({{Raffel et al., section 3.3|https://arxiv.org/html/1910.10683v4#S3.SS3}}). The finding that matters is the shape of the result rather than the numbers: the gap between objective families is large (denoising beats prefix language modelling beats deshuffling), while the gap within the denoising family is close to nothing. BERT-style masking, MASS-style masking, sentinel replacement and outright token dropping all land within about two GLUE points of each other; corruption rates of 10%, 15% and 25% are indistinguishable and only 50% hurts; mean span lengths of 2, 3 and 5 are indistinguishable and 10 is slightly worse.

GLUE average on T5's validation sets, Tables 4 to 7 of the {{T5 paper|https://arxiv.org/html/1910.10683v4#S3.SS3}}, transcribed (so they match by construction); each panel is drawn on the same 72 to 86 scale. The grey band is ±1 standard deviation of the baseline over 10 reruns (0.235, Table 1). derived ranges: families 82.96 − 73.17 = 9.79 points (about 42 standard deviations); corruption rates 10 to 25%: 83.28 − 82.82 = 0.46 (about 2); span lengths 2 to 5: 0.14.

The practical conclusion, and the reason this section is short rather than long: pick a denoising variant on computational cost, not on quality, and do not expect further tuning in this space to pay. The same paper is also why the field's architecture default is worth knowing as a measured result and not a habit: at matched compute the encoder-decoder (83.28 GLUE) beat the decoder-only prefix LM (81.82), both trained with the denoising objective, and the field went decoder-only anyway, for reasons about in-context learning and serving cost that the comparison did not test ({{Table 2|https://arxiv.org/html/1910.10683v4#S3.T2}}).

2 · Reading the loss

L = −(1/T) Σt log pθ(xt | x<t)    perplexity = eL
L
mean cross-entropy per token, in nats (divide by ln 2 for bits)
T
tokens in the evaluated text
perplexity
the effective number of equally likely choices per token

Loss is reported as per-token cross-entropy or perplexity, and downstream ability tracks loss smoothly enough to make scaling laws work: a run's loss at a given size and token count can be forecast from small runs, and the {{Llama 3 paper|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}} forecast benchmark accuracy from it in two steps. Three cautions make the number honest:

  • Per-token loss depends on the tokenizer. A tokenizer with longer tokens has fewer, harder tokens; compare models with different tokenizers in bits per byte (total bits over total bytes), not per token. The NanoGPT speedrun can use per-token loss (3.28) only because its rules fix the tokenizer and the validation stream ({{modded-nanogpt rules|https://github.com/KellerJordan/modded-nanogpt#rules}}).
  • The average hides the floor. Some tokens are predictable (the rest of a word, the answer to a sum), others are genuine choices with an entropy no model can remove. The {{Anneal a toy|#t-toy}} tab shows this on a real run: the loss on the digits of an answer can fall to almost zero, while the loss on the operands of a random sum cannot go below about 3.25 bits per digit (the average of log2 9 for a leading digit and log2 10 for the second).
  • Held-out loss on high-quality text predicts downstream better than training loss, which moves with the data mix: when the mix changes at an anneal, training loss jumps or falls for reasons that have nothing to do with the model getting better.

3 · Size against tokens

  • Kaplan et al. 2020: loss follows power laws in model size N, dataset size D and compute C, and they concluded you should scale N faster than D. See the {{Scaling Laws paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b08a1debb0c15cd252}}.
  • Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al. 2022) fixed Kaplan's learning-rate-schedule artefact (each of Kaplan's shorter runs was read off a cosine schedule planned for a longer one, so it was never annealed): compute-optimal training scales N and D equally, roughly D ≈ 20 tokens per parameter (1.4T / 70B = 20 derived). Chinchilla (70B, 1.4T tokens) beat Gopher (280B) at equal compute. See the {{Chinchilla paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8116b7ddffba5c599f3f}}.
  • Post-Chinchilla practice: everyone deliberately "overtrains" far past compute-optimal because inference cost dominates the lifetime economics; a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to serve. Llama 3 8B: about 15T tokens ({{Llama 3.1 model card|https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}}) over 8B parameters is about 1,875 tokens per parameter derived (1,868 with the exact 8.03B).
  • What the allocation rules leave out: Chinchilla fixed the split between parameters and tokens but treated tokens as interchangeable, and that is the term doing the most work. Decomposing progress from 2019 to 2025 attributes 3.24 times more compute-efficiency gain to data improvements than to model improvements (12.0 against 3.7 times at a 1e19-FLOP budget, 12.0 / 3.7 = 3.24 derived), and finds the two largely independent ({{Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han, September 2026|https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/pretraining-progress-is-mostly-data}}, 25 min). Taken with the rules above it reorders the priority list: allocation is solved, mixture is not, which is why the detail lives in {{Topic: data-curation-and-datasets|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa5ecda8d365ab00f}} and why the next section is the longest.
  • Loss curves are also used prescriptively: fit a scaling law on small runs, then predict the big run's loss and pick hyperparameters. muP (μTransfer, {{Yang et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.03466}}) parameterises the network so the best learning rate and initialisation found on a narrow model transfer to wider ones; used by many 2024+ labs unconfirmed as a count (labs rarely say), and visible in the speedrun's "zero-init projections (muP-like)".

Go deeper: move a compute budget between Chinchilla's, Kaplan's and an over-trained allocation, with presets that show their residual against published runs, on the root page's {{Scaling calculator|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}} tab. Tokens per parameter for eleven models are charted on the Chinchilla paper page.

4 · Data in stages

Modern pretraining is multi-stage, not one homogeneous pass:

  1. Stage 1, bulk (about 90% of tokens or more): filtered web (FineWeb- or DCLM-style), code, papers. OLMo 2's stage 1 was the OLMo 2 Mix: 3.90T tokens, 95.2% DCLM web pages, the rest StarCoder code, peS2o and arXiv papers, Wikipedia and two maths sets from Proof-Pile II ({{OLMo 2, Tables 4 and 10|https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00656v3#S2.T4}}).
  2. Mid-training, or the anneal: as the learning rate decays, up-weight high-quality data (curated maths, instruction-like text, synthetic data). OLMo 2's Dolmino mix during the anneal gives outsized benchmark gains; Llama 3 does the same over its final 40M tokens ({{Llama 3, section 3.4.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}). "Annealing" means three different things in this literature (LR decay, this phase, and a cheap data experiment); the {{Optimisers page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}} separates them.
  3. Long-context extension: short-context pretraining for most tokens, then a final phase at 32K to 128K and beyond with upsampled long documents and RoPE rescaling. SmolLM3 used 2 × 50B tokens (4K to 32K, then 64K); Llama 3 405B about 800B tokens in six steps from 8K to 128K. Mechanics on {{Positional Encodings|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}.

A run, stage by stage

Try: play GPT-3 first: one mix and one cosine from start to end. Then switch to OLMo 2 or SmolLM3 and watch the mix change as the learning rate falls. Each run is drawn to scale on its own token axis; the lower strip magnifies the last 5%, where an anneal of 1.3% of the run would otherwise be a hairline.

What the three recipes show: GPT-3 changed nothing during its run, and its learning rate stopped decaying at 260B of 300B tokens. OLMo 2 planned a cosine over 5T tokens and cut it at 3.90T, with the learning rate still at about 20% of peak derived (0.1 + 0.9 × ½(1 + cos(π × 3.90/5)) = 0.203), then spent 50B tokens, 1.3% of the run derived, decaying it linearly to zero on a different mix, three times over different data orders, and averaged the three ("souping"). SmolLM3 never decayed until the last 10%, and changed its mix twice. The SmolLM3 team chose its mixes by ablations on 3B models trained on 50B to 100B tokens ({{SmolLM3 blog|https://huggingface.co/blog/smollm3}}); Llama 3 judges a new dataset by annealing a half-trained 8B linearly to zero over 40B tokens with 30% of the data from the candidate ({{section 3.1.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}), a cheap experiment instead of a scaling-law study.

Key idea: the data mixture is now the main open lever; the architecture is largely converged (pre-norm decoder, RMSNorm, SwiGLU, RoPE, grouped-query attention; mixture-of-experts for the compute-rich). The {{LLM Architecture Gallery|n:3c65c17b0d0d81be8a07f2562fa2030a}} shows the convergence model by model.

5 · What the anneal buys

An anneal pulls two levers at once: the learning rate falls to zero, and the data switches to the best you have. Any claim that "annealing gave +X" is a compound of the two unless an ablation separated them. OLMo 2 ran exactly that ablation on its 7B: from the checkpoint at 4T tokens, 50B tokens with the learning rate decayed to zero on the unchanged pretraining mix, against the same schedule on high-quality mixes ({{Table 11|https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00656v3#S4.T11}}).

GSM* is 200 questions, so one score has a binomial standard error of about 3.2 to 3.5 points and a difference about 4.5 to 4.7 (√(p(1−p)/200)); the multiple-choice scores average many more questions and are tighter, but the paper gives no error bars for them.

The answer is the most useful single fact about anneals: on knowledge and multiple-choice tasks most of the gain is the learning-rate decay (OLMES +4.4 of +6.1, MMLU +2.0 of +3.3), while on maths all of it is the data (decay alone −1.5, within noise; the new mix +19.5). The shipped OLMo 2 7B, with three souped 50B anneals on the final Dolmino mix, went from 24.1 to 67.5 on full GSM8K and 59.8 to 63.7 on MMLU, +10.6 points on average ({{Table 9|https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00656v3#S4.T9}}). The {{OLMo 2 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}} rebuilds the microanneals that chose the maths data and the soups.

Two more measured facts bound the effect. It shrinks with scale: annealing raised a pre-trained Llama 3 8B by 24.0% on GSM8k and 6.4% on MATH, and the gains on the 405B were negligible ({{Llama 3, section 3.1.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}); OLMo 2 says the same of Dolmino across its sizes. And OLMo 2's ablation has no cell for "new data at a constant learning rate", so it cannot say whether the data switch needs the decay. The {{Anneal a toy|#t-toy}} tab runs all four cells on a small character model, with three seeds.

6 · Open recipes in 2026

Recipes stage by stage, with tokens, mixes, context and post-training for 20 models, are on the root page's {{Open recipes compared|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}} tab. Here, what each open recipe is worth reading for.

OLMo 2 and Olmo 3 (Ai2)

The longest-running fully open recipe: data, code, intermediate checkpoints, training logs. OLMo 2 32B trained to about 6T tokens (6.06T from its last stage-1 checkpoint name, per the {{OLMo 2 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}}) and was post-trained with Tülu 3.1 ({{Ai2, OLMo 2 32B|https://allenai.org/blog/olmo2-32B}}). Olmo 3 (7B and 32B) adds an explicit "model flow": base to Think and Instruct variants, with RL-Zero checkpoints released; its Dolma 3 pool is 9.3T tokens, of which the 7B's pretraining mix is 5.93T ({{Olmo 3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.13961}}). Lab page: {{Ai2: OLMo|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e08697df661f83c3ac}}.

K2 Horizon (Institute of Foundation Models, Abu Dhabi)

The second lab in the fully open category, which is what turns "fully open" from one lab's habit into a category: six base models (0.9B, 3.7B, 7B, 32B, a sparse MoVA-36B-A4B and the 375B-A23B flagship), Apache 2.0, published on Hugging Face on 1 September 2026 with weights, training code, training data and intermediate checkpoints ({{IFM on Hugging Face|https://huggingface.co/IFM}}). Releasing the data is the part that makes contamination auditing and data ablations possible from outside the lab, and it is what separates this tier from open-weight. The 375B saw 7.08T + 8.05T = 15.13T pretraining tokens, then four mid-training stages (1.91T) that extend context to 512K ({{model card|https://huggingface.co/IFM/K2-Horizon-375B-A23B#training-overview}}). Benchmark claims are the lab's own reported.

SmolLM3 (Hugging Face)

A 3B model with the full engineering blueprint published (architecture ablations, the 11.2T-token three-stage mixture, long context, reasoning mid-training, post-training; {{blog|https://huggingface.co/blog/smollm3}}); the companion {{Smol Training Playbook|https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingFaceTB/smol-training-playbook}} (about 5h) documents the decisions. Drawn above.

The NanoGPT speedrun lineage

Karpathy's nanoGPT, then llm.c, then Keller Jordan's modded-nanogpt. The community record for reaching GPT-2 (124M) quality, 3.28 validation loss on FineWeb on 8 H100s, fell from 45 minutes (llm.c, May 2024) to 1.894 minutes by the end of 2025 and to 0.665 minutes (about 40 seconds) on 30 August 2026, with under 330M training tokens against 10B ({{modded-nanogpt|https://github.com/KellerJordan/modded-nanogpt}}). The record that graduated furthest is the Muon optimiser (orthogonalised momentum): MuonClip carried Moonshot's 1T-parameter Kimi K2 through 15.5T tokens ({{Kimi page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f5abf5dbdbac821a12}}). All 92 records, by kind of change: {{Speedrun records|#t-speed}}.

Magic

A pretraining recipe reported at more than 10 times the compute efficiency of leading open-weight base models, published as the recipe and the long-context work rather than as a benchmark table (8 September 2026; {{Magic|https://magic.dev/blog/pretraining}}, 25 min). Vendor-reported and not independently reproduced reported, so the multiple is a claim rather than a measurement, but it is the relevant playbook for pretraining without frontier compute.

7 · Running it well

  • Batch size. Use the critical batch size heuristic: below it, a larger batch buys nearly proportional speed; above it, it wastes compute, and it grows as the loss falls ({{McCandlish et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06162}}; Kaplan's form is plotted on the {{Scaling Laws page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b08a1debb0c15cd252}}). So ramp it rather than fix it: GPT-3 ramped linearly from 32K tokens to the full batch over the first 4 to 12B tokens ({{GPT-3, Appendix B|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165}}); Llama 3 405B went from 4M to 8M tokens after 252M tokens and to 16M after 2.87T ({{section 3.4.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}); the speedrun's record 46 was a batch-size schedule.
  • Learning-rate schedule. Warmup plus cosine is classic; WSD (warmup-stable-decay) is now popular because you can branch anneals off the stable plateau without committing to a total token count, which is how SmolLM3 above was run. The full family comparison (shapes, cooldown length and curve, decay-free options like schedule-free and WSM) lives on {{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}}; the {{WSM paper page|n:3c75c17b0d0d8105b452d2c1bc1d087a}} trains the decay-free option.
  • Stability. z-loss or logit soft-capping, QK-norm (OLMo 2 moved to QK-norm plus reordered norms specifically for stability; its ablations are replayed on the {{OLMo 2 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}}), bf16 with fp32 master weights, gradient clipping at 1.0 (GPT-3, OLMo 2, SmolLM3 and the toy on this page all clip at 1.0), and watch for loss spikes correlated with bad data shards. Llama 3 lowered its early batch size for stability and reports few spikes and no interventions. Precision formats are on {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}; parallelism on {{Distributed Training|n:3c65c17b0d0d8103a916c4824abbd82e}}.
  • Evaluation during training. Track a fixed loss evaluation suite plus few-shot benchmarks at checkpoints; loss on held-out high-quality sets predicts downstream better than training loss. Small models need easier formats to give signal (OLMo 2 uses cloze-form OLMES for exactly this), and an anneal is the cheapest way to ask "how good would this checkpoint be if I stopped now" (Llama 3's 40B-token test anneals; WSM's merges).

Common mistakes

"The anneal added 18 points on maths, so annealing is the trick"
Two levers moved. In OLMo 2's ablation the learning-rate decay alone moved GSM* by −1.5 (noise) and the data by +19.5; on multiple-choice tasks it is the other way round. Ask which lever before copying a recipe.
"Train at 20 tokens per parameter"
Chinchilla's 20 is the compute-optimal split for training cost alone. Anything that will be served heavily is trained far past it (Llama 3 8B at about 1,875), because each extra token of training is paid once and a smaller model is cheaper on every request.
"Lower loss than model X, so better than model X"
Only on the same tokenizer and the same evaluation text. Across tokenizers compare bits per byte; across data mixes compare on a fixed held-out set, not training loss.
"The speedrun record measures what works for large models"
It measures what reaches 3.28 loss fastest on 8 H100s. Some records transfer (Muon did); some exploit the setting, such as record 92's 84.6M-row hashed n-gram embedding table (65B parameters by the README's count) or record 91's masking of impossible continuations during validation. Read each record as a hypothesis for scale, not a result.
"Tuning the masking rate or span length will help"
T5 measured it: 10 to 25% and spans of 2 to 5 are within about two standard deviations of run-to-run noise. Choose on cost.

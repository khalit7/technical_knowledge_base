3 · Compute and scaling laws: how big, for how long

The budget. A dense transformer costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token: 2 for the forward pass (a multiply and an add per weight) and 4 for the backward pass. So a run's compute budget is C ≈ 6ND. Llama 3.1 405B: 6 × 405.85B × 15.6T = 3.80 × 1025 FLOPs, the paper's own 3.8 × 1025 ({{Llama 3, section 3.2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}) derived, independently. A mixture of experts counts only the parameters a token passes through: DeepSeek-V3 is 6 × 37B active × 14.8T = 3.3 × 1024, not 6 × 671B × 14.8T = 6.0 × 1025. Wall-clock is C divided by the hardware's dense peak times the share of it the run sustains (MFU); the root page's {{Scaling calculator|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}} checks that against the GPU-hours labs published, run by run.

The question. For a fixed C, which N (and so D = C / 6N) gives the lowest loss? A bigger model learns more per token but sees fewer tokens. Chinchilla's answer ({{Hoffmann et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556}}) fits loss as three terms:

L(N, D) = E + A / Nα + B / Dβ   ⇒   Nopt = G (C/6)a,   a = β / (α + β),   G = (αA / βB)1/(α+β)
E
the entropy of text itself, which no model removes (the floor of section 2)
A / Nα
what a model of N parameters cannot represent
B / Dβ
what D tokens have not yet taught
a
how fast the optimal size grows with compute; a = 0.5 means size and tokens grow equally, so tokens per parameter stays constant

With Besiroglu et al.'s refit of the constants (E 1.8172, A 482.01, B 2085.43, α 0.3478, β 0.3658; {{Besiroglu et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10102}}), a = 0.513, and at Chinchilla's budget of 5.76 × 1023 FLOPs the optimum is 72.2B parameters on 1.33T tokens, 18 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla itself was 70B on 1.4T, exactly 20. Gopher (280B) had spent the same budget on four times the parameters, and Chinchilla beat it. derived

Why Kaplan got it wrong. {{Kaplan et al. 2020|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b08a1debb0c15cd252}} found the same power laws but concluded that size should grow faster than tokens (N ∝ C0.73): at Chinchilla's budget, 485B parameters on 198B tokens, with a predicted loss 1.9% above the optimum's. Each of their shorter runs had been read off a cosine schedule planned for a longer one, so it was never annealed and looked worse than it was, which made small token counts look worse and pushed the answer towards big models. Chinchilla trained each run with a schedule matched to its length and found a ≈ 0.5: double the budget and both N and D grow by √2, keeping D ≈ 20N. Gopher's 280B sits between the two rules.

The valley is flat, and its position depends on the data. At Llama 3.1 405B's budget the refit says 619B on 10.2T tokens; Meta's own IsoFLOP fit on its own data and tokenizer said 402B on 16.55T, and Meta chose 405B because the curves "become flatter around the minimum as the compute budget increases". Under the refit, 405B is only 0.045% worse in loss than 619B. Two lessons: a few tens of percent off the optimal size costs almost nothing in loss, and a fit's constants belong to the data and tokenizer it was fitted on, so labs refit on their own small runs ({{Llama 3 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}} rebuilds that fit).

Two answers for "Chinchilla-optimal", and where 20 comes from

Hoffmann et al. estimated the split three ways. Approaches 1 and 2 (training-curve envelopes and IsoFLOP valleys) found the optimal size growing as C0.50, so tokens per parameter stays roughly constant; Chinchilla, 70B on 1.4T, is exactly 20 (1.4T / 70B), and Table 3 puts 67B on 1.5T at the same budget. Approach 3 fits the surface above, and with the paper's own constants its exponent is a = 0.457 (0.452 with the rounded Eq. 10 values), so tokens per parameter rise with compute: 59 at Gopher's budget, where it gives a 40B model (32B and 93 with the rounded constants). Besiroglu et al. refitted Approach 3 on 245 points they parsed from the vector graphics of the paper's Figure 4, found that the original optimiser had stopped early, and got a = 0.513, which brings it back to about 20 and agrees with Approaches 1 and 2. The {{Chinchilla paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8116b7ddffba5c599f3f}} refits it in the browser (its Refit Approach 3 tab) and lists the paper's table discrepancies. derived from the constants above.

Beyond Chinchilla: why everyone over-trains

Chinchilla's split minimises training compute for a given loss. A model that will be served pays about 2N FLOPs for every token it ever reads or writes, so labs buy a smaller model with more training tokens: Llama 3.1 8B saw about 15T tokens, 1,868 per parameter ({{model card|https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B}}; 15T / 8.03B); SmolLM3 3,642; Qwen3-235B-A22B about 1,600 per active parameter. derived The question becomes: at the same loss, what does each extra token per parameter cost in training and save in serving? {{Sardana et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.00448}} solve for the optimum given a lifetime demand; the {{Chinchilla paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8116b7ddffba5c599f3f}} animates their answer and charts tokens per parameter for eleven models (its Then and now tab).

Tokens are not interchangeable. The law above treats every token alike, and that is the term doing most of the work now: decomposing progress from 2019 to 2025 attributes 3.24 times more compute-efficiency gain to data improvements than to model improvements (12.0 against 3.7 times at a 1019-FLOP budget, 12.0 / 3.7 derived), with the two largely independent ({{Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han, September 2026|https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/pretraining-progress-is-mostly-data}}, 25 min). And fresh tokens run out: repeating data up to about 4 epochs is nearly as good as fresh (Muennighoff et al.'s law: four epochs are worth 3.73 fresh epochs), about 16 is the useful limit, and the law and a token-budget planner live on {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}}. Allocation is largely solved; the mixture is not, which is why sections 4 and 5 are about data.

Scaling laws also set hyperparameters. Fit on small runs, then predict the big run's loss and pick its settings. muP (μTransfer, {{Yang et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.03466}}) parameterises the network so the best learning rate and initialisation found on a narrow model transfer to wider ones; used by many 2024+ labs unconfirmed as a count (labs rarely say), and visible in the speedrun's "zero-init projections (muP-like)".

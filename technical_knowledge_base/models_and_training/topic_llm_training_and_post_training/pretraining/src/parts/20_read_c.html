1 · The objective

Every generative LLM is pretrained with causal language modelling: predict every token from the tokens before it, with cross-entropy over the vocabulary, on text nobody labelled. Every position of every sequence carries a training signal, and the trained model generates by the same rule it was trained on. It is the most expensive stage by far, and it sets the ceiling every later stage works under (the root page, {{Topic: llm-training-and-post-training|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}}, Axis 1, shows reinforcement learning sharpening what pretraining put there rather than adding to it). The alternatives use the same loss and differ only in what the model sees and which positions it must predict; the figure runs one sentence through each, four steps per objective.

  1. The text. Twelve tokens of an ordinary sentence.
  2. What the model sees. The sentence as it is. A causal mask lets each position look only to its left.
  3. What it must produce. The same sentence shifted by one. Every position predicts the token after it, and the last predicts the end of text.
  4. Where the loss is. On every position. Twelve tokens in, twelve predictions trained; nothing is wasted, and generation later uses exactly this rule.
  1. The text. The same twelve tokens.
  2. What the model sees. About 15% of tokens replaced by [MASK]; here two of twelve. Every position can see both sides.
  3. What it must produce. The original token at each masked position. The output is as long as the input, but only two positions are scored.
  4. Where the loss is. On the two masked positions only. Bidirectional context is what makes encoders good at understanding; the thin signal per token is why they are a poor fit for generation.
  1. The text. The same twelve tokens.
  2. What the model sees. Each corrupted run (here "brown fox" and "dog") collapsed into one sentinel, unique within the example. The encoder input gets shorter.
  3. What it must produce. Only the dropped spans, each after its sentinel, then a closing sentinel. Six target tokens instead of twelve.
  4. Where the loss is. On every target token, so the decoder is short and a step is cheap. This is T5's objective; at 512 tokens the target is about a fifth of the length.
  1. The text. The same twelve tokens.
  2. What the model sees. A mode token, [X] for "extreme" denoising, then the sentence with one long span cut out. UL2 also trains [R] (short spans, 15%) and [S] (prefix LM) examples in the same mixture.
  3. What it must produce. The whole long span. Long spans force the model to generate, not just patch, which is why UL2 mixes them in.
  4. Where the loss is. On every target token. At inference you prepend the mode token that matches the task; one model covers short-span repair, long-span generation and prefix LM.
  1. The text. The same twelve tokens.
  2. What the model sees. The document cut into prefix, middle and suffix, and rearranged as prefix, suffix, middle (PSM) with three sentinels. The model is still an ordinary causal model.
  3. What it must produce. The rearranged sequence shifted by one, as in causal LM. When it reaches <MID>, it has already read both the prefix and the suffix, so it learns to write the middle that joins them.
  4. Where the loss is. On every position, so FIM costs nothing in signal. Bavarian et al. found a high FIM rate leaves left-to-right skill intact; DeepSeek-V3 transforms 10% of documents this way.
  • Masked LM (BERT) hides about 15% of tokens and predicts them from both sides: better for encoders (classification, retrieval), not for generation; the {{Seq vs Seq paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81139cffea35080afe2b}} trains matched encoders and decoders and measures this split.
  • Span corruption (T5) replaces each corrupted run with one sentinel and trains the decoder to emit only the dropped spans, so decoder sequences stay short and a step is cheap. UL2 ({{Tay et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05131}}) mixes denoisers of different corruption rates and span lengths, each example prefixed by a mode token, so one model learns short-span, long-span and prefix-LM denoising at once. Mostly historical for LLMs; still the default for an encoder-decoder ({{Encoder-Decoder Gemma and T5Gemma 2|n:3d45c17b0d0d81a9a22ffaada508db5c}} for the 2025 revival).
  • Fill-in-the-middle (FIM) cuts a document into prefix, middle and suffix and moves the middle to the end, so a causal model learns to infill; standard for code. A high FIM rate leaves left-to-right ability intact, "FIM for free" ({{Bavarian et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.14255}}); DeepSeek-V3 applies FIM to 10% of documents in prefix-suffix-middle order and reports no loss of next-token ability ({{DeepSeek-V3, section 4.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19437v2#S4.SS1}}).

How much the choice is worth, measured. T5 moved only the objective, holding architecture, data, compute and evaluation fixed ({{Raffel et al., section 3.3|https://arxiv.org/html/1910.10683v4#S3.SS3}}): the gap between objective families is large, the gap within denoising is noise.

So pick a denoising variant on cost, not quality. The same paper is also a measured result against the field's habit: at matched compute an encoder-decoder (83.28 GLUE) beat a decoder-only prefix LM (81.82), both trained with denoising, and the field went decoder-only anyway, for in-context learning and serving reasons the comparison did not test ({{Table 2|https://arxiv.org/html/1910.10683v4#S3.T2}}).

2 · Reading the loss

L = −(1/T) Σt log pθ(xt | x<t)    perplexity = eL
L
mean cross-entropy per token, in nats (divide by ln 2 for bits)
T
tokens in the evaluated text
perplexity
the effective number of equally likely choices per token

Downstream ability tracks loss smoothly enough for scaling laws to work: a run's loss at a given size and token count can be forecast from small runs, and the {{Llama 3 paper|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}} forecast benchmark accuracy from it in two steps. Three cautions keep the number honest:

  • Per-token loss depends on the tokenizer. Longer tokens make fewer, harder tokens; compare models with different tokenizers in bits per byte (total bits over total bytes). The NanoGPT speedrun can use per-token loss (3.28) only because its rules fix the tokenizer and the validation stream ({{modded-nanogpt rules|https://github.com/KellerJordan/modded-nanogpt#rules}}).
  • The average hides the floor. Some tokens are predictable (the rest of a word, the answer to a sum), others are real choices whose entropy no model removes. Fig. 2 shows it on a model this page trained (section 5 describes it): the loss on the answer digits of a sum can fall to almost zero, while the random operands cannot go below about 3.25 bits per digit (the average of log2 9 for a leading digit and log2 10 for the second).
  • Held-out loss on high-quality text predicts downstream better than training loss, which moves with the data mix: when the mix changes at an anneal, training loss jumps or falls for reasons that have nothing to do with the model improving.
  1. One passage, one prediction per character. The model reads held-out text and three held-out sums; at every position it predicts the next character from the ones before it (the dots), and its loss there is −log2 p of the character that actually came, in bits. One column per character.
  2. Step 0: a guess among 51 symbols. Untrained, every column stands near log2 51 = 5.67 bits; the passage averages 5.91.
  3. Step 600: spelling arrives first. Letters inside words are down to 1.92 bits and spaces and punctuation to 1.48, but the first letter of a word, where text makes its choice, is still 3.66. A sample from the checkpoint (prompt "it was ", temperature 0.8) reads "it was and from a some inson, a such among had accrovided".
  4. Step 2,000: words, not yet sense. Text averages 1.81 bits; most of what is left sits on word starts (3.23) and on the digits of the sums (operands 4.77, answers 2.67).
  5. Step 6,000: the end of stage 1. Text 1.63 bits, answers still 2.25: with 3% sums in the mix they are mostly guessed. Every anneal of section 5 starts from this checkpoint.
  6. An anneal on the same mix. 1,200 more steps with the learning rate decayed to zero: text 1.52, answers 1.38. A quieter optimiser sharpens what the model has; it does not supply sums it rarely saw.
  7. An anneal on the maths-heavy mix. The answers fall to 0.01 bits: given the operands, the sum is determined. The operands stay at 4.05, above the glass plane at about 3.25 bits that random digits impose, and the passage averages 1.60: one number that hides a skill learned completely and a floor no model can pass.

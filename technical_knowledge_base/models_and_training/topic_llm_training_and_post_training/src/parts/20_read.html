The lifecycle in one screen

A language model is built in stages. Pretraining teaches a randomly initialised network to predict the next token of trillions of tokens of text; mid-training and continued pretraining keep the same objective on a shifted mix; post-training turns the result into an assistant (SFT, preference optimisation, RLVR). Three more stages make it cheaper: distillation, PEFT and quantization. Every stage can be compared on the same four questions:

Building the base

Pretraining (next-token, from scratch; in depth: {{Pretraining|n:3c65c17b0d0d814f82cffd0d5c0dd5ba}})

What it changes. Every weight, from random initialisation, by predicting the next token over the whole vocabulary. It decides what the model knows and sets the ceiling later stages work under: RL raises pass@1 but base models still solve more problems given many samples (Yue et al.).

Data. Llama 3 405B read 15.6T tokens ({{Llama 3|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}}). About 20 tokens per parameter is compute-optimal (Chinchilla: 70B on 1.4T, {{paper|n:3c65c17b0d0d8116b7ddffba5c599f3f}}); serving-bound models go far past it. Made by crawlers and classifiers: filtered web, code, papers. Data improvements gave 12.0 times the compute efficiency of 2019 at a 1e19-FLOP budget, model improvements 3.7 times (Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han).

Cost. About 6 FLOPs per parameter per token: Llama 3 405B took 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs at 38 to 43% utilisation of up to 16,384 H100s ({{Llama 3|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}}). DeepSeek-V3's whole official run was 2.788M H800 hours, $5.576M at an assumed $2 an hour, research excluded ({{DeepSeek-V3|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}}). Your own numbers: {{Scaling calculator|#t-scale}}; disclosed runs: {{Price list|#t-price}}.

What goes wrong. Loss spikes and divergence (OLMo 2's initialisation and normalisation changes cut its spike score from 0.40% to 0.03%, {{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}}), and at scale the cluster: Llama 3 had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days, one every 3.1 hours derived, yet kept over 90% effective training time.

Mid-training, CPT, long context (same objective, shifted mix; in depth: {{Data mixing, curricula and continued-pretraining ratios|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}}, sections 5 to 7)

What it changes. All weights, same next-token objective, on a shifted mix: a high-quality anneal as the learning rate decays (maths, code, instruction-like and synthetic data), a new domain or language, or long documents with rescaled positional encodings for a longer window ({{Positional Encodings|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}).

Data. OLMo 2 7B anneals on 50B tokens three times and averages the results ({{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}}); Thomson Reuters kept 200B tokens of a 19T pool (The Batch); Llama 3 went from 8K to 128K context in six stages over about 800B tokens ({{Llama 3|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}}). Made by curation: classifiers, data vendors, synthetic generation.

Cost. OLMo 2's mid-training took 5 to 10% of the FLOPs and added 9.9 points on its 7B ({{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}}). Thomson Reuters' final mid-training run on Qwen3.5-397B-A17B cost $450,000; its $40M figure is the whole two-year programme, talent included (see the {{Price list|#t-price}}).

What goes wrong. Catastrophic forgetting of general ability, driven mostly by the shift in data (re-warming the learning rate is about a quarter of it in Ibrahim et al.'s Table 12); instruction following breaks until post-training is re-run. Replay of general data (even 1 to 5%) and a lower re-warmed peak are the defences (Ibrahim et al.). Llama 3 ended each long-context stage only once short-context scores had recovered.

Post-training

SFT (imitate demonstrations; in depth: {{Alignment|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}})

What it changes. All weights (or a LoRA adapter), next-token loss on the response tokens only, so the model answers in a chat template instead of continuing the text. It teaches form more than skill: R1's cold-start SFT scored 59.0% on AIME 2024 against 77.9% for pure RL from the same base ({{DeepSeek-R1|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}).

Data. InstructGPT: about 13k prompts with demonstrations, written by about 40 contractors ({{InstructGPT|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}); LIMA: 1,000 curated examples (LIMA). Now mostly written by a stronger model: 400 trajectories from GPT-6 Astra taught a 4B model to plan Postgres queries (write-up).

Cost. InstructGPT's SFT on the 175B model took 4.9 petaflop/s-days against 3,640 for GPT-3's pretraining, 0.13% derived. With LoRA it fits on consumer GPUs: the whole Postgres planner, SFT and RL, cost $1,200 (write-up).

What goes wrong. Off-policy: the model never sees its own mistakes, so it learns the look of an answer. Chat-template mismatches between training and serving are a common silent bug ({{Tokenizers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817187bcd94a1b0f15ea}}).

Preference optimisation (RLHF with PPO, DPO family; in depth: {{Alignment|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}})

What it changes. Shifts probability towards the answer a judge ranked higher, with a KL term keeping the model near its SFT starting point. PPO learns a reward model and optimises against it; DPO turns the same objective into a classification loss on pairs, with no reward model and no sampling ({{DPO|n:3c65c17b0d0d818bb1d8cafd30e20f9e}}). Labelers preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT to the 175B GPT-3.

Data. InstructGPT: 33k prompts with ranked answers for the reward model, 31k unlabelled prompts for PPO ({{InstructGPT|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}). Constitutional AI: 182,831 harmlessness comparisons labelled by a model, against 16 written principles, plus 135,296 human helpfulness comparisons ({{Constitutional AI|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a9b31e9443961c700}}). AI feedback is now the norm for most labels.

Cost. PPO holds policy, reference, reward model and critic: 252 GB at 7B before activations, twice DPO's 126 GB derived (Axis 3). InstructGPT's PPO on 175B took 60 petaflop/s-days, 1.6% of GPT-3's pretraining derived.

What goes wrong. The policy climbs the learned reward while true quality stalls or falls: verbosity, sycophancy, formatting tricks (Gao et al.; {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}). Defences: the KL leash (Axis 4), reward-model ensembles, mixing pretraining gradients back in against the "alignment tax".

RLVR (RL against a checker: GRPO and successors; in depth: {{Alignment|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}})

What it changes. The model's own sampled attempts are rewarded by a program (exact answers, unit tests, an environment's outcome). Long, self-checking chains of thought emerge: DeepSeek-R1-Zero went from 15.6% to 77.9% on AIME 2024 with rule rewards only ({{DeepSeek-R1|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}). It can also train cost: SWE-2 learned all its effort levels in one RL run (Cognition).

Data. Prompts with checkable answers, and for agents whole environments: MiMo-V2.6 trained across 7,000+ task environments (card); Mercor and SkyRL on 1,928 expert-made tasks (Mercor). The answers are the model's own; people or tools make the tasks and checkers.

Cost. Many samples per prompt (16 for R1-Zero, 64 for DeepSeekMath) make it inference-heavy. R1's RL and SFT data: 147K H800 hours, $294K, 5.3% of V3's official run derived; MiMo-V2.6-Pro's final RL stage: $2.62M in under six days. The sandboxes are their own infrastructure: DSec runs about 3 million a day self-reported ({{DSec|n:3e95c17b0d0d81bd927be047bb0d7b0b}}).

What goes wrong. The checker becomes the proxy: hard-coded outputs, edited or skipped tests, exiting before assertions. Long runs lose entropy and stop exploring; large MoE models go unstable (MiMo-V2.6 froze its router). Defences: hidden and mutated tests, a sandboxed grader, an environment that cannot be escaped.

Making it cheaper

Distillation (a student imitates a teacher; in depth: {{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}})

What it changes. A new, smaller student is trained to imitate a teacher: on the teacher's sampled answers (sequence-level), on its full token distributions (logits, which needs a shared tokenizer), or on-policy, where the student writes and the teacher grades every token. It is the main reason 1B to 8B models are useful now.

Data. Qwen2.5-32B fine-tuned on 800K samples from R1 scored 72.6% on AIME 2024 against 47.0% after R1-Zero-style RL on the same base ({{DeepSeek-R1|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}); Gemma 3 12B trained on 500 ToolGrad examples scored 83.1 on BFCL against Gemini 2.5 Pro's 83.2 (Google Research).

Cost. SFT-priced: one pass over teacher outputs, plus the teacher's inference. On-policy distillation is reported to need 9 to 30 times less compute than off-policy SFT on teacher samples, and against reasoning RL about a tenth of the GPU-hours (Qwen3) or 7 to 10 times fewer steps reported (Thinking Machines).

What goes wrong. Off-policy students never see their own mistakes; a teacher far stronger than the student can hurt it; mismatched tokenizers block logit distillation; the student inherits the teacher's quirks and, through terms of service, its legal exposure.

PEFT (LoRA, QLoRA) (train a small add-on; in depth: {{PEFT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba08eef56b3e3b4eb}})

What it changes. The base is frozen and a low-rank update W0 + BA is trained, then merged at no inference cost. On GPT-3 175B, 4.7M trainable parameters reached 73.4% on WikiSQL against 73.8% for full fine-tuning, in a 35 MB adapter instead of a 350 GB copy ({{LoRA|n:3c65c17b0d0d81018f15edd19fa5c28a}}).

Data. PEFT is a way of running SFT, DPO or RL, not a data regime: the data is that stage's. For RL even rank 1 is enough, since policy gradients carry little information per episode (Thinking Machines).

Cost. No optimiser state for frozen weights: QLoRA fine-tunes LLaMA 65B in under 48 GB (45.0 GB measured) where 16-bit fine-tuning needs more than 780 GB ({{QLoRA|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a8f4edc33f9583671}}). The arithmetic, with a calculator on real configs, is PEFT's section "Memory: full fine-tuning, LoRA and QLoRA".

What goes wrong. An adapter cannot absorb billions of tokens of new knowledge (use CPT); LoRA on attention only underperforms; and the claim that LoRA beats full fine-tuning sits inside the noise of the original paper.

Quantization, precision (fewer bits per weight; in depth: {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}})

What it changes. No learning at all after training (post-training quantization), or fake quantization inside training (quantization-aware training). Weights, and sometimes activations, move from bf16 (2 bytes) to FP8 (1) or about 4 bits plus scales; fine-grained FP8 is now used for training itself ({{DeepSeek-V3|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}}).

Data. GPTQ, AWQ and llama.cpp's importance matrix need a small calibration set; dynamic FP8 needs none; quantization-aware training needs training data. A calibration set unlike real use quietly costs capability off its distribution.

Cost. About 0.6 GB per billion parameters at 4 bits; DeepSeek-V3's FP8 training kept the loss within 0.25% of BF16 in its ablations ({{DeepSeek-V3|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}}). llama.cpp's default Q4_K_M is 4.89 effective bits per weight on Llama-3.1-8B (llama.cpp).

What goes wrong. A few channels with huge values wreck naive scaling; dense models fall off a cliff below about 3 bits, while MoE experts tolerate harder quantization; newer, heavily trained small models lose more at the same bits.

Axis 1: what each stage changes

Every stage before compression updates the same weights by gradient descent. What differs is the signal: pretraining and mid-training reward predicting text; SFT rewards imitating a demonstration; preference optimisation rewards the answer a judge ranked higher; RLVR rewards whatever the model itself produced that a checker accepted. Each signal is narrower than the last and moves behaviour more per example. The compression stages change something else: distillation makes a new, smaller model; PEFT changes how many weights move (a low-rank add-on, often under 0.1% of the model); quantization changes how the weights are stored, with no learning at all unless it is quantization-aware training.

What each signal buys, and what it cannot

  • Pretraining sets the ceiling. RL with verifiable rewards raises pass@1, but base models solve more problems when given many tries (Yue et al.): RL sharpens what pretraining put there.
  • SFT teaches the form more than the skill. In DeepSeek-R1, a cold-start SFT on thousands of clean reasoning traces scored 59.0% on AIME 2024, against 77.9% for pure RL from the same base; reasoning RL brought it back to 74.0% (Table 3, {{DeepSeek-R1|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}).
  • A small preference stage moves a lot. Labelers preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT to the 175B GPT-3, 135 times its size ({{InstructGPT|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}).

What one prompt's answer looks like after each stage, on a real open recipe (Tulu 3 8B, stage by stage), is the hero of {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}; how five open recipes chain the stages is its section 8, "How recipes combine the stages".

2026: RL that trains the cost-quality trade itself (SWE-2), and a correction (depth)

RL can now train the cost-quality trade itself. Cognition's SWE-2, RL post-trained from Moonshot's 2.8T-parameter Kimi K3, learned all its reasoning-effort levels in a single RL run, "with each penalty tuned to the local slope of the base model's Pareto frontier", rather than one checkpoint per tier; SWE-2 medium used 58% fewer turns and cost 81% less than SWE-1.7 on the same work. It matched Fable 5.1 on FrontierCode 1.1 Main (50.0% against 50.9%) at a claimed 64% lower cost reported, but scored 27.3% on Terminal-Bench 4 against 55.8% and 57.9% for Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra (Cognition).

Axis 2: what data each stage needs

Down the lifecycle, data gets smaller, more expensive per example, and made by someone other than a web crawler. Pretraining reads trillions of tokens nobody wrote for it; mid-training selects billions of better ones; SFT needs thousands of demonstrations; preference optimisation needs tens of thousands of ranked pairs; RLVR needs prompts or whole environments with a checker, and generates its own answers. The labeller changed too: people, then a model reading a written constitution, then a program that checks the answer, and now the model's own production traffic.

Data quality carries more of pretraining's progress than modelling. From 2019 to 2025, at a 1e19-FLOP budget, data improvements gave 12.0 times and model improvements 3.7 times more compute efficiency, so 12.0 / 3.7 = 3.24 times more from data derived, and the two effects are mostly independent (88% of variance additive) (Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han). Chinchilla's rule of about 20 tokens per parameter still says how to split a compute budget; it never said that one token is worth another. 2026: where the data is coming from now (selection, answer-first synthesis, production traffic, operational records), and corrections (depth)
  • Magic says its pretraining recipe is ">10x more compute-efficient than that of leading open-weight base models", matching DeepSeek V4 Pro Base with about 50 times fewer FLOPs reported, not independently reproduced (Magic, 8 September 2026).
  • Mid-training is a selection problem. Thomson Reuters kept 200B tokens of a 19T-token pool, 1.05% derived, curated with DatologyAI, for a legal and news model on Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (The Batch; report). The technical report mixes about one third general data back in as replay, although Thomson Reuters told LawNext the CPT used its own content exclusively; the details are on {{Data mixing, curricula and continued-pretraining ratios|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}}.
  • Write the answer first, then the question. ToolGrad builds a verified chain of API calls and only then writes the user request that needs it: 99.8% of generated examples valid across 16,000 real APIs, and Gemma 3 12B trained on 500 of them scored 83.1 on BFCL against Gemini 2.5 Pro's 83.2 (Google Research, paper). It works wherever an answer is cheap to check and expensive to find, for evaluation sets as well as training sets.
  • Production traffic as the curriculum. NeoHorse-1 routes requests across a pool of models, turns served interactions into training examples (structural validation, a six-dimensional semantic evaluation, labels for sub-scenes within a conversation), orders them into a three-stage SFT curriculum and adds on-policy distillation. Over ten agent, tool, coding and instruction benchmarks, Qwen3.5-4B went from 58.94 to 64.87 and 9B from 65.60 to 69.04: the 4B closed 89% of the gap to the untrained 9B derived. No experiment switches the routing curriculum off, and the loop ran once ({{NeoHorse-1 paper page|n:3db5c17b0d0d81e3b105e033a359ad24}}).
  • Operational records beat curated corpora in narrow domains. Periodic's Neon, mid-trained and RL-trained on real laboratory data from a Kimi K2.6 base, went from 2.7% to 55.3% on FrontierXRD (134 lab samples), ahead of GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1 at lower cost per analysis reported; the final run used 1,300 H200s (Periodic, 15 September 2026). The shared ingredient with the Postgres planner in Axis 3: a checker that already exists in the domain.

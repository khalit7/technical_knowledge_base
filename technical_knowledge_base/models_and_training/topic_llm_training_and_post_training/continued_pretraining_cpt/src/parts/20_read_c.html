Other findings that transfer

  • Order and staging. The old page said a brief warm-up phase on general data before the domain mix stabilises training, and that staged curricula (general-heavy, then domain-heavy) beat static mixes "in several studies". This page could not find those studies unconfirmed. What is measured is narrower: Llama-3-SynE (Llama 3 8B, about 100B tokens) found an easy-to-difficult curriculum over its scientific data beat random sampling and the reverse order ({{§4.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18743}}), and used two stages (92.5B tokens at Chinese:English 2:8, then 7.5B at Chinese:English:synthetic 1:7:2). And Ibrahim et al. found that ingesting domains one after another, re-warming for each, did worse than one mixture, "of limited practical use" (Appendix A.1).
  • Model scale. The evidence that larger models forget less is mixed. Yıldız et al. found "smaller models are particularly sensitive to continual pretraining, showing the most significant rates of both learning and forgetting" ({{arXiv 2402.17400|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.17400}}); the CMR study found larger models tolerate a larger domain share (29.8% at 460M to 47.8% at 3.1B). But in Ibrahim et al.'s weak shift the relative rise in Pile loss was about the same at both sizes: 12.4% at 405M and 13.1% at 10B with no replay, 2.8% and 2.3% with 5% replay derived ({{Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08763v4#S6.T4}}). Recipes that trained several sizes the same way lean towards the claim: Swallow's English average fell 5.0, 4.8 and 2.3 points at 7B, 13B and 70B ("the performance drop tends to be smaller as the model size increases", {{Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.17790}}); ChipNeMo's MMLU moved −1.1, −2.0 and +0.8.
  • Tokenizer extension. For a new language or a domain the tokenizer splits badly (high fertility: many tokens per word), extend the vocabulary, train the new embeddings first in a brief embedding-only phase, then run full CPT. Chinese-LLaMA added 20,000 Chinese tokens to LLaMA's 32,000 (49,953 after merging) and, for 7B, trained only the new embeddings and output layer before the main stage ({{§2.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2304.08177}}); Swallow added 11,176 Japanese subwords and cut the tokens needed for Japanese text by 56.2%; ChipNeMo added about 9K chip-design tokens, initialised from the mean of their old sub-tokens ({{Recipes compared|#t-rcp}}); the toy here avoided the question by writing German umlauts as two letters. Fertility and its cost are on {{Tokenizers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817187bcd94a1b0f15ea}}.
  • Parameter-level alternatives to replay help when replay tokens are unaffordable or the original data is unknown, but replay plus the schedule remains the default answer. Elastic weight consolidation (EWC) penalises moving weights that mattered for the old data ({{Kirkpatrick et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796}}). LoRA-based CPT forgets less because it learns less ({{PEFT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba08eef56b3e3b4eb}}). Merging: in Ibrahim et al.'s preliminary test, TIES-merging separately trained models scored below continued pretraining with replay on every comparison (10B weak shift, English average 41.94 against 47.68; Appendix A.2, {{Table 6|https://arxiv.org/html/2403.08763v4#A1.T6}}), and merging a continued model back into its base "does not increase performance". Later recipes report the opposite at scale: Thomson Reuters merged its continued model with the pre-CPT checkpoint and "an appropriate merge coefficient can recover most of the general capability lost during continual pre-training" ({{report, §3.3|https://arxiv.org/html/2608.27147}}), and eBay merged e-Llama with its base to set the trade-off. Treat merging as a lever worth a pilot, not a settled answer.
  • Re-run post-training afterwards. CPT the base model, not the chat model, then post-train again (or at least SFT). Continued pretraining of an instruction-tuned model erodes instruction following: Llama 3 8B Instruct continued on 1B tokens of news dropped up to 10 points on the instruction-following set and 3 points on average; adding the "instruction residual" (instruct weights minus base weights) back onto a continued base recovered it without new instruction data ({{Jindal et al. 2024, §4.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10739}}). Every recipe in {{Recipes compared|#t-rcp}} re-runs post-training after CPT.

What labs did: disclosed recipes

Fourteen continued-pretraining recipes could be checked against their sources ({{Recipes compared|#t-rcp}}, with three builders' own mid-training stages and BloombergGPT as references). What they share:

  • Almost everyone replays. General shares run from 1% (Meditron) to 80% (Llama-3-SynE, whose "general" is English kept while adding Chinese); the median of the 13 that state one is 10% derived. Only Chinese-LLaMA describes none.
  • Peaks vary a hundredfold. From the base's own peak (Code Llama, Meditron) to 1/150 of it (DACP); the most common choices are one third and one tenth.
  • Few measure forgetting. Seven of the fourteen report a general benchmark before and after (DeepSeek-Coder-V2, DeepSeekMath, ChipNeMo, Swallow, e-Llama, Thomson Reuters, Llama-3-SynE); the largest drops are about five or six points on one benchmark (NaturalQuestions 53.4 to 47.5 for DeepSeek-Coder-V2, e-Llama 8B's leaderboard 17.2 to 12.6), and some general scores rise, nothing like the collapse of the no-replay runs above.
  • Token budgets range from 24B (ChipNeMo) to 6T (DeepSeek-Coder-V2, continued from a checkpoint before its learning-rate decay, the infinite-schedule idea in practice).
  • From scratch is the exception. BloombergGPT trained 50.6B parameters from scratch on 569B tokens, about half financial; since 2023 the domain models in this table all start from an open base.

What it costs: two documented pipelines

Practitioners almost never publish a budget next to a recipe, which is why these two are worth more than their technical content. Their dollar figures, with what each includes, sit on the parent page's Price list ({{Topic: llm-training-and-post-training|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}}); this page keeps the shape.

Thomson Reuters continued Qwen3.5-397B-A17B into Thomson-1.0, a legal, tax and news model: 200B curated tokens selected from a 19T-token pool with DatologyAI (1.05% kept derived), then DPO alignment against an open-source constitution and GSPO for context compaction and document caching ({{The Batch|https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/custom-models-for-law-news-and-finance}}; {{technical report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27147}}). The technical report fills in the recipe: the mid-training mix is "three roughly equal parts: curated proprietary documents, synthetic data generated from those documents, and curated general-capability replay data", so about a third is replay derived; the learning rate was chosen per scale by pilot sweeps; the continued model was merged with the pre-CPT checkpoint, then aligned with DPO and GSPO. Against its base it measured general average 76.7 against 73.5, with coding (39.9 against 43.9) and factuality (73.7 against 76.2) down ({{§3.3, Table 1|https://arxiv.org/html/2608.27147}}). LawNext quotes Thomson Reuters saying CPT used its proprietary content "exclusively", which the report's replay third contradicts; the report is the technical source. Unusually, it continued an instruction-tuned, value-realigned checkpoint rather than a base model, and relied on the merge and the later DPO to restore behaviour. It is the customise-an-open-base path stated end to end, and its shape is this page's checklist: select hard from a much larger pool, keep some replay, then re-align.

Corrections

The old page said Thomson Reuters mid-trained the model "for a reported $40M in three months", with a 35B open-weights sibling "released alongside". The $40M covers talent and compute over about two years; the CTO put the final training run at $450,000, about 89 times less derived ({{LawNext, 24 Aug 2026|https://www.lawnext.com/2026/08/thomson-reuters-launches-thomson-its-own-proprietary-llm-trained-on-westlaw-and-practical-law-content.html}}); The Batch's "three months" is the outlier among the sources. The 35B sibling, on Qwen3.6-35B, will be released for academic and non-commercial use. Both corrections were made on the parent page first (checks of 2 October 2026), and both figures remain reported, not audited.

Periodic's Neon makes the same argument with a different corpus. Mid-training plus reinforcement learning of Kimi K2.6 on real laboratory records took it from 2.7% to 55.3% on FrontierXRD (134 lab samples), ahead of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 on a hard scientific-analysis evaluation at lower cost per analysis reported; it is deployed in labs analysing superconductor and magnet experiments, and the final run used 1,300 H200 GPUs ({{Periodic, 15 September 2026|https://periodic.com/news/nature-is-our-learning-environment}}). The transferable part is the structural claim: domain training on proprietary operational data, the records an organisation generates anyway rather than a corpus assembled for the purpose, establishes a Pareto-optimal cost-performance frontier against general frontier models. Most organisations sizing a domain-adaptation project already hold that data and do not think of it as a corpus.

Checklist for a CPT run

  1. Define target and guard metrics before training: domain evaluations, general evaluations and safety. Evaluate the base model on all of them first; that is the line forgetting is measured against.
  2. Pilot runs to pick the mixture ratio (CMR- or D-CPT-style: a few short runs, fit, predict) and the peak learning rate. Start the pilots well below the pretraining peak and include one near it, since the published sweeps (0.5× to 2×) trade adaptation against forgetting; use a WSD-style schedule if more stages will follow.
  3. Replay source as close to the original pretraining data as you can get; deduplicate the domain data against your evaluations.
  4. Monitor both loss families during training, general and domain. Forgetting shows early: in the toy and in Ibrahim's runs the general loss jumps in the first few percent of steps, and "relative differences between [replay fractions] appear very early" (§2).
  5. Post-train afterwards, and compare against a no-CPT baseline fine-tuned the same way, to prove CPT was worth it.

Data mixing infrastructure is on {{Topic: data-curation-and-datasets|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa5ecda8d365ab00f}}; the re-alignment step on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}.

Common mistakes

Continuing the chat model instead of the base
Instruction following erodes within a billion tokens (Jindal et al.: up to 10 points). Continue the base, then post-train, or add the instruction residual back.
No general evaluation until the end
Forgetting happens in the first percent of steps. Track a general held-out loss from step zero, next to the domain loss.
Blaming the learning rate for all the forgetting
Under a strong shift most of the damage is the data: Ibrahim's never-re-warmed run still lost 76% as much. Lowering the peak trades adaptation for retention; replay is the lever that is nearly free.
Copying someone else's mixture ratio
CMR's 30 to 48% and D-CPT's 92% domain share are both "optimal" under different tolerances, domains and budgets. Fit your own on pilots.
Comparing losses across tokenizers or scales
Loss per token depends on the tokenizer, and a 0.2 rise means different things at 405M and 10B. Compare relative changes against the same model's base, on the same validation set.
Reading a headline budget as a run cost
Thomson Reuters' $40M is a two-year programme including staff; the final run was $450K. Say which one you compare.

Continued Pretraining (CPT)

What CPT is, and when to use it

Continued pretraining (CPT, also "continual pretraining") resumes next-token training of an existing base model on new data: a domain (finance, medicine, law, code, maths), a new language, fresher data, or longer documents. It keeps the pretraining objective and trains every parameter; what changes is the data.

Pretraining

From random weights, on trillions of tokens of broad text. Details on Pretraining.

Mid-training, the anneal

The base-model builder's last stage: same objective, learning rate decaying, the best data up-weighted (OLMo 2: 50B tokens; Pretraining, section 4).

Continued pretraining

Someone else's finished base, billions to hundreds of billions of new tokens, all parameters, same objective (DeepSeekMath: 500B tokens on a 7B base, paper page).

Fine-tuning (SFT, LoRA)

Demonstrations or preference pairs, millions of tokens; teaches format and behaviour more than knowledge (PEFT, Alignment).

Mid-training and CPT are the same machinery: the difference is who runs it. The base-model builder anneals its own run on curated data; an adapter takes a released checkpoint (usually without its optimizer state or its data) and pushes it towards a domain. Long-context extension is CPT too: the standard 128K recipes are continued pretraining on upsampled long documents with a rescaled rotary base (Positional Encodings). The parent page compares all these stages on one grid (Topic: llm-training-and-post-training).

When it is worth it. CPT is for a large knowledge gap: a body of text the base model has barely seen, measured in billions of tokens. Fine-tuning, including LoRA, does not inject bulk knowledge well: Biderman et al. found LoRA "learns less and forgets less" than full training when continuing to pretrain on code and maths (PEFT, Against full fine-tuning). When the gap is a few documents that change often, retrieval (a RAG system) or a fine-tune is cheaper and easier to keep current; skip CPT.

A worked pattern. A domain-specific financial-services LLM Khalid worked on followed the usual shape: select a base model by benchmark screening; continue pretraining on a mix of domain and general text; then post-train, and evaluate on three axes (domain, general, safety) with a private in-house benchmark. Every step of that shape has a section below.

The central problem: forgetting, watched

Training on a shifted distribution degrades what the model knew. The effect has a name from the 1980s, catastrophic forgetting, and in CPT it shows up immediately as a rise in the loss on the old data while the loss on the new data falls. The animation below is a real experiment run for this page: a small character-level model (815,616 parameters) pretrained on six English books, then continued on six German ones (the strong shift Ibrahim et al. studied at 405M parameters, Pile to German Common Crawl). Same base, same German data, three seeds; the only difference between the two runs is whether a quarter of each batch is English replayed from pretraining.

What the toy shows, and how it lines up with the published runs:

  • Forgetting is fast. Without replay, English loss jumps within the first 50 steps (? bits per character, from ?) and keeps rising while German improves. By the end, asked to continue the English words "it was a", the no-replay model carries on in German (samples under the chart). Ibrahim et al.'s 405M model lost the same way at scale: Pile loss went from 2.17 to 3.56 after 200B German tokens, a 64% rise derived (Table 2).
  • A little replay removes most of it, at almost no cost to the domain. In the toy, 25% replay removes ?% of the English loss rise, for ? bits of German loss. In Ibrahim's strong shift, 1% replay removed 52% of the Pile loss rise, 5% removed 71% and 25% removed 89%, while German loss moved from 1.11 to 1.12, 1.12 and 1.16 derived: (3.56 − replay loss) / (3.56 − 2.17).
  • Replay plus re-warming matches retraining. The paper's headline: with re-warming, re-decaying and replay, the continued model reaches the average loss of a model retrained on all the data, for "substantially less compute" (Figure 1): 405M on German at 25% replay scores 2.33 (Pile) and 1.16 (German) against the union model's 2.26 and 1.25, average 1.75 both, after 200B tokens of training instead of 500B, 40% derived, given that the base already exists. The toy's dashed green lines are its own retrained-from-scratch model.

Toy defaults reproduce Ibrahim et al.'s qualitative result independently (a different model, data and scale); the toy's numbers are not comparable to the paper's, which are in nats per token. One seed difference worth knowing: the paper ran each configuration once ("we did not run our experiments over multiple seeds", §8); the toy ran three, and its bands are narrow next to the effects.

The two levers: replay and the re-warmed peak

Two settings decide most of the trade between learning the domain and keeping the rest.

1. Data mixing (replay). Mix general tokens back into the domain stream. Measured ranges:

  • Even 1 to 5% helps a lot. Meditron kept 1% general text, Llemma 2% (plus 3% code), SaulLM about 2%. Ibrahim et al.'s lowest setting, 1%, "significantly reduces forgetting" on both shifts, and 0.5% already removed 63% of the Pile loss rise on the weak shift derived (Table 2). The D-CPT Law runs show the same at 1.8B: training on chemistry alone raised general loss 21.2%; with 6 to 10% general data the rise was 1.6 to 4.4% derived from Table 5.
  • 10 to 30% when the shift is strong or general ability is precious. Ibrahim et al. chose 25% for English to German and 5% for the weak shift; DeepSeek-Coder-V2 kept 30% natural language over 6T tokens of code CPT (as Ibrahim et al. note in their §2; row in Recipes compared).
  • Domain-heavy, half and half, when domain depth is the goal. Dialpad's DACP mixed 25B tokens of business-call transcripts with 25B tokens sampled from FineWeb-Edu, 1:1, for Llama 3.1 8B and Mistral 7B v0.3 (DACP, 2025).
  • Too much replay costs adaptation. At 50%, Ibrahim's German loss rose from 1.11 to 1.22, "relatively significantly worse".

Choose replay data close to the original pretraining distribution, or the best open approximation when the original is unknown (it usually is: open-weight labs rarely release their mix). Ibrahim et al. replayed the Pile itself, the base model's own data; FineWeb-Edu and DCLM are the common stand-ins.

2. The learning-rate schedule. A released base model has usually finished with its learning rate decayed near a small minimum. Continuing at that minimum adapts too slowly; re-warming to a high peak adapts well but makes the loss on old data spike first. Ibrahim et al.'s rule of thumb: re-warm to the order of the original peak and re-decay to the order of the original minimum; "decreasing the schedule's maximum learning rate can help reduce forgetting, whereas increasing it can improve adaptation" (§2). Gupta et al. found the same on Pythia 410M continued on SlimPajama, and that the length of the warmup barely matters (Gupta et al. 2023). Combine the two levers: replay and a re-warmed, re-decayed schedule together.

Corrections

The old page said re-warming "drives most forgetting". In Ibrahim et al.'s own numbers it does not, under a real shift: the 405M model trained on German at a constant learning rate equal to the old minimum, never re-warmed, still raised its Pile loss from 2.17 to 3.22; re-warming to the old peak raised it to 3.56. So re-warming added 0.34 of the 1.39 rise, 24%, and the data shift the other 76% derived (Table 12; on the weak shift, 0.02 of 0.27, 7%). What is true is narrower: re-warming alone raises the loss even with no shift at all ("every model that re-increases its learning rate ... sees an increase in loss", §7.1, on Pile continued with Pile). The toy separates the two the same way: compare "No replay, peak 0.1×" with "No replay" above.

The old page also gave "an order of magnitude lower peak than pretraining" as a common heuristic. It is common practice, but not what the two controlled studies tested. eBay's e-Llama paper names two paradigms in the literature, "use the same LR_max as for the original pretraining ... or ... a smaller value, typically around 10% of the original", and chose 10% after an ablation (e-Llama, §4). Ibrahim et al. and Gupta et al. tried 0.5×, 1× and 2× the original peak; the closest thing to 0.1× in their tables is the never-re-warmed constant run at the old minimum (least forgetting, worst adaptation: German 1.21 against 1.11). Disclosed recipes span 1× (Code Llama, Meditron) to about 1/150 (DACP), with Llemma and Swallow at one third and ChipNeMo at 1/60 (Recipes compared). ChipNeMo's own ablation shows the risk at the top: at 3e-4 instead of 5e-6, MMLU fell from 44.6 to 26.6 (ChipNeMo). A sensible place to start pilots, not a law.

Schedules that resume without re-warming

Re-warming is needed because a cosine schedule ends low. Ibrahim et al. propose "infinite" schedules instead: warm up, cool down once to a constant learning rate, stay constant across every later dataset, and decay quickly only when a model is to be released (§7.2). The checkpoint to continue from is the one before the final decay, so no re-warm is needed. This is the warmup-stable-decay (WSD) family; the WSM paper page replaces the final decay with checkpoint merging. DeepSeek did this for DeepSeek-Coder-V2: it continued DeepSeek-V2 from a checkpoint before the learning-rate decay (as Ibrahim et al. describe it, §2).

Shapes from Ibrahim et al.'s Equations 1 to 4 with their 405M settings (Table 14): peak 3e-4, minimum 3e-5, constant 1.65e-4 for the infinite schedule, warmup 1%. Two datasets of equal size, drawn to the same token axis; the cooldown and anneal lengths are illustrative. Note: the infinite schedule was tested only at 405M and without a distribution shift (§8).

The evidence is thinner than for replay: infinite schedules matched cosine at 405M on 300B tokens of SlimPajama from scratch, and across three 100B splits of the same data showed "negligible forgetting across dataset boundaries" (Figure 10), but the authors list as limitations that these runs had no distribution shift and only one size.

Choosing the mix: CMR and the D-CPT law

Grid-searching the domain share at full scale is expensive. Two 2024 papers fit scaling laws on small pilot runs and predict the share instead.

The CMR scaling law (Gu et al., EMNLP 2024 Findings). Llama-shaped models of 460M to 3.1B, pretrained on 200B general tokens, then continued for 20B tokens (10,000 steps × 512 × 4,096 = 20.97B derived) on finance or arXiv papers mixed with general text at a constant learning rate of 3e-5. The definitions, read from the paper rather than paraphrased:

  • a mixture ratio R (the domain share) is feasible if, by the end of training, general loss is no more than a tolerance ε above the base model's (ε = 0.05, "based on empirical results by observation") and domain loss has fallen;
  • the Critical Mixture Ratio is the largest feasible R: below it the domain data is under-used, above it general loss rises past the tolerance;
  • final domain and general losses follow L(R) = α·Rs + β in the ratio, and their changes follow power laws in tokens; the CMR itself is fitted as RCMR = α₄·Ts₄ + β₃ (Equation 8).

The CMR law as a calculator

Try: leave 20B tokens and step through the model sizes: the four published CMRs. Then drag the budget: the law says the domain share you can afford grows with training tokens and with model size. Past 20B the curves are extrapolation.

Coefficients from Table 5 (finance). Defaults reproduce the paper's four CMRs (29.8%, 34.9%, 41.4%, 47.8% at 20B) independently from the coefficients. One unit conversion is ours: the paper sets T = 100 for its 20B tokens, so one unit is 0.2B, and its Figure 5 caption's "T=250, which is equivalent to ... 500B tokens" should read 50B derived; the slider uses 0.2B per unit. The law was fitted on one learning rate, one general corpus and two domains.

The D-CPT Law (Que et al., NeurIPS 2024) adds the mixture ratio r to a Chinchilla-style law: L(N, D, r) = E + A/Nα + B·rη/Dβ + C/(r + ε)γ, fitted on Qwen1.5 0.5B, 1.8B and 4B, 0.1B to 26B tokens and six domains, and used three ways: the best domain share under a cap on general-loss damage, the best share when the domain corpus is limited (its "explicit domain-corpus-size" use), and how to split compute between model and data. Its worked example: chemistry, 1.8B, 10B tokens, general loss allowed to rise at most 3% from 2.8602.

Measured general loss at each domain share from Table 5, as a rise over 2.8602; the crossing is interpolated between neighbouring measurements. At the paper's 3% it falls at a domain share of 0.9245, matching the law's prediction of 0.924 independently (the law's predicted general loss there, 2.9458, against a measured 2.9445). Outside 1.6% to 4.3% the table has no points to interpolate.

Why the two "optimal" shares disagree (about 30 to 48% domain in CMR, 92% in D-CPT): they answer different questions. CMR caps the general-loss rise at an absolute 0.05 nats and requires it to be heading down by the end; D-CPT caps it at a relative 3% (about 0.086 here); and the domains, base models, budgets and learning rates differ (CMR: a constant 3e-5 over 20B tokens; D-CPT: 10B tokens on Qwen1.5). Neither ratio transfers to your run; the method does. Practical use: run a few short pilots at different ratios, fit, pick the ratio, then commit the budget.

Other findings that transfer

  • Order and staging. The old page said a brief warm-up phase on general data before the domain mix stabilises training, and that staged curricula (general-heavy, then domain-heavy) beat static mixes "in several studies". This page could not find those studies unconfirmed. What is measured is narrower: Llama-3-SynE (Llama 3 8B, about 100B tokens) found an easy-to-difficult curriculum over its scientific data beat random sampling and the reverse order (§4.2), and used two stages (92.5B tokens at Chinese:English 2:8, then 7.5B at Chinese:English:synthetic 1:7:2). And Ibrahim et al. found that ingesting domains one after another, re-warming for each, did worse than one mixture, "of limited practical use" (Appendix A.1).
  • Model scale. The evidence that larger models forget less is mixed. Yıldız et al. found "smaller models are particularly sensitive to continual pretraining, showing the most significant rates of both learning and forgetting" (arXiv 2402.17400); the CMR study found larger models tolerate a larger domain share (29.8% at 460M to 47.8% at 3.1B). But in Ibrahim et al.'s weak shift the relative rise in Pile loss was about the same at both sizes: 12.4% at 405M and 13.1% at 10B with no replay, 2.8% and 2.3% with 5% replay derived (Table 4). Recipes that trained several sizes the same way lean towards the claim: Swallow's English average fell 5.0, 4.8 and 2.3 points at 7B, 13B and 70B ("the performance drop tends to be smaller as the model size increases", Table 4); ChipNeMo's MMLU moved −1.1, −2.0 and +0.8.
  • Tokenizer extension. For a new language or a domain the tokenizer splits badly (high fertility: many tokens per word), extend the vocabulary, train the new embeddings first in a brief embedding-only phase, then run full CPT. Chinese-LLaMA added 20,000 Chinese tokens to LLaMA's 32,000 (49,953 after merging) and, for 7B, trained only the new embeddings and output layer before the main stage (§2.2); Swallow added 11,176 Japanese subwords and cut the tokens needed for Japanese text by 56.2%; ChipNeMo added about 9K chip-design tokens, initialised from the mean of their old sub-tokens (Recipes compared); the toy here avoided the question by writing German umlauts as two letters. Fertility and its cost are on Tokenizers.
  • Parameter-level alternatives to replay help when replay tokens are unaffordable or the original data is unknown, but replay plus the schedule remains the default answer. Elastic weight consolidation (EWC) penalises moving weights that mattered for the old data (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017). LoRA-based CPT forgets less because it learns less (PEFT). Merging: in Ibrahim et al.'s preliminary test, TIES-merging separately trained models scored below continued pretraining with replay on every comparison (10B weak shift, English average 41.94 against 47.68; Appendix A.2, Table 6), and merging a continued model back into its base "does not increase performance". Later recipes report the opposite at scale: Thomson Reuters merged its continued model with the pre-CPT checkpoint and "an appropriate merge coefficient can recover most of the general capability lost during continual pre-training" (report, §3.3), and eBay merged e-Llama with its base to set the trade-off. Treat merging as a lever worth a pilot, not a settled answer.
  • Re-run post-training afterwards. CPT the base model, not the chat model, then post-train again (or at least SFT). Continued pretraining of an instruction-tuned model erodes instruction following: Llama 3 8B Instruct continued on 1B tokens of news dropped up to 10 points on the instruction-following set and 3 points on average; adding the "instruction residual" (instruct weights minus base weights) back onto a continued base recovered it without new instruction data (Jindal et al. 2024, §4.1). Every recipe in Recipes compared re-runs post-training after CPT.

What labs did: disclosed recipes

Fourteen continued-pretraining recipes could be checked against their sources (Recipes compared, with three builders' own mid-training stages and BloombergGPT as references). What they share:

  • Almost everyone replays. General shares run from 1% (Meditron) to 80% (Llama-3-SynE, whose "general" is English kept while adding Chinese); the median of the 13 that state one is 10% derived. Only Chinese-LLaMA describes none.
  • Peaks vary a hundredfold. From the base's own peak (Code Llama, Meditron) to 1/150 of it (DACP); the most common choices are one third and one tenth.
  • Few measure forgetting. Seven of the fourteen report a general benchmark before and after (DeepSeek-Coder-V2, DeepSeekMath, ChipNeMo, Swallow, e-Llama, Thomson Reuters, Llama-3-SynE); the largest drops are about five or six points on one benchmark (NaturalQuestions 53.4 to 47.5 for DeepSeek-Coder-V2, e-Llama 8B's leaderboard 17.2 to 12.6), and some general scores rise, nothing like the collapse of the no-replay runs above.
  • Token budgets range from 24B (ChipNeMo) to 6T (DeepSeek-Coder-V2, continued from a checkpoint before its learning-rate decay, the infinite-schedule idea in practice).
  • From scratch is the exception. BloombergGPT trained 50.6B parameters from scratch on 569B tokens, about half financial; since 2023 the domain models in this table all start from an open base.

What it costs: two documented pipelines

Practitioners almost never publish a budget next to a recipe, which is why these two are worth more than their technical content. Their dollar figures, with what each includes, sit on the parent page's Price list (Topic: llm-training-and-post-training); this page keeps the shape.

Thomson Reuters continued Qwen3.5-397B-A17B into Thomson-1.0, a legal, tax and news model: 200B curated tokens selected from a 19T-token pool with DatologyAI (1.05% kept derived), then DPO alignment against an open-source constitution and GSPO for context compaction and document caching (The Batch; technical report). The technical report fills in the recipe: the mid-training mix is "three roughly equal parts: curated proprietary documents, synthetic data generated from those documents, and curated general-capability replay data", so about a third is replay derived; the learning rate was chosen per scale by pilot sweeps; the continued model was merged with the pre-CPT checkpoint, then aligned with DPO and GSPO. Against its base it measured general average 76.7 against 73.5, with coding (39.9 against 43.9) and factuality (73.7 against 76.2) down (§3.3, Table 1). LawNext quotes Thomson Reuters saying CPT used its proprietary content "exclusively", which the report's replay third contradicts; the report is the technical source. Unusually, it continued an instruction-tuned, value-realigned checkpoint rather than a base model, and relied on the merge and the later DPO to restore behaviour. It is the customise-an-open-base path stated end to end, and its shape is this page's checklist: select hard from a much larger pool, keep some replay, then re-align.

Corrections

The old page said Thomson Reuters mid-trained the model "for a reported $40M in three months", with a 35B open-weights sibling "released alongside". The $40M covers talent and compute over about two years; the CTO put the final training run at $450,000, about 89 times less derived (LawNext, 24 Aug 2026); The Batch's "three months" is the outlier among the sources. The 35B sibling, on Qwen3.6-35B, will be released for academic and non-commercial use. Both corrections were made on the parent page first (checks of 2 October 2026), and both figures remain reported, not audited.

Periodic's Neon makes the same argument with a different corpus. Mid-training plus reinforcement learning of Kimi K2.6 on real laboratory records took it from 2.7% to 55.3% on FrontierXRD (134 lab samples), ahead of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 on a hard scientific-analysis evaluation at lower cost per analysis reported; it is deployed in labs analysing superconductor and magnet experiments, and the final run used 1,300 H200 GPUs (Periodic, 15 September 2026). The transferable part is the structural claim: domain training on proprietary operational data, the records an organisation generates anyway rather than a corpus assembled for the purpose, establishes a Pareto-optimal cost-performance frontier against general frontier models. Most organisations sizing a domain-adaptation project already hold that data and do not think of it as a corpus.

Checklist for a CPT run

  1. Define target and guard metrics before training: domain evaluations, general evaluations and safety. Evaluate the base model on all of them first; that is the line forgetting is measured against.
  2. Pilot runs to pick the mixture ratio (CMR- or D-CPT-style: a few short runs, fit, predict) and the peak learning rate. Start the pilots well below the pretraining peak and include one near it, since the published sweeps (0.5× to 2×) trade adaptation against forgetting; use a WSD-style schedule if more stages will follow.
  3. Replay source as close to the original pretraining data as you can get; deduplicate the domain data against your evaluations.
  4. Monitor both loss families during training, general and domain. Forgetting shows early: in the toy and in Ibrahim's runs the general loss jumps in the first few percent of steps, and "relative differences between [replay fractions] appear very early" (§2).
  5. Post-train afterwards, and compare against a no-CPT baseline fine-tuned the same way, to prove CPT was worth it.

Data mixing infrastructure is on Topic: data-curation-and-datasets; the re-alignment step on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR.

Common mistakes

Continuing the chat model instead of the base
Instruction following erodes within a billion tokens (Jindal et al.: up to 10 points). Continue the base, then post-train, or add the instruction residual back.
No general evaluation until the end
Forgetting happens in the first percent of steps. Track a general held-out loss from step zero, next to the domain loss.
Blaming the learning rate for all the forgetting
Under a strong shift most of the damage is the data: Ibrahim's never-re-warmed run still lost 76% as much. Lowering the peak trades adaptation for retention; replay is the lever that is nearly free.
Copying someone else's mixture ratio
CMR's 30 to 48% and D-CPT's 92% domain share are both "optimal" under different tolerances, domains and budgets. Fit your own on pilots.
Comparing losses across tokenizers or scales
Loss per token depends on the tokenizer, and a 0.2 rise means different things at 405M and 10B. Compare relative changes against the same model's base, on the same validation set.
Reading a headline budget as a run cost
Thomson Reuters' $40M is a two-year programme including staff; the final run was $450K. Say which one you compare.

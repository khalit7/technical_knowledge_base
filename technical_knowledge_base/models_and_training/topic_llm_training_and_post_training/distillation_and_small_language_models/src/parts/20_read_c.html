Whose text: off-policy against on-policy

Sequence-level and logit distillation train on text the teacher wrote: off-policy. At serving time the student continues from its own tokens, and the first time it drifts off the teacher's path it is somewhere training never showed it; errors compound (exposure bias). GKD: "current KD methods for auto-regressive sequence models suffer from distribution mismatch between output sequences seen during training and those generated by the student during inference" ({{Agarwal et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649}}).

On-policy distillation fixes it the way RL does: the student writes, the teacher grades every token. It keeps RL's relevance (training happens where the student goes) and adds a dense signal: one teacher opinion per token instead of one reward per answer. Thinking Machines sets each token's advantage to the negative reverse KL, estimated from the teacher's log-probability of the sampled token, with "a discount factor of zero", and runs it through an ordinary RL loss ({{blog|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/}}); GKD and Qwen3 match the teacher's full distribution at every student position instead. A middle way coarsens the distributions: NeoHorse-1 takes the reverse KL over K + 1 bins, the student's top-K candidate tokens at each position plus one bin for all remaining probability, so each position compares K + 1 probabilities instead of the whole vocabulary ({{NeoHorse-1|n:3db5c17b0d0d81e3b105e033a359ad24}}, Eq. 3).

Step through: one prompt from this page's toy task (find a route between two nodes of a fixed graph, at most two steps longer than the shortest) and one partly trained student, graded three ways. Divergence first, rollout policy second

A controlled strong-to-weak study varied rollout policy, KL direction and learning rate one at a time (Llama3 and Qwen2.5 students; scientific, medical and arithmetic reasoning): "rollout policy does not necessarily play a central role". Forward KL was "remarkably robust to rollout policy"; reverse KL "is substantially more sensitive and favours student-generated rollouts" ({{On-Policy or Off-Policy Learning?|n:3fe5c17b0d0d8136888ff145ba3a7bbb}}, {{arXiv 2609.35259|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35259}}). The choices come as a pair: reverse KL wants the student's own text; forward KL hardly cares whose text it sees. This page's toy agrees: seven ways of training the same 19,811-parameter student from the same 803,875-parameter teacher, three seeds each (standard deviation at most 2.2 points per method).

  • Soft targets beat hard labels on the same text (87.8% of routes correct against 80.6%), and temperature 2 helped a lot (94.7%, reaching 85% after 450 steps instead of 1,650 to 1,950).
  • The divergence mattered more than whose text: reverse KL 95.4% on the teacher's routes and 94.6% on the student's own; forward KL 87.8% and 88.2%. Reverse KL paid in variety: 4.8 to 4.9 distinct correct routes per 8 samples against 5.0 to 5.1 (the teacher: 5.7).
  • On-policy was cheaper for the same result: 3.0 × 1012 model FLOPs against 5.9 × 1012, because the 41-times-smaller student writes the text. The Qwen3 schedule works: SFT students at 75 to 77% halfway finished at 93.4% after switching to on-policy distillation, against 80.6% for those that stayed on SFT.
What the toy cannot show, and the study's other findings (depth)

The toy's routes are at most 9 tokens, so an off-policy student rarely strays far from the teacher's states, which is exactly when on-policy training has least to add. That the gap grows on long reasoning traces is the claim of GKD, MiniLLM and Thinking Machines, not something this toy tests. Every curve, the compute and the routes are in {{Distil a toy student|#t-toy}}.

In the controlled study, learning rate governed forgetting and update sparsity. On-policy data did help generalisation to harder Countdown variants under both directions, but "this advantage does not reliably persist after subsequent RLVR".

The teacher is a reward model, and can be hacked

The teacher's log-probabilities act as an implicit reward model, and they can reward student behaviour the teacher rarely produces itself. On-policy distillation "improves performance without expanding the student's capabilities": when the implicit reward is reliable it makes correct answers easier to sample; when it misaligns with quality, the student collapses into excessively long, repetitive output that the teacher scores well, reward hacking of the teacher ({{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}). Masking such unhealthy responses, or starting from an SFT checkpoint, each mitigated the collapse ({{Gains and Collapse in OPD (arXiv 2610.03185)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03185}}). What to check is how reliably the teacher grades student text, not how well it writes.

In multi-turn agent tasks the failure has a shape of its own, distillation overshoot: a weaker student passes its teacher early in on-policy distillation, then regresses, because the teacher keeps pulling it off the choices on which it was already succeeding faster than it repairs its mistakes. Masking the teacher's supervision on the student's successful choices and putting a floor under the student's probabilities gave up to 15.2 points more final success ({{SPOD (arXiv 2610.25034)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25034}}). Track the student's success rate during training, not only the divergence.

Distil, or train the small model with RL?

For reasoning, every published comparison points the same way: when a stronger teacher exists, distilling it beats RL on the small model, at a fraction of the cost.

  • DeepSeek-R1: Qwen2.5-32B trained with large-scale RL "over 10K steps" lands level with QwQ-32B-Preview; the same base fine-tuned on R1's samples beats it on every benchmark, by 25.6 points on AIME 2024 derived (72.6 − 47.0), with SFT only. The paper: "distilling more powerful models into smaller ones yields excellent results, whereas smaller models relying on the large-scale RL mentioned in this paper require enormous computational power and may not even achieve the performance of distillation" ({{DeepSeek-R1, Table 16|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}).
  • Qwen3: from the same off-policy-distilled Qwen3-8B, on-policy distillation reached 74.4 on AIME'24 against 67.6 for RL, in 1,800 GPU-hours against 17,920, derived 10.0%. Its pass@64 rose too (90.0 to 93.3, one question of 30), where RL's did not ({{Qwen3 §4.5, Table 21|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a19006e6b096a6e14b}}).
  • Thinking Machines (Qwen3-8B-Base student): from a checkpoint fine-tuned on 400K prompts of OpenThoughts-3 (60% on AIME'24), on-policy distillation reached 70% "in about 150 steps" (about 77K prompts, 4 samples each). Against continuing off-policy SFT to an extrapolated 2M prompts for the same 70%, that is 9 times cheaper when the SFT data already exists and about 30 times when the teacher's sampling is counted too: derived 1.5e21 / (8.4e19 + 8.2e19) = 9.0, and (3.4e21 + 1.5e21) / 1.66e20 = 29.5 ({{blog|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/}}).

The limit runs through all three: the student can only be as good as the teacher on what the teacher was asked, and in Qwen3's case "the whole pass@64 gain is one question per year" ({{Qwen3 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a19006e6b096a6e14b}}, which runs a toy Table 21 with three seeds). At the frontier there is no stronger teacher, so the signal has to come from RL: distillation copies capability, it does not create it. The nearest thing to a teacher there is the model itself: in JOLT one model is both student and a teacher conditioned on privileged information, trained jointly, with a reverse-KL student objective. Perplexity and NVIDIA report JOLT+ over GRPO at +4.2 points on MATH, +9.9 on AppWorld and +2.7 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, and the same MATH accuracy with 6.5 times fewer training tokens (vendor figures; {{blog, 21 October 2026|https://www.perplexity.ai/hub/blog/a-good-teacher-meets-the-student-where-they-are}}; {{arXiv 2610.10447|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10447}}). The paper's own caution: privileged conditioning "does not guarantee" that the distillation update improves the student.

The recipes behind the two tables, and two corrections (depth)

R1's distillation data: 804,745 samples (Math 395,285, Code 211,129, General 177,812, Logic 10,395, STEM 10,124) averaging 5,355.3 tokens, so 4.31B tokens per epoch derived, fine-tuned for 2 to 3 epochs; reasoning traces came from rejection sampling an RL checkpoint and keeping verified answers. The distilled models had SFT only, "even though incorporating RL could substantially boost model performance" (Table 5, B.3.3, B.4.3).

Thinking Machines' distil-back test: distilling an RL-trained policy back into its own starting point took "approximately 7-10x fewer gradient steps" than the RL had, "a compute efficiency of 50-100x".

Qwen3's small models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and 30B-A3B): off-policy distillation from teacher outputs in both thinking modes, then on-policy logit distillation against Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B (§4.5).

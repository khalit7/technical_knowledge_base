What the teacher knows: soft targets and temperature

A trained model rates every possible next token, and the wrong ones are not noise. In {{Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531}}'s example, a BMW "may only have a very small chance of being mistaken for a garbage truck, but that mistake is still many times more probable than mistaking it for a carrot." Those ratios say how the model generalises (later called dark knowledge), and a one-hot label throws them away. Fig. 1 runs real next-token logits from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct through the softmax.

Predict, then watch: on the confident prompts (the capital, the multiplication), how high must T go before the second answer gets 10%? Then compare the open prompt (the favourite animal), where the top token has only 9.4% at T = 1.

  1. The logits. The model scores all 151,936 tokens of its vocabulary after the prompt; the back row is the top seven: Canberra 17.98, Melbourne 13.50, Sydney 12.97.
  2. Softmax at T = 1: the soft target. Each logit goes through exp(zi / T) and the glass plate normalises: Canberra 96.9%, Melbourne 1.1%, Sydney 0.6%; the grey column is the other 151,929 tokens together, 0.7%.
  3. The hard label. A one-hot target keeps only Canberra at 100% (orange outline) and throws away the 3.1% the teacher spread over the rest, including which wrong answers it found plausible.
  4. T = 2. Halving every logit flattens the row: second over first rises from 0.011 to 0.106, but the top token falls to 13.3% and the long tail takes 82.2%.
  5. T = 4. The tail holds 99.6%; the second answer never reaches 10% (its peak is 3.2% near T = 1.5). With 152K tokens a high temperature mostly teaches the tail, which is why LLM distillation keeps T near 1 (below).

A confident teacher's wrong-answer probabilities are tiny, so they barely move a cross-entropy loss. Hinton's fix is the same temperature in the teacher's and the student's softmax during training, and a mixed loss:

qi = exp(zi / T) / Σj exp(zj / T)
L = (1 − α) · CE(y, q(T=1)) + α · T2 · KL(pteacher(T) ‖ qstudent(T))
zi
the logit of token i; T = 1 is the ordinary softmax
y
the true next token (the hard label), when there is one
T2
"the magnitudes of the gradients produced by the soft targets scale as 1/T2", so the soft term is multiplied back up to keep the mix stable as T changes; "a considerably lower weight" on the hard term worked best (§2)
Predict: soft targets with 3% of the data

Hinton's speech model (85M parameters) reaches 58.9% frame accuracy trained on all 2,000 hours. On only 3% of the data with hard labels it overfits and peaks at 44.5%. On the same 3% with soft targets from the model that saw everything, what does it reach?

57.0%, within 1.9 points of the full-data model, and "we did not have to do early stopping: the system with soft targets simply 'converged' to 57%" ({{Table 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531}}): derived (57.0 − 44.5) / (58.9 − 44.5) = 86.8% of the gap closed by the soft targets alone.

What changes for language models

  • The distribution is huge. One soft target is 151,936 numbers for Qwen2.5's vocabulary and 262K for Gemma's, against one integer for a hard label. Gemma 3 keeps a sample: "We sample 256 logits per token, weighted by teacher probabilities", trains on those with cross-entropy and sets the rest to zero ({{Gemma 3 §2.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2503.19786}}); why storing them all is impractical over 14T tokens is drawn on the {{Google DeepMind page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8173a0aafcb27cf7a5f3}}.
  • The temperature is usually 1. Minitron tried τ = 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 and 3.0 when distilling a pruned LLM and did best at 1.0, because "LLM logit distributions have higher entropy" than an image classifier's; keeping only the teacher's top K ≤ 100 logits gave "a significant drop in accuracy" ({{Minitron, appendix|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.14679}}). Gemma 2 and 3 and Qwen3 §4.5 report no temperature. With 150K tokens the tail already carries a lot at T = 1.
Hinton's other results, and temperature for small students (depth)

The same paper distilled a 10-model ensemble (61.1%) into one model (60.8%) that kept 86% of the ensemble's gain over the baseline (Table 1), and an MNIST student that never saw a 3 still got 98.6% of the test 3s right once its bias for 3 was corrected, purely from what the soft targets for other digits said about 3s (§3).

For a small MNIST student (30 units per layer), "temperatures in the range 2.5 to 4 worked significantly better than higher or lower temperatures"; with 300 or more units per layer, everything above 8 was similar (§3). Tune T and anneal the mix α of hard and soft loss; this page's toy runs one variant at T = 2 ({{Distil a toy student|#t-toy}}).

Forward or reverse KL

A student smaller than its teacher cannot match it everywhere, so the divergence decides where it is wrong. With teacher p and student q:

forward KL(p ‖ q) = Σx p(x) log [p(x) / q(x)]    reverse KL(q ‖ p) = Σx q(x) log [q(x) / p(x)]
forward
expectation under the teacher: missing anything the teacher finds likely is expensive, so the student covers every mode, even by putting mass between them
reverse
expectation under the student: mass where the teacher has little is expensive, so the student seeks one mode and ignores the rest

Predict, then watch: a two-mode teacher and a student that can make only one bump (a position and a width). Where will each objective put the student? Both runs start from the same student and take real gradient steps on the exact KL, in your browser.

  1. The teacher. Two modes over 40 tokens, centred on 11 and 29, with 60% of the mass on the left; the shaded valley (tokens 16 to 24) holds 3.5% of it.
  2. One student, two copies. A single narrow bump at token 22 (width 2), the same start for both objectives: the blue row will follow forward KL, the orange row reverse KL.
  3. Forward KL, 40 steps. The error is weighted by the teacher's probability, so both modes pull (the streams): the student widens and settles at centre 17.8, width 10.8, with 33.9% of its mass in the valley.
  4. Reverse KL, 40 steps. The error is weighted by the student's own probability, so only the mode it already overlaps pulls: it slides to the right mode and narrows, to centre 29.0, width 2.5.
  5. Cover or commit. In the valley: forward 33.9%, reverse 3.6%, teacher 3.5%. Forward KL samples tokens the teacher almost never writes; reverse KL never does, and never writes the other mode either. It picked the right mode because it started nearer, even though that mode holds less of the teacher's mass.

In a language model the same happens per position, across 150K tokens: a forward-KL student hedges between the teacher's ways of saying something and samples blends of them; a reverse-KL student commits to one and says it well, at the cost of diversity. Classic logit distillation and SFT on teacher samples both minimise forward KL. The published choices disagree because the jobs differ:

  • {{MiniLLM|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08543}}: forward KL makes the student "overestimate the low-probability regions of the teacher distribution", text the teacher would never write, so it uses reverse KL for generation.
  • {{GKD|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649}} makes the divergence a parameter ("useful when the student lacks the expressivity to mimic the teacher's distribution"): a generalised Jensen-Shannon divergence for translation, forward KL for summarisation and arithmetic (Figure 1).
  • Minitron, healing a pruned model with pretraining-style logit distillation, measured the opposite of MiniLLM: forward KL reached an LM loss of 2.155 against 2.665 for reverse KL (Table 16).
  • Thinking Machines chose reverse KL for on-policy distillation because it is "mode seeking": "it learns one specific behavior (the teacher's) instead of spreading its distribution across several suboptimal options" ({{blog|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/}}).

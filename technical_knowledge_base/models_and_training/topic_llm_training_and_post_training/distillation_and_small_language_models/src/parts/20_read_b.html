Whose text: off-policy against on-policy

Sequence-level and logit distillation both train on text the teacher wrote (or a fixed corpus). That is off-policy: at serving time the student continues from its own previous tokens, and the first time it drifts off the teacher's path it is somewhere training never showed it. Errors compound, a problem known as exposure bias. GKD's diagnosis: "current KD methods for auto-regressive sequence models suffer from distribution mismatch between output sequences seen during training and those generated by the student during inference" ({{Agarwal et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649}}).

On-policy distillation fixes the mismatch the way reinforcement learning does: the student writes the text, then the teacher grades every token of it. It keeps RL's relevance (training happens exactly where the student goes) and adds what RL lacks, a dense signal: one teacher opinion per token instead of one reward per answer. Thinking Machines' recipe sets each token's advantage to the negative reverse KL, estimated from the teacher's log-probability of the token the student sampled, with "a discount factor of zero", and runs it through an ordinary RL loss ({{blog|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/}}). GKD and Qwen3 match the teacher's full distribution at every student position instead.

Try: one prompt from this page's toy task (find a route between two nodes of a fixed graph, at most two steps longer than the shortest), and one partly trained student, graded three ways. What the toy shows once the students are trained

Seven ways of training the same 19,811-parameter student from the same 803,875-parameter teacher, same initial weights, same prompts per step, same number of steps, three seeds each. The {{Distil a toy student|#t-toy}} tab has every curve, the compute each method spent, and the routes each student writes.

Four results, consistent across the three seeds (standard deviation at most 2.2 points per method):

  • Soft targets beat hard labels on the same text. Trained on the very same teacher routes, the student that matched the teacher's whole distribution got 87.8% of its routes right; the one trained on the sampled tokens alone got 80.6%.
  • Temperature 2 helped a lot here. Forward KL with both distributions softened at T = 2 reached 94.7%, and reached 85% after 450 steps instead of 1,650 to 1,950: in this small, confident student, Hinton's dark knowledge is real.
  • The divergence mattered more than whose text. Reverse KL scored 95.4% on the teacher's routes and 94.6% on the student's own; forward KL 87.8% and 88.2%. Reverse KL bought its accuracy with variety: 4.8 to 4.9 distinct correct routes per 8 samples against 5.0 to 5.1 for forward KL (the teacher: 5.7). The routes are short (at most 9 tokens), so a student rarely strays far from where the teacher goes, which is exactly when on-policy training has least to add; that this gap grows on long reasoning traces is the claim of GKD, MiniLLM and Thinking Machines, not something this toy can show.
  • On-policy was cheaper for the same result. On-policy reverse KL spent 3.0 × 1012 model FLOPs against 5.9 × 1012 for reverse KL on the teacher's routes, because the 41-times-smaller student writes the text instead of the teacher. And the Qwen3 schedule works: the SFT students, at 75 to 77% at the halfway point, finished at 93.4% after switching to on-policy distillation, against 80.6% for those that carried on with SFT.

What carries over to real models, and what does not: the toy shows the mechanisms (dense per-token grading, training on the student's own states, mode-seeking against mass-covering) on a task where correctness can be checked exactly. It cannot show the effect sizes of a 32B teacher and an 8B student; those are in the next section, from the labs' own runs.

Distil, or train the small model with RL?

For reasoning, the published comparisons all point the same way: when a strong teacher exists, distilling it beats running reinforcement learning on the small model, and costs far less.

  • DeepSeek-R1: Qwen2.5-32B trained with large-scale RL "over 10K steps" lands level with QwQ-32B-Preview; the same base fine-tuned on R1's samples beats it on every benchmark, by 25.6 points on AIME 2024 derived (72.6 − 47.0). The distilled models had SFT only, "even though incorporating RL could substantially boost model performance". The data: 804,745 samples (Math 395,285, Code 211,129, General 177,812, Logic 10,395, STEM 10,124) averaging 5,355.3 tokens, so 4.31B tokens per epoch derived, fine-tuned for 2 to 3 epochs; reasoning traces came from rejection sampling an RL checkpoint and keeping verified answers ({{DeepSeek-R1, Table 5, Table 16, B.3.3, B.4.3|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}). The paper's conclusion: "distilling more powerful models into smaller ones yields excellent results, whereas smaller models relying on the large-scale RL mentioned in this paper require enormous computational power and may not even achieve the performance of distillation."
  • Qwen3: from the same off-policy-distilled Qwen3-8B, on-policy distillation reached 74.4 on AIME'24 against 67.6 for RL, in 1,800 GPU-hours against 17,920, derived 10.0%. Its pass@64 rose too (90.0 to 93.3, one question of 30), where RL's did not. The small Qwen3 models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and 30B-A3B) were all made this way: off-policy distillation from teacher outputs in both thinking modes, then on-policy logit distillation against Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B ({{Qwen3 §4.5, Table 21|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a19006e6b096a6e14b}}).
  • Thinking Machines reproduced the idea with Qwen3-8B-Base as the student. From a checkpoint fine-tuned on 400K prompts of OpenThoughts-3 (60% on AIME'24), on-policy distillation reached 70% "in about 150 steps" (about 77K prompts, 4 samples each). Their cost table puts this against continuing the off-policy SFT to an extrapolated 2M prompts for the same 70%: 9 times cheaper when the SFT data already exists, about 30 times when the teacher's sampling for SFT is counted too. Both reproduce: derived 1.5e21 / (8.4e19 + 8.2e19) = 9.0, and (3.4e21 + 1.5e21) / 1.66e20 = 29.5 ({{blog|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/}}). In a separate test, distilling an RL-trained policy back into its own starting point took "approximately 7-10x fewer gradient steps" than the RL had, "a compute efficiency of 50-100x".
Corrected from the old page
The old page credited on-policy distillation with "~10-30x compute efficiency vs RL". In Thinking Machines' post, the 9 to 30 times is against off-policy distillation (SFT extrapolated to 2M prompts), not against RL. Against RL, the published figures are Qwen3's one tenth of the GPU-hours (one run, Table 21) and Thinking Machines' 7 to 10 times fewer steps in their distil-back experiment. The old page also said Qwen3's small models were distilled "from the flagship"; §4.5 names Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B as the teacher.

The caveat that runs through all three: the student can only be as good as the teacher on what the teacher was asked, and "the whole pass@64 gain is one question per year" in Qwen3's case ({{Qwen3 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a19006e6b096a6e14b}}, which also runs a toy version of Table 21 with three seeds). When no stronger teacher exists, as at the frontier, RL is the only option: distillation copies capability, it does not create it.

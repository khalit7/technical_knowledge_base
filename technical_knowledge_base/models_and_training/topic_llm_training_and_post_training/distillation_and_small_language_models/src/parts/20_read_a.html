In one screen

Knowledge distillation trains a small model, the student, to reproduce what a large model, the teacher, does. It is one of four ways to make a model smaller, and the one that buys the most quality per parameter: almost every capable model under 10B parameters in 2026 was taught by a bigger one.

The four levers, and where each is explained in depth:

1 · Pruning

Remove weights: single weights (unstructured sparsity) or whole structures (attention heads, MLP channels, the embedding width, or entire layers, which is depth pruning). Quality drops, so a pruned model is healed by more training, now usually distillation from the unpruned original. NVIDIA's Minitron recipe (prune a big model's width or depth, then distil into it) produced Llama-3.1-Minitron and the Nemotron Nano line for a fraction of the tokens of training from scratch. Token budgets below.

2 · Quantization

Store each weight in fewer bits; no new model is trained unless the quantization is quantization-aware. The current marker for how far it goes: Ternary Bonsai 2 27B (PrismML, 17 September 2026) holds Qwen3.8 27B's weights at 1.76 effective bits, 5.9 GB, and keeps 98.2% of its aggregate benchmark score (83.9 against 85.4). Nine times smaller for about 1.5 points is a better trade than most distillation achieves, which argues for trying this lever first. Details and the arithmetic: {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}.

3 · Low-rank factorisation

Replace a weight matrix W by a product of two thin ones (or compress it with an SVD). Rarely used alone to shrink an LLM; it lives on in LoRA's low-rank updates ({{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba08eef56b3e3b4eb}}) and in architecture choices such as DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention, which stores a low-rank latent instead of full keys and values ({{DeepSeek|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}).

4 · Distillation

Train the student on the teacher's outputs. It is the dominant lever for quality per parameter, and it combines with the other three: prune then distil, distil then quantize. The rest of this page.

Distillation comes in three flavours, which differ in whose text the student trains on and how much of the teacher's opinion it sees at each position:

What the teacher knows: soft targets and temperature

A trained model assigns a probability to every possible next token, not just the right one. The wrong ones are not noise. In {{Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531}}'s example, an image of a BMW "may only have a very small chance of being mistaken for a garbage truck, but that mistake is still many times more probable than mistaking it for a carrot." Those ratios say how the model generalises, and a one-hot label throws them away. This is what later writers called dark knowledge.

The problem is that a confident model's wrong-answer probabilities are tiny, so they barely move a cross-entropy loss. Hinton's fix is a temperature T in the softmax, applied to the teacher and the student alike during training:

qi = exp(zi / T) / Σj exp(zj / T)
L = (1 − α) · CE(y, q(T=1)) + α · T2 · KL(pteacher(T) ‖ qstudent(T))
zi
the logit of token i; T = 1 is the ordinary softmax, higher T flattens it
y
the true next token (the hard label), when there is one
T2
"the magnitudes of the gradients produced by the soft targets scale as 1/T2", so the soft term is multiplied by T2 to keep the mix stable as T changes; Hinton found "a considerably lower weight" on the hard term worked best (§2)

Predict: soft targets with 3% of the data

Hinton's speech model (85M parameters) reaches 58.9% frame accuracy trained on all 2,000 hours. Trained on only 3% of the data with hard labels, it overfits and peaks at 44.5%. Trained on the same 3% but with soft targets from the model that saw everything, what does it reach?

57.0%, within 1.9 points of the full-data model, and "we did not have to do early stopping: the system with soft targets simply 'converged' to 57%" ({{Table 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531}}). That is derived (57.0 − 44.5) / (58.9 − 44.5) = 86.8% of the gap closed by labels alone. The same paper distilled a 10-model ensemble (61.1%) into one model (60.8%) that kept 86% of the ensemble's gain over the baseline (Table 1), and an MNIST student that never saw a 3 still got 98.6% of the test 3s right once its bias for 3 was corrected, purely from what the soft targets for other digits said about 3s (§3).

What changes for language models

  • The distribution is huge. A soft target at one position is 151,936 numbers for Qwen2.5's vocabulary and 262K for Gemma's, against one integer for a hard label. Gemma 3 keeps a sample: "We sample 256 logits per token, weighted by teacher probabilities", trains on those with cross-entropy and sets the rest to zero ({{Gemma 3 §2.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2503.19786}}); why storing them all is impractical over 14T tokens is drawn on the {{Google DeepMind page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8173a0aafcb27cf7a5f3}}.
  • The temperature is usually 1. Minitron tried τ = 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 and 3.0 when distilling a pruned LLM and got the best results at 1.0, reasoning that "LLM logit distributions have higher entropy" than an image classifier's spiky ones; keeping only the teacher's top K ≤ 100 logits gave "a significant drop in accuracy" ({{Minitron, appendix|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.14679}}). Gemma 2 and 3 and Qwen3 §4.5 do not report a temperature at all. With 150K tokens, the tail already carries a lot at T = 1: in the animal prompt above, the top token has only 9.4%.
  • Hinton tuned it. For a small MNIST student (30 units per layer), "temperatures in the range 2.5 to 4 worked significantly better than higher or lower temperatures"; with 300 or more units per layer, everything above 8 was similar (§3). Tune T and anneal the mix α of hard and soft loss; this page's toy runs one variant at T = 2 ({{Distil a toy student|#t-toy}}).

Forward or reverse KL

A student that is smaller than its teacher cannot match it everywhere, so the choice of what to minimise decides where it is wrong. With teacher distribution p and student q:

forward KL(p ‖ q) = Σx p(x) log [p(x) / q(x)]    reverse KL(q ‖ p) = Σx q(x) log [q(x) / p(x)]
forward
expectation under the teacher: punishes the student for missing anything the teacher considers likely, so it covers every mode, even if that means putting mass between them
reverse
expectation under the student: punishes the student for putting mass where the teacher puts little, so it seeks one mode and ignores the rest

Classic logit distillation and SFT on teacher samples both minimise forward KL. {{MiniLLM|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08543}} argued that for generation this is the wrong way round: forward KL makes the student "overestimate the low-probability regions of the teacher distribution", which when sampled produce text the teacher would never write, so it switched to reverse KL. {{GKD|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649}} treats the divergence as a choice ("useful when the student lacks the expressivity to mimic the teacher's distribution") and in its own experiments picked differently per task: a generalised Jensen-Shannon divergence for translation, forward KL for summarisation and arithmetic (Figure 1). For pretraining-style logit distillation of a pruned model, Minitron measured the opposite of MiniLLM: forward KL reached an LM loss of 2.155 against 2.665 for reverse KL (Table 16). Thinking Machines chose reverse KL for on-policy distillation because it is "mode seeking": "it learns one specific behavior (the teacher's) instead of spreading its distribution across several suboptimal options" ({{blog|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/}}).

The picture is the whole argument in one dimension. In a language model the same thing happens per position, across 150K tokens and thousands of positions: a forward-KL student hedges between the teacher's ways of saying something and samples blends of them; a reverse-KL student commits to one way and says it well, at the cost of diversity. Which matters more depends on the job, which is why GKD made it a parameter. The toy below measures both on a real task: success and the number of distinct correct answers.

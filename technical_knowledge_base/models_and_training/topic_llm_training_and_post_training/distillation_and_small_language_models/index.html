Distillation and Small Language Models

How a small model learns from a big one: what a teacher's probabilities carry that a correct answer does not, forward against reverse KL, the teacher's text against the student's own, pruning before distilling, and where the 0.3B to 16B tier stands on independent benchmarks. In one screen

Knowledge distillation trains a small model, the student, to reproduce what a large model, the teacher, does. It is one of four ways to make a model smaller, and the one that buys the most quality per parameter: almost every capable model under 10B parameters in 2026 was taught by a bigger one.

The four levers, and where each is explained in depth:

1 · Pruning

Remove weights: single weights (unstructured sparsity) or whole structures (attention heads, MLP channels, the embedding width, or entire layers, which is depth pruning). Quality drops, so a pruned model is healed by more training, now usually distillation from the unpruned original. NVIDIA's Minitron recipe (prune a big model's width or depth, then distil into it) produced Llama-3.1-Minitron and the Nemotron Nano line for a fraction of the tokens of training from scratch. Token budgets below.

2 · Quantization

Store each weight in fewer bits; no new model is trained unless the quantization is quantization-aware. The current marker for how far it goes: Ternary Bonsai 2 27B (PrismML, 17 September 2026) holds Qwen3.8 27B's weights at 1.76 effective bits, 5.9 GB, and keeps 98.2% of its aggregate benchmark score (83.9 against 85.4). Nine times smaller for about 1.5 points is a better trade than most distillation achieves, which argues for trying this lever first. Details and the arithmetic: Quantization and Precision.

3 · Low-rank factorisation

Replace a weight matrix W by a product of two thin ones (or compress it with an SVD). Rarely used alone to shrink an LLM; it lives on in LoRA's low-rank updates (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT)) and in architecture choices such as DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention, which stores a low-rank latent instead of full keys and values (DeepSeek).

4 · Distillation

Train the student on the teacher's outputs. It is the dominant lever for quality per parameter, and it combines with the other three: prune then distil, distil then quantize. The rest of this page.

Distillation comes in three flavours, which differ in whose text the student trains on and how much of the teacher's opinion it sees at each position:

What the teacher knows: soft targets and temperature

A trained model assigns a probability to every possible next token, not just the right one. The wrong ones are not noise. In Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015)'s example, an image of a BMW "may only have a very small chance of being mistaken for a garbage truck, but that mistake is still many times more probable than mistaking it for a carrot." Those ratios say how the model generalises, and a one-hot label throws them away. This is what later writers called dark knowledge.

The problem is that a confident model's wrong-answer probabilities are tiny, so they barely move a cross-entropy loss. Hinton's fix is a temperature T in the softmax, applied to the teacher and the student alike during training:

qi = exp(zi / T) / Σj exp(zj / T)
L = (1 − α) · CE(y, q(T=1)) + α · T2 · KL(pteacher(T) ‖ qstudent(T))
zi
the logit of token i; T = 1 is the ordinary softmax, higher T flattens it
y
the true next token (the hard label), when there is one
T2
"the magnitudes of the gradients produced by the soft targets scale as 1/T2", so the soft term is multiplied by T2 to keep the mix stable as T changes; Hinton found "a considerably lower weight" on the hard term worked best (§2)

Predict: soft targets with 3% of the data

Hinton's speech model (85M parameters) reaches 58.9% frame accuracy trained on all 2,000 hours. Trained on only 3% of the data with hard labels, it overfits and peaks at 44.5%. Trained on the same 3% but with soft targets from the model that saw everything, what does it reach?

57.0%, within 1.9 points of the full-data model, and "we did not have to do early stopping: the system with soft targets simply 'converged' to 57%" (Table 5). That is derived (57.0 − 44.5) / (58.9 − 44.5) = 86.8% of the gap closed by labels alone. The same paper distilled a 10-model ensemble (61.1%) into one model (60.8%) that kept 86% of the ensemble's gain over the baseline (Table 1), and an MNIST student that never saw a 3 still got 98.6% of the test 3s right once its bias for 3 was corrected, purely from what the soft targets for other digits said about 3s (§3).

What changes for language models

  • The distribution is huge. A soft target at one position is 151,936 numbers for Qwen2.5's vocabulary and 262K for Gemma's, against one integer for a hard label. Gemma 3 keeps a sample: "We sample 256 logits per token, weighted by teacher probabilities", trains on those with cross-entropy and sets the rest to zero (Gemma 3 §2.2); why storing them all is impractical over 14T tokens is drawn on the Google DeepMind page.
  • The temperature is usually 1. Minitron tried τ = 0.1, 0.5, 1.0 and 3.0 when distilling a pruned LLM and got the best results at 1.0, reasoning that "LLM logit distributions have higher entropy" than an image classifier's spiky ones; keeping only the teacher's top K ≤ 100 logits gave "a significant drop in accuracy" (Minitron, appendix). Gemma 2 and 3 and Qwen3 §4.5 do not report a temperature at all. With 150K tokens, the tail already carries a lot at T = 1: in the animal prompt above, the top token has only 9.4%.
  • Hinton tuned it. For a small MNIST student (30 units per layer), "temperatures in the range 2.5 to 4 worked significantly better than higher or lower temperatures"; with 300 or more units per layer, everything above 8 was similar (§3). Tune T and anneal the mix α of hard and soft loss; this page's toy runs one variant at T = 2 (Distil a toy student).

Forward or reverse KL

A student that is smaller than its teacher cannot match it everywhere, so the choice of what to minimise decides where it is wrong. With teacher distribution p and student q:

forward KL(p ‖ q) = Σx p(x) log [p(x) / q(x)]    reverse KL(q ‖ p) = Σx q(x) log [q(x) / p(x)]
forward
expectation under the teacher: punishes the student for missing anything the teacher considers likely, so it covers every mode, even if that means putting mass between them
reverse
expectation under the student: punishes the student for putting mass where the teacher puts little, so it seeks one mode and ignores the rest

Classic logit distillation and SFT on teacher samples both minimise forward KL. MiniLLM argued that for generation this is the wrong way round: forward KL makes the student "overestimate the low-probability regions of the teacher distribution", which when sampled produce text the teacher would never write, so it switched to reverse KL. GKD treats the divergence as a choice ("useful when the student lacks the expressivity to mimic the teacher's distribution") and in its own experiments picked differently per task: a generalised Jensen-Shannon divergence for translation, forward KL for summarisation and arithmetic (Figure 1). For pretraining-style logit distillation of a pruned model, Minitron measured the opposite of MiniLLM: forward KL reached an LM loss of 2.155 against 2.665 for reverse KL (Table 16). Thinking Machines chose reverse KL for on-policy distillation because it is "mode seeking": "it learns one specific behavior (the teacher's) instead of spreading its distribution across several suboptimal options" (blog).

Illustrative teacher (two Gaussian bumps over 40 tokens, centres 11 and 29) and student family; the optimisation is real: plain gradient descent on the exact KL, gradients by central differences, 40 steps: 8 × the gradient on the position (capped at 1.5 tokens a step) and 0.15 × the gradient on the log-width (capped at 0.15).

The picture is the whole argument in one dimension. In a language model the same thing happens per position, across 150K tokens and thousands of positions: a forward-KL student hedges between the teacher's ways of saying something and samples blends of them; a reverse-KL student commits to one way and says it well, at the cost of diversity. Which matters more depends on the job, which is why GKD made it a parameter. The toy below measures both on a real task: success and the number of distinct correct answers.

Whose text: off-policy against on-policy

Sequence-level and logit distillation both train on text the teacher wrote (or a fixed corpus). That is off-policy: at serving time the student continues from its own previous tokens, and the first time it drifts off the teacher's path it is somewhere training never showed it. Errors compound, a problem known as exposure bias. GKD's diagnosis: "current KD methods for auto-regressive sequence models suffer from distribution mismatch between output sequences seen during training and those generated by the student during inference" (Agarwal et al.).

On-policy distillation fixes the mismatch the way reinforcement learning does: the student writes the text, then the teacher grades every token of it. It keeps RL's relevance (training happens exactly where the student goes) and adds what RL lacks, a dense signal: one teacher opinion per token instead of one reward per answer. Thinking Machines' recipe sets each token's advantage to the negative reverse KL, estimated from the teacher's log-probability of the token the student sampled, with "a discount factor of zero", and runs it through an ordinary RL loss (blog). GKD and Qwen3 match the teacher's full distribution at every student position instead.

What the toy shows once the students are trained

Seven ways of training the same 19,811-parameter student from the same 803,875-parameter teacher, same initial weights, same prompts per step, same number of steps, three seeds each. The Distil a toy student tab has every curve, the compute each method spent, and the routes each student writes.

Four results, consistent across the three seeds (standard deviation at most 2.2 points per method):

  • Soft targets beat hard labels on the same text. Trained on the very same teacher routes, the student that matched the teacher's whole distribution got 87.8% of its routes right; the one trained on the sampled tokens alone got 80.6%.
  • Temperature 2 helped a lot here. Forward KL with both distributions softened at T = 2 reached 94.7%, and reached 85% after 450 steps instead of 1,650 to 1,950: in this small, confident student, Hinton's dark knowledge is real.
  • The divergence mattered more than whose text. Reverse KL scored 95.4% on the teacher's routes and 94.6% on the student's own; forward KL 87.8% and 88.2%. Reverse KL bought its accuracy with variety: 4.8 to 4.9 distinct correct routes per 8 samples against 5.0 to 5.1 for forward KL (the teacher: 5.7). The routes are short (at most 9 tokens), so a student rarely strays far from where the teacher goes, which is exactly when on-policy training has least to add; that this gap grows on long reasoning traces is the claim of GKD, MiniLLM and Thinking Machines, not something this toy can show.
  • On-policy was cheaper for the same result. On-policy reverse KL spent 3.0 × 1012 model FLOPs against 5.9 × 1012 for reverse KL on the teacher's routes, because the 41-times-smaller student writes the text instead of the teacher. And the Qwen3 schedule works: the SFT students, at 75 to 77% at the halfway point, finished at 93.4% after switching to on-policy distillation, against 80.6% for those that carried on with SFT.

What carries over to real models, and what does not: the toy shows the mechanisms (dense per-token grading, training on the student's own states, mode-seeking against mass-covering) on a task where correctness can be checked exactly. It cannot show the effect sizes of a 32B teacher and an 8B student; those are in the next section, from the labs' own runs.

Distil, or train the small model with RL?

For reasoning, the published comparisons all point the same way: when a strong teacher exists, distilling it beats running reinforcement learning on the small model, and costs far less.

  • DeepSeek-R1: Qwen2.5-32B trained with large-scale RL "over 10K steps" lands level with QwQ-32B-Preview; the same base fine-tuned on R1's samples beats it on every benchmark, by 25.6 points on AIME 2024 derived (72.6 − 47.0). The distilled models had SFT only, "even though incorporating RL could substantially boost model performance". The data: 804,745 samples (Math 395,285, Code 211,129, General 177,812, Logic 10,395, STEM 10,124) averaging 5,355.3 tokens, so 4.31B tokens per epoch derived, fine-tuned for 2 to 3 epochs; reasoning traces came from rejection sampling an RL checkpoint and keeping verified answers (DeepSeek-R1, Table 5, Table 16, B.3.3, B.4.3). The paper's conclusion: "distilling more powerful models into smaller ones yields excellent results, whereas smaller models relying on the large-scale RL mentioned in this paper require enormous computational power and may not even achieve the performance of distillation."
  • Qwen3: from the same off-policy-distilled Qwen3-8B, on-policy distillation reached 74.4 on AIME'24 against 67.6 for RL, in 1,800 GPU-hours against 17,920, derived 10.0%. Its pass@64 rose too (90.0 to 93.3, one question of 30), where RL's did not. The small Qwen3 models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and 30B-A3B) were all made this way: off-policy distillation from teacher outputs in both thinking modes, then on-policy logit distillation against Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B (Qwen3 §4.5, Table 21).
  • Thinking Machines reproduced the idea with Qwen3-8B-Base as the student. From a checkpoint fine-tuned on 400K prompts of OpenThoughts-3 (60% on AIME'24), on-policy distillation reached 70% "in about 150 steps" (about 77K prompts, 4 samples each). Their cost table puts this against continuing the off-policy SFT to an extrapolated 2M prompts for the same 70%: 9 times cheaper when the SFT data already exists, about 30 times when the teacher's sampling for SFT is counted too. Both reproduce: derived 1.5e21 / (8.4e19 + 8.2e19) = 9.0, and (3.4e21 + 1.5e21) / 1.66e20 = 29.5 (blog). In a separate test, distilling an RL-trained policy back into its own starting point took "approximately 7-10x fewer gradient steps" than the RL had, "a compute efficiency of 50-100x".
Corrected from the old page
The old page credited on-policy distillation with "~10-30x compute efficiency vs RL". In Thinking Machines' post, the 9 to 30 times is against off-policy distillation (SFT extrapolated to 2M prompts), not against RL. Against RL, the published figures are Qwen3's one tenth of the GPU-hours (one run, Table 21) and Thinking Machines' 7 to 10 times fewer steps in their distil-back experiment. The old page also said Qwen3's small models were distilled "from the flagship"; §4.5 names Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B as the teacher.

The caveat that runs through all three: the student can only be as good as the teacher on what the teacher was asked, and "the whole pass@64 gain is one question per year" in Qwen3's case (Qwen3 paper page, which also runs a toy version of Table 21 with three seeds). When no stronger teacher exists, as at the frontier, RL is the only option: distillation copies capability, it does not create it.

Prune, then distil: the token budgets

Pruning starts the student from the teacher's own weights instead of from random ones, so it has much less to learn. NVIDIA's recipe ranks every attention head, MLP channel, embedding dimension and layer by its effect on activations over a small calibration set (1,024 samples), cuts the least important to the target size in one shot, then heals the result with logit distillation from the unpruned model. Its best practices: prefer width pruning to depth pruning, and "train the largest one and prune+distill iteratively to smaller LLMs" (Minitron, §4.1). The headline is the token budget, drawn here against the tokens the teacher (or the paper's comparison model) was trained on.

MN-Minitron 8B's 40 times is against Llama 3.1 8B's 15T, not against its own teacher, Mistral NeMo 12B, whose token count is not public.
  • A family is cheaper than its members. Minitron prices training the 15B, 8B and 4B Nemotron-4 sizes from scratch at (4.4 + 2.5 + 1.2) × 1017 FLOPs per step, against (4.4 + 2.5/40 + 1.2/40) × 1017 when the small two are pruned from the big one: derived 1.80 times cheaper, the paper's 1.8. The saving is bounded by the big model, which still has to be trained in full.
  • Not every pruned model is cheap. Llama 3.2 1B and 3B were pruned from Llama 3.1 8B in one shot and then pretrained on "up to 9T tokens" with the 8B's and 70B's logits as token-level targets. Their card prices it: 370K and 460K H100-hours of training, plus 86K hours just to generate the teachers' logits, derived 10.4% on top of the students' own training. (The card's "916k" cumulative total does not match its own table's 833K + 86K = 919K.) (Llama 3.2 model card; the parent recipe: Llama 3 Herd.)
  • Distillation without pruning, for the whole of pretraining. Gemma 2's 2B and 9B were trained on 2T and 8T tokens of teacher distributions, "more than 50×" the compute-optimal amount (by 20 tokens per non-embedding parameter it is 49 and 48 times, derived, so the report's rule is not stated). The ablation that justified it: a 2B trained on 500B tokens scored 60.3 from scratch and 67.7 distilled from a 7B teacher (Table 6), and the gain held from 200M to 1B parameters (Table 7) (Gemma 2). Gemma 3 distilled every size, 1B to 27B, on 2T to 14T tokens (Gemma 3).
  • Pruning without distillation works too, less well: Sheared-LLaMA pruned LLaMA 2 7B to 2.7B and healed it with 50B tokens of plain continued pretraining (Sheared LLaMA); the Minitron papers' ablations found distillation recovers more than conventional fine-tuning (Minitron in practice, Figure 7).

Defaults reproduce Minitron's 40x and 1.8x, the Llama-3.1-Minitron 150x (as 159.6, which the paper rounds down) and MN-Minitron's 40x independently from the papers' token counts; Gemma 2's "more than 50x" does not reproduce exactly at 20 tokens per parameter (49 and 48), shown as such.

The small tier in 2026

The 0.5B to 9B tier is now genuinely capable: tool use, a thinking mode, 128K context and images in some. Three things drove it: better data, token budgets far past Chinchilla's 20 per parameter, and distillation from frontier teachers. The Small models compared tab places 110 small open-weight models on Artificial Analysis's own measurements over time; the lab names below are the ones worth knowing, and how each was made.

Qwen3 0.6B to 14B (Alibaba)

Strong-to-weak distillation: off-policy, then on-policy logit distillation from Qwen3-32B or 235B-A22B, with both thinking modes in one model (Qwen3 §4.5). Lab page: Alibaba: Qwen.

SmolLM3 3B (Hugging Face)

The fully open blueprint: 11.2T pretraining tokens in three stages, then 1.8B tokens of SFT (1B without reasoning, 0.8B with), much of it generated by "prompting Qwen3-32B in reasoning mode", and preference pairs with Qwen3-32B's answer as chosen and Qwen3-0.6B's as rejected. Claims to beat Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen2.5-3B and to stay competitive with 4B models reported (SmolLM3 blog, 8 July 2025).

Gemma 4 (Google)

E2B and E4B for devices, a dense 12B Unified and 31B, and 26B A4B, a mixture of experts with 25.2B weights and 3.8B active per token (8 of 128 experts plus a shared one). You pay for 26B of weights in memory to run about 4B of matmuls per token: the right trade when latency-bound and memory-rich, the wrong one on a phone. Apache 2.0 (Google DeepMind page).

Phi-4 family (Microsoft)

14B and mini (3.8B): the synthetic-data lineage. Phi-4's pretraining and DPO data were generated with GPT-4o, sequence-level distillation at scale, and the report notes it surpasses its teacher on STEM questions (Phi-4 report). Strong benchmarks; its "mixed real-world reputation" is the old page's judgement unconfirmed, and a generator that has seen the evaluations makes contamination hard to rule out (Other notable providers).

Llama 3.2 1B and 3B (Meta)

Pruned from Llama 3.1 8B, then pretrained on up to 9T tokens against the 8B's and 70B's logits (above). Lab page: Meta: Llama.

Nemotron Nano (NVIDIA) and OLMo 2 1B (Ai2)

The pruning-and-distillation lane and the fully open lane. Nemotron Nano 9B v2 was pruned from a 12B hybrid Mamba-Transformer trained on 20T tokens and healed with about 136B tokens of forward-KL distillation (Nemotron Nano 2 §4). OLMo publishes weights, data and code at every size (Ai2: OLMo).

MiniCPM5-2B (OpenBMB)

A dense on-device model under Apache 2.0 for local assistants and coding, averaging 53.9 across 34 benchmarks, the lab's own figure reported (model card). The card calls it 2.5B; Artificial Analysis counts 2.6B and measured 12.5 on its Intelligence Index v4.3, the third-highest measured score under 16B on 3 October 2026.

K2 Horizon 0.9B, 3.7B, 7B (IFM)

The small tier of a fully open fleet, with the lab claiming state of the art at all three sizes reported (Pretraining). Because the training data shipped with the weights, the claim can be audited for contamination rather than argued about. Independent check: Artificial Analysis measured 20.6 and 15.6 for the 7B and 3.7B, the two highest measured scores of any open model under 16B; the 0.9B scored 3.0 (Small models compared).

The recurring recipe: overtrain a small dense model on the best data available, distil from a frontier teacher (increasingly on-policy), then run the same post-training pipeline as the big models (SFT, preference optimisation, RL with verifiable rewards; see Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR).

Whose terms: the contractual layer

Distilling a proprietary model through its API is the same mechanism as sequence-level distillation: collect the teacher's answers, fine-tune on them. That is why frontier terms of service restrict it, and, the old page added, why labs watermark or perturb the log-probabilities they expose unconfirmed. The restriction is contractual rather than technical, and enforcement is now public.

  • The accusations. On 10 September 2026 Anthropic said it had identified and disrupted distillation attacks from seven labs based in China, through proxy services using false identities, fake or stolen cards and stolen API keys. It attributes over 151 million exchanges to Alibaba between May and July 2026, peaking at "nearly 3 million exchanges per day launched from more than 3,500 fraudulent accounts", in two pools of accounts, the first of "nearly 5,000"; over 23 million to Moonshot (Anthropic threat report, September 2026). The Batch adds that several labs routed their own customers' queries to Claude and presented the answers as their own (issue 371). These are accusations, unadjudicated.
  • The counterweight. The Batch's editorial note: "You can't distill your way to a frontier model", and the accused labs "have achieved significant technical breakthroughs, many of them published openly". That matches the technical point above: a student copies what its teacher was asked to do.
  • The other direction. Y Combinator's Garry Tan argued for "an American distillation regime": US open-weight labs should be free to distil US frontier models through the front door (TechCrunch, 11 September 2026).
Corrected from the old page
The old page said Alibaba ran 151 million exchanges "through about 5,000 fraudulent accounts between May and June 2026", and attributed the counterweight to Andrew Ng. Anthropic's report gives May to July, a peak from more than 3,500 accounts, and nearly 5,000 as the first of two account pools; the counterweight is The Batch's unsigned editorial note in issue 371, not Ng's letter (checked in the 21 September tech news issue).

Whose terms you build a distillation pipeline on is a design decision, not a formality. Open-weight teachers often allow it explicitly: DeepSeek-R1's licence section says the series supports "any modifications and derivative works, including, but not limited to, distillation for training other LLMs", and notes that its students also come under the terms of their own base models (Apache 2.0 for the Qwen2.5 ones, the Llama 3.1 and 3.3 licences for the Llama ones) (DeepSeek-R1 model card, §7).

Practical notes

A shared vocabulary, or no logits

Logit distillation compares two distributions over the same vocabulary, position by position. Across model families the vocabularies differ, and so do the positions: the same text splits into different tokens. Real tokenizations from this page's data script:

With different splits there is no position at which to compare, so across families you either distil at the sequence level (generated text works with any tokenizer) or use a cross-tokenizer loss such as ULD, which "uses optimal transport theory to enable distillation across different architectures and tokenizers" (Boizard et al.).

The capacity gap

  • A much stronger teacher is not always better. "The student network performance degrades when the gap between student and teacher is large"; an intermediate teacher assistant bridges it (Mirzadeh et al.). On-policy distillation is the other mitigation: the student is graded on what it can actually produce.
  • It depends on how long you train. Gemma 3 trained the same student with a small and a large teacher: "for short training horizons, the smaller teacher is better, but the trend is reversed for longer training", which they suspect explains the common finding, since most such studies are short (§5.4). Gemma 2's 7B teacher helped students from 200M to 1B alike (Table 7).
  • Correct the teacher first when you cannot use its original data: Minitron fine-tuned the teacher on about 100B tokens of the distillation set, which "yields over a 6% reduction in LM validation loss" (Minitron in practice).

Reasoning traces: fewer, better, verified

Quality and diversity of the teacher's traces matter more than their number, and filtering for verified-correct traces (rejection sampling) is standard: DeepSeek-R1 kept only correct samples from its RL checkpoint. s1 fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on 1,000 traces distilled from Gemini Thinking Experimental and chosen for quality, difficulty and diversity: 50.0 on AIME 2024, against 53.3 for all 59K candidates (a difference inside its 95% interval) and 36.7 for 1,000 chosen at random (s1, Table 2). The same lever at the smallest scale: a Qwen 3.8 4B query planner was taught with 400 GPT-6 Astra trajectories (440 generated, 40 kept for validation) through a LoRA (PEFT).

Evaluate beyond the benchmarks

Distilled models inherit their teacher's style and can overfit its quirks, including what it refuses and how it fails. Check robustness and refusal behaviour independently of the teacher's benchmark suite, and remember that a student distilled on benchmark-like prompts looks better on benchmarks than in use (Reward Hacking covers the general failure of optimising a proxy).

When distillation does not pay

  • T5Gemma 2 dropped the distillation its predecessor used: UL2 with and without it scored within 0.4 points, not worth the data-loading overhead (Encoder-Decoder Gemma and T5Gemma 2).
  • Ettin's small encoders, trained without distillation, beat distilled baselines: 87.2 GLUE for the 68M against DistilRoBERTa's 83.8 (Seq vs Seq (Ettin)).
  • At the frontier there is no stronger teacher: distillation copies, it does not discover (Distil or RL?).

What the toy shows, and what it cannot

  • It shows the mechanisms with exact numbers: soft targets against hard labels on the same text, temperature, mass-covering against mode-seeking (success against distinct routes), and what each method costs when the teacher must generate the text. Its results are summarised in the Reading tab (Reading, section "Whose text").
  • It does not reproduce any published figure, by design: no paper ran this task. Its effect sizes are the toy's, at one student size, one teacher, one learning rate, and 2,400 steps.
  • It cannot show the long-horizon exposure bias that on-policy distillation was invented for. Its answers are at most 9 tokens, so off-policy students rarely end up far from the teacher's states, and on-policy training matched off-policy on success while costing about half the compute. On reasoning traces thousands of tokens long, GKD, MiniLLM, Qwen3 and Thinking Machines report on-policy ahead on quality too.
  • Exact losses, not estimates. Every KL is summed over the whole 35-token vocabulary; Thinking Machines' recipe uses one sampled token's log-probability per position, a noisier estimate of the same reverse KL.

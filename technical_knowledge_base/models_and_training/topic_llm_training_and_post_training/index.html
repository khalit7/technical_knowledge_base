The lifecycle in one screen

A language model is built in stages. Pretraining teaches a randomly initialised network to predict the next token of trillions of tokens of text; mid-training and continued pretraining keep the same objective on a shifted mix (curated data, a new domain, longer documents); post-training turns the result into an assistant: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on demonstrations, preference optimisation on ranked answers, and reinforcement learning against a checker (RLVR). Three more stages make it cheaper: distillation into a smaller student, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) that trains a small add-on instead of the whole model, and quantization to fewer bits per weight.

Axis 1: what each stage changes

Every stage before compression updates the same weights by gradient descent. What differs is the signal: pretraining and mid-training reward predicting text; SFT rewards imitating a demonstration; preference optimisation rewards the answer a judge ranked higher; RLVR rewards whatever the model itself produced that a checker accepted. Each signal is narrower than the last and moves behaviour more per example. The compression stages change something else: distillation makes a new, smaller model; PEFT changes how many weights move (a low-rank add-on, often under 0.1% of the model); quantization changes how the weights are stored, with no learning at all unless it is quantization-aware training.

Try: the same prompt through five checkpoints of one imaginary model. Watch the base model continue the text instead of answering, SFT answer tersely, the preference-tuned model grow friendlier and longer, and the RL model think, check, and answer.

Prompt: A shop sells 3 pens for $2. How much do 12 pens cost?

Illustrative outputs, written to show each stage's typical behaviour, not sampled from a model; token counts are of these texts. The behaviours are documented: base models continue text (InstructGPT), reward models favour length (verbosity bias, Reward Hacking), and outcome-rewarded RL grows long self-checking chains (DeepSeek-R1).

What each signal buys, and what it cannot

  • Pretraining sets the ceiling. RL with verifiable rewards raises pass@1, but base models solve more problems when given many tries (Yue et al.): RL sharpens what pretraining put there.
  • SFT teaches the form more than the skill. In DeepSeek-R1, a cold-start SFT on thousands of clean reasoning traces scored 59.0% on AIME 2024, against 77.9% for pure RL from the same base; reasoning RL brought it back to 74.0% (Table 3, DeepSeek-R1).
  • A small preference stage moves a lot. Labelers preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT to the 175B GPT-3, 135 times its size (InstructGPT).
  • RL can now train the cost-quality trade itself. Cognition's SWE-2, RL post-trained from Moonshot's 2.8T-parameter Kimi K3, learned all its reasoning-effort levels in a single RL run, "with each penalty tuned to the local slope of the base model's Pareto frontier", rather than one checkpoint per tier; SWE-2 medium used 58% fewer turns and cost 81% less than SWE-1.7 on the same work. It matched Fable 5.1 on FrontierCode 1.1 Main (50.0% against 50.9%) at a claimed 64% lower cost reported, but scored 27.3% on Terminal-Bench 4 against 55.8% and 57.9% for Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra (Cognition).

Axis 2: what data each stage needs

Down the lifecycle, data gets smaller, more expensive per example, and made by someone other than a web crawler. Pretraining reads trillions of tokens nobody wrote for it; mid-training selects billions of better ones; SFT needs thousands of demonstrations; preference optimisation needs tens of thousands of ranked pairs; RLVR needs prompts or whole environments with a checker, and generates its own answers. The labeller changed too: people, then a model reading a written constitution, then a program that checks the answer, and now the model's own production traffic.

Where the data is coming from now

  • Data quality carries more of pretraining's progress than modelling. From 2019 to 2025, at a 1e19-FLOP budget, data improvements gave 12.0 times and model improvements 3.7 times more compute efficiency, so 12.0 / 3.7 = 3.24 times more from data derived, and the two effects are mostly independent (88% of variance additive) (Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han). Chinchilla's rule of about 20 tokens per parameter still says how to split a compute budget; it never said that one token is worth another. Curation is on Topic: data-curation-and-datasets. Magic says its pretraining recipe is ">10x more compute-efficient than that of leading open-weight base models", matching DeepSeek V4 Pro Base with about 50 times fewer FLOPs reported, not independently reproduced (Magic, 8 September 2026).
  • Mid-training is a selection problem. Thomson Reuters kept 200B tokens of a 19T-token pool, 1.05% derived, curated with DatologyAI, for a legal and news model on Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (The Batch; report).
  • Write the answer first, then the question. ToolGrad builds a verified chain of API calls and only then writes the user request that needs it: 99.8% of generated examples valid across 16,000 real APIs, and Gemma 3 12B trained on 500 of them scored 83.1 on BFCL against Gemini 2.5 Pro's 83.2 (Google Research, paper). It works wherever an answer is cheap to check and expensive to find, for evaluation sets as well as training sets.
  • Production traffic as the curriculum. NeoHorse-1 routes requests across a pool of models, turns served interactions into training examples (structural validation, a six-dimensional semantic evaluation, labels for sub-scenes within a conversation), orders them into a three-stage SFT curriculum and adds on-policy distillation. Over ten agent, tool, coding and instruction benchmarks, Qwen3.5-4B went from 58.94 to 64.87 and 9B from 65.60 to 69.04: the 4B closed 89% of the gap to the untrained 9B derived. No experiment switches the routing curriculum off, and the loop ran once (NeoHorse-1 paper page).
  • Operational records beat curated corpora in narrow domains. Periodic's Neon, mid-trained and RL-trained on real laboratory data from a Kimi K2.6 base, went from 2.7% to 55.3% on FrontierXRD (134 lab samples), ahead of GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1 at lower cost per analysis reported; the final run used 1,300 H200s (Periodic, 15 September 2026). The shared ingredient with the Postgres planner in Axis 3: a checker that already exists in the domain.

Axis 3: what each stage costs

Pretraining's cost is arithmetic: about 6 floating-point operations per parameter per token, so C ≈ 6ND, divided by what the cluster sustains. Post-training used to be a rounding error on top of it, and reasoning and agentic RL changed that, by moving the cost from the backward pass to generating rollouts and running the environments they act in:

  • 2022: InstructGPT's RLHF on the 175B model took 60 petaflop/s-days against 3,640 to pretrain GPT-3: 60 / 3,640 = 1.6% derived, and SFT 0.13% more (InstructGPT, §5.1).
  • 2025: DeepSeek-R1's RL and SFT data took 147K H800 hours ($294K at an assumed $2 an hour), on top of 2,788K for DeepSeek-V3's official run: 147 / 2,788 = 5.3% derived (DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V3).
  • 2026: the final RL stage of Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Pro (1.02T total, 42B active) ran live for under six days across 7,000+ task environments for $2.62M; Flash's cost about $850,000 (model card, alphaXiv). A stage short enough to rerun weekly is limited by how many environments and evaluations a lab can supply, not by the run.

Those are the ratios; every disclosed run with its dollar figure and what that figure includes (one stage, one run, or a whole programme) is on the Price list, and FLOPs and GPU-days for any model and token count, Chinchilla-optimal or over-trained, are in the Scaling calculator.

Same batch, three algorithms

The memory side of post-training is set by how many models must sit on the GPUs at once. PPO, the algorithm of InstructGPT-style RLHF, holds four: the policy being trained, a frozen reference copy for the KL penalty, a reward model, and a critic that estimates how good each partial answer is. GRPO (from DeepSeekMath) drops the critic: it samples a group of answers per prompt and scores each against the group's mean. DPO drops sampling altogether and trains on fixed pairs.

Try: step one batch of four prompts through each. PPO's memory bar is twice GRPO's at the same model size; GRPO pays instead in rollouts, 8 per prompt here where PPO samples one.

Rollouts

Memory derived: a trained model holds 16 bytes per parameter (bf16 weights and gradients, fp32 master weights and two Adam moments, ZeRO §3.1), a frozen one 2; activations and the rollout engine's cache are left out. PPO 36 bytes per parameter, 252 GB at 7B; GRPO with a verifier and DPO 18, 126 GB: "roughly half the memory", as the RL page puts it. Group size 8 and the right or wrong answers are illustrative; DeepSeekMath used 64 per prompt and R1-Zero 16. DPO's reference log-probabilities can be computed once and cached, which removes the reference from memory during training (4 forward passes per pair, 2 cacheable: DPO).

The trade is visible in what each wins. DPO is cheap and stable and was adopted everywhere; on harder tasks, later controlled studies found well-tuned on-policy RL ahead, which is why reasoning models use GRPO and its successors. GRPO's memory saving was argued, not measured, in the paper that introduced it (DeepSeekMath); its standing rests on cost and on R1.

The environment is now the expensive part

  • Sandboxes at rollout rate. Each agentic rollout needs its own isolated machine. DeepSeek's DSec offers four isolation levels behind one SDK (function call, container, microVM, full VM), so a rollout pays only for the isolation it needs, and keeps stateful rollouts alive when the GPU trainer is preempted. One unit of about 160 nodes runs about 3 million sandboxes a day and creates over 5,000 a second, peaking near 380,000 at once self-reported: about 31 creations per node per second derived (DSec, arXiv). A lab without such a layer cannot run the same experiments, whatever recipe it copies.
  • The harness decides as much as the algorithm. Mercor and SkyRL's account of RL on Qwen3.5-397B-A17B over 1,928 expert-made tasks is mostly token accounting, asynchronous RL, environment robustness and harness design; APEX-Agents Pass@1 rose from 16.11% to 27.29%, +69.4% relative (Mercor). Terminal-Universe industrialises the environment supply.
  • The cheap end: one task, one checker. A Qwen 3.8 4B model learned to write Postgres query plans from 400 GPT-6 Astra trajectories (SFT through a 21.2M-parameter LoRA) and then anchored-GRPO RL on 13,646 queries: 1.81 times the built-in planner's speed (geometric mean, 113 held-out queries) for $1,200 in total (write-up). With a cheap verifier, specialisation beats scale.
  • Fewer GPU hours on the wrong experiment. Meta FAIR's AI Research Preference Models have frozen language models rank 15 candidate experiments in a pairwise knockout before running any: average normalised score 0.684 for random choice, 0.711 and 0.729 for the two variants, reaching the baseline's 24-hour result about 1.6 times sooner (arXiv); the margins are narrow and pairwise judges carry the biases of Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges. Dream-RSI re-scores new search policies against a finished search tree instead of paying for new evaluations (a Lasso-solver search fell from 550 to 317 agent calls), on single runs whose kernel speedups shrink at matched performance (Dream-RSI).

Axis 4: what goes wrong

Each stage has a characteristic failure, and the dangerous ones look like success on the stage's own metric: the loss keeps falling, the reward keeps rising.

Reward hacking, and the leash against it

A learned reward model is a proxy for what people want. Optimised hard enough, the policy finds what the proxy over-rewards: the proxy score keeps climbing while the true ("gold") score peaks and falls. Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured the shape: as a function of how far the policy has moved from where it started, d = √KL, the gold reward under RL follows d(α − β ln d), rising and then falling, and the gap grows smoothly with optimisation and shrinks with reward-model size (Gao et al.). The KL penalty is the leash: it charges the policy for distance, so it stops near where the proxy still tracks the gold.

Try: run the same training twice. Without the leash the proxy rises all the way while the gold reward turns over around step 35 and ends far below where it started; with it, the policy settles where both are still high.

Gao et al.'s functional form with illustrative coefficients (gold α = 1, β = 0.6; proxy α = 1, β = 0.08; penalty 0.22 × KL), simulated as gradient ascent on proxy minus penalty; recomputed in src/read/recompute.py. The shape is the published one; the numbers are not any real run's.

RLVR replaces the learned proxy with a checker, which removes this curve but not the problem: the checker is the new proxy, narrower and still gameable. Two 2026 responses move the defence out of the reward. DSec treats misbehaviour, reward hacking included, as a property of the sandbox: an environment that cannot be escaped removes a class of shortcut before the reward has to resist it (DSec). And reward hacking learned in training can generalise into broad misalignment, which telling the model during training that the hack is acceptable ("inoculation prompting") largely prevents (Anthropic; detail on Reward Hacking).

Loops that train on their own history narrow. Dream-RSI's replay is valid only inside the region its history covers, so it sharpens a mapped search rather than extending it (Dream-RSI). NVIDIA's Agora had 13 agents publish 1,703 contributions over 11 days and 19 hours as Git commits, taking a language-model evaluator from 3.39 to 1.899 bits per byte with 165 independent reproductions and no reported failure, but effort converged on one lineage ("shared visibility supported reuse, but did not by itself sustain broad exploration"), and the authors stepped in several times during the run; with one task, one run and no comparison, their changes cannot be separated from the effect of the system's diversity views (Agora).

The machinery under every stage

Every stage runs on the same machinery: a memory budget per parameter, a way of splitting the model across GPUs, a number format, and a cluster that fails. The stages differ mainly in how much of it they need.

Bytes per parameter

Training with Adam in mixed precision holds about 16 bytes per parameter before activations: 2 for bf16 weights, 2 for gradients, 12 for fp32 master weights and two moments (ZeRO). Freezing the base (PEFT) drops that to the 2 bytes of the frozen weights plus a small adapter; storing the frozen base in 4 bits (QLoRA) to about half a byte; serving needs only the weights, in whatever format they ship.

Try: 65B is selected, the QLoRA paper's case: full fine-tuning needs over a terabyte, QLoRA's frozen base 33.5 GB, under one 48 GB card. Pick 405B for Llama 3's size.

Precision, kernels, clusters

  • Precision: bf16 is the training default; DeepSeek-V3 showed fine-grained FP8 training at 671B, under 0.25% relative loss gap against BF16 in its ablations (DeepSeek-V3), and Blackwell's NVFP4 and MXFP4 are now arriving for training, not just storage (NVFP4 pretraining).
  • Kernels: FlashAttention computes exact attention in tiles that stay in on-chip memory, moving 9 times fewer bytes through GPU memory on GPT-2 medium and making attention memory linear in sequence length (FlashAttention).
  • Clusters: Llama 3 trained at 38 to 43% model FLOPs utilisation on 8,192 to 16,384 H100s (Llama 3); the schedulers, frameworks (Megatron-Core, torchtitan, NeMo) and checkpoint cadence are on Training Infrastructure. The Hugging Face Ultra-Scale Playbook distils 4,000+ benchmarked runs into how to combine the splits above.
  • Components fixed before training: the tokenizer (vocabularies grew from 32K to 128K to 256K entries: fewer tokens per text, larger embedding matrices; Tokenizers) and the positional encoding (rotary embeddings, RoPE, are the default, rotating queries and keys so attention depends only on relative distance, RoFormer; extending context means rescaling them during a long-document CPT phase, Positional Encodings).
  • The inference end: how a trained model is sampled changes what it appears to have learned. Temperature, then truncation (top-p, or min-p, which keeps tokens above a fraction of the top token's probability), then sampling; greedy decoding and beam search repeat themselves on open-ended text; constrained decoding forces valid JSON or a grammar. Sampling also feeds training: RL and best-of-n draw from the same stack (Sampling and Decoding).

How we got here

The lifecycle was assembled in about seven years, mostly by papers that changed one stage each. The recipes that put them together in the open, stage by stage, are compared in Open recipes compared.

2019

Pretraining becomes an experiment you can control: T5 fixes one pipeline and moves one choice at a time; denoising beats plain language modelling, cleaned web text (C4, 745 GB) beats eight times more unfiltered text, and multi-task pretraining then fine-tuning only matches plain pretraining (T5). ZeRO and Megatron-LM make models bigger than one GPU trainable.

2020

Scaling laws: loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute; the paper's advice to spend compute mostly on parameters was later overturned (Kaplan et al.).

2021

LoRA makes fine-tuning a 35 MB adapter instead of a 350 GB copy (LoRA); RoPE (RoFormer) and top-1 MoE routing (Switch Transformers) arrive.

2022

Chinchilla: grow parameters and tokens together, about 20 tokens per parameter; a 70B model on 1.4T tokens beat the 280B Gopher at the same compute, 67.6% against 60.0% on MMLU (Chinchilla). InstructGPT fixes the three-step recipe (SFT, reward model, PPO with a KL penalty) (InstructGPT); Constitutional AI replaces human harmlessness labels with a model reading 16 written principles (Constitutional AI). FlashAttention (paper).

2023

DPO removes the reward model and the RL loop (DPO); QLoRA fine-tunes 65B on one GPU (QLoRA); LIMA argues 1,000 curated examples suffice for SFT (LIMA).

2024

GRPO drops PPO's critic (DeepSeekMath); Llama 3 publishes the most detailed frontier-scale run, with six rounds of reward model, rejection sampling, SFT and DPO (Llama 3); DeepSeek-V3 trains in FP8; OLMo 2 releases everything, logs included (OLMo 2).

2025

DeepSeek-R1: outcome-only RL on a base model produces long self-checking reasoning, and SFT on 800K of its samples beats RL for smaller models (DeepSeek-R1). Open replications follow; DAPO releases algorithm, data and code, reaching 50 points on AIME 2024 from the Qwen2.5-32B base (GitHub, paper), the runnable baseline for RLVR.

2026

RL moves into environments (MiMo-V2.6, SWE-2, Mercor's 397B run) and its infrastructure gets published (DSec); training data comes from production traffic (NeoHorse-1); and models do part of the research. Anthropic's internal measurements: over 80% of code merged into its production codebase written by Claude by May 2026; the model's research suggestions beating the human choice 64% of the time in April 2026, against 51% in November 2025; Claude leading 26% of its AI research work, with over 30,000 agents active on its most-used internal platform; continuous-integration load up 25 times in six months self-reported (When AI builds itself, measuring the pace). Z.ai reports GLM-5.3 building the inference stack that serves it in 13 days, while saying it has not reached recursive self-improvement because humans kept the objectives (Z.ai). Both labs draw the line in the same place: the loop closes on execution, not on choosing goals. The full series with every check is in the 21 September tech news issue.

Common mistakes

Treating "20 tokens per parameter" as how long to train
Chinchilla's ratio minimises training compute for a given loss. A model that will serve many tokens is trained far longer, because a smaller model is cheaper to run: Llama 3 405B saw 15.6T tokens, 38.5 per parameter derived, and its small models many times more. Compare both regimes in the Scaling calculator.
Reading a published training cost as the cost of the model
DeepSeek-V3's $5.576M is 2.788M H800 hours at an assumed $2 for the final official run only, with research and ablations excluded; R1's $294K sits on top of it. Each figure on the Price list says what it includes.
"DPO is RLHF without the downsides"
It removes the reward model and the sampling loop, and with them on-policy learning: DPO never sees its own new answers. Its wins over PPO in the original paper are single runs about one standard error apart (DPO); reasoning models use online RL.
"SFT taught it to reason" or "RL gave it new abilities"
SFT teaches the format of reasoning and RL sharpens what the base model already reaches with enough samples (see Axis 1). A capability absent from pretraining rarely appears later.
Reading a rising reward as a better model
Against a learned reward, the proxy keeps rising after the true quality has peaked (Axis 4). Track a held-out gold measure and the KL from the reference.
"LoRA always matches full fine-tuning"
It does for typical post-training datasets when applied to every weight matrix (Thinking Machines); it does not absorb billions of tokens of new knowledge, and LoRA on attention only underperforms. The LoRA paper's own margins over full fine-tuning sit inside the noise (LoRA).
"Quantization is free"
At 8 bits it nearly is. At about 4.9 bits (llama.cpp's Q4_K_M) perplexity rose by 0.05 on LLaMA-7B, but newer, smaller, more heavily trained models lose more, and dense models fall off a cliff below about 3 bits (Quantization and Precision).
Reading a self-improvement paper as measured self-improvement
NeoHorse-1's loop ran once; Agora and Dream-RSI are single runs without a comparison; Anthropic's figures are internal measurements on internal tasks. The mechanisms are real; the compounding is not yet measured.
Comparing token counts across models
Token counts depend on the tokenizer: the same text costs several times more tokens in a low-resource language, and a 256K vocabulary packs more text per token than a 32K one (Tokenizers).

    The calculator

    Training a dense Transformer costs about six floating-point operations per parameter per token: two for the forward pass (one multiply, one add), four for the backward pass. That gives the rule every lab uses, C ≈ 6ND. The rest of this tab hangs off that one number: how long it takes on real hardware, what the Chinchilla fit says the compute should have bought, and what serving the model afterwards adds.

    Two answers for "Chinchilla-optimal"

    "Twenty tokens per parameter" and "the Chinchilla fit" are not the same rule. Hoffmann et al. estimated the optimal split three ways. Approaches 1 and 2 (training-curve envelopes and IsoFLOP valleys) found the optimal size growing as C0.50, so tokens per parameter stays roughly constant; Chinchilla itself, 70B on 1.4T tokens, is exactly 20, and the paper's Table 3 puts 67B on 1.5T (22) at the same budget. Approach 3 fits the whole loss surface, L(N, D) = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ, and its exponent a = β/(α + β) = 0.457 is below one half, so its tokens per parameter rise with compute: 59 at Gopher's budget, where it gives the paper's 40B (unrounded constants; the rounded ones printed in Eq. 10 give 32B and 93). Besiroglu et al. (2024) refitted Approach 3 on points read from the paper's Figure 4, found the original fit had stopped early, and got a = 0.513, which brings it back to about 20 and agrees with Approaches 1 and 2.

    Lines: tokens per parameter on each fit's compute-optimal frontier, derived from Eq. 4 of Hoffmann et al. Dots: the presets, at their 6ND compute; MoE models at their active parameters. The ringed dot is the calculator's current model.

    What it shows. Most models trained since 2023 sit far above every line: Llama 3.1 8B at about 1,900 tokens per parameter, SmolLM3 at 3,600, Qwen3's MoE at 1,600 per active parameter. The exception is the largest dense model, Llama 3.1 405B at 38, which Meta sized with its own IsoFLOP fit (402B on 16.55T tokens at 3.8 × 10²⁵). The others were not mistakes. The Chinchilla frontier minimises training compute for a given loss, and these labs were minimising something else: the cost of serving the model afterwards (below).

    One budget, three allocations

    The same compute spent three ways, drawn on one scale: Kaplan et al.'s 2020 rule (N ∝ C0.73, most of the budget into parameters), Chinchilla's frontier, and Llama 3.1 8B's ratio of 1,868 tokens per parameter applied to the same budget. Loss comes from the scaling-law fit chosen above.

    Derived throughout: sizes from each rule's formula at the chosen budget, loss from the chosen fit (an extrapolation beyond the largest runs it was fitted on, about 16B parameters), serving cost as 2N FLOPs per generated token. Kaplan's rule is used as Chinchilla's Appendix D.4 reads it (Kaplan Table 6 with Cmin = C/2), which reproduces their 4.68B at 10²¹ FLOPs. The animation stops when it is off screen, and starts paused, one step at a time, if your system asks for reduced motion.

    What it shows. At a fixed budget the Chinchilla split has the lowest loss by construction; Kaplan's rule buys a model several times larger that is both worse and costlier to serve, so it loses on every count. The over-trained model loses a little loss for a far smaller model: every token it later generates costs a fraction of the Chinchilla model's. The last step says after how many generated tokens that pays back.

    Why everyone now trains past Chinchilla-optimal

    Serving a model costs about 2N FLOPs per token, input or output. Sardana et al. (2024, "Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal") asked the question the labs actually face: for a target quality and an expected lifetime demand of Dinf tokens, which N and D minimise training plus inference, 6ND + 2N·Dinf? The answer moves to smaller models trained on more data as demand grows. This section takes the calculator's model, keeps its predicted loss fixed, and slides along the curve of every (N, D) pair with that loss.

    Every point on a curve has the calculator model's predicted loss under the chosen fit. Training counted as 6ND, attention left out, as Sardana et al. do. Inference demand is the reader's assumption; labs do not publish it.

    Mistakes to avoid

    Using an MoE's total parameters in 6ND
    Each token only passes through the active experts, so compute follows active parameters: DeepSeek-V3 is 6 × 37B × 14.8T = 3.3 × 10²⁴, not 6 × 671B × 14.8T = 6.0 × 10²⁵. Total parameters set memory and the number of GPUs needed to hold the weights, not FLOPs.
    Reading a model card's GPU-hours as the pretraining run
    Cards count whatever the lab chose to count. Llama 3.1's are "total GPU time required for training each model", which is why the 8B implies 14% MFU; DeepSeek-V3 separates pretraining (2.664M) from context extension and post-training (124K more). None include ablations, failed runs or the research that preceded the final run.
    Quoting MFU against the wrong peak
    Datasheets print sparse figures, twice the dense peak no training run uses; this calculator uses dense throughout. An FP8 run measured against the BF16 peak looks twice as efficient as against the FP8 peak: DeepSeek-V3's same GPU-hours are 35% or 17%. Compare MFU only at matched precision. MFU also differs from hardware FLOPs utilisation (HFU), which counts recomputed activations.
    Treating "Chinchilla-optimal" as "best"
    It is the split that minimises training compute for a given loss, nothing more. It ignores serving cost, data limits (repeating data loses value after a few epochs) and the fact that a smaller model is easier to fine-tune, quantise and run on a laptop.
    Comparing losses across fits or datasets
    Chinchilla's fit predicts loss on MassiveText with its tokenizer; Kaplan's on WebText2 with GPT-2's, counting non-embedding parameters only. The absolute numbers are not comparable with each other or with any other model's loss; only the changes within one fit mean something.
    Reading the dollar figure as what the model cost
    GPU-hours times a rental rate prices the final run only. DeepSeek's own "$5.576M" is exactly that (2.788M H800 hours at an assumed $2), and the report says it excludes prior research and ablations. The disclosed costs of real runs, with what each includes, are on the Price list tab.

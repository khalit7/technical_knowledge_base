The algorithms

All three learn a vocabulary from a training corpus that has first been pre-tokenized: split into words by a fixed rule (spaces, punctuation, digits), so that no token can cross a word boundary. Counting happens over distinct words with their frequencies, not over the raw text.

BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding)

Start from characters (or bytes) and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token until the target vocabulary size. It guarantees no OOV, since the worst case falls back to characters or bytes; the cost is that unfamiliar words shatter into many small fragments. Encoding applies the learned merges in order. Originally a compression algorithm, brought to translation by {{Sennrich, Haddow and Birch|https://arxiv.org/abs/1508.07909}} to make the model "capable of open-vocabulary translation by encoding rare and unknown words as sequences of subword units".

Byte-level BPE

BPE over the 256 raw byte values (GPT-2 onward, Llama 3, every tiktoken vocabulary). Any Unicode input is representable with zero OOV and no normalisation headaches. GPT-2's implementation maps each byte to a printable character, which is why a space shows up as Ġ in its vocabulary files and why a Hindi character shows up as two or three odd Latin symbols.

WordPiece

BERT's method. Same skeleton as BPE, different merge criterion: merge the pair maximising freq(ab) / (freq(a) × freq(b)), a likelihood ratio (pointwise mutual information up to a constant) rather than raw frequency. A pair of common parts scores low even if it is frequent. Encoding is greedy longest-match-first with ## continuation prefixes. The original criterion was the language-model likelihood gain of each candidate unit ({{Schuster and Nakajima 2012|https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//pubs/archive/37842.pdf}}); the score above is the Hugging Face course's reconstruction, because "Google never open-sourced its implementation of the training algorithm" ({{course|https://huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/chapter6/6}}).

Unigram LM

Kudo's method runs the other way: start with a large candidate vocabulary, fit a unigram language model (each token has a probability, a word's probability is the product of its tokens'), and repeatedly prune the tokens whose removal least hurts corpus likelihood, 10% or 20% at a time, never single characters, until the target size. A string is tokenized by its highest-probability segmentation (Viterbi). Because it is a probabilistic model it can also sample other segmentations, which Kudo used as training-time augmentation ("subword regularization", {{Kudo 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.10959}}).

SentencePiece

A toolkit, not an algorithm. Language-agnostic: it treats raw text as a stream of characters including whitespace, which it writes as the meta symbol ▁ (U+2581), so it needs no pre-tokenization and round-trips losslessly: "Hello▁world." becomes [Hello] [▁wor] [ld] [.] and decodes by replacing ▁ with a space ({{SentencePiece §3.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.06226}}). It trains either BPE or Unigram underneath. Used by T5, LLaMA 1 and 2, Gemma and many multilingual models.

The course's printed BPE list differs from the library's at ties, as the course itself notes: when two pairs are equally frequent the course takes the first one it meets, the library the one whose token ids are smallest. The library has no WordPiece trainer of its own (its WordPieceTrainer runs BPE with ## prefixes), and its Unigram trainer is SentencePiece's EM algorithm, so for those two the course's code is the reference; the page's Unigram is the course's simplified version (probabilities from substring counts, no EM re-estimation, exact removal losses).

Encoding is a different algorithm

Training builds the vocabulary once; encoding runs on every request, and each method encodes differently. BPE replays its merges in the order learned (the library does it as "apply the lowest-ranked merge present, repeat", which gives the same result). WordPiece stores no merges, only the vocabulary, and takes the longest vocabulary entry that matches from the left; if any part of a word cannot be matched, the whole word becomes [UNK]. Unigram finds the segmentation with the highest product of token probabilities by dynamic programming (Viterbi). So the same vocabulary entry can be reached differently: the course's example vocabulary tokenizes "hugs" as hug ##s by longest match, while BPE replaying the same merges gives hu ##gs ({{course|https://huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/chapter6/6}}).

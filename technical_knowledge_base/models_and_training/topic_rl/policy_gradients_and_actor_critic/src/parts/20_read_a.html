Policy gradients and actor-critic in one screen

Value-based methods learn how good each action is and act greedily on that. Policy-gradient methods parameterise the policy itself, πθ(a | s), and climb the gradient of its expected return. Every method on this page uses the same gradient, and differs in only two places: what number weights each action's log-probability (the return, the return minus a baseline, a critic's estimate), and how far one batch of data is allowed to move the policy.

1Why optimise the policy directly

A value-based agent acts by arg maxa Q(s, a). That needs a maximisation over actions at every step, which is awkward when the action is a vector of joint torques and expensive when it is one of 100,000 tokens, and it only ever yields a deterministic policy (plus whatever exploration noise is bolted on). Parameterising the policy instead has four advantages ({{Sutton and Barto §13.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=343}}):

  • Continuous and huge action spaces are natural: output the mean and spread of a Gaussian over torques, or a softmax over tokens.
  • The optimal policy may be stochastic. In card games with hidden information, or when the agent cannot tell states apart, the best policy randomises with specific probabilities. An ε-greedy action-value method cannot represent that; a softmax policy can.
  • Smooth change. The action probabilities move continuously with θ, whereas in ε-greedy selection an arbitrarily small change in the estimated values can flip which action is greedy. "Largely because of this, stronger convergence guarantees are available for policy-gradient methods than for action-value methods" ({{§13.2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=346}}).
  • Prior knowledge goes into the form of the policy, which the book calls "often the most important reason for using a policy-based learning method".

A worked case: the short corridor ({{Example 13.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=345}}). Three states, reward −1 per step, actions right and left, except that in the second state they are switched; all states share the same features, so a policy is one number, p = probability of right. Solving the three Bellman equations gives the value of the start state J(p) = 2(2 − p) / (p(p − 1)), maximised at p* = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.586 with J* = −(6 + 4√2) ≈ −11.66 derived. The two policies an ε-greedy method can reach (ε = 0.1) choose right with probability 0.95 or 0.05 and score −44.2 and −82.1. The book: "The best probability is about 0.59, which achieves a value of about −11.6." The live tab draws the curve and lets three learners find the optimum.

Two policy parameterisations and their score functions

Everything below needs only ∇θ log π(a | s, θ), the score function (Sutton and Barto call it the eligibility vector). The two parameterisations in nearly every implementation:

Softmax over action preferences: π(a | s) = exp(θ⊤x(s, a)) / Σb exp(θ⊤x(s, b)),   ∇ log π(a | s) = x(s, a) − Σb π(b | s) x(s, b)
Gaussian: a ~ 𝒩(μθ(s), σθ(s)2),   ∂ log π / ∂μ = (a − μ) / σ2,   ∂ log π / ∂ log σ = (a − μ)2 / σ2 − 1
x(s, a)
a feature vector of the state-action pair; with a network, the logits play the role of θ⊤x and the score with respect to the logits is the one-hot vector of a minus the probabilities
μ, σ
the mean and standard deviation the network outputs ({{§13.7|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=357}}); a sample above the mean pushes μ up if it turned out well, a sample far from the mean pushes σ up

2The policy gradient theorem, derived

The objective is the expected return of trajectories τ = (s0, a0, r1, s1, ...) sampled by running the policy, J(θ) = 𝔼τ~πθ[G(τ)]. The difficulty: θ changes which trajectories happen, through a state distribution that depends on unknown dynamics. Four steps remove it.

  1. The log-derivative trick. For any distribution, ∇θ 𝔼x~pθ[f(x)] = Σx f(x) ∇pθ(x) = Σx f(x) pθ(x) ∇ log pθ(x) = 𝔼x~pθ[f(x) ∇ log pθ(x)], because ∇p = p ∇ log p. A gradient of an expectation becomes an expectation of something we can sample.
  2. The dynamics drop out. log pθ(τ) = log p(s0) + Σt [log πθ(at | st) + log p(st+1, rt+1 | st, at)]. Only the policy terms depend on θ, so ∇ log pθ(τ) = Σt ∇ log πθ(at | st): no model of the environment is ever needed, only the ability to sample it and differentiate the policy.
  3. Causality. That gives ∇J = 𝔼[G(τ) Σt ∇ log π(at | st)]. A reward received before time t is independent of at given the past, and 𝔼a~π[∇ log π(a | s)] = ∇ Σa π(a | s) = ∇1 = 0, so those rewards contribute nothing in expectation and can be dropped: each score is weighted only by the reward-to-go Gt = Σk≥0 γk rt+k+1. Same expectation, less variance.
  4. From returns to action values. 𝔼[Gt | st, at] = Qπ(st, at), which gives the theorem.
∇θ J(θ) = 𝔼πθ [ Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · Qπ(st, at) ]   ∝   Σs μ(s) Σa qπ(s, a) ∇θ π(a | s, θ)
∇ log π(at | st)
the direction in parameter space that makes the action actually taken more likely in that state
Qπ(st, at)
how good that action turned out to be, in expectation, if the policy is followed afterwards
μ(s)
the on-policy state distribution; in the episodic case the constant of proportionality is the average episode length, in the continuing case it is 1 ({{Sutton and Barto, Eq. 13.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=348}})

In words: push up the log-probability of each action in proportion to how good it was. The second form is the statement in {{Sutton et al. (1999)|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/1999/file/464d828b85b0bed98e80ade0a5c43b0f-Paper.pdf}} and in the book, which proves it from first principles by unrolling ∇vπ(s) = Σa [∇π(a | s) qπ(s, a) + π(a | s) ∇qπ(s, a)] repeatedly through the Bellman equation ({{the box on p. 325|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=347}}). Its point is that the gradient does not involve the derivative of the state distribution, which depends on the unknown environment.

Compatible function approximation. The same paper's Theorem 2 says when a learned fw(s, a) can replace Qπ without biasing the gradient: if fw is a local optimum of the squared error to Qπ under π, and its gradient in w equals the policy's score, ∂fw/∂w = ∇θ π(s, a) / π(s, a). Its Theorem 3 then shows that "a form of policy iteration with function approximation is convergent to a locally optimal policy" under standard step-size conditions. Practical critics are not compatible in this sense; the theorem is why "a critic that is exactly right gives the exact gradient" holds and an arbitrary one does not.

3REINFORCE: the Monte Carlo policy gradient

REINFORCE ({{Williams 1992|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00992696}}) samples the theorem directly: run an episode, then use each step's return as the estimate of Qπ. The name is an acronym, "REward Increment = Nonnegative Factor times Offset Reinforcement times Characteristic Eligibility", which describes the update's form, Δw = α(r − b)e: a step size, a reward minus a baseline, and the score.

REINFORCE (Sutton and Barto's boxed algorithm, {{p. 328|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=350}}): for each episode S0, A0, R1, ..., ST−1, AT−1, RT generated by πθ, for t = 0, ..., T − 1:   G ← Σk=t+1T γk−t−1 Rk;   θ ← θ + α γt G ∇ log π(At | St, θ)
γt
the factor the discounted objective (the value of the start state) requires; most deep-RL code drops it and weights every step equally, which optimises a slightly different objective
∇ log π = ∇π / π
the update is proportional to the return and inversely proportional to the action's probability, "otherwise actions that are selected frequently are at an advantage ... and might win out even if they do not yield the highest return" (p. 327)

Its properties follow from the derivation. It is unbiased: by construction the expected update is in the direction of the gradient, which assures improvement for a small enough α and convergence to a local optimum with decreasing step sizes ({{§13.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=351}}). It has high variance: one episode's return carries the randomness of every later action and transition. It needs episodes that end, and it updates only afterwards. And when every reward is positive, every sampled action is pushed up, the good ones merely more than the bad; when every reward is negative (the short corridor), every sampled action is pushed down. The policy still improves on average, slowly.

On the short corridor, with θ started so that p(right) = 0.05 and 100 runs averaged, REINFORCE with α = 2−13 climbs from −82 to within about one unit of J* over 1,000 episodes, and α = 2−14 more slowly ({{Figure 13.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=350}}, rerun in {{Short corridor, live|#t-cor}} with the exact numbers per episode range).

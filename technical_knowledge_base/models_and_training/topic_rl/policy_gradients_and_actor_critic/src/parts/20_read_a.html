The idea in one screen

Push up the log-probability of each action in proportion to how good it turned out. Every method on this page uses that one gradient, ∇θ log πθ(a | s) × weight, and differs in only two places: what number the weight is (the return, the return minus a baseline, a critic's estimate) and how far one batch of data may move the policy (a step size, a KL limit, a clip, an entropy bonus).

Two dials define every method: what weights the score, and how far one batch may move the policy

First dial: from the first box to the last, the weight relies less on sampled rewards and more on a learned critic, so variance falls and the critic's bias enters. Second dial: on-policy methods limit the step on fresh data (step size, KL, clip); off-policy ones reuse a replay buffer and need target networks, and SAC adds entropy to the objective. Tap a box to jump to its section.

You need: TD errors, TD(λ) and importance sampling from {{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}} (the critics here learn those targets); replay buffers and target networks from {{Value-based deep RL|n:3ee5c17b0d0d818689b2c0dbce434ece}} (DDPG, TD3 and SAC borrow them). The one-state version of the policy gradient is the gradient bandit on {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}.

1Why optimise the policy directly

A value-based agent acts by arg maxa Q(s, a): a maximisation over actions at every step, awkward when the action is a vector of joint torques and expensive when it is one of 100,000 tokens, and it only yields a deterministic policy plus bolted-on exploration noise. A parameterised policy has four advantages ({{Sutton and Barto §13.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=343}}): continuous and huge action spaces are natural (a Gaussian over torques, a softmax over tokens); the optimal policy may be stochastic (card games with hidden information, or states the agent cannot tell apart: an ε-greedy action-value method cannot represent specific probabilities; a softmax policy can); smooth change, since probabilities move continuously with θ while an arbitrarily small change in estimated values can flip an ε-greedy choice ("Largely because of this, stronger convergence guarantees are available for policy-gradient methods than for action-value methods", {{§13.2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=346}}); and prior knowledge goes into the form of the policy, "often the most important reason for using a policy-based learning method".

The short corridor ({{Example 13.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=345}}) is the smallest problem on which a policy-gradient method beats every action-value method that uses the same features, and it runs through sections 3 to 5. Three states, reward −1 per step, actions right and left, switched in the second state; every state has the same features, so a policy is one number, p = probability of right.

No deterministic policy can do well here; the best one flips a biased coin

The value of the start state is J(p) = 2(2 − p) / (p(p − 1)), maximised at p* = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.586 with J* = −(6 + 4√2) ≈ −11.66 derived. The two policies an ε-greedy method can reach (ε = 0.1) choose right with probability 0.95 or 0.05 and score −44.2 and −82.1. The book: "The best probability is about 0.59, which achieves a value of about −11.6."

Where J(p) and the optimum come from (Exercise 13.1) 1 min

In the first and third states right moves right and left moves left (left in the first state bumps the wall). Write v1, v2, v3 for the three states under "right with probability p". The Bellman equations are v1 = −1 + p v2 + (1 − p) v1; v2 = −1 + p v1 + (1 − p) v3 (switched); v3 = −1 + (1 − p) v2. Solving gives J(p) = v1 = 2(2 − p) / (p(p − 1)). Setting dJ/dp = 0 gives p* = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.5858 and J* ≈ −11.657 derived. The ε-greedy policies with ε = 0.1 choose their greedy action with probability 1 − ε/2 = 0.95: J(0.95) = −44.21 and J(0.05) = −82.11, the book's "less than −44 and −82" derived.

The two policy parameterisations and their score functions

Everything below needs only the score ∇θ log π(a | s, θ). Nearly every implementation uses one of two:

Softmax: π(a | s) = exp(θ⊤x(s, a)) / Σb exp(θ⊤x(s, b)),   ∇ log π(a | s) = x(s, a) − Σb π(b | s) x(s, b)
Gaussian: a ~ 𝒩(μθ(s), σθ(s)2),   ∂ log π / ∂μ = (a − μ) / σ2,   ∂ log π / ∂ log σ = (a − μ)2 / σ2 − 1
x(s, a)
a feature vector of the pair; with a network the logits play the role of θ⊤x, and the score with respect to the logits is the one-hot vector of a minus the probabilities
μ, σ
the mean and standard deviation the network outputs ({{§13.7|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=357}}); a sample above the mean pushes μ up if it turned out well, a sample far from the mean pushes σ up

2The policy gradient theorem, derived

The objective is the expected return of trajectories τ = (s0, a0, r1, s1, ...) sampled by running the policy, J(θ) = 𝔼τ~πθ[G(τ)]. The difficulty: θ changes which trajectories happen, through a state distribution that depends on unknown dynamics. Four steps remove it.

  1. The log-derivative trick. ∇θ 𝔼x~pθ[f(x)] = Σx f(x) ∇pθ(x) = 𝔼x~pθ[f(x) ∇ log pθ(x)], because ∇p = p ∇ log p. A gradient of an expectation becomes an expectation of something we can sample.
  2. The dynamics drop out. log pθ(τ) = log p(s0) + Σt [log πθ(at | st) + log p(st+1, rt+1 | st, at)]. Only the policy terms depend on θ, so ∇ log pθ(τ) = Σt ∇ log πθ(at | st): no model of the environment is ever needed, only samples and a differentiable policy.
  3. Causality. That gives ∇J = 𝔼[G(τ) Σt ∇ log π(at | st)]. A reward received before time t does not depend on at, and 𝔼a~π[∇ log π(a | s)] = ∇ Σa π(a | s) = ∇1 = 0, so those rewards contribute nothing in expectation: each score is weighted only by the reward-to-go Gt = Σk≥0 γk rt+k+1. Same expectation, less variance (the figure).
  4. From returns to action values. 𝔼[Gt | st, at] = Qπ(st, at), which gives the theorem.

Causality deletes the lower triangle: an action is weighted only by the rewards that come after it

One four-step episode with rewards 1, 0, 2, −1 (γ = 1) illustrative. Each row is one action's weight; each column one reward. Left, the whole return weights every action by 2. Right, the reward-to-go weights them 2, 1, 1, −1: the greyed cells are rewards earned before the action was taken, which average to zero against its score and only add noise.

∇θ J(θ) = 𝔼πθ [ Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · Qπ(st, at) ]   ∝   Σs μ(s) Σa qπ(s, a) ∇θ π(a | s, θ)
∇ log π(at | st)
the direction that makes the action actually taken more likely in that state
Qπ(st, at)
how good that action turned out to be, in expectation, if the policy is followed afterwards
μ(s)
the on-policy state distribution; in the episodic case the constant of proportionality is the average episode length, in the continuing case it is 1 ({{Sutton and Barto, Eq. 13.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=348}})

The second form is the statement in {{Sutton et al. (1999)|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/1999/file/464d828b85b0bed98e80ade0a5c43b0f-Paper.pdf}} and in the book, which proves it by unrolling ∇vπ(s) = Σa [∇π(a | s) qπ(s, a) + π(a | s) ∇qπ(s, a)] through the Bellman equation ({{the box on p. 325|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=347}}). Its point: the gradient does not involve the derivative of the state distribution, which depends on the unknown environment.

Compatible function approximation: when a learned critic leaves the gradient exact 1 min

The same paper's Theorem 2 says when a learned fw(s, a) can replace Qπ without biasing the gradient: if fw is a local optimum of the squared error to Qπ under π, and its gradient in w equals the policy's score, ∂fw/∂w = ∇θ π(s, a) / π(s, a). Its Theorem 3 then shows that "a form of policy iteration with function approximation is convergent to a locally optimal policy" under standard step-size conditions. Practical critics are not compatible in this sense; the theorem is why "a critic that is exactly right gives the exact gradient" holds and an arbitrary one does not.

3REINFORCE: the Monte Carlo policy gradient

REINFORCE ({{Williams 1992|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00992696}}) samples the theorem directly: run an episode, then use each step's return as the estimate of Qπ.

For t = 0, ..., T − 1:   G ← Σk=t+1T γk−t−1 Rk;   θ ← θ + α γt G ∇ log π(At | St, θ)
the episode
S0, A0, R1, ..., ST−1, AT−1, RT, generated by πθ (Sutton and Barto's boxed algorithm, {{p. 328|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=350}})
γt
the factor the discounted objective (the value of the start state) requires; most deep-RL code drops it and weights every step equally, which optimises a slightly different objective
∇ log π = ∇π / π
the update is inversely proportional to the action's probability, "otherwise actions that are selected frequently are at an advantage ... and might win out even if they do not yield the highest return" (p. 327)

It is unbiased: the expected update is the gradient, which assures improvement for a small enough α and convergence to a local optimum with decreasing step sizes ({{§13.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=351}}). It has high variance: one episode's return carries the randomness of every later action and transition. It needs episodes that end and updates only afterwards. And when every reward is positive, every sampled action is pushed up, the good ones merely more than the bad; when every reward is negative (the short corridor), every sampled action is pushed down. The policy still improves on average, slowly. With θ started at p(right) = 0.05 and 100 runs averaged, REINFORCE with α = 2−13 climbs from −82 to within about one unit of J* over 1,000 episodes, and α = 2−14 more slowly ({{Figure 13.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=350}}, rerun in section 4).

The name under 1 min

REINFORCE is an acronym, "REward Increment = Nonnegative Factor times Offset Reinforcement times Characteristic Eligibility", which describes the update's form, Δw = α(r − b)e: a step size, a reward minus a baseline, and the score.

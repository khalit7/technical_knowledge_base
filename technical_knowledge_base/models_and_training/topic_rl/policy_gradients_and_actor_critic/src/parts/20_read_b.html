4Baselines: same mean, less variance

Subtract from the return any baseline b(s) that may depend on the state but not on the action. The gradient is unchanged in expectation, because the subtracted term averages to zero:

𝔼a~π[ ∇ log π(a | s) b(s) ] = b(s) Σa ∇π(a | s) = b(s) ∇ Σa π(a | s) = b(s) ∇1 = 0
b(s)
"can be any function, even a random variable, as long as it does not vary with a" ({{Sutton and Barto §13.4|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=351}}); a baseline that depends on the action taken breaks the second equality and biases the gradient

The natural choice is b(s) = Vπ(s), which turns the weight into the advantage Aπ(s, a) = Qπ(s, a) − Vπ(s): how much better than average the action was. Better-than-average actions are pushed up and worse-than-average ones down. "In some states all actions have high values and we need a high baseline to differentiate the higher valued actions from the less highly valued ones; in other states all actions will have low values and a low baseline is appropriate" (§13.4).

Worked example illustrative: one state, three actions with rewards 1, 2 and 6, a softmax with all logits 0, so each action has probability 1/3 and V = 3. The score with respect to the logits is ea − π, the one-hot vector of a minus the probabilities. The exact gradient is πk(Rk − V) = (−0.667, −0.333, 1).

  • Sample action 1 (reward 1) without a baseline: 1 × (2/3, −1/3, −1/3) = (0.667, −0.333, −0.333), which raises the worst action. With baseline 3: (1 − 3) × (2/3, −1/3, −1/3) = (−1.333, 0.667, 0.667), which lowers it.
  • Sample action 3 (reward 6): (−2, −2, 4) without a baseline, (−1, −1, 2) with it: the same direction, half the size.
  • Averaged over the three actions, both estimators give exactly (−0.667, −0.333, 1), but the total variance (summed over components) falls from 7.56 to 1.56 derived.

Which baseline minimises the variance? For a constant b, the variance of (Ra − b)∇ log π(a) is a parabola in b with its minimum at the score-weighted mean b* = 𝔼[‖∇ log π‖2 R] / 𝔼[‖∇ log π‖2], not at V = 𝔼[R] ({{Greensmith, Bartlett and Baxter 2004|https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume5/greensmith04a/greensmith04a.pdf}}, who "show that the popular approach of using the average reward to define the baseline can be suboptimal"). When every action's score vector has the same length, as at the uniform policy above, the two coincide; otherwise V is close, and it is what practice uses because it can be learned. A learned baseline. In practice V is unknown, so it is learned alongside the policy: Sutton and Barto's "REINFORCE with baseline" fits v̂(s, w) to the returns by Monte Carlo and updates θ with Gt − v̂(St, w) ({{box on p. 330|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=352}}). On the short corridor a one-number baseline, v̂ = w, with αθ = 2−9 and αw = 2−6, reaches the optimum within about 100 episodes, where REINFORCE at its best step size needs most of 1,000 (Figure 13.2; {{rerun live|#t-cor}}). The baseline also allows a 16 times larger policy step without diverging.

A baseline does not make an actor-critic
"In REINFORCE with baseline, the learned state-value function estimates the value of the first state of each state transition. This estimate sets a baseline for the subsequent return, but is made prior to the transition's action and thus cannot be used to assess that action" ({{§13.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=353}}). So REINFORCE with a learned baseline, RLOO (each sample's reward minus the mean of the other samples for the same prompt; {{Kool, van Hoof and Welling 2019|https://openreview.net/forum?id=r1lgTGL5DE}}) and GRPO (the group's mean and standard deviation; {{Shao et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300}}) are REINFORCE-family policy methods, not actor-critics, as the root's Method atlas records. Their use on language models is on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}.

5Actor-critic: a critic judges each action

An actor-critic learns two things: an actor π(a | s, θ), updated by the policy gradient, and a critic v̂(s, w), trained by TD learning, whose estimate of the next state turns one real reward into an assessment of the action just taken. The idea is old: {{Barto, Sutton and Anderson (1983)|https://doi.org/10.1109/TSMC.1983.6313077}} balanced a pole with an "adaptive critic element" and an "associative search element", and the root's Method atlas dates the family there.

δt = Rt+1 + γ v̂(St+1, w) − v̂(St, w);   w ← w + αw δt ∇v̂(St, w);   θ ← θ + αθ I δt ∇ log π(At | St, θ);   I ← γI
δt
the TD error, with v̂ = 0 at a terminal state. If the critic were exact, 𝔼[δt | St, At] = Qπ − Vπ = Aπ(St, At): the TD error is an unbiased sample of the advantage
I
the running γt factor; this is the one-step actor-critic of {{Sutton and Barto, p. 332|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=354}}, fully online: each transition is used once, as it happens

The trade is the Monte Carlo against TD trade of {{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}}, moved to policies. One real reward and the critic's estimate give low variance, and updates every step without waiting for the episode to end; but when the critic is wrong the weight is biased. The book adds a subtlety: "the bias in the gradient estimate is not due to bootstrapping as such; the actor would be biased even if the critic was learned by a Monte Carlo method." Between the two ends sit n-step returns and λ-returns, which the next section turns into an advantage estimator.

When the critic is too poor. The short corridor shows the bias at its worst derived. Give the one-step actor-critic the same one-number critic as the baseline above, v̂(s, w) = w. Every state looks the same to it, so δ = −1 + w − w = −1 on every step except the last, where δ = −1 − w > 0. The last action of every episode is right from the third state, so the critic keeps crediting right, and the policy drifts past the optimum to the 0.95 floor (−44 per episode) within a few hundred episodes, while REINFORCE with the same baseline settles at the optimum ({{Short corridor, live|#t-cor}}, "add one-step actor-critic"). A baseline only has to be a number; a critic has to tell states apart.

6Generalised advantage estimation: λ is the bias-variance dial

Between the one-step TD error and the full return there is a family of k-step advantage estimators, Â(k)t = Σl<k γl δt+l = rt + γrt+1 + ... + γk−1rt+k−1 + γkV(st+k) − V(st) (the sum telescopes). GAE ({{Schulman et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02438}}) averages all of them with weights (1 − λ)λk−1, exactly as TD(λ) averages n-step returns, and the average collapses to a discounted sum of TD errors:

the right-hand form is how every implementation computes it: one backward pass over the rollout, resetting at episode ends; the critic's regression target is then Ât + V(st), the λ-return

The paper separates the two parameters: "Taking γ < 1 introduces bias into the policy gradient estimate, regardless of the value function's accuracy. On the other hand, λ < 1 introduces bias only when the value function is inaccurate." Its experiments found the best results at intermediate values, γ ∈ [0.96, 0.99] and λ ∈ [0.92, 0.99] on cart-pole and γ ∈ [0.99, 0.995], λ ∈ [0.96, 0.99] on the 3D biped, and conclude that "choosing an appropriate intermediate value of λ in the range [0.9, 0.99] usually results in the best performance." With it, TRPO learned 3D biped and quadruped locomotion from raw joint states, with simulated experience equal to "1-2 weeks of real time" for the bipeds. PPO's reference settings use λ = 0.95 with γ = 0.99 ({{PPO, Tables 3 and 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347}}).

Worked example (γ = 1): a three-step episode with rewards 0, 0, 1 and critic values V(s0) = 0.5, V(s1) = 0.6, V(s2) = 0.8. The TD errors are δ0 = 0.1, δ1 = 0.2, δ2 = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2. The first action's advantage is 0.1 at λ = 0; 0.1 + 0.95 × 0.2 + 0.952 × 0.2 = 0.4705 at λ = 0.95; and 0.5 at λ = 1, which is the return 1 minus V(s0) = 0.5 derived. 7A3C and A2C: parallel actors instead of replay

On-policy methods cannot use a replay buffer (old data comes from old policies), yet consecutive transitions of one trajectory are highly correlated, which is what made naive deep RL unstable. A3C, asynchronous advantage actor-critic ({{Mnih et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.01783}}), decorrelates the data differently: many actor-learners, each with its own copy of the environment, explore different states at the same moment and apply their gradients to shared weights asynchronously. Each uses an n-step return minus V as its advantage, and an entropy bonus: "adding the entropy of the policy π to the objective function improved exploration by discouraging premature convergence to suboptimal deterministic policies" (weight β = 0.01 on Atari). It "surpasses the current state-of-the-art on the Atari domain while training for half the time on a single multi-core CPU instead of a GPU", with 16 actor-learner threads.

A2C is the synchronous version: wait for every actor to finish its segment, average, take one batched step. OpenAI's Baselines release found "Our synchronous A2C implementation performs better than our asynchronous implementations; we have not seen any evidence that the noise introduced by asynchrony provides any performance benefit", and that it "is more cost-effective than A3C when using single-GPU machines, and is faster than a CPU-only A3C implementation when using larger policies" ({{OpenAI Baselines: ACKTR and A2C, 2017|https://web.archive.org/web/20190213165907/https://blog.openai.com/baselines-acktr-a2c/}}, Wayback copy). So A2C matched or beat A3C: the parallel actors helped, the asynchrony did not (one of the root Method atlas's corrections). A batch of rollouts from parallel environments, then one update, is the shape of PPO and of every LLM RL loop since.

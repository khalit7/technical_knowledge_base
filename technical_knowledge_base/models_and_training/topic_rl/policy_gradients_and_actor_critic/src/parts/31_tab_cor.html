The short corridor, live

Sutton and Barto's Example 13.1 is the smallest problem on which a policy-gradient method beats every action-value method that uses the same features, and it is small enough to rerun their Figures 13.1 and 13.2 in your browser ({{Sutton and Barto, pp. 323 and 328 to 330|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=345}}).

Three states and a goal; every step costs −1. In the first and third states right moves right and left moves left (left in the first state bumps the wall); in the second state the actions are switched. The agent cannot tell the states apart: both actions have the same feature vector in every state, x(s, right) = [1, 0] and x(s, left) = [0, 1], so a policy is a single number, the probability p of choosing right.

How good is each policy? The exact value of the start state, J(p) = 2(2 − p) / (p(p − 1))

The curve is exact, from the Bellman equations of the three states (derivation in the box below). The dots are single learning runs on the same random numbers, one frame every 10 episodes: REINFORCE with α = 2−13, REINFORCE with a learned baseline (αθ = 2−9, αw = 2−6), the book's settings for Figure 13.2, and optionally one-step actor-critic with the same step sizes (not in the book).

Where J(p) and the optimum come from (Exercise 13.1)

Write v1, v2, v3 for the three states under "right with probability p". The Bellman equations are v1 = −1 + p v2 + (1 − p) v1; v2 = −1 + p v1 + (1 − p) v3 (switched); v3 = −1 + (1 − p) v2. Solving gives J(p) = v1 = 2(2 − p) / (p(p − 1)). Setting dJ/dp = 0 gives p* = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.5858 and J* = −(6 + 4√2) ≈ −11.657 derived. The ε-greedy policies with ε = 0.1 choose their greedy action with probability 1 − ε/2 = 0.95: J(0.95) = −44.21 and J(0.05) = −82.11, the book's "less than −44 and −82" derived.

What these defaults reproduce, and what they do not
  • Exactly, independently: the optimum p* ≈ 0.59 with value ≈ −11.6, and the ε-greedy values below −44 and −82 (Example 13.1), from the Bellman equations above.
  • In shape: Figure 13.2's result that a learned baseline (here v̂(s, w) = w, one number, as in the book) makes REINFORCE learn much faster: with 100 runs the baseline curve is within about 0.5 of J* by episodes 100 to 200, while REINFORCE at 2−13 is still near −25 there and approaches −12.6 by the end. Figure 13.1's 2−13 and 2−14 curves have the same shape as the book's (the smaller step is slower).
  • Not reproduced: in the book's Figure 13.1 the largest step, 2−12, levels off well below the others. Here, with the 0.05 floor, 2−12 learns fastest of the three; without the floor some of its runs drive p to 0 or 1, episodes become thousands of steps long and the average collapses. The book does not state its starting policy or whether probabilities were bounded, so this page uses the widely used reproduction by Zhang and Bondariev ({{short_corridor.py|https://github.com/ShangtongZhang/reinforcement-learning-an-introduction/blob/master/chapter13/short_corridor.py}}): θ starts at [−1.47, 1.47] (p = 0.05, near ε-greedy left) and the smaller probability is never allowed below 0.05. Two departures from that code: the book's step sizes (powers of two) instead of its 2e-4, 2e-5, 2e-3, and the book's order of updates in REINFORCE with baseline (δ computed before w moves).
  • New here: one-step actor-critic with the same one-number critic. Because every state looks the same to the critic, the TD error is −1 + w − w = −1 on every step but the last, which is −1 − w > 0; the last action of an episode is always right (from the third state), so the critic keeps crediting right and the policy drifts to the 0.95 floor (−44). With a critic this poor, the actor-critic's bias wins over its lower variance; a smaller actor step (2−12) delays the drift but does not stop it.

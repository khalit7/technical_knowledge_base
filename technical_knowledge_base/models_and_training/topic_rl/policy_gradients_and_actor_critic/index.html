Policy gradients and actor-critic in one screen

Value-based methods learn how good each action is and act greedily on that. Policy-gradient methods parameterise the policy itself, πθ(a | s), and climb the gradient of its expected return. Every method on this page uses the same gradient, and differs in only two places: what number weights each action's log-probability (the return, the return minus a baseline, a critic's estimate), and how far one batch of data is allowed to move the policy.

1Why optimise the policy directly

A value-based agent acts by arg maxa Q(s, a). That needs a maximisation over actions at every step, which is awkward when the action is a vector of joint torques and expensive when it is one of 100,000 tokens, and it only ever yields a deterministic policy (plus whatever exploration noise is bolted on). Parameterising the policy instead has four advantages (Sutton and Barto §13.1):

  • Continuous and huge action spaces are natural: output the mean and spread of a Gaussian over torques, or a softmax over tokens.
  • The optimal policy may be stochastic. In card games with hidden information, or when the agent cannot tell states apart, the best policy randomises with specific probabilities. An ε-greedy action-value method cannot represent that; a softmax policy can.
  • Smooth change. The action probabilities move continuously with θ, whereas in ε-greedy selection an arbitrarily small change in the estimated values can flip which action is greedy. "Largely because of this, stronger convergence guarantees are available for policy-gradient methods than for action-value methods" (§13.2).
  • Prior knowledge goes into the form of the policy, which the book calls "often the most important reason for using a policy-based learning method".

A worked case: the short corridor (Example 13.1). Three states, reward −1 per step, actions right and left, except that in the second state they are switched; all states share the same features, so a policy is one number, p = probability of right. Solving the three Bellman equations gives the value of the start state J(p) = 2(2 − p) / (p(p − 1)), maximised at p* = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.586 with J* = −(6 + 4√2) ≈ −11.66 derived. The two policies an ε-greedy method can reach (ε = 0.1) choose right with probability 0.95 or 0.05 and score −44.2 and −82.1. The book: "The best probability is about 0.59, which achieves a value of about −11.6." The live tab draws the curve and lets three learners find the optimum.

Two policy parameterisations and their score functions

Everything below needs only ∇θ log π(a | s, θ), the score function (Sutton and Barto call it the eligibility vector). The two parameterisations in nearly every implementation:

Softmax over action preferences: π(a | s) = exp(θ⊤x(s, a)) / Σb exp(θ⊤x(s, b)),   ∇ log π(a | s) = x(s, a) − Σb π(b | s) x(s, b)
Gaussian: a ~ 𝒩(μθ(s), σθ(s)2),   ∂ log π / ∂μ = (a − μ) / σ2,   ∂ log π / ∂ log σ = (a − μ)2 / σ2 − 1
x(s, a)
a feature vector of the state-action pair; with a network, the logits play the role of θ⊤x and the score with respect to the logits is the one-hot vector of a minus the probabilities
μ, σ
the mean and standard deviation the network outputs (§13.7); a sample above the mean pushes μ up if it turned out well, a sample far from the mean pushes σ up

2The policy gradient theorem, derived

The objective is the expected return of trajectories τ = (s0, a0, r1, s1, ...) sampled by running the policy, J(θ) = 𝔼τ~πθ[G(τ)]. The difficulty: θ changes which trajectories happen, through a state distribution that depends on unknown dynamics. Four steps remove it.

  1. The log-derivative trick. For any distribution, ∇θ 𝔼x~pθ[f(x)] = Σx f(x) ∇pθ(x) = Σx f(x) pθ(x) ∇ log pθ(x) = 𝔼x~pθ[f(x) ∇ log pθ(x)], because ∇p = p ∇ log p. A gradient of an expectation becomes an expectation of something we can sample.
  2. The dynamics drop out. log pθ(τ) = log p(s0) + Σt [log πθ(at | st) + log p(st+1, rt+1 | st, at)]. Only the policy terms depend on θ, so ∇ log pθ(τ) = Σt ∇ log πθ(at | st): no model of the environment is ever needed, only the ability to sample it and differentiate the policy.
  3. Causality. That gives ∇J = 𝔼[G(τ) Σt ∇ log π(at | st)]. A reward received before time t is independent of at given the past, and 𝔼a~π[∇ log π(a | s)] = ∇ Σa π(a | s) = ∇1 = 0, so those rewards contribute nothing in expectation and can be dropped: each score is weighted only by the reward-to-go Gt = Σk≥0 γk rt+k+1. Same expectation, less variance.
  4. From returns to action values. 𝔼[Gt | st, at] = Qπ(st, at), which gives the theorem.
∇θ J(θ) = 𝔼πθ [ Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) · Qπ(st, at) ]   ∝   Σs μ(s) Σa qπ(s, a) ∇θ π(a | s, θ)
∇ log π(at | st)
the direction in parameter space that makes the action actually taken more likely in that state
Qπ(st, at)
how good that action turned out to be, in expectation, if the policy is followed afterwards
μ(s)
the on-policy state distribution; in the episodic case the constant of proportionality is the average episode length, in the continuing case it is 1 (Sutton and Barto, Eq. 13.5)

In words: push up the log-probability of each action in proportion to how good it was. The second form is the statement in Sutton et al. (1999) and in the book, which proves it from first principles by unrolling ∇vπ(s) = Σa [∇π(a | s) qπ(s, a) + π(a | s) ∇qπ(s, a)] repeatedly through the Bellman equation (the box on p. 325). Its point is that the gradient does not involve the derivative of the state distribution, which depends on the unknown environment.

Compatible function approximation. The same paper's Theorem 2 says when a learned fw(s, a) can replace Qπ without biasing the gradient: if fw is a local optimum of the squared error to Qπ under π, and its gradient in w equals the policy's score, ∂fw/∂w = ∇θ π(s, a) / π(s, a). Its Theorem 3 then shows that "a form of policy iteration with function approximation is convergent to a locally optimal policy" under standard step-size conditions. Practical critics are not compatible in this sense; the theorem is why "a critic that is exactly right gives the exact gradient" holds and an arbitrary one does not.

3REINFORCE: the Monte Carlo policy gradient

REINFORCE (Williams 1992) samples the theorem directly: run an episode, then use each step's return as the estimate of Qπ. The name is an acronym, "REward Increment = Nonnegative Factor times Offset Reinforcement times Characteristic Eligibility", which describes the update's form, Δw = α(r − b)e: a step size, a reward minus a baseline, and the score.

REINFORCE (Sutton and Barto's boxed algorithm, p. 328): for each episode S0, A0, R1, ..., ST−1, AT−1, RT generated by πθ, for t = 0, ..., T − 1:   G ← Σk=t+1T γk−t−1 Rk;   θ ← θ + α γt G ∇ log π(At | St, θ)
γt
the factor the discounted objective (the value of the start state) requires; most deep-RL code drops it and weights every step equally, which optimises a slightly different objective
∇ log π = ∇π / π
the update is proportional to the return and inversely proportional to the action's probability, "otherwise actions that are selected frequently are at an advantage ... and might win out even if they do not yield the highest return" (p. 327)

Its properties follow from the derivation. It is unbiased: by construction the expected update is in the direction of the gradient, which assures improvement for a small enough α and convergence to a local optimum with decreasing step sizes (§13.3). It has high variance: one episode's return carries the randomness of every later action and transition. It needs episodes that end, and it updates only afterwards. And when every reward is positive, every sampled action is pushed up, the good ones merely more than the bad; when every reward is negative (the short corridor), every sampled action is pushed down. The policy still improves on average, slowly.

On the short corridor, with θ started so that p(right) = 0.05 and 100 runs averaged, REINFORCE with α = 2−13 climbs from −82 to within about one unit of J* over 1,000 episodes, and α = 2−14 more slowly (Figure 13.1, rerun in Short corridor, live with the exact numbers per episode range).

4Baselines: same mean, less variance

Subtract from the return any baseline b(s) that may depend on the state but not on the action. The gradient is unchanged in expectation, because the subtracted term averages to zero:

𝔼a~π[ ∇ log π(a | s) b(s) ] = b(s) Σa ∇π(a | s) = b(s) ∇ Σa π(a | s) = b(s) ∇1 = 0
b(s)
"can be any function, even a random variable, as long as it does not vary with a" (Sutton and Barto §13.4); a baseline that depends on the action taken breaks the second equality and biases the gradient

The natural choice is b(s) = Vπ(s), which turns the weight into the advantage Aπ(s, a) = Qπ(s, a) − Vπ(s): how much better than average the action was. Better-than-average actions are pushed up and worse-than-average ones down. "In some states all actions have high values and we need a high baseline to differentiate the higher valued actions from the less highly valued ones; in other states all actions will have low values and a low baseline is appropriate" (§13.4).

Worked example illustrative: one state, three actions with rewards 1, 2 and 6, a softmax with all logits 0, so each action has probability 1/3 and V = 3. The score with respect to the logits is ea − π, the one-hot vector of a minus the probabilities. The exact gradient is πk(Rk − V) = (−0.667, −0.333, 1).

  • Sample action 1 (reward 1) without a baseline: 1 × (2/3, −1/3, −1/3) = (0.667, −0.333, −0.333), which raises the worst action. With baseline 3: (1 − 3) × (2/3, −1/3, −1/3) = (−1.333, 0.667, 0.667), which lowers it.
  • Sample action 3 (reward 6): (−2, −2, 4) without a baseline, (−1, −1, 2) with it: the same direction, half the size.
  • Averaged over the three actions, both estimators give exactly (−0.667, −0.333, 1), but the total variance (summed over components) falls from 7.56 to 1.56 derived.

Which baseline minimises the variance? For a constant b, the variance of (Ra − b)∇ log π(a) is a parabola in b with its minimum at the score-weighted mean b* = 𝔼[‖∇ log π‖2 R] / 𝔼[‖∇ log π‖2], not at V = 𝔼[R] (Greensmith, Bartlett and Baxter 2004, who "show that the popular approach of using the average reward to define the baseline can be suboptimal"). When every action's score vector has the same length, as at the uniform policy above, the two coincide; otherwise V is close, and it is what practice uses because it can be learned. A learned baseline. In practice V is unknown, so it is learned alongside the policy: Sutton and Barto's "REINFORCE with baseline" fits v̂(s, w) to the returns by Monte Carlo and updates θ with Gt − v̂(St, w) (box on p. 330). On the short corridor a one-number baseline, v̂ = w, with αθ = 2−9 and αw = 2−6, reaches the optimum within about 100 episodes, where REINFORCE at its best step size needs most of 1,000 (Figure 13.2; rerun live). The baseline also allows a 16 times larger policy step without diverging.

A baseline does not make an actor-critic
"In REINFORCE with baseline, the learned state-value function estimates the value of the first state of each state transition. This estimate sets a baseline for the subsequent return, but is made prior to the transition's action and thus cannot be used to assess that action" (§13.5). So REINFORCE with a learned baseline, RLOO (each sample's reward minus the mean of the other samples for the same prompt; Kool, van Hoof and Welling 2019) and GRPO (the group's mean and standard deviation; Shao et al. 2024) are REINFORCE-family policy methods, not actor-critics, as the root's Method atlas records. Their use on language models is on RL for LLMs.

5Actor-critic: a critic judges each action

An actor-critic learns two things: an actor π(a | s, θ), updated by the policy gradient, and a critic v̂(s, w), trained by TD learning, whose estimate of the next state turns one real reward into an assessment of the action just taken. The idea is old: Barto, Sutton and Anderson (1983) balanced a pole with an "adaptive critic element" and an "associative search element", and the root's Method atlas dates the family there.

δt = Rt+1 + γ v̂(St+1, w) − v̂(St, w);   w ← w + αw δt ∇v̂(St, w);   θ ← θ + αθ I δt ∇ log π(At | St, θ);   I ← γI
δt
the TD error, with v̂ = 0 at a terminal state. If the critic were exact, 𝔼[δt | St, At] = Qπ − Vπ = Aπ(St, At): the TD error is an unbiased sample of the advantage
I
the running γt factor; this is the one-step actor-critic of Sutton and Barto, p. 332, fully online: each transition is used once, as it happens

The trade is the Monte Carlo against TD trade of Model-free prediction and control, moved to policies. One real reward and the critic's estimate give low variance, and updates every step without waiting for the episode to end; but when the critic is wrong the weight is biased. The book adds a subtlety: "the bias in the gradient estimate is not due to bootstrapping as such; the actor would be biased even if the critic was learned by a Monte Carlo method." Between the two ends sit n-step returns and λ-returns, which the next section turns into an advantage estimator.

When the critic is too poor. The short corridor shows the bias at its worst derived. Give the one-step actor-critic the same one-number critic as the baseline above, v̂(s, w) = w. Every state looks the same to it, so δ = −1 + w − w = −1 on every step except the last, where δ = −1 − w > 0. The last action of every episode is right from the third state, so the critic keeps crediting right, and the policy drifts past the optimum to the 0.95 floor (−44 per episode) within a few hundred episodes, while REINFORCE with the same baseline settles at the optimum (Short corridor, live, "add one-step actor-critic"). A baseline only has to be a number; a critic has to tell states apart.

6Generalised advantage estimation: λ is the bias-variance dial

Between the one-step TD error and the full return there is a family of k-step advantage estimators, Â(k)t = Σl<k γl δt+l = rt + γrt+1 + ... + γk−1rt+k−1 + γkV(st+k) − V(st) (the sum telescopes). GAE (Schulman et al. 2015) averages all of them with weights (1 − λ)λk−1, exactly as TD(λ) averages n-step returns, and the average collapses to a discounted sum of TD errors:

The paper separates the two parameters: "Taking γ < 1 introduces bias into the policy gradient estimate, regardless of the value function's accuracy. On the other hand, λ < 1 introduces bias only when the value function is inaccurate." Its experiments found the best results at intermediate values, γ ∈ [0.96, 0.99] and λ ∈ [0.92, 0.99] on cart-pole and γ ∈ [0.99, 0.995], λ ∈ [0.96, 0.99] on the 3D biped, and conclude that "choosing an appropriate intermediate value of λ in the range [0.9, 0.99] usually results in the best performance." With it, TRPO learned 3D biped and quadruped locomotion from raw joint states, with simulated experience equal to "1-2 weeks of real time" for the bipeds. PPO's reference settings use λ = 0.95 with γ = 0.99 (PPO, Tables 3 and 5).

Worked example (γ = 1): a three-step episode with rewards 0, 0, 1 and critic values V(s0) = 0.5, V(s1) = 0.6, V(s2) = 0.8. The TD errors are δ0 = 0.1, δ1 = 0.2, δ2 = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2. The first action's advantage is 0.1 at λ = 0; 0.1 + 0.95 × 0.2 + 0.952 × 0.2 = 0.4705 at λ = 0.95; and 0.5 at λ = 1, which is the return 1 minus V(s0) = 0.5 derived. 7A3C and A2C: parallel actors instead of replay

On-policy methods cannot use a replay buffer (old data comes from old policies), yet consecutive transitions of one trajectory are highly correlated, which is what made naive deep RL unstable. A3C, asynchronous advantage actor-critic (Mnih et al. 2016), decorrelates the data differently: many actor-learners, each with its own copy of the environment, explore different states at the same moment and apply their gradients to shared weights asynchronously. Each uses an n-step return minus V as its advantage, and an entropy bonus: "adding the entropy of the policy π to the objective function improved exploration by discouraging premature convergence to suboptimal deterministic policies" (weight β = 0.01 on Atari). It "surpasses the current state-of-the-art on the Atari domain while training for half the time on a single multi-core CPU instead of a GPU", with 16 actor-learner threads.

A2C is the synchronous version: wait for every actor to finish its segment, average, take one batched step. OpenAI's Baselines release found "Our synchronous A2C implementation performs better than our asynchronous implementations; we have not seen any evidence that the noise introduced by asynchrony provides any performance benefit", and that it "is more cost-effective than A3C when using single-GPU machines, and is faster than a CPU-only A3C implementation when using larger policies" (OpenAI Baselines: ACKTR and A2C, 2017, Wayback copy). So A2C matched or beat A3C: the parallel actors helped, the asynchrony did not (one of the root Method atlas's corrections). A batch of rollouts from parallel environments, then one update, is the shape of PPO and of every LLM RL loop since.

8Natural gradients and TRPO: measure the step in policy space

Why the step size is dangerous. The policy gradient is only valid near the policy that collected the data. In supervised learning a too-large step costs one bad update; in RL the next batch is sampled from the broken policy, so the data itself degrades and training may never recover. Two goals follow: the largest step that is still safe, and several gradient steps on one expensive batch of rollouts.

The step is measured in the wrong units. A step of fixed length in θ can move the policy a lot or barely at all, depending on where it is. Take two actions with logit difference z, so p = σ(z). From p = 0.5, a step Δz = 1 moves p to 0.731, a KL divergence of 0.120; from p = 0.99 the same step moves it to 0.996, a KL of 0.0037, 33 times less derived. Gradient ascent is not covariant: reparameterise the policy and its steps change. The fix is to measure distance by how much the action distribution changes. To second order, KL(πθ ‖ πθ+Δ) ≈ ½ Δ⊤FΔ, where F = 𝔼[∇ log π ∇ log π⊤] is the Fisher information matrix (for the two-action case, F = p(1 − p): 0.25 at p = 0.5, 0.0099 at p = 0.99).

Natural gradient step:   Δθ = √( 2δ / (g⊤F−1g) ) · F−1g
g
the ordinary policy gradient
F−1g
the steepest-ascent direction when distance is the KL (to second order) rather than Euclidean distance in θ
δ
the KL budget for the step; the square root scales the step so that ½ Δ⊤FΔ = δ

Kakade (2001) introduced this natural policy gradient: "the standard gradient descent rule is non-covariant ... we present a covariant gradient by defining a metric based on the underlying structure of the policy", and showed that "the natural gradient is moving toward choosing a greedy optimal action rather than just a better action." (He also notes that the averaged F is not fully invariant, because of the weighting by the state distribution.) In the example, a KL budget of 0.01 allows Δz = 0.283 at p = 0.5 (exact KL 0.00997) and Δz = 1.42 at p = 0.99 (exact KL 0.0066: the quadratic approximation is loose far from the centre, which is why TRPO checks the true KL with a line search) derived.

TRPO, trust region policy optimisation (Schulman et al. 2015), turns this into an algorithm with a guarantee. With the importance ratio rt(θ) = πθ(at | st) / πθold(at | st) between the policy being optimised and the one that collected the batch, maximising the surrogate 𝔼t[rt(θ) Ât] has the same gradient as the policy gradient at θ = θold, and the ratio corrects for the drift as θ moves away (the importance-sampling correction of Model-free prediction and control, applied to a slightly stale policy). The paper bounds how far the surrogate can mislead: from its Theorem 1 (stated with total variation) it derives η(π̃) ≥ Lπ(π̃) − C · maxs KL(π ‖ π̃), with C = 4εγ / (1 − γ)2 and ε = max |Aπ(s, a)| (Eq. 9), so maximising the penalised surrogate improves the true return monotonically. The penalty coefficient that theory gives is far too conservative, so TRPO uses a hard constraint instead:

maxθ 𝔼t[ rt(θ) Ât ]   subject to   𝔼t[ KL(πθold(· | st) ‖ πθ(· | st)) ] ≤ δ
δ
the trust region: "We used δ = 0.01 for all experiments"
the solver
a natural-gradient direction by conjugate gradient using Fisher-vector products (no matrix is ever formed; "k = 10" iterations "to be quite effective"), then a line search that shrinks the step "until the objective improves. Without this line search, the algorithm occasionally computes large steps that cause a catastrophic degradation" (Appendix C)

The paper tried two ways of collecting data, "single path" (ordinary rollouts) and "vine" (branching rollouts from states along a trunk, which needs a resettable simulator), and learned "simulated robotic swimming, hopping, and walking gaits; and playing Atari games using images of the screen as input". It works, but the second-order machinery is heavy, and TRPO is "not compatible with architectures that include noise (such as dropout) or parameter sharing (between the policy and value function, or with auxiliary tasks)" (PPO, §1). PPO keeps the idea and drops the machinery.

9PPO: the trust region from a clipped first-order objective

PPO, proximal policy optimisation (Schulman et al. 2017), replaces the constraint with a pessimistic objective that plain stochastic gradient ascent can optimise:

LCLIP(θ) = 𝔼t[ min( rt(θ) Ât,  clip(rt(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Ât ) ]
clip(x, 1 − ε, 1 + ε)
forces the ratio into that interval
ε
the clip range; among 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 on the continuous-control tasks 0.2 did best; the Atari runs used 0.1 × α with α annealed linearly from 1 to 0
min
takes the more pessimistic of the clipped and unclipped terms, so clipping can only remove an incentive, never add one

How the min works, one case at a time:

  • Good action (Â > 0). The surrogate wants r up. Once r > 1 + ε the clipped term is the smaller one and it is constant in θ: the objective goes flat, the gradient is zero, there is no incentive to push further.
  • Bad action (Â < 0). The surrogate wants r down. Once r < 1 − ε the objective goes flat in the same way.
  • A ratio that moved the wrong way (down for a good action, up for a bad one) is never clipped: there the unclipped term is the smaller one, so the full gradient pulls it back. The clip removes the incentive to exploit further; it never stops the correction of a mistake.

PPO is on-policy, approximately
It reuses each batch for several epochs, and the ratio corrects for the drift within those epochs; it cannot learn from data collected by much older policies, and the batch is thrown away afterwards. That is the "approximately on-policy" cell of the root's Method atlas, and the reason PPO needs far more environment steps than the off-policy methods below.

10What actually makes PPO work: the implementation

PPO's paper describes an objective; its results come from an implementation. Engstrom et al. (2020) isolated nine "code-level optimisations" in the reference code (value function clipping, reward scaling, orthogonal initialisation and layer scaling, Adam learning-rate annealing, reward clipping, observation normalisation, observation clipping, tanh activations, global gradient clipping) and found they "are responsible for most of PPO's gain in cumulative reward over TRPO". With them, TRPO ("TRPO+") matched or beat PPO: on Hopper, PPO 2513, PPO without them ("PPO-M") 2142, TRPO 2043, TRPO+ 2466; on Humanoid TRPO+ 1030 against PPO's 806. And the clip does not do what its name suggests: "all three algorithms fail to maintain a ratio-based trust region, despite PPO and PPO-M being trained directly with a ratio-clipping objective", although all keep a KL-based one. Their summary: "code-level optimizations contribute to algorithms' increased performance often significantly more than the choice of algorithm."

Huang et al., "The 37 Implementation Details of Proximal Policy Optimization" (ICLR blog track, 2022) reproduced the official code detail by detail: 13 core details, 9 for Atari, 9 for continuous actions, 5 for LSTMs and 1 for multi-discrete actions. The 13 core ones:

  1. Vectorised environments (many copies stepped together; the rollout is N × T)
  2. Orthogonal initialisation of weights, constant initialisation of biases (policy output layer scaled by 0.01, value output by 1)
  3. Adam's epsilon parameter (1e-5 in the reference code, not PyTorch's default 1e-8)
  4. Adam learning-rate annealing to 0
  5. Generalised advantage estimation, with correct handling of episode ends and of truncation at the rollout's end (bootstrap from V)
  6. Minibatch updates over shuffled indices
  7. Normalisation of advantages, per minibatch
  8. The clipped surrogate objective
  9. Value function loss clipping
  10. The overall loss and entropy bonus
  11. Global gradient clipping (norm 0.5)
  12. Debug variables: approximate KL, clip fraction, explained variance
  13. Shared or separate networks for policy and value

The largest study, Andrychowicz et al. (2021), trained "over 250'000 agents in five continuous control environments" across more than 50 choices. Its recommendations: "Use the PPO policy loss. Start with the clipping threshold set to 0.25"; "Initialize the last policy layer with 100× smaller weights" and use softplus for the standard deviation; "Always use observation normalization"; "Use GAE with λ = 0.9 but neither Huber loss nor PPO-style value loss clipping"; the discount γ "is one of the most important hyperparameters and should be tuned per environment (start with γ = 0.99)"; and Adam with 0.0003 as "a safe default".

Seeds and reporting. Deep RL results vary so much across random seeds that one run misleads. Henderson et al. (2018) split ten TRPO runs on HalfCheetah into two groups of five seeds and got significantly different curves (t = −9.09, p = 0.0016): "the variance between runs is enough to create statistically different distributions just from varying random seeds." Agarwal et al. (2021) recommend "interval estimates via stratified bootstrap confidence intervals, as opposed to point estimates", and the interquartile mean, which "discards the bottom and top 25% of the runs".

What to watch while PPO trains
Approximate KL between old and new policy per update (a sudden jump means the step is too large); clip fraction (the share of samples whose ratio left 1 ± ε; a steady rise means the policy is moving further per batch); policy entropy (a collapse to near zero means exploration has stopped); explained variance of the critic (near 0 or negative means the advantages are mostly noise). These are the "debug variables" of detail 12, and the same four are watched in LLM RL.

11Deterministic policy gradients and DDPG

For a robot arm the action is a vector of torques, and Q-learning's max over actions becomes an optimisation problem at every step. The off-policy actor-critics solve it by training an actor that performs the max. Their theory is the deterministic policy gradient (Silver et al. 2014): for a deterministic policy a = μθ(s),

∇θ J(μθ) = 𝔼s~ρμ[ ∇θ μθ(s) · ∇a Qμ(s, a) |a=μθ(s) ]
∇aQ
the critic's slope: which way to nudge the action to make it better
∇θμ
the chain rule through the actor: how to change θ to move the action that way

There is no log-probability and no integral over actions, only over states, so "the deterministic policy gradient can be estimated much more efficiently than the usual stochastic policy gradient", and it is "the limiting case, as policy variance tends to zero, of the stochastic policy gradient" (Theorem 2). Because the expectation is only over states, it can be learned from data gathered by any exploratory behaviour policy, without importance ratios on actions.

DDPG, deep deterministic policy gradient (Lillicrap et al. 2015), put this on networks with DQN's two stabilisers: a replay buffer (106 transitions) and target networks, here updated softly, θ′ ← τθ + (1 − τ)θ′ with τ = 0.001. The critic regresses onto r + γQ′(s′, μ′(s′)); the actor climbs ∇aQ. Exploration is noise added to the actor's output (an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process with θ = 0.15, σ = 0.2). Other settings: Adam with learning rates 10−4 (actor) and 10−3 (critic), L2 weight decay 10−2 on the critic, γ = 0.99, minibatch 64, batch normalisation. It "robustly solves more than 20 simulated physics tasks", nearly all "within 2.5 million steps of experience (and usually far fewer), a factor of 20 fewer steps than DQN requires" for Atari. In practice it proved brittle and sensitive to its settings, which is what TD3 set out to fix. (Replay and target networks are explained on Value-based deep RL.)

12TD3: three fixes for overestimation

The maximisation bias of Q-learning (the max of noisy estimates is biased upwards) does not need an explicit max: an actor trained to climb a noisy critic finds and exploits the critic's overestimates just as well. Fujimoto, van Hoof and Meger (2018) showed "this problem persists in an actor-critic setting" and fixed DDPG with three changes:

  1. Clipped double Q-learning. Two critics; the target uses the smaller: y = r + γ mini=1,2 Qθ′i(s′, πφ′(s′) + ε). Taking a minimum is deliberately pessimistic: for two independent unbiased estimates with noise σ, 𝔼[min] = −σ/√π ≈ −0.56σ below the truth, where 𝔼[max] is +0.56σ above derived. An underestimate is safer: "underestimated actions will not be explicitly propagated" through the policy update, because the actor moves away from them.
  2. Delayed policy updates. Update the actor (and the target networks) once every d = 2 critic updates, so the actor climbs a critic whose error has had time to fall.
  3. Target policy smoothing. Add clipped noise to the target action, ε ~ clip(𝒩(0, 0.2), −0.5, 0.5), so the target averages the critic over a small neighbourhood and a narrow spurious peak cannot be exploited.

Paper settings: exploration noise 𝒩(0, 0.1), τ = 0.005, Adam 10−3, minibatch 100, and purely random actions for the first 10,000 steps (HalfCheetah, Ant) or 1,000 (the others). The authors' current code uses batch 256, learning rate 3 × 10−4 and 25,000 random steps, and its README warns it is "no longer exactly representative of the code used in the paper" (sfujim/TD3).

13SAC and maximum-entropy RL

SAC, soft actor-critic (Haarnoja et al. 2018a; 2018b), changes the objective: maximise reward and the policy's entropy.

J(π) = Σt 𝔼[ r(st, at) + α ℋ(π(· | st)) ],   ℋ(π(· | s)) = −𝔼a~π[ log π(a | s) ]
ℋ
the entropy of the action distribution (a sum for discrete actions, an integral for continuous ones)
α
the temperature: how much randomness is worth relative to reward. A policy split evenly between two actions has entropy ln 2 ≈ 0.693, worth 0.2 × 0.693 ≈ 0.139 reward per step at α = 0.2; a deterministic policy earns nothing derived

The entropy enters the Bellman equation too. The soft value is V(s) = 𝔼a~π[Q(s, a) − α log π(a | s)] and the critic's target is r + γ𝔼[V(s′)], with the minimum of two Q networks as in TD3. Policy improvement moves π toward the Boltzmann distribution of the critic, πnew = arg minπ′ KL(π′(· | s) ‖ exp(Q(s, ·)/α) / Z(s)); in practice the actor minimises 𝔼[α log π(a | s) − Q(s, a)] with actions drawn by the reparameterisation trick, a = tanh(μ(s) + σ(s) ⊙ ξ), ξ ~ 𝒩(0, I), so the gradient flows through the sampled action into the critic ("a lower variance estimator" than the score function). The tanh keeps actions bounded, and changes the density: log π(a | s) = log μ(u | s) − Σi log(1 − tanh2(ui)) (Appendix C), a correction that implementations forget at their peril.

The temperature is learned. The 2018b version adjusts α so that the policy's entropy stays near a target ℋ̄, minimising J(α) = 𝔼[−α log π(a | s) − αℋ̄]; its gradient is ℋ(π) − ℋ̄, so α falls while the policy is more random than the target and rises when it is less. The default target is "−dim(A) (e.g., −6 for HalfCheetah-v1)". The widget shows what α does on a one-dimensional action illustrative reward, exact computation: a tall narrow reward peak at +0.6, a lower wide one at −0.5, and an effort cost 0.5a2. For each α it computes the soft-optimal policy exp(r/α)/Z and the best Gaussian actor (the maximiser of 𝔼[r] + αℋ over all Gaussians, which is SAC's projection onto its policy class).

Try: slide α from 0.005 up. Below about 0.16 the best Gaussian sits narrowly on the tall peak; just above, it jumps to the wide peak, where it can be much wider for little lost reward; by α ≈ 0.3 it covers both. The soft-optimal policy (purple) changes smoothly and is bimodal in between: a single Gaussian cannot represent it, which is the price of SAC's policy class. Expected rewards and entropies are exact (closed form for the Gaussian, an 801-point grid for the soft-optimal policy); the Gaussian is found on a 481 × 241 grid of means and widths.

The paper's claim is stability as much as sample efficiency: "in contrast to other off-policy algorithms, our approach is very stable, achieving very similar performance across different random seeds" (2018a). It also trained real robots: a Minitaur quadruped "learns to walk from 160k environment steps, or approximately 400 episodes ... approximately 2 hours of real-world training time", and a 3-finger dexterous hand learned to turn a valve from camera images in about 300,000 steps, "the equivalent of 20 hours of training" (from the valve's position alone, 3 hours, against 7.4 hours for PPO) (2018b, §7). Its settings are the defaults most libraries copied: Adam 3 × 10−4, γ = 0.99, replay 106, minibatch 256, τ = 0.005, two hidden layers of 256 units.

The same idea in language-model RL
Entropy bonuses and KL penalties toward a reference policy play the role of α ℋ in LLM training: both keep the policy from collapsing onto one mode. A KL to a fixed reference is not the same as an entropy bonus (it pulls toward a particular distribution, not toward uniform); RL for LLMs covers how each is used there.

14Continuous control in practice

  • The Gaussian head. A network outputs the mean of each action dimension; the dimensions are treated as independent (the log-probability of an action is the sum over dimensions). PPO's reference code keeps a state-independent log standard deviation, a learned vector initialised to 0; SAC outputs a state-dependent one. Andrychowicz et al. found the performance of the two "very close" (37 details, continuous section).
  • Bounded actions. A Gaussian sample can be any number. PPO's reference code clips the sampled action to the valid range when stepping the environment but stores and trains on the unclipped sample; SAC squashes with tanh and corrects the density (above). Andrychowicz et al. found tanh squashing better.
  • Normalise observations and scale rewards. Running mean and variance for observations, clipped to [−10, 10]; rewards divided by the standard deviation of a running discounted return, also clipped. Engstrom et al. found reward scaling "can significantly affect" results, and Andrychowicz et al. say "always use observation normalization".
  • On-policy or off-policy. PPO is simple, robust to settings and parallelises over many simulators, but discards every batch after a few epochs. SAC and TD3 reuse every transition from replay many times and need far fewer environment steps, which matters when the environment is a real robot. Library defaults reflect this: Stable-Baselines3's PPO takes 2,048 steps per rollout, 10 epochs of minibatch 64, γ 0.99, λ 0.95, clip 0.2, value coefficient 0.5, gradient norm 0.5, learning rate 3 × 10−4 and no entropy bonus; its SAC uses a 106 buffer, minibatch 256, τ 0.005, automatic α with target −dim(A), and starts learning after 100 steps (SB3 PPO, SB3 SAC).
  • Report many seeds. Five seeds can disagree significantly with five others (section 10); report interquartile means with bootstrap intervals.

16Common mistakes and misconceptions

"A baseline biases the gradient."
Any baseline that depends only on the state leaves it unbiased (section 4). One that depends on the action taken would bias it.
"The value function is the best baseline."
It is the natural one and close to the best, but the variance-minimising constant is the score-weighted mean of the returns, which differs from V whenever the score vectors differ in length (the baseline widget; Greensmith et al. 2004).
"REINFORCE with a learned baseline, RLOO and GRPO are actor-critic methods."
Their baseline is computed before the action and never bootstraps from the next state, so it cannot assess the action (Sutton and Barto §13.5). They are REINFORCE-family methods.
"A critic is always an improvement."
It trades variance for bias. A critic that cannot tell states apart turns the trade into failure (the short corridor's actor-critic drifts to −44 while baselined REINFORCE finds −11.7).
"PPO's clip bounds the ratio."
It bounds only the incentive. The ratio can leave [1 − ε, 1 + ε] during several epochs (the ten-epoch animation; Engstrom et al. found PPO does not keep a ratio-based trust region), and the gradient that corrects a wrong-way move is never clipped. Watch the KL and the clip fraction.
"PPO is on-policy, so it cannot reuse data."
It reuses each batch for several epochs; the ratio corrects for the drift, which is why it is only approximately on-policy. It cannot use data from much older policies.
"PPO beats TRPO because of the clipped objective."
Engstrom et al. attribute most of the gap to code-level optimisations; TRPO with the same optimisations matched or beat PPO on their tasks.
"A3C works because its actors are asynchronous."
The synchronous A2C performed as well or better; the parallel actors help, the asynchrony does not.
"Off-policy means offline."
DDPG, TD3 and SAC interact with the environment online and learn off-policy from a replay buffer. Learning from a fixed dataset with no interaction is offline RL (Offline RL and imitation).
"Entropy bonuses are a hack."
In SAC the entropy is part of the objective being optimised, with a temperature that is itself learned toward a target entropy.
"The policy gradient needs a model of the environment."
The dynamics drop out of ∇ log p(τ) (section 2); only samples and the ability to differentiate the policy are needed.

17Where this goes next

  • Language models. PPO with a learned reward and a value model is the RLHF recipe; GRPO and RLOO delete the critic and use a group baseline; the clip, the KL and the ratio all return in token form. RL for LLMs owns that, including the 2025 to 2026 corrections to GRPO.
  • Planning. AlphaZero's network is an actor-critic (a policy head and a value head) trained by search rather than by a policy gradient: Model-based RL and planning.
  • No interaction. Offline actor-critics (CQL, IQL) and behaviour cloning: Offline RL and imitation.

Check yourself

Why does a state-dependent baseline not bias the policy gradient?
Because 𝔼a~π[∇ log π(a | s)] = ∇ Σa π(a | s) = ∇1 = 0, so b(s) times it averages to zero.
You subtract a baseline that depends on the action taken. What happens?
The zero-expectation proof needs b to be constant across actions; an action-dependent baseline breaks it and biases the gradient.
In the GAE example (δ = 0.1, 0.2, 0.2; γ = 1), what is Â0 at λ = 1?
0.1 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.5, which is the return 1 minus V(s0) = 0.5.
With ε = 0.2 and Â = −1, which ratios give zero gradient from PPO's objective?
Any r < 1 − ε = 0.8: at r = 0.7, min(−0.7, −0.8) = −0.8, the constant clipped term. At r = 1.5 the unclipped −1.5 is smaller, so the gradient pushes the probability down.
Why can DQN or SAC use a replay buffer but A2C cannot (without corrections)?
Their targets do not depend on which policy chose the stored action (Q-learning's max, SAC's soft value under the current policy). A policy gradient is an expectation under the current policy, so stale data needs importance ratios, which PPO uses for slightly stale data only.
What do TD3's three changes each fix?
Twin critics with a minimum fight overestimation; delayed actor updates let the critic's error fall before the actor climbs it; target policy smoothing stops the critic being exploited at narrow peaks.
On the short corridor, why can no ε-greedy method reach −11.66?
With identical features in every state it can only choose right with probability 1 − ε/2 or ε/2 everywhere (−44.2 or −82.1 at ε = 0.1). The optimum needs right with probability 2 − √2 ≈ 0.586, a stochastic policy.
What does SAC's temperature do when the policy's entropy is above the target?
The gradient of J(α) is ℋ(π) − ℋ̄ > 0, so gradient descent lowers α: randomness is worth less, and the policy narrows.

The short corridor, live

Sutton and Barto's Example 13.1 is the smallest problem on which a policy-gradient method beats every action-value method that uses the same features, and it is small enough to rerun their Figures 13.1 and 13.2 in your browser (Sutton and Barto, pp. 323 and 328 to 330).

Three states and a goal; every step costs −1. In the first and third states right moves right and left moves left (left in the first state bumps the wall); in the second state the actions are switched. The agent cannot tell the states apart: both actions have the same feature vector in every state, x(s, right) = [1, 0] and x(s, left) = [0, 1], so a policy is a single number, the probability p of choosing right.

How good is each policy? The exact value of the start state, J(p) = 2(2 − p) / (p(p − 1))

The curve is exact, from the Bellman equations of the three states (derivation in the box below). The dots are single learning runs on the same random numbers, one frame every 10 episodes: REINFORCE with α = 2−13, REINFORCE with a learned baseline (αθ = 2−9, αw = 2−6), the book's settings for Figure 13.2, and optionally one-step actor-critic with the same step sizes (not in the book).

Where J(p) and the optimum come from (Exercise 13.1)

Write v1, v2, v3 for the three states under "right with probability p". The Bellman equations are v1 = −1 + p v2 + (1 − p) v1; v2 = −1 + p v1 + (1 − p) v3 (switched); v3 = −1 + (1 − p) v2. Solving gives J(p) = v1 = 2(2 − p) / (p(p − 1)). Setting dJ/dp = 0 gives p* = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.5858 and J* = −(6 + 4√2) ≈ −11.657 derived. The ε-greedy policies with ε = 0.1 choose their greedy action with probability 1 − ε/2 = 0.95: J(0.95) = −44.21 and J(0.05) = −82.11, the book's "less than −44 and −82" derived.

Figures 13.1 and 13.2, rerun: total reward per episode, averaged over runs
What these defaults reproduce, and what they do not
  • Exactly, independently: the optimum p* ≈ 0.59 with value ≈ −11.6, and the ε-greedy values below −44 and −82 (Example 13.1), from the Bellman equations above.
  • In shape: Figure 13.2's result that a learned baseline (here v̂(s, w) = w, one number, as in the book) makes REINFORCE learn much faster: with 100 runs the baseline curve is within about 0.5 of J* by episodes 100 to 200, while REINFORCE at 2−13 is still near −25 there and approaches −12.6 by the end. Figure 13.1's 2−13 and 2−14 curves have the same shape as the book's (the smaller step is slower).
  • Not reproduced: in the book's Figure 13.1 the largest step, 2−12, levels off well below the others. Here, with the 0.05 floor, 2−12 learns fastest of the three; without the floor some of its runs drive p to 0 or 1, episodes become thousands of steps long and the average collapses. The book does not state its starting policy or whether probabilities were bounded, so this page uses the widely used reproduction by Zhang and Bondariev (short_corridor.py): θ starts at [−1.47, 1.47] (p = 0.05, near ε-greedy left) and the smaller probability is never allowed below 0.05. Two departures from that code: the book's step sizes (powers of two) instead of its 2e-4, 2e-5, 2e-3, and the book's order of updates in REINFORCE with baseline (δ computed before w moves).
  • New here: one-step actor-critic with the same one-number critic. Because every state looks the same to the critic, the TD error is −1 + w − w = −1 on every step but the last, which is −1 − w > 0; the last action of an episode is always right (from the third state), so the critic keeps crediting right and the policy drifts to the 0.95 floor (−44). With a critic this poor, the actor-critic's bias wins over its lower variance; a smaller actor step (2−12) delays the drift but does not stop it.

11Function approximation, and the deadly triad

Tables need one entry per state, and real problems have too many states to visit even once: Go has about 2 × 10170 legal positions ({{Tromp|https://tromp.github.io/go/legal.html}}), far more than the roughly 1080 atoms in the observable universe, and an Atari screen is 210 × 160 pixels of colour. The fix is to approximate the value function with a parameterised function, v̂(s, w) ≈ Vπ(s): a linear function of hand-made features, or a neural network. Approximation is what lets an agent generalise: an update at one state moves the estimates of similar states, including ones never visited. It is also what breaks the table guarantees of section 9, because an update aimed at one state now changes others.

w ← w + α [ Rt+1 + γ v̂(St+1, w) − v̂(St, w) ] ∇w v̂(St, w)
semi-gradient TD
the TD update for a parameterised estimate: it follows the gradient of the estimate at St but treats the target as a constant, ignoring that the target also depends on w. That is why it is only a "semi" gradient, and why it can fail.

With on-policy data and linear features, semi-gradient TD converges, to a point near the best linear fit ({{Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1997|https://doi.org/10.1109/9.580874}}). Sutton and Barto name the combination that can make it diverge: function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy training, the deadly triad. Any two are safe; all three together can send the estimates to infinity ({{Sutton and Barto §11.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=286}}). The smallest example is two states whose estimates share one weight, w and 2w ({{§11.2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=282}}). The transition from the first to the second pays 0, so its TD error is γ·2w − w = (2γ − 1)w, and each update multiplies w by 1 + α(2γ − 1): greater than 1 whenever γ > 0.5, whatever the step size. Off-policy, nothing ever corrects it; on-policy, the agent would also visit the 2w state and update it toward what follows, which pulls w back.

Starting from w = 10, as in Sutton and Barto's text. On-policy here means each visit to the first state is followed by the 2w state's own update, a transition to the end with reward 0 (its gradient is 2, its feature value). The chart's vertical axis is logarithmic in |w|.

In practice, deep Q-learning lives with the triad. When van Hasselt and colleagues ran hundreds of DQN variants on Atari, value estimates often grew past the largest value achievable in the game (100 with clipped rewards and γ = 0.99), which they call soft divergence, but never became unbounded ({{van Hasselt et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02648}}). The tricks of the next section, the replay buffer and the frozen target network, are there to keep the triad tame. Two more practical problems that tables never had: consecutive transitions are strongly correlated, which a network trained by stochastic gradient descent dislikes, and the regression target moves every time the network is updated.

12Deep value methods: DQN and its fixes

DQN (deep Q-network) is Q-learning with a convolutional network as Q(s, a; θ), two stabilisers, and one set of hyperparameters for every game ({{Mnih et al. 2015, Nature|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf}}; the 2013 workshop version used replay only, on seven games, {{arXiv 1312.5602|https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.5602}}):

L(θ) = 𝔼(s, a, r, s′) ~ buffer [ ( r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ−) − Q(s, a; θ) )² ]
buffer
the experience replay memory: the last 1 million transitions, sampled uniformly in minibatches of 32. Random sampling breaks the correlation of consecutive transitions and reuses each one in many updates; it is legitimate because the Q-learning target is off-policy.
θ−
the target network: a frozen copy of the weights, refreshed every C updates (10,000 in the paper), so the regression target stops moving while the network chases it.

The rest of the recipe: the input is the last four 84 × 84 greyscale frames (section 2's Markov fix); rewards clipped to [−1, 1] so one learning rate fits all games; γ = 0.99; ε-greedy with ε annealed from 1.0 to 0.1 over the first million frames; 50 million frames of training per game (about 38 days of play). Across 49 Atari games it outperformed the best earlier RL methods on 43 and reached more than 75% of a professional human tester's score on 29 ({{Nature letter|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf}}). The scores and what came after, through Agent57 above the human baseline on all 57 games ({{Badia et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350}}), are in {{Milestones and benchmarks|#t-miles}}.

Overestimation, and Double DQN. The max in the Q-learning target is biased upward whenever the estimates are noisy: the maximum of several noisy estimates is, on average, above the maximum of their true values, even if each estimate is unbiased (maximisation bias, {{Sutton and Barto §6.7|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=156}}). Double Q-learning decouples choosing the action from valuing it; Double DQN does it with the two networks DQN already has: y = r + γ Q(s′, argmaxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ); θ−), the online network picks, the target network values ({{van Hasselt et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461}}, which found substantial overestimation in DQN on several games).

Maximisation bias: every action is worth 0, every estimate is unbiased, and still the max is positive

The Rainbow of fixes. Hessel and colleagues combined six extensions: Double DQN; dueling heads that split Q into a state value and action advantages ({{Wang et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06581}}); prioritised replay that samples transitions with large TD error more often ({{Schaul et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05952}}); multi-step targets (section 10's dial); distributional RL that learns the whole return distribution over 51 atoms ({{Bellemare et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06887}}); and noisy nets for exploration. In their ablation, prioritised replay and multi-step returns mattered most, distributional RL next, and Double DQN and dueling made no significant aggregate difference once the rest were in ({{Hessel et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02298}}).

13Policy gradients, baselines and actor-critic

Value methods learn values and act greedily; policy-gradient methods parameterise the policy πθ(a | s) directly and climb the gradient of its expected return J(θ). That handles continuous and huge action spaces (no max over actions needed), learns stochastic policies naturally, and changes the policy smoothly. The key identity is the log-derivative trick, ∇θ π = π ∇θ log π, which turns the gradient of an expectation into an expectation you can sample, without differentiating through the environment. The result is the policy gradient theorem ({{Sutton et al. 1999|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1999/hash/464d828b85b0bed98e80ade0a5c43b0f-Abstract.html}}):

∇θ J(θ) = 𝔼πθ [ Σt ∇θ log πθ(At | St) · Ât ]
J(θ)
the expected return of the policy πθ with parameters θ
∇ log πθ(At | St)
the direction in parameter space that makes the action actually taken more likely
Ât
a weight saying how good that action turned out: the return itself, or the return minus a baseline, which is called an advantage

Raising the log-probability of actions in proportion to Ât is the whole of policy-gradient RL. What differs between methods is, again, what stands in for Ât:

  • REINFORCE ({{Williams 1992|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00992696}}) uses the sampled return Gt: unbiased, very high variance, needs episodes that end.
  • A baseline b(s) subtracted from the return leaves the gradient unbiased, because 𝔼a~π[∇ log π(a | s) b(s)] = b(s) ∇ Σa π(a | s) = b(s) ∇ 1 = 0, while it can cut the variance a lot. The natural baseline is V(s), which turns the return into the advantage A(s, a) = Q(s, a) − V(s): how much better the action did than expected ({{Sutton and Barto §13.4|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=351}}).
  • Actor-critic: learn that baseline with a critic, and use the critic's TD error δt = Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St), or GAE's λ-weighted sum of them (section 10), as the advantage. Lower variance; biased by the critic. A3C (asynchronous advantage actor-critic) ran parallel workers asynchronously on CPUs and beat the Atari state of the art in half the training time ({{Mnih et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.01783}}); A2C is its synchronous, batched version, which matched or beat A3C (the asynchrony was not what helped; see the {{Method atlas|#t-atlas}}) and is structurally what modern LLM RL loops are. A learned baseline alone does not make a method actor-critic: REINFORCE with a baseline, RLOO and GRPO never bootstrap from it, so Sutton and Barto keep them out of the family ({{§13.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=353}}).

The animation below runs the smallest possible case: one state, three actions with rewards 1, 2 and 6, a softmax policy over three logits that starts uniform, and the same sequence of random draws for both runs. Without a baseline, every sampled action has a positive reward, so every update pushes the sampled action up, right or wrong; with the exact value as baseline, an action below average is pushed down. Both are unbiased; one is much noisier. With rewards 1, 2 and 6 the noise is small enough that both learn; add an offset of 10 to every reward (which changes nothing about which action is best) and, without a baseline, 86 of 200 seeded runs lock onto a worse action within 60 updates, against none with the baseline derived.

14Trust regions: TRPO and PPO

A policy-gradient step is only trustworthy near the policy that collected the data: step too far and the new policy visits states the data never covered, the estimate is wrong, and one bad update can collapse performance. To reuse a batch for several updates you also need the importance ratio of section 9, rt(θ) = πθ(At | St) / πθold(At | St), between the policy being trained and the one that sampled. TRPO (trust region policy optimisation) made "take the largest step that is still safe" precise: maximise 𝔼[rt Ât] subject to the average KL divergence between old and new policies staying below δ, solved with conjugate gradients and a line search ({{Schulman et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05477}}). It works, at the cost of second-order machinery. PPO (proximal policy optimisation) gets the same effect first-order by clipping the ratio ({{Schulman et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347}}):

LCLIP(θ) = 𝔼t [ min( rt(θ) Ât,  clip(rt(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Ât ) ]
ε
the clip range, 0.2 in the paper's continuous-control runs (Atari started at 0.1 and annealed toward 0)
min
takes the more pessimistic of the clipped and unclipped objectives, so clipping can only remove an incentive, never add one

Once the ratio has moved past 1 + ε in the direction a positive advantage wants (or below 1 − ε for a negative one), the objective goes flat and its gradient is zero: there is no reward for moving further. That is what makes several epochs of minibatch SGD on one rollout batch safe (10 epochs in the paper's MuJoCo runs, 3 on Atari). In the paper's own ablation over seven MuJoCo tasks, no clipping or penalty scored −0.39 (normalised) against 0.82 for clipping at ε = 0.2, the best of every variant tried, including fixed and adaptive KL penalties (0.62 to 0.74; {{PPO, Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347}}). The full PPO loss adds a value-function term for the critic and an entropy bonus, with GAE advantages.

Implementation matters as much as the objective. PPO's results depend on details its paper mentions briefly or not at all: advantage normalisation, value clipping, learning-rate annealing, observation normalisation, orthogonal initialisation ({{"The 37 implementation details of PPO"|https://iclr-blog-track.github.io/2022/03/25/ppo-implementation-details/}}; {{Engstrom et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12729}}). More broadly, deep RL results vary so much across random seeds that single runs mislead ({{Henderson et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06560}}; {{Agarwal et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264}}): report many seeds and intervals.

15Continuous control: DDPG, TD3 and SAC

For a robot arm the action is a vector of torques, so Q-learning's max over actions is itself an optimisation problem. The off-policy actor-critics solve it by training an actor to output the action that maximises the critic:

  • DDPG (deep deterministic policy gradient) learns a deterministic actor μ(s) by following ∇a Q(s, a) through it, with DQN's replay and target networks ({{Lillicrap et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.02971}}). Sample-efficient, but brittle.
  • TD3 (twin delayed DDPG) found that overestimation (section 12) persists in actor-critics and fixed it with three changes: two critics and the minimum of their targets, delayed actor updates, and noise added to the target action ({{Fujimoto et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.09477}}).
  • SAC (soft actor-critic) maximises reward plus the policy's entropy, J(π) = Σt 𝔼[ r(st, at) + α H(π(· | st)) ], with a stochastic actor ({{Haarnoja et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01290}}). The entropy term keeps exploration from collapsing and smooths learning; α sets the trade (a later version tunes it automatically to a target entropy, {{Haarnoja et al. 2018b|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05905}}). Off-policy with replay, it became a common default for continuous control and robotics.

The trade-off among the families shows here most clearly. PPO is robust and simple and handles any action space, but it is on-policy, so every batch is used for a few epochs and discarded; SAC and TD3 reuse every transition many times from replay and need far fewer environment steps, which matters when the environment is a real robot.

16Models and search: Dyna, AlphaZero, MuZero, world models

Everything since section 9 has been model-free. Model-based RL learns or is given a model of the environment and uses it to plan, which can save enormous amounts of real experience: every imagined transition is free. Dyna ({{Sutton 1991; Sutton and Barto §8.2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=183}}) is the simplest form: learn a model from real transitions, then run extra Q-learning updates on transitions the model imagines. The catch is that errors in a learned model compound over a plan: a small error per step becomes a large one fifty steps out, and a planner happily exploits the model's mistakes. So model-based methods tend to plan short, re-plan often, or plan in a learned space that only needs to predict what matters.

Search as policy improvement. When the model is exact (the rules of a board game), the best planner is a tree search guided by learned networks. Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) runs many simulated games from the current position, choosing moves in the tree by a mix of the network's prior, the values found so far and an exploration bonus (PUCT, a UCB descendant from section 7), and returns visit counts that are a better policy than the network's own prior. That turns search into the improvement step of generalised policy iteration (section 8): the search's move distribution is the policy target, the game's outcome the value target.

ℓ = (z − v)² − π⊤ log p + c‖θ‖²
fθ(s) = (p, v)
one network giving move probabilities p and a value v
z
the game's final outcome; π the search's visit-count distribution; c a weight-decay coefficient (AlphaZero's loss, {{Silver et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815}})
  • AlphaGo (2016) trained on human games first, then improved by self-play, and searched with policy and value networks ({{Nature 2016|https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16961}}). AlphaGo Zero (2017) dropped the human data ({{Nature 2017|https://www.nature.com/articles/nature24270}}).
  • AlphaZero generalised it: given no domain knowledge except the rules, it reached superhuman play in chess, shogi and Go within 24 hours of training and defeated a world-champion program in each ({{Silver et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815}}).
  • MuZero dropped the rules: it learns a model that, applied in a latent space, predicts only what planning needs (the reward, the policy and the value), never the observations. It matched AlphaZero in Go, chess and shogi without knowing the rules and set a new state of the art on 57 Atari games ({{Schrittwieser et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.08265}}).
  • World models learn to imagine. Ha and Schmidhuber trained a policy inside a learned generative model's "dream" ({{2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10122}}); DreamerV3 learns a world model and improves its behaviour on imagined trajectories, outperformed specialised methods across over 150 tasks with a single configuration, and was the first algorithm to collect diamonds in Minecraft from scratch without human data or curricula ({{Hafner et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04104}}).

"Model-based" here means the agent predicts transitions and plans with them. It does not mean "learned model": dynamic programming and AlphaZero are model-based with a given model, and of the methods on this page only MuZero and the world-model agents learn one. The milestones these systems hit are in {{Milestones and benchmarks|#t-miles}}.

17Learning without interacting: imitation and offline RL

Sometimes the agent cannot explore: a hospital cannot try random treatments, and a self-driving car cannot crash to learn. Two families learn from a fixed dataset instead.

  • Imitation learning. Behaviour cloning is supervised learning on expert (state, action) pairs. Its flaw is compounding error: a small mistake takes the agent to a state the expert never visited, where it makes bigger mistakes, so errors grow with the square of the horizon rather than linearly. DAgger fixes it by letting the learner act and asking the expert to label the states the learner actually reaches ({{Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011|https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686}}). Inverse RL infers the reward the expert seems to optimise and then runs RL on it; GAIL does this adversarially ({{Ho and Ermon 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03476}}). Supervised fine-tuning of a language model on demonstrations is behaviour cloning.
  • Offline (batch) RL learns a policy better than the one that collected a fixed dataset, with no further interaction ({{Levine et al. 2020, tutorial|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01643}}). Its central problem is distribution shift: the max in a Q-learning target queries actions the data never tried, whose values are pure extrapolation, and the policy is drawn to exactly those overestimated actions. Offline methods therefore stay close to the data: Conservative Q-learning pushes down the values of unseen actions ({{Kumar et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04779}}); Implicit Q-learning never evaluates actions outside the dataset ({{Kostrikov et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06169}}); Decision Transformer treats RL as sequence modelling, conditioning on the return it wants ({{Chen et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01345}}).

"On-policy means online, off-policy means offline" is a common confusion. On- and off-policy say which policy the values describe; online and offline say whether the agent still interacts. Q-learning and DQN are off-policy while interacting online; PPO reuses each batch for several epochs and is still approximately on-policy. DPO, which trains a language model directly on a fixed set of preference pairs (section 18), is the best-known offline method in LLM post-training. The {{Taxonomy|#t-tax}} tab sets on-policy, off-policy and offline side by side.

3The eight pages on the three axes that matter most

Three questions place any method: what it stores (values, a policy, or both: value-based, policy-based, actor-critic), whether it uses a model of the environment (given, learned, or none), and whose experience it learns from (on-policy, off-policy, offline). 4Each family: the definition, and what only shows across pages

One short paragraph each; the mechanisms, derivations and worked examples are on the page named in "Go deeper", by section.

Exploration: bandits

Strip away states and delay and one problem is left: a row of slot machines with unknown payouts, the multi-armed bandit, where the only question is when to try an action whose value is uncertain at the cost of reward now. Greedy locks onto the first decent arm; ε-greedy explores at random, UCB where it is uncertain, Thompson sampling in proportion to the chance an arm is best; regret, the reward lost against always pulling the best arm, compares them. Across the pages the same question returns at every layer: ε-greedy in DQN, entropy bonuses in policy gradients and SAC, several answers per prompt at a temperature around 1 in language-model RL.

Go deeper: {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}: the strategies (section 3), regret and its floor (section 4), contextual bandits and A/B tests (section 5), exploration in full RL (section 6) and in LLM training (section 7).

Planning with a known model: dynamic programming

If every transition probability and reward is known, the expectation can be computed. Dynamic programming turns the Bellman equation into an update and sweeps it over every state: policy iteration alternates evaluating the policy with making it greedy on those values; value iteration applies the optimality equation directly. Each sweep shrinks the error by at least γ, so it always converges, but each sweep touches every state, and ten variables with ten values each are already ten billion states. So DP is the ideal every other page approximates, and its rhythm, evaluate a little and improve a little (generalised policy iteration), is the shape of almost every RL algorithm up to PPO for language models.

Go deeper: {{Dynamic programming: planning with a known model|n:3c65c17b0d0d81baada7e1c05ce69d01}}: policy iteration and value iteration (sections 6 and 7), why it converges (section 9), the curse of dimensionality (section 11), asynchronous DP (section 12).

Learning from samples: Monte Carlo, TD, SARSA, Q-learning

Without a model the agent replaces the expectation with experience: Monte Carlo waits for the actual return (honest, noisy), temporal-difference learning ({{Sutton 1988|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00115009}}) updates after every step toward one real reward plus its own guess (less noisy, only as good as its guesses). For control it learns Q: SARSA bootstraps from the action it actually takes next and so learns the value of its own exploring behaviour (on-policy); Q-learning ({{Watkins and Dayan 1992|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00992698}}) bootstraps from the best next action and so learns the greedy policy while exploring (off-policy), which is what later lets DQN learn from replayed old experience. Learning from another policy's data in general needs importance sampling, reweighting each sample by how much likelier the target policy was to take that action; PPO's and GRPO's ratios are this correction.

Go deeper: {{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}}: Monte Carlo against TD (section 1), the n-step and TD(λ) dial (section 2), SARSA against Q-learning on cliff walking (section 4), maximisation bias (section 5), importance sampling (section 6), function approximation and the deadly triad (section 7).

Deep value methods: DQN and its fixes

Tables need an entry per state; an Atari screen has astronomically many. Replace the table with a network and the agent generalises across states, but function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy learning together, Sutton and Barto's deadly triad ({{§11.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=286}}), can make values diverge. DQN is Q-learning with a convolutional network and two stabilisers, an experience replay buffer and a frozen target network ({{Mnih et al. 2015|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf}}); each later fix targets one known flaw (Double DQN the max's overestimation), and Rainbow combined six ({{Hessel et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02298}}).

Go deeper: {{Value-based deep RL: from DQN to Rainbow|n:3ee5c17b0d0d818689b2c0dbce434ece}}: how often the triad bites on Atari (section 2), DQN (section 3), Double DQN (section 4), Rainbow's ablation (section 10), reading Atari numbers (section 12). The triad itself, with Baird's counterexample, is on Model-free, section 7.

Policy gradients and actor-critic, up to PPO

Instead of learning values and acting greedily, learn the policy itself: make the actions that turned out well more likely, in proportion to how well they turned out, the policy gradient theorem ({{Sutton et al. 1999|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1999/hash/464d828b85b0bed98e80ade0a5c43b0f-Abstract.html}}). The methods differ in that weight: REINFORCE ({{Williams 1992|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00992696}}) uses the whole return, a baseline turns it into an advantage ("better or worse than expected"), an actor-critic learns that baseline and bootstraps from it, GAE blends the two with λ (the same dial as section 2). And in how far one batch may move the policy: TRPO with a KL constraint, PPO by clipping the probability ratio ({{Schulman et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347}}). For continuous control with expensive samples, off-policy actor-critics with replay (DDPG, TD3, SAC with its entropy bonus) win on data.

Go deeper: {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}: the theorem (section 2), REINFORCE and baselines (sections 3 and 4), actor-critic and GAE (sections 5 and 6), TRPO and PPO (sections 8 to 10), DDPG, TD3 and SAC (sections 11 to 13), which method when (section 15).

Model-based RL and search

A model lets the agent rehearse: every imagined step is free experience. When the model is exact, as in a board game, Monte Carlo tree search guided by a policy and a value network returns a better move distribution than the network alone, so the search acts as the improvement step and the network learns to imitate it (AlphaZero); MuZero learns a model of only what planning needs (reward, policy and value, never the screen); Dreamer learns a world model and trains its actor in imagination. The cost that only shows across pages: a learned model's errors compound over a plan, and a planner happily exploits them, the same failure as offline RL's (an optimiser trusting values where it has no data).

Go deeper: {{Model-based RL and planning|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8116be5fd39e10bd94c5}}: Dyna (section 2), MCTS (section 5), AlphaGo to MuZero (sections 6 to 8), learned and video world models (sections 10 and 11), why model error compounds (section 13), samples against compute (section 14).

Offline RL and imitation

Sometimes the agent may not explore: a hospital cannot try random treatments. A learner on a fixed dataset fails where it leaves the data. Behaviour cloning copies expert actions by supervised learning (supervised fine-tuning of a language model is exactly this) and drifts into states the expert never visited, where it errs more; DAgger asks the expert to label the states the learner actually reaches. Offline RL tries to beat the policy that collected the data, and its max picks actions the data never tried, whose values are pure guesswork, so its methods stay close to the data ({{Levine et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01643}}).

Go deeper: {{Offline RL and imitation|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81d4995ec47e428de9b3}}: behaviour cloning and DAgger (sections 2 and 3), inverse RL and GAIL (section 4), preferences (section 5), extrapolation error (section 6), BCQ, CQL and IQL (sections 7 to 9), Decision Transformer (section 10), evaluation (section 12).

RL for language models

Generating text is an MDP with odd proportions: the state is the prompt plus the tokens so far, the action is the next token from a vocabulary of about 105, the transition is trivial (append the token), and there is usually one reward at the end; the hard parts are credit assignment across thousands of tokens and the reward itself. Read as a lineage, the methods are two deletions. RLHF learns a reward model from people's comparisons and optimises it with PPO, a critic and a KL penalty to the starting model ({{Ouyang et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}}); GRPO deletes the critic, using the mean of a group of answers to the same prompt as the baseline ({{DeepSeekMath|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300}}), the Monte Carlo end of section 2's dial (a group in which every answer scores the same teaches nothing); RLVR deletes the learned reward model where a program can check the answer (the name is Tülu 3's, {{Lambert et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}), the recipe of DeepSeek-R1 ({{Guo et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948}}). Any reward the policy optimises is a proxy it can exploit, learned or programmatic. DPO skips both the reward model and the RL loop by training directly on preference pairs, offline ({{DPO page|n:3c65c17b0d0d818bb1d8cafd30e20f9e}}).

Go deeper: {{RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}: the token MDP (section 1), PPO for RLHF (section 2), GRPO and RLOO (section 3), RLVR (section 5), R1 (section 6), the fixes to GRPO (section 7), pass@1 against pass@k (section 9). Around it: {{Alignment|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}} (the pipeline), {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}} (what goes wrong), and the paper pages {{InstructGPT|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}, {{DeepSeekMath|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} and {{DeepSeek-R1|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}.

Ten confusions that cross the pages
  • "Reward and value are the same." Reward is one step's signal; value is the expected sum of what follows.
  • "The observation is the state." One frame, or the last chat message, can leave out what the future depends on.
  • "γ = 1 is always wrong." It is standard when every episode ends, as in language-model RL.
  • "Model-based means a learned model." Dynamic programming and AlphaZero use a given one.
  • "The reward model makes RLHF model-based." Nothing plans with it; it stands in for the reward.
  • "On-policy means online, off-policy means offline." Q-learning is off-policy while interacting; PPO reuses each batch for several epochs and is still approximately on-policy.
  • "Any learned baseline makes an actor-critic." REINFORCE with a baseline, RLOO and GRPO never bootstrap from it, so they are policy-based ({{Sutton and Barto §13.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=353}}).
  • "Deep RL is a different theory." Its targets are the classical ones; what is new is function approximation and the tricks that keep it stable.
  • "Token generation is a hard environment." The transition is trivial; credit assignment and a trustworthy reward are the hard parts.
  • "A higher reward means a better model." With a proxy reward, the policy can climb it while true quality falls (reward hacking).

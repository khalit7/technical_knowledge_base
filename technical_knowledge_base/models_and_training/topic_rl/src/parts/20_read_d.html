18RL for language models: RLHF, GRPO and RLVR

Token generation is an MDP with unusual proportions. The state is the prompt plus the tokens generated so far (fully observable, section 2); the action is the next token, from a vocabulary of about 105; the transition is deterministic and known (append the token); the episode ends at an end-of-sequence token; and there is usually one reward, at the end, for the whole response, with γ = 1. So the environment model is trivial, and the hard parts are two: credit assignment (which of thousands of tokens earned the final score?) and the reward itself (where does a trustworthy score for "a good answer" come from?). Rollouts, sampling whole responses from a large model, dominate the cost.

RLHF: a learned reward and PPO

Reinforcement learning from human feedback learns the reward from people's comparisons, then optimises the policy against it. It began with simulated robots and Atari learning from human preferences between clips, optimised there with A2C and TRPO ({{Christiano et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741}}); PPO arrived with fine-tuning language models from preferences ({{Ziegler et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08593}}); then it moved to summarisation ({{Stiennon et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325}}), and became the standard recipe with InstructGPT ({{Ouyang et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}}; verified on the {{InstructGPT page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}):

  1. Supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations (behaviour cloning, section 17).
  2. A reward model (6B parameters) trained on rankings of 4 to 9 answers per prompt with a Bradley-Terry loss, −log σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)) for every preferred-rejected pair.
  3. PPO (section 14) against that reward, with a per-token KL penalty to the frozen SFT model: the per-token reward is rt = 1[t = T] rφ(x, y) − β log(πθ(yt) / πref(yt)), β = 0.02, plus a pretraining-gradient mix ("PPO-ptx") that limits regressions on public benchmarks.

The headline: outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT model were preferred to those of 175B GPT-3, a comparison made through a common baseline (win rates of 52.7% and 24.0% against 175B SFT), and 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 85 ± 3% of the time. The RL stage cost 60 petaflop/s-days against 3,640 for pretraining GPT-3, about 1.6%. PPO for RLHF keeps four models in memory: the policy, the frozen reference, the reward model and the value model (the critic), which is usually as large as the policy. The KL penalty does three jobs at once: it keeps the policy on the distribution where the reward model is accurate, it preserves what pretraining taught, and it acts as a trust region.

Corrections carried from the paper page
InstructGPT is "about 1.4 to 1.5 times as truthful" by human labels, not "twice"; it is 16 to 20% less toxic at 175B when asked to be respectful, not "25%", and no less biased on Winogender or CrowS-Pairs ({{InstructGPT page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}).

GRPO: delete the critic

A per-token critic for a language model is as large as the policy and hard to fit from one end-of-sequence reward. GRPO (group relative policy optimisation, {{DeepSeekMath, Shao et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300}}) deletes it: sample a group of G responses to the same prompt, score each, and use the group's own statistics as the baseline. Every token of response i gets the same advantage:

Âi = ( ri − mean(r1, ..., rG) ) / std(r1, ..., rG)
J(θ) = 𝔼 [ (1/G) Σi (1/|oi|) Σt { min( ρi,t Âi, clip(ρi,t, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Âi ) − β DKL[πθ ‖ πref] } ]
oi
the i-th response, |oi| its length in tokens; ρi,t the per-token importance ratio (section 14)
DKL
moved from the reward into the loss and estimated per token with the "k3" estimator x − log x − 1, x = πref/πθ

It is a Monte Carlo return (section 9) with a sampled baseline (section 13): the group mean plays the role V(s) plays in an actor-critic. DeepSeekMath's recipe used G = 64, β = 0.04, learning rate 10−6 and a single update per batch (so the clip barely binds), and lifted its 7B instruct model from 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH and 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K. GRPO is close to REINFORCE with a group baseline, a family that also includes RLOO, whose baseline for each response is the mean of the other responses (the estimator of {{Kool et al. 2019|https://openreview.net/forum?id=r1lgTGL5DE}}; {{Ahmadian et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740}}, which found such REINFORCE-style methods matching or beating PPO for RLHF).

Corrections carried from the DeepSeekMath page
The paper never trains PPO: GRPO's memory saving is asserted, not measured, and the first head-to-head is in DeepSeek-R1's supplementary material. And differentiating k3 in the loss gives, in expectation, the gradient of KL(πref ‖ πθ), not of KL(πθ ‖ πref) ({{DeepSeekMath page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}}).
One GRPO group, step by step

Click a response to flip its score. Response lengths are illustrative; scores are a verifier's pass (1) or fail (0). Dr. GRPO removes the division by the standard deviation and replaces the 1/|oi| per-response length normalisation by a constant (here the longest length); RLOO subtracts the mean of the other responses. The 4-response case is the old page's worked example (+1.732, −0.577); 1 of 16 against 8 of 16 is Dr. GRPO's "difficulty bias" (+3.873 against +1.000).

Two biases in GRPO's normalisation were pointed out in 2025 ({{Dr. GRPO, Liu et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783}}). Dividing by |oi| gives each token of a long wrong answer a smaller penalty than each token of a short one, so wrong answers drift longer (length bias); dividing by the group's standard deviation gives the lone correct answer in a hard group (1 of 16) nearly four times the weight of a correct answer in a medium one (8 of 16) (difficulty bias). DAPO ({{Yu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476}}) added an asymmetric clip (0.2 below, 0.28 above, "clip-higher", so rare tokens can grow), dynamic sampling that drops groups whose responses all score the same (they carry zero advantage and teach nothing), a token-level loss and a penalty for overlong answers, and reached 50 on AIME 2024 from the Qwen2.5-32B base with half the training steps of DeepSeek's 47. GSPO ({{Zheng et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18071}}) moved the ratio and the clip from tokens to whole sequences. And because rollouts come from an inference engine whose probabilities differ slightly from the trainer's, LLM RL is mildly off-policy (section 9); current frameworks correct it with truncated importance weights.

RLVR: delete the learned reward

Where answers can be checked by a program (a maths answer matches, code compiles and passes tests), the reward model can be replaced by a verifier: reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards. The name comes from Tülu 3 (November 2024, optimised there with PPO and a value model; {{Lambert et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}). It removes reward-model error and the hacking it invites, and it is limited to checkable tasks. Read as a lineage, the three are two deletions: GRPO deletes RLHF's critic, and RLVR deletes its learned reward model.

DeepSeek-R1 ({{Guo et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948}}; verified on the {{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}) made GRPO plus RLVR the default open recipe. R1-Zero ran it directly on the DeepSeek-V3 base with no supervised step: rule-based accuracy and format rewards only, no neural reward model "because of reward hacking". Its AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% in the paper's Nature version (71.0% in the January 2025 preprint), while answers grew from about 500 to about 14,200 tokens. R1 added a cold-start SFT stage, reasoning RL, rejection sampling into about 800K new SFT samples, and a final RL stage that used preference reward models only in its last 400 steps. Its RL and data generation cost 147K H800 GPU-hours (about $294K at $2 per hour) on top of the base model's 2.788M hours: a post-training bill, not "the cost of R1" (the {{Milestones and benchmarks|#t-miles}} tab keeps the dated figures side by side). Distilling R1's outputs into Qwen2.5-32B by plain SFT reached 72.6 on AIME, against 47.0 for R1-Zero-style RL on the same base.

Corrections carried from the R1 page
R1-Zero's AIME figure depends on the paper version: 71.0% in v1, 77.9% in v2 and Nature; do not mix them. The "aha moment" was not created by RL: the base model already produces reflective words, and RL changed how often and how long ({{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}).

Reward hacking and the limits of a proxy

Every RL method optimises the reward it is given, not the one intended (section 1's reward hypothesis). Classic RL has a long list of examples ({{DeepMind's specification-gaming list|https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/specification-gaming-the-flip-side-of-ai-ingenuity/}}); with language models the proxy is a reward model or a test suite, and the policy finds length, flattery, format tricks and special-cased tests. Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured it: as the policy moves away from its start (measured as √KL), the proxy reward keeps rising while the true ("gold") reward rises, peaks and falls; bigger reward models over-optimise later ({{Gao et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760}}). The KL penalty is the standard leash; the training topic animates it ({{Topic: llm-training-and-post-training, axis 4|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}}), and {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}} catalogues measured cases and defences. Verifiers help but can be hacked too (a test that can be bypassed is a reward model with a bug).

Does RL teach or elicit? RLVR reliably turns pass@k into pass@1: it makes the model's best answers its usual answers. Whether it adds abilities the base model lacked is contested: Yue et al. found that at large k base models solve more problems than their RLVR-trained versions ({{2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837}}), and DeepSeekMath already saw RL raise majority-vote accuracy but not pass@64. Other open threads are entropy collapse during RL, process rewards that score steps rather than outcomes, and agentic RL over many tool-using turns, where environment design and exact token accounting matter as much as the algorithm (Mercor's 397B-parameter run with SkyRL reports this reported; see {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}).

DPO, and where RL stops. Direct preference optimisation skips both the reward model and the RL loop: it rewrites the RLHF objective so that the policy itself is trained on preference pairs with a classification-style loss, −log σ(β log[πθ(yw)/πref(yw)] − β log[πθ(yl)/πref(yl)]) ({{Rafailov et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290}}; verified on the {{DPO page|n:3c65c17b0d0d818bb1d8cafd30e20f9e}}). It is offline (section 17) and much cheaper; well-tuned online RL has generally matched or beaten it since. Constitutional AI replaces human harmlessness labels with AI feedback (RLAIF; {{Constitutional AI page|n:3c65c17b0d0d815a9b31e9443961c700}}). The whole pipeline around the RL step, SFT, reward models and the DPO family, is on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}; how PPO, GRPO and DPO compare in memory and cost on one batch is on {{Topic: llm-training-and-post-training|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}}.

Four more trade-offs that cut across the table. Value-based, policy-based or actor-critic: value-based methods are sample-efficient with discrete actions and can learn off-policy from replay, but acting needs a max over actions; policy-based methods handle any action space and stochastic policies, with high-variance gradients; actor-critic buys lower variance with a learned critic and costs a second model (in PPO-based RLHF as large as the policy, which is exactly what GRPO deletes). Model-free or model-based: model-free methods need no model but learn only about the states they visit and usually need many samples; model-based methods can plan and reuse the model, but a learned model's errors compound over a plan. Monte Carlo or TD: unbiased, high-variance and episode-bound against biased, low-variance and online. On- or off-policy: simpler and more stable but data-hungry, against data reuse at the price of corrections and instability.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Reward and value are the same thing."
The reward is the one-step signal; the return is the discounted sum of rewards along one trajectory; the value is the expected return. A state with a negative reward can have a high value (Class 2 in the student MRP at γ = 1: reward −2, value 1.5).
"The observation is the state."
A single Atari frame, or the last message in a chat, can leave out what the future depends on. Markov is a property of the representation you choose (section 2).
"The history includes the action being chosen."
Ht ends at Ot, Rt, and the reward for At is Rt+1.
"γ = 1 is always wrong."
It is standard when every episode terminates, as in LLM RL. It is only a problem for continuing tasks, where returns can be infinite.
"A greedy policy can be read off V."
Greedy on V needs a one-step lookahead, so a model; greedy on Q does not. A model-free agent that stores only V cannot pick actions from it.
"The optimality equation can be solved by matrix inversion."
Matrix inversion solves the linear expectation equation for a fixed policy; the optimality equation's max makes it nonlinear, so it needs iteration.
"Model-based means a learned model."
Dynamic programming and AlphaZero are model-based with a given model; of the methods here, only MuZero and the world-model agents learn one.
"The reward model makes RLHF model-based."
It replaces the reward signal and nothing plans with it, so PPO-based RLHF is model-free. Model-based means predicting transitions and planning with them.
"On-policy means online, off-policy means offline."
On- and off-policy say which policy the values describe; online and offline say whether the agent interacts. Q-learning and DQN are off-policy while interacting online; PPO reuses each batch for several epochs and is still approximately on-policy.
"Q-learning is off-policy, so it needs importance sampling."
One-step Q-learning needs no ratio: its target takes the max over next actions instead of using the action the behaviour policy chose. Multi-step off-policy targets do need corrections.
"A baseline biases the policy gradient."
Any baseline that depends only on the state leaves the gradient unbiased, because the expected score function is zero; it only changes the variance (section 13).
"PPO's clip bounds the ratio."
It removes the incentive to move further; a large step can still overshoot 1 + ε (the animation in section 14 does it). Watch the KL and the fraction of clipped samples.
"GRPO has no value estimate, so it is something new."
It is a policy-gradient method whose baseline is sampled rather than learned: the group mean plays the role V(s) plays in an actor-critic. With one update per batch it is REINFORCE with a group baseline.
"Deep RL is a different theory."
Its targets are the classical ones: the Q-learning target in DQN, TD errors in PPO's critic, Monte Carlo returns in REINFORCE and GRPO. What is new is function approximation and the tricks that keep it stable.
"Token generation is a hard environment."
The transition is trivial (append the token). What is hard is assigning the credit for one end-of-sequence reward across thousands of tokens, and making the reward itself trustworthy.
"A higher reward means a better model."
When the reward is a learned proxy, the policy can keep climbing it while the true objective stalls or degrades (reward hacking), which is why RLHF keeps a KL penalty to a reference model and why RLVR prefers verifiers.
"Any goal can be written as a reward."
That is the reward hypothesis, an assumption rather than a theorem. When the scalar reward is a proxy for an intent with no mathematical definition ("helpful", "honest"), maximising it can defeat the intent (section 1).
"A pass@1 gain means a new capability."
It can be the model's best answers becoming its usual ones; compare pass@k at large k before claiming new abilities (section 18).
"One seed shows it works."
Deep RL results vary widely across seeds and implementation details; report many runs with intervals (section 14).

Open problems

  • Sample efficiency. Model-free deep RL needs millions of steps for tasks a person learns in minutes; model-based methods and better reuse of data (replay, offline RL) are the main answers, and none is general yet.
  • Exploration with sparse rewards. Section 7's strategies explore locally; deep exploration over long horizons, and exploration beyond what a pretrained model already does, remain open.
  • Reward specification. The reward hypothesis holds only as well as the reward is written: reward hacking scales with optimisation pressure ({{Gao et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760}}), and verifiers exist only for checkable tasks. Scoring open-ended work (rubrics, generative verifiers, AI feedback) is a main research front.
  • Credit assignment over long horizons. One reward at the end of a thousands-token answer, or of a hundred-turn agent episode, says little about which step mattered; process rewards and learned critics for LLMs are unresolved.
  • Generalisation. Agents overfit their training environments; procedurally generated benchmarks showed how far ({{Cobbe et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01588}}), and sim-to-real transfer in robotics is the practical version.
  • Stability and evaluation. The deadly triad has no general cure, and results vary across seeds; honest evaluation with many runs is still not the norm ({{Agarwal et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264}}).
  • What RL adds to language models. Whether RLVR teaches new reasoning or sharpens what pretraining put there, and how far it scales, is the field's most debated question in 2026 ({{Yue et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837}}; {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}).

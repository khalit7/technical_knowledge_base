5Decision-time planning: rollouts and Monte Carlo tree search

Rollout algorithms ({{§8.10|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=205}}) estimate each action's value at the current state by averaging the returns of simulated episodes that start with that action and then follow a fixed rollout policy, act on the best, and repeat. By the policy improvement theorem ({{Dynamic programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81baada7e1c05ce69d01}}), acting greedily on qπ(s, ·) is at least as good as π, so a rollout algorithm is one step of policy iteration at one state; its aim "is to improve upon the rollout policy; not to find an optimal policy".

Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) is a rollout algorithm that remembers: it keeps value estimates for the state-action pairs near the root in a tree it grows, so later simulations follow the beginnings of earlier good ones. Its ideas are from 2006: Coulom's backup operators in Crazy Stone ({{Coulom 2006|https://www.remi-coulom.fr/CG2006/CG2006.pdf}}; the survey credits him with the name) and Kocsis and Szepesvári's UCT ({{2006|http://ggp.stanford.edu/readings/uct.pdf}}). MCTS "is largely responsible for the improvement in computer Go from a weak amateur level in 2005 to a grandmaster level (6 dan or more) in 2015" ({{§8.11|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=207}}). Each iteration has four steps, which the animation below walks through: selection down the tree by a tree policy; expansion of a new node; simulation of a complete episode with the rollout policy (or, since AlphaGo Zero, evaluation of the leaf by a network); backup of the result into every edge on the path. When time runs out the agent plays the most visited root action, which is robust to a value inflated by a few lucky simulations.

The tree policy is a bandit at every node. UCT applies UCB1 (derived on {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}) to each node; PUCT, the AlphaGo family's rule, adds a network's prior.

UCT:  a = argmaxa [ Q(s, a) + c √( ln N(s) / N(s, a) ) ]     PUCT:  a = argmaxa [ Q(s, a) + c P(s, a) √N(s) / (1 + N(s, a)) ]
Q(s, a)
mean result of the simulations through that edge, for the player to move at s
N(s), N(s, a)
visits to the node and to the edge
c
exploration constant (UCB1's √2 for rewards in [0, 1])
P(s, a)
a policy network's prior; with a value network scoring leaves, no rollouts are needed

The animation runs both rules from one tic-tac-toe position, 30 simulations each. O is to move and X threatens to complete the middle row: the only move that does not lose is the right-hand middle cell. Predict: will plain UCT with random playouts find the block in 30 simulations?

Even the untrained network gets there (12 of 30), because tic-tac-toe is short and a few simulations reach real game ends, whose values are exact. The root's visit counts, normalised, are the search policy π; in AlphaGo Zero they become the training target for the network's prior (§7).

Heuristic search, and rollouts in backgammon (1 min)

Heuristic search, the classical AI planner, builds a tree of continuations from the current state, applies an approximate value function at the leaves and backs values up to the root with max backups, then picks the best root action and throws the rest away ({{§8.9|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=203}}). With a perfect model and an imperfect value function, deeper search usually gives better moves; Tesauro found TD-Gammon played better the deeper it searched, at the price of time per move. A search tree is just many one-step backups ordered from the leaves up, so its strength comes from "the focus and concentration of updates on states and actions immediately downstream from the current state".

The name "rollout" comes from backgammon, where rolling a position out with random dice estimates its equity. Tesauro and Galperin ran rollouts on IBM supercomputers over base players from random to TD-Gammon and reported error rates reduced "by as much as a factor of 5 or more"; "potentially 80% or more of the base player's equity loss can be eliminated" ({{NIPS 1996|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1996/file/996009f2374006606f4c0b0fda878af1-Paper.pdf}}). In MCTS, expansion adds one or more children on some iterations, and nothing is stored for the rollout beyond the tree.

UCT's constant is 2Cp with Cp = 1/√2 for rewards in [0, 1], which gives UCB1's √2; Kocsis and Szepesvári proved the failure probability at the root goes to zero ("consistent").

6AlphaGo (2016): networks inside the search

Go's rules are a perfect model, but the tree is too wide and deep for search alone; the strongest programs were MCTS with hand-made rollout policies, such as Pachi, ranked 2 amateur dan on KGS. AlphaGo ({{Silver et al., Nature 529, 2016|https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16961}}; {{free PDF|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf}}) put four learned functions into MCTS. Toggle to see what AlphaGo Zero changed: the same search, fed by one network and feeding it back.

AlphaGo searches to play; AlphaZero searches to learn

The SL policy gives the prior of a PUCT rule (cpuct = 5); each leaf is scored as V(sL) = (1 − λ) vθ(sL) + λ zL with λ = 0.5, where zL is one fast rollout's result. The mix won at least 95% of games against value-only (λ = 0) and rollout-only (λ = 1) variants; even λ = 0 beat every other Go program. Two findings are counter-intuitive: the weaker SL policy made the better prior, "presumably because humans select a diverse beam of promising moves, whereas RL optimizes for the single best move"; but the value network trained on the stronger RL policy's games made the better evaluator. AlphaGo won 494 of 495 games against other programs, beat the European champion Fan Hui 5-0 in October 2015, and a larger version, AlphaGo Lee on 48 TPUs, beat Lee Sedol 4-1 in March 2016 (results and compute on the root's {{Milestones and benchmarks tab of Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}}; the whole story is in {{Sutton and Barto §16.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=463}}).

The four networks in numbers, and the match hardware (1 min)
  • SL policy network pσ: 13 layers, trained on 30 million positions from the KGS Go Server to predict the expert's move; 57.0% accurate with all input features (55.7% from the raw board and history), against 44.4% for the previous best.
  • Rollout policy pπ: a linear softmax over small patterns; 24.2% accurate but "using just 2 μs to select an action, rather than 3 ms for the policy network"; about 1,000 full-game rollouts per second per search thread.
  • RL policy network pρ: the SL network improved by policy-gradient self-play (50 GPUs, one day). It won "more than 80%" of games against the SL network and, with no search, 85% against Pachi, which does 100,000 simulations per move.
  • Value network vθ: predicts the winner (15 layers, 50 GPUs for one week). Trained on full human games it memorised outcomes (test MSE 0.37 against 0.19 on training data), so the team generated 30 million positions "each sampled from a separate game" of RL-policy self-play: MSE 0.226 train, 0.234 test.

The match program ran 40 search threads on 48 CPUs and 8 GPUs; the distributed version used 1,202 CPUs and 176 GPUs.

7AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero: search as the policy improvement operator

AlphaGo Zero ({{Silver et al., Nature 550, 2017; author copy|https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10045895/1/agz_unformatted_nature.pdf}}) used no human data, only the stones as input, one network fθ(s) = (p, v) and no rollouts. Its loop is policy iteration with MCTS as the improvement step: "MCTS may therefore be viewed as a powerful policy improvement operator", and self-play with search, scored by the winner, "a powerful policy evaluation operator".

A worked loss (illustrative numbers). The player went on to win (z = 1), the network predicted v = 0.6, search visited three moves in proportions π = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) and the network gave them p = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2). Value term (1 − 0.6)² = 0.16; policy term −(0.7 ln 0.5 + 0.2 ln 0.3 + 0.1 ln 0.2) = 0.4852 + 0.2408 + 0.1609 = 0.887; loss before weight decay 1.047. For a softmax output the gradient on the logits is p − π = (−0.2, 0.1, 0.1): the update raises the move search liked. In general:

ℓ = (z − v)² − π⊤ log p + c‖θ‖²
z
the game's result from the player to move's view: +1, 0 (AlphaZero, which allows draws), −1
v, p
the network's value and move probabilities
π
the search's visit distribution at that position
c
L2 weight decay, 10−4; the two loss terms are weighted equally

The 3-day run passed AlphaGo Lee after 36 hours and beat it 100-0 after 72 hours. The 40-day run, 29 million games, beat AlphaGo Master 89-11 and reached an Elo of 5,185. The same network with no search rated 3,055: search was worth about 2,100 Elo on top of the network that learned from it.

The details that made it work, and the 3-day run's numbers (1 min)

AlphaGo Zero Methods: 1,600 simulations per move, about 0.4 s; moves sampled in proportion to visit counts for the first 30 moves (temperature τ = 1), then the most visited; Dirichlet noise at the root, P = 0.75 p + 0.25 η with η ∼ Dir(0.03), so every move can be tried "but the search may still overrule bad moves"; resignation, with the threshold set so fewer than 5% of resigned games could have been won (checked by playing out 10% of them); and an evaluator gate: a new network replaced the self-play player only if it won more than 55% of 400 games. The 3-day run generated 4.9 million games, updated on 700,000 mini-batches of 2,048 positions with a 20-block residual network, and played on a single machine with 4 TPUs. Four TPUs is the playing machine: the paper does not give the self-play training hardware.

AlphaZero ({{Silver et al. 2017, arXiv v1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815}}) removed what was specific to Go: it optimises the expected outcome, so draws count; no symmetry augmentation, since chess and shogi are not symmetric; one network updated continually, with no evaluator gate; the same hyperparameters for every game except the Dirichlet noise, α = 0.3, 0.15 and 0.03 for chess, shogi and Go ("scaled in inverse proportion to the approximate number of legal moves"). From the rules alone it reached superhuman play in all three "within 24 hours" and defeated a world-champion program in each (Stockfish, Elmo and the 3-day AlphaGo Zero). It passed Stockfish "after just 4 hours (300k steps)", which is 4 hours on 5,000 first-generation TPUs generating games and 64 second-generation TPUs training.

AlphaZero's search and training budget (under 1 min)

800 simulations per move; about 80 thousand positions searched per second in chess, against 70 million for Stockfish; 700,000 training steps of 4,096 positions; the Stockfish it beat had a 1 GB hash and one minute per move.

8MuZero (2019): planning with a learned model of what matters

AlphaZero needs the rules to step its search. MuZero ({{Schrittwieser et al. 2019; Nature 588, 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.08265}}) learns a model instead, three functions in one network:

s0 = hθ(o1, ..., ot)    rk, sk = gθ(sk−1, ak)    pk, vk = fθ(sk)
h (representation)
encodes the past observations into a hidden root state
g (dynamics)
from a hidden state and an action, the reward and the next hidden state
f (prediction)
a policy and a value, as AlphaZero's network gives from a real position

The search is AlphaZero's, run on hidden states: expanding a node calls g instead of the rules. What the hidden state is not trained to do is the point: there is "no direct constraint or requirement for the hidden state to capture all information necessary to reconstruct the original observation ... nor is there any requirement for the hidden state to match the unknown, true state of the environment". It only has to make rewards, values and policies right along imagined action sequences. Such a model is value-equivalent ({{Grimm et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.03506}}): it may be wrong about everything planning does not use.

The same search path, stepped by the rules or by a learned model, and what trains it

A diagram, not data: hidden states are strips of cells meaning "a vector the network chose". The structure (which function is called when, which target trains which output) is the paper's (Figure 1 and Section 3).

Training. From a real trajectory in the replay buffer, the model starts at s0 = h(observations up to t) and is unrolled K = 5 steps with the actions actually taken; at every step three predictions are matched to targets:

ℓt(θ) = Σk=0..K [ ℓr(ut+k, rk) + ℓv(zt+k, vk) + ℓp(πt+k, pk) ] + c‖θ‖²
u
the reward actually observed
z
an n-step return bootstrapped from the search value, zt = ut+1 + γut+2 + ... + γn−1ut+n + γnνt+n, with n = 10 in Atari; in board games, the final outcome
π
the visit distribution of the search run at that real step
losses
squared error for reward and value in board games; cross-entropy over a categorical representation in Atari, "more stable" with values of variable scale; cross-entropy for the policy

Results (compute on the root's Milestones tab). Without the rules it matched AlphaZero's superhuman play in Go, chess and shogi; on 57 Atari games, "in which model-based planning approaches have historically struggled", it set a new state of the art: median 2,041.1% and mean 4,999.2% human-normalised, ahead of R2D2 on 42 of 57 games, from 20 billion frames per game. How much the learned model is worth:

  • Go: trained at 800 simulations (about 0.1 s), the learned model kept improving with searches up to 10 s, "two orders of magnitude longer", and matched a perfect simulator.
  • Atari: gains "plateaued at around 100 simulations", "perhaps because of greater model inaccuracy"; even 1 simulation played well, "suggesting that ... the raw policy has learned to internalise the benefits of search".
Reanalyze, Ms. Pac-Man, search settings, and MuZero on the root's taxonomy (2 min)

MuZero Reanalyze re-runs the search on old trajectories with the latest network to refresh the targets (80% of policy targets, n = 5); with 200 million frames it reached a median of 731%, against 192%, 231% and 431% for IMPALA, Rainbow and LASER.

Ms. Pac-Man: trained with only 6 simulations per move, fewer than the 8 actions, it still learned an effective policy; a model-free variant with the same network matched R2D2 but learned much more slowly, which the authors attribute to search giving "a stronger learning signal" than Q-learning targets.

800 simulations per move in board games and 50 in Atari (searching every fourth frame and repeating the action four times). PUCT with a slowly growing exploration weight, c1 + log((ΣN + c2 + 1)/c2) with c1 = 1.25 and c2 = 19,652; since Atari values are unbounded, Q is rescaled to [0, 1] by the minimum and maximum seen in the tree so far. The dynamics are deterministic; stochastic transitions were "left for future work".

On the root's {{Taxonomy and Method atlas tabs of Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}}: AlphaGo and AlphaZero store a policy and a value (actor-critic heads), use a given model (the rules), learn from self-play, and act in discrete action spaces. MuZero stores the same two heads plus a learned latent model, and learns from its own games replayed from a buffer, also with discrete actions. With a known model and a combinatorial search space, AlphaZero-style search; with no model but a need to plan, MuZero, at a large compute cost per decision.

9EfficientZero (2021): MuZero with little data

The Atari 100k benchmark allows 100,000 agent steps per game (400,000 frames, about two hours of play) on 26 games. EfficientZero ({{Ye et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00210}}) made "three critical changes" to MuZero, each aimed at a weak learning signal or a compounding error:

  1. Temporal consistency. A self-supervised loss (SimSiam-style, as SPR) makes the hidden state predicted by g agree with the encoding h of the observation that really followed. MuZero's model learns only from rewards, values and policies, which are sparse with little data.
  2. Value prefix. An LSTM predicts the sum of rewards so far directly instead of summing per-step predictions, "helping to alleviate the compounding error in the model".
  3. Model-based off-policy correction. Old trajectories came from older policies, so it uses fewer real rewards the older the data and re-searches the last state with the current network to bootstrap.

It reached 194.3% mean and 109.0% median human-normalised score (Table 1 of the current version), "the first time an algorithm achieves super-human performance on Atari games with such little data"; the same table gives 70.4% and 41.5% for SPR and 56.2% and 22.7% for MuZero at the same budget, and the authors call it "close to DQN's performance at 200 million frames while we consume 500 times less data". The paper gives no training time or hardware; the code's instructions use 4 GPUs.

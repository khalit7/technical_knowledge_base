5Decision-time planning: heuristic search, rollouts, Monte Carlo tree search

Heuristic search, the classical AI planner, builds a tree of continuations from the current state, applies an approximate value function at the leaves and backs values up to the root with max backups, then picks the best root action and throws the rest away ({{§8.9|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=203}}). With a perfect model and an imperfect value function, deeper search usually gives better moves, because errors in the leaf values are pushed further away; Tesauro found TD-Gammon played better the deeper it searched, at the price of time per move. The book's observation: a search tree is just many one-step backups ordered from the leaves up, so its strength comes not from multi-step updates as such but from "the focus and concentration of updates on states and actions immediately downstream from the current state".

Rollout algorithms ({{§8.10|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=205}}) estimate each action's value at the current state by averaging the returns of many simulated episodes that start with that action and then follow a fixed rollout policy, act on the best, and repeat from the next state. By the policy improvement theorem (on {{Dynamic programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81baada7e1c05ce69d01}}), acting greedily on qπ(s, ·) is at least as good as π, so a rollout algorithm is one step of policy iteration applied at one state; its aim "is to improve upon the rollout policy; not to find an optimal policy". The name comes from backgammon, where rolling a position out with random dice estimates its equity. Tesauro and Galperin ran it on IBM supercomputers over base players from random to TD-Gammon and reported error rates reduced "by as much as a factor of 5 or more"; "potentially 80% or more of the base player's equity loss can be eliminated" ({{NIPS 1996|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1996/file/996009f2374006606f4c0b0fda878af1-Paper.pdf}}).

Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) is a rollout algorithm that remembers. It keeps value estimates for the state-action pairs near the root, in a tree it grows as it goes, so later simulations follow the beginnings of earlier good ones. Its central ideas are from 2006: Coulom's backup operators that move from averaging to min-max as simulations grow, in the program Crazy Stone ({{Coulom 2006|https://www.remi-coulom.fr/CG2006/CG2006.pdf}}; the survey credits him with the name), and Kocsis and Szepesvári's UCT ({{2006|http://ggp.stanford.edu/readings/uct.pdf}}). MCTS "is largely responsible for the improvement in computer Go from a weak amateur level in 2005 to a grandmaster level (6 dan or more) in 2015" ({{§8.11|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=207}}). Each iteration has four steps:

  1. Selection. From the root, a tree policy picks actions using the statistics on the tree's edges until it reaches a leaf.
  2. Expansion. One or more children are added for untried actions (on some iterations).
  3. Simulation. From the new node a complete episode is played with the rollout policy (or, since AlphaGo Zero, the leaf is simply evaluated by a network).
  4. Backup. The result updates the visit counts and value estimates of every edge on the path; nothing is stored for the rollout beyond the tree.

When time runs out the agent plays the root action with the largest value or, more robustly, the most visits. The tree policy is a bandit at every node. UCT applies UCB1 (derived on {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}) to each node as its own bandit:

UCT:  a = argmaxa [ Q(s, a) + c √( ln N(s) / N(s, a) ) ]     PUCT:  a = argmaxa [ Q(s, a) + c P(s, a) √N(s) / (1 + N(s, a)) ]
Q(s, a)
mean result of the simulations through that edge, for the player to move at s
N(s), N(s, a)
visits to the node and to the edge
c
exploration constant: UCT's 2Cp with Cp = 1/√2 for rewards in [0, 1], which gives UCB1's √2; Kocsis and Szepesvári proved the failure probability at the root goes to zero ("consistent")
P(s, a)
a prior from a policy network; PUCT, the AlphaGo family's rule, explores where the prior points and needs no rollouts once a value network scores the leaves

What it shows: random playouts make blocking look unnecessary, because a random X often fails to complete the row it threatens; after 30 simulations UCT has spent only 5 on the block and prefers another move. Over 40 random seeds UCT chose the block 30% of the time at 32 simulations, 50% at 64, 92.5% at 128 and every time from 256 on. With the trained network's prior and value, 26 of 30 simulations go to the block. Even the untrained network gets there (12 of 30), because tic-tac-toe is short: a few simulations reach real game ends, whose values are exact. The visit counts at the root, normalised, are the search policy π; in AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero they are the training target for the network's prior p, which is how search improves the policy and the network distils it (section 7).

6AlphaGo (2016): networks inside the search

Go's rules are a perfect model, but the tree is too wide and deep for search alone; the strongest programs were MCTS with hand-made rollout policies, such as Pachi, ranked 2 amateur dan on KGS. AlphaGo ({{Silver et al., Nature 529, 2016|https://www.nature.com/articles/nature16961}}; {{free PDF|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/alphago/AlphaGoNaturePaper.pdf}}) put four learned functions into MCTS:

  • SL policy network pσ: 13 layers, trained on 30 million positions from the KGS Go Server to predict the expert's move; 57.0% accurate with all input features (55.7% from the raw board and history), against 44.4% for the previous best.
  • Rollout policy pπ: a linear softmax over small patterns; 24.2% accurate but "using just 2 μs to select an action, rather than 3 ms for the policy network". About 1,000 full-game rollouts per second per search thread.
  • RL policy network pρ: the SL network improved by policy-gradient self-play (50 GPUs, one day). It won "more than 80%" of games against the SL network and, with no search at all, 85% against Pachi, an MCTS program doing 100,000 simulations per move.
  • Value network vθ: predicts the winner from a position (15 layers, 50 GPUs for one week). Trained on full human games it memorised outcomes (test MSE 0.37 against 0.19 on training data), so the team generated 30 million positions "each sampled from a separate game" of RL-policy self-play: MSE 0.226 train, 0.234 test.

In the search, the SL network's move probabilities become the prior P(s, a) of a PUCT rule (cpuct = 5), and each leaf is scored by mixing the value network with one fast rollout:

V(sL) = (1 − λ) vθ(sL) + λ zL,   λ = 0.5
vθ(sL)
the value network's prediction at the leaf
zL
the result of a rollout from the leaf with the fast rollout policy

The mixed evaluation won at least 95% of games against the variants with only the value network (λ = 0) or only rollouts (λ = 1); even λ = 0 beat every other Go program. Two findings are counter-intuitive and worth remembering. The weaker SL policy made the better prior: "presumably because humans select a diverse beam of promising moves, whereas RL optimizes for the single best move". But the value network trained on the stronger RL policy's games made the better evaluator. The match program ran 40 search threads on 48 CPUs and 8 GPUs; the distributed version used 1,202 CPUs and 176 GPUs. It won 494 of 495 games against other programs and beat the European champion Fan Hui 5-0 in October 2015; a larger version, AlphaGo Lee on 48 TPUs, beat Lee Sedol 4-1 in March 2016. Those results and their compute sit in the root's {{Milestones and benchmarks tab of Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}}; Sutton and Barto tell the whole story in {{§16.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=463}}.

7AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero: search as the policy improvement operator

AlphaGo used search to play, after learning. AlphaGo Zero ({{Silver et al., Nature 550, 2017; author copy|https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10045895/1/agz_unformatted_nature.pdf}}) used search to learn, at every move of self-play. It differed in four ways: no human data, starting from random play; only the stones as input; one network fθ(s) = (p, v) with a policy head and a value head; and a search with no rollouts, every leaf scored by the network. The loop is policy iteration ({{Dynamic programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81baada7e1c05ce69d01}}) with MCTS in the improvement step: "MCTS may therefore be viewed as a powerful policy improvement operator", and self-play with search, scored by the game's winner, "a powerful policy evaluation operator".

ℓ = (z − v)² − π⊤ log p + c‖θ‖²
z
the game's result from the player to move's view: +1, 0 (AlphaZero, which allows draws), −1
v
the network's predicted value
π
the search's visit distribution at that position
p
the network's move probabilities
c
L2 weight decay, 10−4; the two loss terms are weighted equally

Worked example (illustrative numbers, from the earlier Deep RL page). The player went on to win (z = 1), the network predicted v = 0.6, search visited three moves in proportions (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) and the network gave them (0.5, 0.3, 0.2). The value term is (1 − 0.6)² = 0.16; the policy term is −(0.7 ln 0.5 + 0.2 ln 0.3 + 0.1 ln 0.2) = 0.4852 + 0.2408 + 0.1609 = 0.887; the loss before weight decay is 1.047. For a softmax output the gradient of the policy term with respect to the logits is p − π = (−0.2, 0.1, 0.1): the update raises the move search liked and lowers the others. Search improves, the network distils, and the better network makes the next search stronger.

The details that made it work (AlphaGo Zero Methods): 1,600 simulations per move, about 0.4 s; moves sampled in proportion to visit counts for the first 30 moves of a game (temperature τ = 1), then the most visited; Dirichlet noise at the root, P = 0.75 p + 0.25 η with η ∼ Dir(0.03), so every move can be tried "but the search may still overrule bad moves"; resignation, with the threshold set so fewer than 5% of resigned games could have been won (checked by playing out 10% of them); and an evaluator gate: a new network replaced the self-play player only if it won more than 55% of 400 games. The 3-day run generated 4.9 million games, updated on 700,000 mini-batches of 2,048 positions with a 20-block residual network, passed AlphaGo Lee after 36 hours and beat it 100-0 after 72 hours, playing on a single machine with 4 TPUs (the paper does not give the training hardware). The 40-day run, 29 million games, beat AlphaGo Master 89-11 and reached an Elo of 5,185. The same network with no search at all rated 3,055: search was worth about 2,100 Elo on top of the network that learned from it.

AlphaZero ({{Silver et al. 2017, arXiv v1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.01815}}) removed what was specific to Go: it optimises the expected outcome, so draws count; it does not augment data with board symmetries, since chess and shogi are not symmetric; it keeps one network updated continually, with no evaluator gate; and it uses the same hyperparameters for every game except the Dirichlet noise, α = 0.3, 0.15 and 0.03 for chess, shogi and Go ("scaled in inverse proportion to the approximate number of legal moves"). From random play and given no domain knowledge except the rules, it reached superhuman play in chess, shogi and Go "within 24 hours" of training and defeated a world-champion program in each (Stockfish, Elmo and the 3-day AlphaGo Zero). It used 800 simulations per move and searched about 80 thousand positions per second in chess, against 70 million for Stockfish. Results and compute are on the root's Milestones tab: 700,000 steps of 4,096 positions, 5,000 first-generation TPUs generating games and 64 second-generation TPUs training; it passed Stockfish in chess "after just 4 hours (300k steps)", which is 4 hours on those 5,000 TPUs.

The two AlphaZero papers differ: quote one
The preprint (arXiv v1, December 2017) and the peer-reviewed paper ({{Science 362, 2018|https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aar6404}}; {{author preprint|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/DeepMind.com/Blog/alphazero-shedding-new-light-on-chess-shogi-and-go/alphazero_preprint.pdf}}) give different numbers. Training TPUs: 64 second-generation in v1, 16 in Science. Search speed: 80,000 against Stockfish's 70 million positions per second in v1; 60,000 against 60 million in Science. The match: v1 played 100 games against Stockfish with 64 threads, a 1 GB hash and one minute per move (28 wins, 72 draws, 0 losses); Science played 1,000 games at its 2016 TCEC superfinal settings (44 threads, a 32 GB hash, 3 hours plus 15 seconds per move) and AlphaZero won 155 and lost 6. Science also gives training times of about 9 hours (chess), 12 hours (shogi) and 13 days (Go). This page and the root's Milestones tab use v1's numbers where they quote one.

Our small replication (the {{Self-play lab|#t-zero}}): the same loop on tic-tac-toe, with a one-hidden-layer network (64 units) instead of a residual tower, 50 simulations per move, 48 games per iteration, 60 iterations: 2,880 games, about half a minute on a laptop CPU. The network's top move was optimal (by exact game-tree solution) in 53.7% of the 4,520 non-terminal positions at random initialisation, 94.3% at the end; with a 50-simulation search on top, 95.8% and 99.0%. Against a perfect opponent over 100 games, the untrained network lost 48 playing on its prior alone and 34 with search; from iteration 10 on it lost none, with or without search.

8MuZero (2019): planning with a learned model of what matters

AlphaZero needs the rules to step its search. MuZero ({{Schrittwieser et al. 2019; Nature 588, 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.08265}}) learns a model instead, with three functions in one network:

s0 = hθ(o1, ..., ot)    rk, sk = gθ(sk−1, ak)    pk, vk = fθ(sk)
h (representation)
encodes the past observations into a hidden root state
g (dynamics)
from a hidden state and a candidate action, predicts the immediate reward and the next hidden state
f (prediction)
from a hidden state, a policy and a value, as AlphaZero's network does from a real position

The search is AlphaZero's, run on hidden states: expanding a node calls g instead of the rules. The key design choice is what the hidden state is not trained to do. There is "no direct constraint or requirement for the hidden state to capture all information necessary to reconstruct the original observation ... nor is there any requirement for the hidden state to match the unknown, true state of the environment". It only has to make the predicted rewards, values and policies right along imagined action sequences. A model of this kind is called value-equivalent ({{Grimm et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.03506}}): it may be wrong about everything planning does not use.

One search path, with the real rules (AlphaZero) or the learned model (MuZero), and how MuZero is trained

A diagram, not data: hidden states are drawn as strips of cells to say "a vector the network chose", not to show real activations. The structure (which function is called when, and which target trains which output) is the paper's (Figure 1 and Section 3).

Training. From a real trajectory in the replay buffer, the model starts at s0 = h(observations up to t) and is unrolled K = 5 steps with the actions actually taken. At every unrolled step k three predictions are matched to targets:

ℓt(θ) = Σk=0..K [ ℓr(ut+k, rk) + ℓv(zt+k, vk) + ℓp(πt+k, pk) ] + c‖θ‖²
u
the reward actually observed
z
the value target: an n-step return bootstrapped from the search value, zt = ut+1 + γut+2 + ... + γn−1ut+n + γnνt+n, with n = 10 in Atari; in board games, the final outcome
π
the visit distribution of the search that was run at that real step
losses
squared error for reward and value in board games, as AlphaZero; cross-entropy over a categorical representation in Atari, "more stable" with values of variable scale; cross-entropy for the policy

Search settings: 800 simulations per move in board games and 50 in Atari (searching every fourth frame and repeating the action four times). The selection rule is PUCT with a slowly growing exploration weight, c1 + log((ΣN + c2 + 1)/c2) with c1 = 1.25 and c2 = 19,652, and since Atari values are unbounded, Q is rescaled to [0, 1] by the minimum and maximum seen in the tree so far. The dynamics are deterministic; stochastic transitions were "left for future work".

Results (with compute on the root's Milestones tab). Without the rules it matched AlphaZero's superhuman play in Go, chess and shogi, and on 57 Atari games, "in which model-based planning approaches have historically struggled", it set a new state of the art: median 2,041.1% and mean 4,999.2% of human-normalised score, ahead of R2D2 on 42 of 57 games, from 20 billion frames per game. MuZero Reanalyze re-runs the search on old trajectories with the latest network to refresh the targets (used for 80% of policy targets, with n = 5); with 200 million frames it reached a median of 731%, against 192%, 231% and 431% for IMPALA, Rainbow and LASER. Three experiments in the paper say how much the learned model is worth:

  • Go, more search than trained for: trained at 800 simulations (about 0.1 s), the learned model kept improving with searches up to 10 s, "two orders of magnitude longer", and matched a perfect simulator.
  • Atari, less gain from search: improvements "plateaued at around 100 simulations", "perhaps because of greater model inaccuracy"; even 1 simulation (the policy network alone) played well, "suggesting that ... the raw policy has learned to internalise the benefits of search".
  • Ms. Pac-Man, very little search: trained with only 6 simulations per move, fewer than the 8 actions, it still learned an effective policy; a model-free variant with the same network matched R2D2 but learned much more slowly than MuZero, which the authors attribute to search giving "a stronger learning signal" than Q-learning targets.

On the root's taxonomy axes (the {{Taxonomy and Method atlas tabs of Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}}): AlphaGo and AlphaZero store a policy and a value (actor-critic heads), use a given model (the rules), learn from self-play, and act in discrete action spaces. MuZero stores the same two heads plus a learned latent model, and learns from its own games replayed from a buffer (self-play and replay), also with discrete actions. The trade-off in one line: with a known model and a combinatorial search space, AlphaZero-style search; with no model but a need to plan, MuZero, at a large compute cost per decision.

9EfficientZero (2021): MuZero with little data

MuZero used billions of frames. The Atari 100k benchmark allows 100,000 agent steps per game (400,000 frames, about two hours of play) on 26 games. EfficientZero ({{Ye et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00210}}) made "three critical changes" to MuZero:

  1. Temporal consistency. A self-supervised loss (SimSiam-style, as SPR) makes the hidden state predicted by g agree with the encoding h of the observation that really followed. MuZero's model gets learning signal only from rewards, values and policies, which are sparse with little data.
  2. Value prefix. Instead of predicting each step's reward and summing (so that errors add up), an LSTM over the unrolled hidden states predicts the sum of rewards so far directly, "helping to alleviate the compounding error in the model".
  3. Model-based off-policy correction. Old trajectories were collected by older policies, so their n-step value targets are stale; EfficientZero uses fewer real rewards the older the data, and redoes a search with the current network at the last state to bootstrap.

It reached 194.3% mean and 109.0% median human-normalised score (Table 1 of the current version), which the authors call "the first time an algorithm achieves super-human performance on Atari games with such little data"; for comparison, the same table gives 70.4% and 41.5% for SPR and 56.2% and 22.7% for MuZero at the same budget; the authors add that this is "close to DQN's performance at 200 million frames while we consume 500 times less data". The paper gives no training time or hardware; the code's instructions use 4 GPUs.

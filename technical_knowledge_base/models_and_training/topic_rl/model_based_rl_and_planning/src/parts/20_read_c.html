10Learned world models: World Models, PlaNet, Dreamer

A second line learns a model of the observations themselves, compressed into a small latent state, and trains or plans inside it. Unlike MuZero's, these models are usually trained to reconstruct what they see, which gives a dense learning signal even when rewards are rare.

World Models ({{Ha and Schmidhuber 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10122}}; {{interactive version|https://worldmodels.github.io}}) split the agent in three: V, a variational autoencoder compressing each frame to a latent z (32 numbers for CarRacing, 64 for Doom); M, a recurrent network (256 hidden units for CarRacing, 512 for Doom) predicting the next z as a mixture of 5 Gaussians; and C, a linear controller with "a mere 867 parameters", trained by CMA-ES. On CarRacing-v0, where 900 counts as solved, it scored 906 ± 21 over 100 trials. For DoomTakeCover the controller was trained entirely inside the dream generated by M, and it learned to game the dream: it moved so that the dream's monsters never fired ("Cheating the World Model"). Making the dream noisier with a temperature τ fixed that (their Table 2; survival steps over 100 trials, 750 counts as solved):

A policy that scores 2,086 in an exploitable dream scores 193 in reality: the clearest small example of a planner exploiting model error (§13).

PlaNet ({{Hafner et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04551}}) plans instead of learning a policy: a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) with a deterministic and a stochastic path ("crucial for high planning performance"), and MPC in latent space with the cross-entropy method (horizon 12, 10 iterations, 1,000 candidate action sequences refit to the best 100, replanning every step). On six DeepMind Control tasks from pixels it used 1,000 episodes where A3C and D4PG used 100,000.

Dreamer ({{Hafner et al. 2019, ICLR 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01603}}) keeps the RSSM but learns an actor and a critic purely from trajectories imagined in latent space, "propagating analytic gradients of learned state values back through trajectories imagined" by the model. It is Dyna's idea scaled to images (the root's Method atlas lists Dreamer as Dyna-Q's descendant).

The model learns from real frames; the behaviour learns only from imagined latent steps

Top: real observations from the replay buffer are encoded into latent states, and the RSSM learns to predict each from the last. Bottom (blue, dashed): from a real latent state the actor picks actions and the model predicts the next latent state and reward, for an imagination horizon of 15, with no images decoded; the critic learns λ-returns (γ = 0.99, λ = 0.95) on these steps and the actor is trained by gradients through them.

Dreamer beat the model-free D4PG's score on 20 visual control tasks with 20 times fewer environment steps (5 × 106 against 108). DreamerV2 was the first to reach human level on the 55-game Atari benchmark by learning behaviours inside a separately trained world model. DreamerV3 ({{Hafner et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04104}}; {{Nature 640, 2025|https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08744-2}}) uses one configuration that "outperforms specialized methods across over 150 diverse tasks", and is "the first algorithm to collect diamonds ... from scratch without human data or curricula" in Minecraft, on 1 GPU for 9 days.

Dreamer, DreamerV2 and DreamerV3 in numbers (2 min)

On 20 visual control tasks Dreamer averaged 823 after 5 × 106 environment steps, against 332 for PlaNet and 786 for the model-free D4PG after 108 steps, at about 3 hours per million steps on one V100 (PlaNet 11, D4PG 24 to reach similar performance). DreamerV2 ({{Hafner et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02193}}) replaced the Gaussian latents with 32 categorical variables of 32 classes and added KL balancing (the prior pulled towards the posterior faster than the reverse, weights 0.8 and 0.2); it "constitutes the first agent that achieves human-level performance on the Atari benchmark of 55 tasks by learning behaviors inside a separately trained world model": gamer-normalised median 2.15 at 200 million frames with sticky actions, against 1.47 for Rainbow and 1.29 for IQN, in under 10 days on one V100, learning from 468 billion imagined states, 10,000 times its 50 million real inputs.

DreamerV3 (the 2023 preprint and the Nature version) is about robustness: one configuration "outperforms specialized methods across over 150 diverse tasks". The changes handle scale: symlog targets, sign(x) ln(|x| + 1), so rewards and values of any magnitude train the same networks; free bits, no penalty on KL terms below 1 nat; and return normalisation by the 5th to 95th percentile range, only ever scaling large returns down. In Minecraft it is "the first algorithm to collect diamonds ... from scratch without human data or curricula" (the Nature version adds "to our knowledge"), within 100 million environment steps, on 1 GPU for 9 days; the paper contrasts VPT's 720 GPUs for 9 days with human video. Larger models, from 12 million to 400 million parameters, scored higher and needed less interaction (Crafter and one DMLab task).

PlaNet's 50 or 200 times, and latent overshooting (under 1 min)
PlaNet: 50 or 200 times?
The first preprint and Google's blog ("5000% more data efficient") say 50 times less environment interaction; the final ICML 2019 version says "on average 200× less", with Table 1's per-task gains over D4PG ranging from 40 to over 500.

PlaNet proposed a multi-step training objective, latent overshooting, but the final RSSM agent "does not require it".

11World models from video (2024 to 2025)

Since 2024 the term "world model" also names large generative models that predict the next video frame given an action, trained mostly on video. They are the same object as M in World Models, at a far larger scale, and they meet the same limit: an autoregressive model feeds its own output back in, so "inaccuracies tend to accumulate over time" (DeepMind on Genie 3), which is §13's compounding error. Planning inside them is still mostly a research direction; the sources below report simulators, and two report agents.

12Model-predictive control, PETS and MBPO

Model-predictive control (MPC) comes from control engineering: "the current control action is obtained by solving, at each sampling instant, a finite horizon open-loop optimal control problem, using the current state of the plant as the initial state; the optimization yields an optimal control sequence and the first control in this sequence is applied to the plant" ({{Mayne et al. 2000|https://doi.org/10.1016/S0005-1098(99)00214-9}}, abstract). Replanning every step is what keeps model errors from accumulating past a few steps.

Plan a short horizon, apply one action, throw the rest away, plan again

Grey: steps already taken. Solid blue: the one action applied at that step. Dashed: the rest of the planned sequence, discarded when the next real state arrives. Drawn with PlaNet's horizon of 12 (8 at phone width). With a learned neural model the optimisation is done by sampling: random shooting (many random action sequences, keep the best first action) or the cross-entropy method (CEM: sample, keep the best few, refit a Gaussian, repeat).

PETS ({{Chua et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12114}}, probabilistic ensembles with trajectory sampling) made this work on standard continuous-control benchmarks. Each of 5 networks ("We found B = 5 sufficient") outputs a Gaussian over the next state, capturing aleatoric uncertainty, the noise in the data; the disagreement between the 5, each trained on its own bootstrap sample, captures epistemic uncertainty, "our subjective uncertainty due to a lack of data", which is largest away from the data. Planning propagates particles through randomly chosen members and optimises with CEM. It "matches the asymptotic performance of model-free algorithms on several challenging benchmark tasks, while requiring significantly fewer samples (e.g., 8 and 125 times fewer samples than Soft Actor Critic and Proximal Policy Optimization respectively on the half-cheetah task)".

MBPO ({{Janner et al. 2019, "When to Trust Your Model"|https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253}}) is Dyna with a neural ensemble and SAC: from states in the real replay buffer it runs short branched rollouts of k steps in the model and trains SAC mostly on that model data; in its own words the branched rollout "resembles the scheme proposed in the original Dyna algorithm ... which can be viewed as a special case of a length 1 branched rollouts". Its bound on the true return subtracts a penalty whose model-error part is

2 rmax · k (εm + 2επ) / (1 − γ)
k
rollout length in the model
εm
the model's error, a bound on the total-variation distance between its next-state distribution and the true one
επ
how far the policy has moved from the one that collected the data

Error grows linearly with k while the benefit of model data does not, so the bound favours short rollouts. "MBPO's performance on the Ant task at 300 thousand steps matches that of SAC at 3 million steps", "an order of magnitude faster on some tasks". On Hopper its model was accurate enough for 200-step rollouts, yet they trained worse than short ones, and "500-step rollouts are too inaccurate for effective learning".

MBPO's bound at k = 0, and its settings (under 1 min)

Taken literally the bound is best at k = 0; a refined version, using the model's error on the new policy's own states, justifies some k > 0 when that error is low. Settings: k = 1 on three tasks and schedules growing to 15 or 25 on the others, an ensemble of 7, 400 model rollouts and 20 to 40 policy updates per real step; final performance comparable to SAC's.

13Why model error compounds, and what to do about it

A model trained to predict one step is used to predict many, by feeding its own output back in. "If there is any error or approximation in the one-step predictions, then it can compound to make the long-term predictions wildly inaccurate. In practice this is often what happens" ({{Sutton and Barto §17.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=489}}). Three reasons:

  • Errors feed on themselves. "A flawed sample is given as input to the model, which causes more errors, and so on" ({{Talvitie 2014|https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.0834}}); the model is now asked about states it was never trained on, "breaking the train-test i.i.d assumption" ({{Venkatraman et al. 2015|https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/9590}}), whose bound on the T-step error grows exponentially in T when the dynamics expand errors (Lipschitz constant above 1), and is tight.
  • The planner seeks the errors out. An optimiser maximising return under the model finds wherever the model is too optimistic: the Doom agent that stopped the dream's monsters firing (§10).
  • One-step accuracy is the wrong target. "The likelihood of one-step ahead predictions is not always correlated with control performance" ({{Lambert et al. 2020, objective mismatch|https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.04523}}).

The first effect, on real numbers: Gymnasium's Pendulum-v1 ({{source|https://gymnasium.farama.org/environments/classic_control/pendulum/}}: θ = 0 upright, g = 10, time step 0.05 s, torque in [−2, 2]) against an ensemble of five small networks (two layers of 24 units) that this page fitted, PETS-style on bootstrap resamples, to 2,000 transitions from random torques (src/fit_pendulum.py). Each network's one-step angle error averages 0.0016 rad. Predict: after 50 steps, how many times larger is the error?

A tiny one-step error decides whether the model's pendulum goes over the top

Both pendulums get the same 50 random torques. Real dynamics against network 1 of the ensemble, test start 6 of 200: it starts 0.41 rad from upright, falls, and swings back to within about 0.2 rad of the top near step 30, where a small error decides whether it goes over. The model's goes over; the real one falls back. Right: the angle error |θmodel − θreal| by step, wrapped to [−π, π]. The branched mode restarts the model from the real state every 5 steps (MBPO-style).

Error grows 125 times over 50 steps; restarting from real states every 5 steps keeps it small

Over 200 random starts, the error grows from 0.0016 rad after one step to 0.044 after 25 and 0.20 after 50, 125 times the one-step error, and the mean hides a heavy tail: for network 1 the median error at step 50 is 0.024 rad but 11 of the 200 rollouts are off by more than 0.5 rad, the ones that went over the top the wrong way. The spread between the five networks grows with the error (0.18 rad at step 50): disagreement flags where the model should not be trusted. Restarting every 5 steps keeps the mean error over 50 steps at 0.0044 rad, against 0.071 open loop.

The remedies. Plan short and replan often (MPC, MBPO: short branched rollouts and replanning are the same remedy). Measure uncertainty and stay where the model is confident (PETS's ensembles). Make the dream harder than reality (World Models' temperature). Learn only what planning needs (MuZero, value equivalence). Ground the model in real targets (EfficientZero's consistency loss and value prefix). Prefer search in a perfect model whenever one exists (AlphaZero). Sutton and Barto's verdict: "cases of full model-based reinforcement learning, in which the environment model is learned from data and then used for planning, are rare ... More work is needed before planning with learned models can be effective" ({{§17.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=495}}).

Planning tends to pay when real experience is expensive and computation is not (robots, physical systems, users); when a perfect or good model exists and the problem is combinatorial (board games, puzzles, theorem proving); when rewards are sparse and planning carries them back faster than real experience (Dyna's maze); and when one model is reused for new goals. It tends not to pay when a fast simulator makes data nearly free, so model-free methods run at scale (Dactyl, OpenAI Five); when the environment is hard to model and the planner will exploit the gaps; and when actions must be chosen faster than a search can run (AlphaGo Zero took about 0.4 s per move at 1,600 simulations). Planning is free of real experience, not of computation, and model-based agents have more parts to tune: a model, its uncertainty, a planner, and how much to trust each.

Language models. In text generation the transition model is known exactly (the next state is the prompt plus the chosen token), so the open question is only whether decision-time search pays. {{TS-LLM|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17179}} applied AlphaZero-like tree search to decoding and training, and AlphaProof coupled a pretrained language model with "the AlphaZero reinforcement learning algorithm" to prove olympiad problems in Lean (root Milestones tab). DeepSeek-R1's authors tried MCTS and dropped it: the token search space is exponentially larger than chess, capping each node's expansions traps the search in local optima, and a fine-grained value model is hard to train ({{DeepSeek-R1 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}, Failed attempts).

Long chain-of-thought as search: an interpretation (under 1 min)

An interpretation, not a claim of the papers: long chain-of-thought in o1- and R1-style models can be read as a sequential search in token space, with the policy proposing and checking steps; {{OpenAI reported|http://web.archive.org/web/20240913000000/https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/}} that o1 "consistently improves with more reinforcement learning (train-time compute) and with more time spent thinking (test-time compute)".

Misconceptions worth a second look (under 1 min)

"A model with lower one-step error will plan better." Not necessarily: errors compound and planners exploit them (objective mismatch); MBPO's 200-step rollouts were accurate yet trained worse than short ones.

"Planning with a model explores for you." A pessimistic model can hide a better option forever (plain Dyna-Q and the shortcut); planning needs an exploration incentive such as Dyna-Q+'s κ√τ bonus or R-max's optimism.

"AlphaZero beat Stockfish after 4 hours of training." Four hours on 5,000 TPUs generating games and 64 more training (arXiv v1), against a Stockfish at one minute per move; the Science version replayed the match at championship settings: 155 wins, 6 losses in 1,000 games.

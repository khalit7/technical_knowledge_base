10Learned world models: World Models, PlaNet, Dreamer

A second line learns a model of the observations themselves, compressed into a small latent state, and trains or plans inside it. Unlike MuZero's, these models are usually trained to reconstruct what they see, which gives them a dense learning signal even when rewards are rare.

World Models ({{Ha and Schmidhuber 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.10122}}; {{interactive version|https://worldmodels.github.io}}) split the agent in three: V, a variational autoencoder compressing each frame to a latent z (32 numbers for CarRacing, 64 for Doom); M, a recurrent network (256 hidden units for CarRacing, 512 for Doom) predicting the next z as a mixture of 5 Gaussians; and C, a linear controller from [z, h] to the action with "a mere 867 parameters", trained by the evolution strategy CMA-ES. On CarRacing-v0, where 900 counts as solved, it scored 906 ± 21 over 100 trials. For DoomTakeCover the controller was trained entirely inside the dream generated by M and then run in the real game. The agent found that the dream could be gamed: it learned to move so that the dream's monsters never fired ("Cheating the World Model"). Making the dream noisier with a temperature τ fixed that; the trade-off is in their Table 2:

PlaNet ({{Hafner et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04551}}) plans instead of learning a policy: a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) with both a deterministic and a stochastic path ("crucial for high planning performance"), and model-predictive control in latent space with the cross-entropy method: horizon 12, 10 iterations, 1,000 candidate action sequences, refit to the best 100, replanning at every step. On six DeepMind Control tasks from pixels it used 1,000 episodes where A3C and D4PG used 100,000. It proposed a multi-step training objective, latent overshooting, but the final RSSM agent "does not require it".

PlaNet: 50 or 200 times?
The first preprint and Google's blog ("5000% more data efficient") say 50 times less environment interaction; the final ICML 2019 version says "on average 200× less", with Table 1's per-task gains over D4PG ranging from 40 to over 500.

Dreamer ({{Hafner et al. 2019, ICLR 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.01603}}) keeps the RSSM but learns an actor and a critic purely from imagined trajectories in latent space, "propagating analytic gradients of learned state values back through trajectories imagined" by the model, with an imagination horizon of 15 and λ-returns (γ = 0.99, λ = 0.95). On 20 visual control tasks it averaged 823 after 5 × 106 environment steps, against 332 for PlaNet and 786 for the model-free D4PG after 108 steps, at about 3 hours per million steps on one V100 (PlaNet 11, D4PG 24 to reach similar performance). This is Dyna's idea, learning behaviour on simulated experience, scaled to images (the root's Method atlas lists Dreamer as Dyna-Q's descendant).

DreamerV2 ({{Hafner et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02193}}) replaced the Gaussian latents with 32 categorical variables of 32 classes each, and added KL balancing (the prior is pulled towards the posterior faster than the reverse, weights 0.8 and 0.2). It "constitutes the first agent that achieves human-level performance on the Atari benchmark of 55 tasks by learning behaviors inside a separately trained world model": gamer-normalised median 2.15 at 200 million frames with sticky actions, against 1.47 for Rainbow and 1.29 for IQN, in under 10 days on one V100. Over those 50 million real inputs it learned from 468 billion imagined states, 10,000 times more.

DreamerV3 ({{Hafner et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04104}}; {{Nature 640, 2025|https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08744-2}}) is about robustness: one configuration "outperforms specialized methods across over 150 diverse tasks". The changes are mostly about scale: symlog targets, sign(x) ln(|x| + 1), so rewards and values of any magnitude train the same networks; free bits, no penalty on the KL terms below 1 nat; and return normalisation by the 5th to 95th percentile range, only ever scaling large returns down. In Minecraft it is "the first algorithm to collect diamonds ... from scratch without human data or curricula" (the Nature version adds "to our knowledge"), within 100 million environment steps, on 1 GPU for 9 days; the paper contrasts VPT's 720 GPUs for 9 days with human video. Larger models, from 12 million to 400 million parameters, scored higher and needed less environment interaction, shown on Crafter and one DMLab task.

11Model-predictive control, PETS and MBPO

Model-predictive control (MPC) comes from control engineering: "the current control action is obtained by solving, at each sampling instant, a finite horizon open-loop optimal control problem, using the current state of the plant as the initial state; the optimization yields an optimal control sequence and the first control in this sequence is applied to the plant" ({{Mayne et al. 2000|https://doi.org/10.1016/S0005-1098(99)00214-9}}, abstract). It is decision-time planning with a receding horizon, and replanning every step is what keeps model errors from accumulating past a few steps. With a learned neural model there is no gradient-friendly solver, so the optimisation is done by sampling: random shooting (try many random action sequences, keep the best first action) or the cross-entropy method (CEM: sample, keep the best few, refit a Gaussian, repeat).

PETS ({{Chua et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12114}}, probabilistic ensembles with trajectory sampling) made this work on standard continuous-control benchmarks. Each of 5 networks ("We found B = 5 sufficient") outputs a Gaussian over the next state, capturing aleatoric uncertainty, the noise in the data; the disagreement between the 5, each trained on its own bootstrap sample, captures epistemic uncertainty, "our subjective uncertainty due to a lack of data", which is largest away from the data. Planning propagates particles through randomly chosen members and optimises with CEM. It "matches the asymptotic performance of model-free algorithms on several challenging benchmark tasks, while requiring significantly fewer samples (e.g., 8 and 125 times fewer samples than Soft Actor Critic and Proximal Policy Optimization respectively on the half-cheetah task)".

MBPO ({{Janner et al. 2019, "When to Trust Your Model"|https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.08253}}) is Dyna with a neural ensemble and SAC: from states in the real replay buffer it runs short branched rollouts of k steps in the model and trains SAC mostly on that model data. Its analysis bounds the true return of a policy by its return under k-step branched rollouts minus a penalty; the model-error part of the penalty is

2 rmax · k (εm + 2επ) / (1 − γ)
k
rollout length in the model
εm
the model's error, a bound on the total-variation distance between its next-state distribution and the true one
επ
how far the policy has moved from the one that collected the data

Error grows linearly with the rollout length while the benefit of model data does not, so the bound favours short rollouts; taken literally it is best at k = 0, and a refined version, using the model's error on the new policy's own states, justifies some k > 0 when that error is low. In practice MBPO used k = 1 on three tasks and schedules growing to 15 or 25 on the others, an ensemble of 7, 400 model rollouts and 20 to 40 policy updates per real step. "MBPO's performance on the Ant task at 300 thousand steps matches that of SAC at 3 million steps", "an order of magnitude faster on some tasks", with comparable final performance. On Hopper its model was accurate enough for 200-step rollouts, yet they trained worse than short ones, and "500-step rollouts are too inaccurate for effective learning". MBPO's own description of where it comes from: the branched rollout "resembles the scheme proposed in the original Dyna algorithm ... which can be viewed as a special case of a length 1 branched rollouts".

12Why model error compounds, and what to do about it

A model trained to predict one step is used to predict many, by feeding its own output back in. "If there is any error or approximation in the one-step predictions, then it can compound to make the long-term predictions wildly inaccurate. In practice this is often what happens" ({{Sutton and Barto §17.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=489}}). Three reasons, each with its source:

  • Errors feed on themselves. "A flawed sample is given as input to the model, which causes more errors, and so on" ({{Talvitie 2014|https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.0834}}). Worse, the model is now asked about states it was never trained on: compounding errors "change the input distribution for future prediction steps, breaking the train-test i.i.d assumption" ({{Venkatraman et al. 2015|https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/9590}}), whose bound on the T-step error grows exponentially in T when the dynamics expand errors (Lipschitz constant above 1), and is tight.
  • The planner seeks the errors out. An optimiser maximising return under the model will find wherever the model is too optimistic: the Doom agent that stopped the dream's monsters firing (section 10).
  • One-step accuracy is the wrong target. "The likelihood of one-step ahead predictions is not always correlated with control performance" ({{Lambert et al. 2020, objective mismatch|https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.04523}}).

The animation shows the first effect on real numbers. The environment is Gymnasium's Pendulum-v1 ({{source|https://gymnasium.farama.org/environments/classic_control/pendulum/}}: θ = 0 upright, g = 10, time step 0.05 s, torque in [−2, 2]). The model is an ensemble of five small networks (two layers of 24 units) that this page fitted, PETS-style on bootstrap resamples, to 2,000 transitions from random torques, about what an agent has early on (src/fit_pendulum.py). Each network's one-step angle error averages 0.0016 rad. Both pendulums below get the same 50 random torques.

Three things to read off. The error grows from 0.0016 rad after one step to 0.044 after 25 and 0.20 after 50, 125 times the one-step error, and the mean hides a heavy tail: for network 1 the median error at step 50 is 0.024 rad but 11 of the 200 rollouts are off by more than 0.5 rad, the ones that went over the top the wrong way. The spread between the five networks grows with the error (0.18 rad at step 50), which is why ensembles help: disagreement flags where the model should not be trusted. And restarting from real states every 5 steps keeps the mean error over 50 steps at 0.0044 rad, against 0.071 open loop: short branched rollouts (MBPO) and replanning every step (MPC) are the same remedy.

The remedies, in one list. Plan short and replan often (MPC, MBPO). Measure uncertainty and stay where the model is confident (PETS's ensembles). Make the dream harder than reality (World Models' temperature). Learn only what planning needs, so the model spends no capacity on irrelevant detail (MuZero, value equivalence). Ground the model in real targets (EfficientZero's consistency loss and value prefix). Prefer search in a perfect model whenever one exists (AlphaZero). Sutton and Barto's verdict on the general problem: "cases of full model-based reinforcement learning, in which the environment model is learned from data and then used for planning, are rare ... More work is needed before planning with learned models can be effective" ({{§17.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=495}}).

Planning tends to pay when real experience is expensive and computation is not (robots, physical systems, users); when a perfect or good model already exists and the problem is combinatorial (board games, puzzles, theorem proving), so decision-time search is cheap to trust; when rewards are sparse and planning can carry them back faster than real experience (Dyna's maze); and when the same model can be reused for new goals. It tends not to pay when a fast simulator is available, so data is nearly free and model-free methods can be run at scale (Dactyl, OpenAI Five); when the environment is hard to model and the planner will exploit the gaps; and when actions must be chosen faster than a search can run (AlphaGo Zero took about 0.4 s per move at 1,600 simulations). Model-based agents also have more parts to tune: a model, its uncertainty, a planner, and a rule for how much to trust each.

Language models. In text generation the transition model is known exactly (the next state is the prompt plus the chosen token), so the open question is only whether decision-time search pays. Attempts exist: {{TS-LLM|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17179}} applied AlphaZero-like tree search to decoding and training, and AlphaProof coupled a pretrained language model with "the AlphaZero reinforcement learning algorithm" to prove olympiad problems in Lean (root Milestones tab). DeepSeek-R1's authors tried MCTS and dropped it: the token search space is exponentially larger than chess, capping each node's expansions traps the search in local optima, and a fine-grained value model is hard to train (see the {{DeepSeek-R1 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}, Failed attempts). An interpretation from the earlier Deep RL page, not a claim of the papers: long chain-of-thought in o1- and R1-style models can be read as a sequential search in token space, with the policy itself proposing and checking steps, and {{OpenAI reported|http://web.archive.org/web/20240913000000/https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/}} that o1 "consistently improves with more reinforcement learning (train-time compute) and with more time spent thinking (test-time compute)". The RL side of this is on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Model-based means a learned model."
Dynamic programming, AlphaGo and AlphaZero are model-based with a given model. Of the methods on this page, Dyna, MuZero, EfficientZero, the world-model agents, PETS and MBPO learn one.
"The reward model makes RLHF model-based."
Nothing plans with it; it stands in for the reward. A model in the RL sense predicts transitions (and rewards) so that an agent can simulate.
"MuZero learns a model of the environment."
It learns a model of what planning needs: rewards, values and policies along action sequences. Its hidden state is never trained to reproduce the observation or to match the true state.
"MCTS needs random rollouts."
Classic MCTS and AlphaGo used them; AlphaGo Zero, AlphaZero and MuZero score each new leaf with a value network and do no rollouts at all.
"Planning is free experience."
It is free of real experience, not of computation: n = 50 Dyna does 50 updates per real step, AlphaGo Zero spent about 0.4 s and 1,600 network evaluations per move, AlphaZero used 5,000 TPUs to generate its games.
"A model with lower one-step error will plan better."
Not necessarily: errors compound over long rollouts and planners exploit them, and one-step likelihood "is not always correlated with control performance" (objective mismatch). MBPO's 200-step rollouts were accurate yet trained worse than short ones.
"Planning with a model explores for you."
A pessimistic model can hide a better option forever: plain Dyna-Q never found the shortcut. Planning needs an exploration incentive such as Dyna-Q+'s κ√τ bonus or R-max's optimism.
"AlphaZero beat Stockfish after 4 hours of training."
Four hours on 5,000 TPUs generating games and 64 more training (arXiv v1), against a Stockfish with a 1 GB hash at one minute per move. The Science version replayed the match at championship settings over 1,000 games: 155 wins, 6 losses.
"AlphaGo Zero trained on 4 TPUs."
Four TPUs is the single machine it played its matches on. The paper does not give the self-play training hardware.
"The final move is the one with the highest value."
It can be, but programs usually play the most visited move, which is robust to a value estimate inflated by a few lucky simulations ({{Sutton and Barto §8.11|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=209}}).

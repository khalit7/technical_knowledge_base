Model-based RL and planning in one screen

A model predicts what the environment will do: given a state and an action, the next state and the reward. Planning is any computation that takes a model and produces or improves a policy, without touching the real environment. Model-free methods learn only from real experience; model-based methods also learn from experience they imagine. Two questions sort every method on this page: where does the model come from (given, as the rules of Go are, or learned from data), and when does the planning happen (in the background, improving values that every later decision uses, or at decision time, searching from the state the agent is in).

The thread through the page
Planning and learning are the same operation, a backup of values, applied to different experience: simulated or real. What separates the methods is where the simulated experience comes from (a table, the rules, a network), where the backups are spent (uniformly, by priority, from the current state), and how far a learned model is trusted before its errors pile up.

1What a model is, and what planning means

"By a model of the environment we mean anything that an agent can use to predict how the environment will respond to its actions. Given a state and an action, a model produces a prediction of the resultant next state and next reward" (Sutton and Barto §8.1). Two kinds:

  • A distribution model gives every possible next state and reward with its probability, p(s′, r | s, a). Dynamic programming needs this kind, because its expected updates sum over all successors.
  • A sample model returns one possible outcome, drawn with the right probability. The book's example is the sum of a dozen dice: a program that rolls twelve dice is easy to write; the table of all sums and their probabilities is "harder and more error-prone". Sample models are what simulators, game engines and most learned models are.

A model has two parts, transitions (where an action leads) and rewards (what it pays), and either can be given or learned. The rules of Go give both exactly. A robot's physics simulator is given but approximate. Dyna learns both as a table; MuZero learns neither in the usual sense, only what its search needs (section 8). Learning a model from data is what control engineers call system identification; Sutton and Barto borrow direct RL (values from real experience) and indirect RL (values through a model) from the adaptive control literature (chapter 8 notes, p. 192).

Planning, in the book's sense, is "any computational process that takes a model as input and produces or improves a policy for interacting with the modeled environment". This page is about state-space planning: search through states, computing values on the way. (Plan-space planning, which edits whole plans, is "difficult to apply efficiently to the stochastic sequential decision problems" of RL, and is left aside.) The chapter's unifying claim has two parts: all state-space planning computes value functions as the step towards a better policy, and it computes them by backups applied to simulated experience. So:

model → simulated experience → backups → values → policy
learning
the same backups applied to real experience from the environment
planning
the same backups applied to experience generated by the model

The smallest example makes the point: random-sample one-step tabular Q-planning picks a state and action at random, asks a sample model for a reward and next state, and applies the Q-learning update to them. It converges to the optimal policy for the model under the same conditions as Q-learning in the real environment (§8.1). The Q-learning rule itself is on Model-free prediction and control.

Two times to plan. In background planning (DP, Dyna), simulated experience gradually improves a value table or network, and an action is later read off it cheaply. In decision-time planning (heuristic search, rollouts, MCTS), planning starts when a new state is reached and its output is one action; most of the computed values are then discarded (§8.8). Decision-time planning suits problems with time to think per move, such as chess; background planning suits those that need fast reactions. AlphaZero does both: search at every move, and a network that learns from the search.

What "model-based" does not mean. It does not mean "learned model": DP and AlphaZero use a given one. And the reward model of RLHF does not make RLHF model-based: nothing plans with it; it stands in for the reward (RL for LLMs).

2Dyna: learning, planning and acting together

Dyna (Sutton 1990, "Integrated architectures for learning, planning, and reacting", cited in Sutton and Barto's chapter 8 notes) is the simplest agent that does all of it at once. Real experience is used twice: for direct RL, updating values at once, and for model learning. The model then generates simulated experience, and planning applies the same RL update to it, "just as if they had really happened". The choice of which simulated transitions to generate is called search control. Learning and planning share "almost all the same machinery, differing only in the source of their experience" (§8.2). The trade-off between the two routes is stated plainly: indirect methods "often make fuller use of a limited amount of experience"; direct methods "are much simpler and are not affected by biases in the design of the model".

Tabular Dyna-Q (Sutton and Barto, p. 164) Initialise Q(s, a) and Model(s, a) for all s, a Loop forever: (a) S ← current state (b) A ← ε-greedy(S, Q) (c) take A; observe R, S′ (d) direct RL: Q(S, A) ← Q(S, A) + α [R + γ max_a Q(S′, a) − Q(S, A)] (e) model learning: Model(S, A) ← R, S′ (deterministic environment assumed) (f) planning, repeat n times: S ← a random previously observed state A ← a random action previously taken in S R, S′ ← Model(S, A) Q(S, A) ← Q(S, A) + α [R + γ max_a Q(S′, a) − Q(S, A)]

Without (e) and (f) this is one-step Q-learning. The model is a table that remembers the last outcome of each state-action pair, and planning only replays pairs actually experienced, so the model is never asked about something it has not seen.

Example 8.1, the Dyna maze. A 6 × 9 grid with 47 open cells, four actions, reward 0 everywhere except +1 for entering the goal, γ = 0.95, α = 0.1, ε = 0.1, ties broken at random, 30 repetitions. In the book, the agent with no planning (n = 0) needed "about 25 episodes" to reach ε-optimal performance, n = 5 about five, and n = 50 "only three episodes" (Example 8.1). In episode 1 nothing can be planned: every value is zero, and the first non-zero value appears only on the final step into the goal. In episode 2 the non-planning agent learns one more step per episode, since a value moves back one cell each time the agent walks through. The planning agent replays remembered transitions 50 times per real step, so the goal's value spreads back along everything it has already walked while it is still wandering near the start. The planning steps are not free: n = 50 does 50 table updates for every real one. Dyna's point is that when real steps are expensive (a robot, a user) and computation is cheap, those updates are worth it. The Dyna lab tab reruns the curves with any n, α and ε.

3When the model is wrong

Dyna's maze model started empty and was only ever filled with correct facts. In general, models are wrong because the environment is stochastic and few samples were seen, because function approximation generalised badly, or because the environment changed (§8.3). The book separates two cases.

  • An optimistic model corrects itself. If the model promises more than the world gives, the planned policy goes to collect it and finds out. In the blocking maze (Example 8.2) a short path to the right of a wall is closed after 1,000 steps and a longer one opens on the left. Both agents stall while they bump into the new wall, then find the new route.
  • A pessimistic model may never be corrected. In the shortcut maze (Example 8.3) the long path on the left stays open and, after 3,000 steps, a shortcut opens on the right. Plain Dyna-Q "never switched to the shortcut. In fact, it never realized that it existed. Its model said that there was no shortcut, so the more it planned, the less likely it was to step to the right and discover it."

Dyna-Q+ treats this as exploration of the model. It records, for each state-action pair, how many steps τ have passed since it was last tried for real, and plans as if that transition paid a bonus:

planned reward = r + κ √τ
r
the reward the model predicts for that transition
τ
real time steps since the pair was last tried
κ
a small constant (the book gives no value; this page uses 10−3)

The bonus is used only in planning updates, so the values themselves become optimistic about long-untested actions and the agent goes to test them, even through long detours. Two more changes are in the book's footnote: actions never tried from a state may be planned with, and their initial model leads back to the same state with reward 0.

Exploration bonuses taken "to its logical extreme" become provably efficient model-based algorithms: E3 (Kearns and Singh 2002) and R-max (Brafman and Tennenholtz 2003) assume every incompletely explored choice is maximally rewarding and plan to test it, and find a near-optimal policy in time polynomial in the number of states and actions; "usually too slow for practical algorithms but ... probably the best that can be done in the worst case" (chapter 8 notes). Exploration in general is on Bandits and exploration.

4Where to spend planning: priorities, samples and trajectories

Dyna picks remembered transitions uniformly at random, which is wasteful: at the start of episode 2 of the maze only the pair leading into the goal has a non-zero value, so almost every replayed transition moves zero into zero. Three refinements decide where backups go. They are covered as forms of asynchronous DP on Dynamic programming (section 14 and its Sweep lab, which races them on this same maze); here is what each adds to a planning agent.

  • Prioritised sweeping (§8.4; Moore and Atkeson 1993, Peng and Williams 1993) keeps a queue of state-action pairs ordered by how much their value would change, |R + γ maxa Q(S′, a) − Q(S, A)|, and after each update pushes the predecessors of the changed state. It works backwards from wherever values changed, so the model must also predict predecessors. On mazes of the Dyna maze's shape at growing resolution it reached the optimal solution with fewer updates, "often by a factor of 5 to 10" (Example 8.4, with at most n = 5 updates per real step).
  • Sample against expected updates (§8.5). An expected update of one pair costs about b times a sample update, where b is the number of possible next states. With equally likely successors and correct successor values, t sample updates leave an error of √((b − 1)/(b t)) of the initial error, so for large b a small fraction of b samples already removes most of it. The book concludes sample updates are likely better "on problems with large stochastic branching factors and too many states to be solved exactly". Learned models in deep RL are sample models for this reason.
  • Trajectory sampling and RTDP (§8.6 to 8.7). Simulating whole trajectories under the current policy puts updates where the policy goes. In the book's random tasks this helped early and hurt late, and helped more with more states and fewer branches; real-time DP solved a racetrack with about half the updates of sweeping value iteration. Both are on the Dynamic programming page.

Decision-time planning, next, is the extreme of this forward focusing: all computation goes to the state the agent is in.

5Decision-time planning: heuristic search, rollouts, Monte Carlo tree search

Heuristic search, the classical AI planner, builds a tree of continuations from the current state, applies an approximate value function at the leaves and backs values up to the root with max backups, then picks the best root action and throws the rest away (§8.9). With a perfect model and an imperfect value function, deeper search usually gives better moves, because errors in the leaf values are pushed further away; Tesauro found TD-Gammon played better the deeper it searched, at the price of time per move. The book's observation: a search tree is just many one-step backups ordered from the leaves up, so its strength comes not from multi-step updates as such but from "the focus and concentration of updates on states and actions immediately downstream from the current state".

Rollout algorithms (§8.10) estimate each action's value at the current state by averaging the returns of many simulated episodes that start with that action and then follow a fixed rollout policy, act on the best, and repeat from the next state. By the policy improvement theorem (on Dynamic programming), acting greedily on qπ(s, ·) is at least as good as π, so a rollout algorithm is one step of policy iteration applied at one state; its aim "is to improve upon the rollout policy; not to find an optimal policy". The name comes from backgammon, where rolling a position out with random dice estimates its equity. Tesauro and Galperin ran it on IBM supercomputers over base players from random to TD-Gammon and reported error rates reduced "by as much as a factor of 5 or more"; "potentially 80% or more of the base player's equity loss can be eliminated" (NIPS 1996).

Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) is a rollout algorithm that remembers. It keeps value estimates for the state-action pairs near the root, in a tree it grows as it goes, so later simulations follow the beginnings of earlier good ones. Its central ideas are from 2006: Coulom's backup operators that move from averaging to min-max as simulations grow, in the program Crazy Stone (Coulom 2006; the survey credits him with the name), and Kocsis and Szepesvári's UCT (2006). MCTS "is largely responsible for the improvement in computer Go from a weak amateur level in 2005 to a grandmaster level (6 dan or more) in 2015" (§8.11). Each iteration has four steps:

  1. Selection. From the root, a tree policy picks actions using the statistics on the tree's edges until it reaches a leaf.
  2. Expansion. One or more children are added for untried actions (on some iterations).
  3. Simulation. From the new node a complete episode is played with the rollout policy (or, since AlphaGo Zero, the leaf is simply evaluated by a network).
  4. Backup. The result updates the visit counts and value estimates of every edge on the path; nothing is stored for the rollout beyond the tree.

When time runs out the agent plays the root action with the largest value or, more robustly, the most visits. The tree policy is a bandit at every node. UCT applies UCB1 (derived on Bandits and exploration) to each node as its own bandit:

UCT:  a = argmaxa [ Q(s, a) + c √( ln N(s) / N(s, a) ) ]     PUCT:  a = argmaxa [ Q(s, a) + c P(s, a) √N(s) / (1 + N(s, a)) ]
Q(s, a)
mean result of the simulations through that edge, for the player to move at s
N(s), N(s, a)
visits to the node and to the edge
c
exploration constant: UCT's 2Cp with Cp = 1/√2 for rewards in [0, 1], which gives UCB1's √2; Kocsis and Szepesvári proved the failure probability at the root goes to zero ("consistent")
P(s, a)
a prior from a policy network; PUCT, the AlphaGo family's rule, explores where the prior points and needs no rollouts once a value network scores the leaves

The visit counts at the root, normalised, are the search policy π; in AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero they are the training target for the network's prior p, which is how search improves the policy and the network distils it (section 7).

6AlphaGo (2016): networks inside the search

Go's rules are a perfect model, but the tree is too wide and deep for search alone; the strongest programs were MCTS with hand-made rollout policies, such as Pachi, ranked 2 amateur dan on KGS. AlphaGo (Silver et al., Nature 529, 2016; free PDF) put four learned functions into MCTS:

  • SL policy network pσ: 13 layers, trained on 30 million positions from the KGS Go Server to predict the expert's move; 57.0% accurate with all input features (55.7% from the raw board and history), against 44.4% for the previous best.
  • Rollout policy pπ: a linear softmax over small patterns; 24.2% accurate but "using just 2 μs to select an action, rather than 3 ms for the policy network". About 1,000 full-game rollouts per second per search thread.
  • RL policy network pρ: the SL network improved by policy-gradient self-play (50 GPUs, one day). It won "more than 80%" of games against the SL network and, with no search at all, 85% against Pachi, an MCTS program doing 100,000 simulations per move.
  • Value network vθ: predicts the winner from a position (15 layers, 50 GPUs for one week). Trained on full human games it memorised outcomes (test MSE 0.37 against 0.19 on training data), so the team generated 30 million positions "each sampled from a separate game" of RL-policy self-play: MSE 0.226 train, 0.234 test.

In the search, the SL network's move probabilities become the prior P(s, a) of a PUCT rule (cpuct = 5), and each leaf is scored by mixing the value network with one fast rollout:

V(sL) = (1 − λ) vθ(sL) + λ zL,   λ = 0.5
vθ(sL)
the value network's prediction at the leaf
zL
the result of a rollout from the leaf with the fast rollout policy

The mixed evaluation won at least 95% of games against the variants with only the value network (λ = 0) or only rollouts (λ = 1); even λ = 0 beat every other Go program. Two findings are counter-intuitive and worth remembering. The weaker SL policy made the better prior: "presumably because humans select a diverse beam of promising moves, whereas RL optimizes for the single best move". But the value network trained on the stronger RL policy's games made the better evaluator. The match program ran 40 search threads on 48 CPUs and 8 GPUs; the distributed version used 1,202 CPUs and 176 GPUs. It won 494 of 495 games against other programs and beat the European champion Fan Hui 5-0 in October 2015; a larger version, AlphaGo Lee on 48 TPUs, beat Lee Sedol 4-1 in March 2016. Those results and their compute sit in the root's Milestones and benchmarks tab of Topic: rl; Sutton and Barto tell the whole story in §16.6.

7AlphaGo Zero and AlphaZero: search as the policy improvement operator

AlphaGo used search to play, after learning. AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., Nature 550, 2017; author copy) used search to learn, at every move of self-play. It differed in four ways: no human data, starting from random play; only the stones as input; one network fθ(s) = (p, v) with a policy head and a value head; and a search with no rollouts, every leaf scored by the network. The loop is policy iteration (Dynamic programming) with MCTS in the improvement step: "MCTS may therefore be viewed as a powerful policy improvement operator", and self-play with search, scored by the game's winner, "a powerful policy evaluation operator".

ℓ = (z − v)² − π⊤ log p + c‖θ‖²
z
the game's result from the player to move's view: +1, 0 (AlphaZero, which allows draws), −1
v
the network's predicted value
π
the search's visit distribution at that position
p
the network's move probabilities
c
L2 weight decay, 10−4; the two loss terms are weighted equally

Worked example (illustrative numbers, from the earlier Deep RL page). The player went on to win (z = 1), the network predicted v = 0.6, search visited three moves in proportions (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) and the network gave them (0.5, 0.3, 0.2). The value term is (1 − 0.6)² = 0.16; the policy term is −(0.7 ln 0.5 + 0.2 ln 0.3 + 0.1 ln 0.2) = 0.4852 + 0.2408 + 0.1609 = 0.887; the loss before weight decay is 1.047. For a softmax output the gradient of the policy term with respect to the logits is p − π = (−0.2, 0.1, 0.1): the update raises the move search liked and lowers the others. Search improves, the network distils, and the better network makes the next search stronger.

The details that made it work (AlphaGo Zero Methods): 1,600 simulations per move, about 0.4 s; moves sampled in proportion to visit counts for the first 30 moves of a game (temperature τ = 1), then the most visited; Dirichlet noise at the root, P = 0.75 p + 0.25 η with η ∼ Dir(0.03), so every move can be tried "but the search may still overrule bad moves"; resignation, with the threshold set so fewer than 5% of resigned games could have been won (checked by playing out 10% of them); and an evaluator gate: a new network replaced the self-play player only if it won more than 55% of 400 games. The 3-day run generated 4.9 million games, updated on 700,000 mini-batches of 2,048 positions with a 20-block residual network, passed AlphaGo Lee after 36 hours and beat it 100-0 after 72 hours, playing on a single machine with 4 TPUs (the paper does not give the training hardware). The 40-day run, 29 million games, beat AlphaGo Master 89-11 and reached an Elo of 5,185. The same network with no search at all rated 3,055: search was worth about 2,100 Elo on top of the network that learned from it.

AlphaZero (Silver et al. 2017, arXiv v1) removed what was specific to Go: it optimises the expected outcome, so draws count; it does not augment data with board symmetries, since chess and shogi are not symmetric; it keeps one network updated continually, with no evaluator gate; and it uses the same hyperparameters for every game except the Dirichlet noise, α = 0.3, 0.15 and 0.03 for chess, shogi and Go ("scaled in inverse proportion to the approximate number of legal moves"). From random play and given no domain knowledge except the rules, it reached superhuman play in chess, shogi and Go "within 24 hours" of training and defeated a world-champion program in each (Stockfish, Elmo and the 3-day AlphaGo Zero). It used 800 simulations per move and searched about 80 thousand positions per second in chess, against 70 million for Stockfish. Results and compute are on the root's Milestones tab: 700,000 steps of 4,096 positions, 5,000 first-generation TPUs generating games and 64 second-generation TPUs training; it passed Stockfish in chess "after just 4 hours (300k steps)", which is 4 hours on those 5,000 TPUs.

The two AlphaZero papers differ: quote one
The preprint (arXiv v1, December 2017) and the peer-reviewed paper (Science 362, 2018; author preprint) give different numbers. Training TPUs: 64 second-generation in v1, 16 in Science. Search speed: 80,000 against Stockfish's 70 million positions per second in v1; 60,000 against 60 million in Science. The match: v1 played 100 games against Stockfish with 64 threads, a 1 GB hash and one minute per move (28 wins, 72 draws, 0 losses); Science played 1,000 games at its 2016 TCEC superfinal settings (44 threads, a 32 GB hash, 3 hours plus 15 seconds per move) and AlphaZero won 155 and lost 6. Science also gives training times of about 9 hours (chess), 12 hours (shogi) and 13 days (Go). This page and the root's Milestones tab use v1's numbers where they quote one.

Our small replication (the Self-play lab): the same loop on tic-tac-toe, with a one-hidden-layer network (64 units) instead of a residual tower, 50 simulations per move, 48 games per iteration, 60 iterations: 2,880 games, about half a minute on a laptop CPU. The network's top move was optimal (by exact game-tree solution) in 53.7% of the 4,520 non-terminal positions at random initialisation, 94.3% at the end; with a 50-simulation search on top, 95.8% and 99.0%. Against a perfect opponent over 100 games, the untrained network lost 48 playing on its prior alone and 34 with search; from iteration 10 on it lost none, with or without search.

8MuZero (2019): planning with a learned model of what matters

AlphaZero needs the rules to step its search. MuZero (Schrittwieser et al. 2019; Nature 588, 2020) learns a model instead, with three functions in one network:

s0 = hθ(o1, ..., ot)    rk, sk = gθ(sk−1, ak)    pk, vk = fθ(sk)
h (representation)
encodes the past observations into a hidden root state
g (dynamics)
from a hidden state and a candidate action, predicts the immediate reward and the next hidden state
f (prediction)
from a hidden state, a policy and a value, as AlphaZero's network does from a real position

The search is AlphaZero's, run on hidden states: expanding a node calls g instead of the rules. The key design choice is what the hidden state is not trained to do. There is "no direct constraint or requirement for the hidden state to capture all information necessary to reconstruct the original observation ... nor is there any requirement for the hidden state to match the unknown, true state of the environment". It only has to make the predicted rewards, values and policies right along imagined action sequences. A model of this kind is called value-equivalent (Grimm et al. 2020): it may be wrong about everything planning does not use.

Training. From a real trajectory in the replay buffer, the model starts at s0 = h(observations up to t) and is unrolled K = 5 steps with the actions actually taken. At every unrolled step k three predictions are matched to targets:

ℓt(θ) = Σk=0..K [ ℓr(ut+k, rk) + ℓv(zt+k, vk) + ℓp(πt+k, pk) ] + c‖θ‖²
u
the reward actually observed
z
the value target: an n-step return bootstrapped from the search value, zt = ut+1 + γut+2 + ... + γn−1ut+n + γnνt+n, with n = 10 in Atari; in board games, the final outcome
π
the visit distribution of the search that was run at that real step
losses
squared error for reward and value in board games, as AlphaZero; cross-entropy over a categorical representation in Atari, "more stable" with values of variable scale; cross-entropy for the policy

Search settings: 800 simulations per move in board games and 50 in Atari (searching every fourth frame and repeating the action four times). The selection rule is PUCT with a slowly growing exploration weight, c1 + log((ΣN + c2 + 1)/c2) with c1 = 1.25 and c2 = 19,652, and since Atari values are unbounded, Q is rescaled to [0, 1] by the minimum and maximum seen in the tree so far. The dynamics are deterministic; stochastic transitions were "left for future work".

Results (with compute on the root's Milestones tab). Without the rules it matched AlphaZero's superhuman play in Go, chess and shogi, and on 57 Atari games, "in which model-based planning approaches have historically struggled", it set a new state of the art: median 2,041.1% and mean 4,999.2% of human-normalised score, ahead of R2D2 on 42 of 57 games, from 20 billion frames per game. MuZero Reanalyze re-runs the search on old trajectories with the latest network to refresh the targets (used for 80% of policy targets, with n = 5); with 200 million frames it reached a median of 731%, against 192%, 231% and 431% for IMPALA, Rainbow and LASER. Three experiments in the paper say how much the learned model is worth:

  • Go, more search than trained for: trained at 800 simulations (about 0.1 s), the learned model kept improving with searches up to 10 s, "two orders of magnitude longer", and matched a perfect simulator.
  • Atari, less gain from search: improvements "plateaued at around 100 simulations", "perhaps because of greater model inaccuracy"; even 1 simulation (the policy network alone) played well, "suggesting that ... the raw policy has learned to internalise the benefits of search".
  • Ms. Pac-Man, very little search: trained with only 6 simulations per move, fewer than the 8 actions, it still learned an effective policy; a model-free variant with the same network matched R2D2 but learned much more slowly than MuZero, which the authors attribute to search giving "a stronger learning signal" than Q-learning targets.

On the root's taxonomy axes (the Taxonomy and Method atlas tabs of Topic: rl): AlphaGo and AlphaZero store a policy and a value (actor-critic heads), use a given model (the rules), learn from self-play, and act in discrete action spaces. MuZero stores the same two heads plus a learned latent model, and learns from its own games replayed from a buffer (self-play and replay), also with discrete actions. The trade-off in one line: with a known model and a combinatorial search space, AlphaZero-style search; with no model but a need to plan, MuZero, at a large compute cost per decision.

9EfficientZero (2021): MuZero with little data

MuZero used billions of frames. The Atari 100k benchmark allows 100,000 agent steps per game (400,000 frames, about two hours of play) on 26 games. EfficientZero (Ye et al. 2021) made "three critical changes" to MuZero:

  1. Temporal consistency. A self-supervised loss (SimSiam-style, as SPR) makes the hidden state predicted by g agree with the encoding h of the observation that really followed. MuZero's model gets learning signal only from rewards, values and policies, which are sparse with little data.
  2. Value prefix. Instead of predicting each step's reward and summing (so that errors add up), an LSTM over the unrolled hidden states predicts the sum of rewards so far directly, "helping to alleviate the compounding error in the model".
  3. Model-based off-policy correction. Old trajectories were collected by older policies, so their n-step value targets are stale; EfficientZero uses fewer real rewards the older the data, and redoes a search with the current network at the last state to bootstrap.

It reached 194.3% mean and 109.0% median human-normalised score (Table 1 of the current version), which the authors call "the first time an algorithm achieves super-human performance on Atari games with such little data"; for comparison, the same table gives 70.4% and 41.5% for SPR and 56.2% and 22.7% for MuZero at the same budget; the authors add that this is "close to DQN's performance at 200 million frames while we consume 500 times less data". The paper gives no training time or hardware; the code's instructions use 4 GPUs.

EfficientZero's numbers moved between versions
The first arXiv version's abstract says 190.4% mean and 116.0% median; the current version's abstract and Table 1 say 194.3% and 109.0%, while its Section 5.2 still prints the old 1.904 and 1.160. An independent rerun in the BBF paper gives a median of 1.116 (Schwarzer et al. 2023). Atari 100k results also depend on how runs are aggregated (Agarwal et al. 2021): the root's Milestones tab keeps each table's protocol separate.

10Learned world models: World Models, PlaNet, Dreamer

A second line learns a model of the observations themselves, compressed into a small latent state, and trains or plans inside it. Unlike MuZero's, these models are usually trained to reconstruct what they see, which gives them a dense learning signal even when rewards are rare.

World Models (Ha and Schmidhuber 2018; interactive version) split the agent in three: V, a variational autoencoder compressing each frame to a latent z (32 numbers for CarRacing, 64 for Doom); M, a recurrent network (256 hidden units for CarRacing, 512 for Doom) predicting the next z as a mixture of 5 Gaussians; and C, a linear controller from [z, h] to the action with "a mere 867 parameters", trained by the evolution strategy CMA-ES. On CarRacing-v0, where 900 counts as solved, it scored 906 ± 21 over 100 trials. For DoomTakeCover the controller was trained entirely inside the dream generated by M and then run in the real game. The agent found that the dream could be gamed: it learned to move so that the dream's monsters never fired ("Cheating the World Model"). Making the dream noisier with a temperature τ fixed that; the trade-off is in their Table 2:

PlaNet (Hafner et al. 2019) plans instead of learning a policy: a recurrent state-space model (RSSM) with both a deterministic and a stochastic path ("crucial for high planning performance"), and model-predictive control in latent space with the cross-entropy method: horizon 12, 10 iterations, 1,000 candidate action sequences, refit to the best 100, replanning at every step. On six DeepMind Control tasks from pixels it used 1,000 episodes where A3C and D4PG used 100,000. It proposed a multi-step training objective, latent overshooting, but the final RSSM agent "does not require it".

PlaNet: 50 or 200 times?
The first preprint and Google's blog ("5000% more data efficient") say 50 times less environment interaction; the final ICML 2019 version says "on average 200× less", with Table 1's per-task gains over D4PG ranging from 40 to over 500.

Dreamer (Hafner et al. 2019, ICLR 2020) keeps the RSSM but learns an actor and a critic purely from imagined trajectories in latent space, "propagating analytic gradients of learned state values back through trajectories imagined" by the model, with an imagination horizon of 15 and λ-returns (γ = 0.99, λ = 0.95). On 20 visual control tasks it averaged 823 after 5 × 106 environment steps, against 332 for PlaNet and 786 for the model-free D4PG after 108 steps, at about 3 hours per million steps on one V100 (PlaNet 11, D4PG 24 to reach similar performance). This is Dyna's idea, learning behaviour on simulated experience, scaled to images (the root's Method atlas lists Dreamer as Dyna-Q's descendant).

DreamerV2 (Hafner et al. 2020) replaced the Gaussian latents with 32 categorical variables of 32 classes each, and added KL balancing (the prior is pulled towards the posterior faster than the reverse, weights 0.8 and 0.2). It "constitutes the first agent that achieves human-level performance on the Atari benchmark of 55 tasks by learning behaviors inside a separately trained world model": gamer-normalised median 2.15 at 200 million frames with sticky actions, against 1.47 for Rainbow and 1.29 for IQN, in under 10 days on one V100. Over those 50 million real inputs it learned from 468 billion imagined states, 10,000 times more.

DreamerV3 (Hafner et al. 2023; Nature 640, 2025) is about robustness: one configuration "outperforms specialized methods across over 150 diverse tasks". The changes are mostly about scale: symlog targets, sign(x) ln(|x| + 1), so rewards and values of any magnitude train the same networks; free bits, no penalty on the KL terms below 1 nat; and return normalisation by the 5th to 95th percentile range, only ever scaling large returns down. In Minecraft it is "the first algorithm to collect diamonds ... from scratch without human data or curricula" (the Nature version adds "to our knowledge"), within 100 million environment steps, on 1 GPU for 9 days; the paper contrasts VPT's 720 GPUs for 9 days with human video. Larger models, from 12 million to 400 million parameters, scored higher and needed less environment interaction, shown on Crafter and one DMLab task.

11Model-predictive control, PETS and MBPO

Model-predictive control (MPC) comes from control engineering: "the current control action is obtained by solving, at each sampling instant, a finite horizon open-loop optimal control problem, using the current state of the plant as the initial state; the optimization yields an optimal control sequence and the first control in this sequence is applied to the plant" (Mayne et al. 2000, abstract). It is decision-time planning with a receding horizon, and replanning every step is what keeps model errors from accumulating past a few steps. With a learned neural model there is no gradient-friendly solver, so the optimisation is done by sampling: random shooting (try many random action sequences, keep the best first action) or the cross-entropy method (CEM: sample, keep the best few, refit a Gaussian, repeat).

PETS (Chua et al. 2018, probabilistic ensembles with trajectory sampling) made this work on standard continuous-control benchmarks. Each of 5 networks ("We found B = 5 sufficient") outputs a Gaussian over the next state, capturing aleatoric uncertainty, the noise in the data; the disagreement between the 5, each trained on its own bootstrap sample, captures epistemic uncertainty, "our subjective uncertainty due to a lack of data", which is largest away from the data. Planning propagates particles through randomly chosen members and optimises with CEM. It "matches the asymptotic performance of model-free algorithms on several challenging benchmark tasks, while requiring significantly fewer samples (e.g., 8 and 125 times fewer samples than Soft Actor Critic and Proximal Policy Optimization respectively on the half-cheetah task)".

MBPO (Janner et al. 2019, "When to Trust Your Model") is Dyna with a neural ensemble and SAC: from states in the real replay buffer it runs short branched rollouts of k steps in the model and trains SAC mostly on that model data. Its analysis bounds the true return of a policy by its return under k-step branched rollouts minus a penalty; the model-error part of the penalty is

2 rmax · k (εm + 2επ) / (1 − γ)
k
rollout length in the model
εm
the model's error, a bound on the total-variation distance between its next-state distribution and the true one
επ
how far the policy has moved from the one that collected the data

Error grows linearly with the rollout length while the benefit of model data does not, so the bound favours short rollouts; taken literally it is best at k = 0, and a refined version, using the model's error on the new policy's own states, justifies some k > 0 when that error is low. In practice MBPO used k = 1 on three tasks and schedules growing to 15 or 25 on the others, an ensemble of 7, 400 model rollouts and 20 to 40 policy updates per real step. "MBPO's performance on the Ant task at 300 thousand steps matches that of SAC at 3 million steps", "an order of magnitude faster on some tasks", with comparable final performance. On Hopper its model was accurate enough for 200-step rollouts, yet they trained worse than short ones, and "500-step rollouts are too inaccurate for effective learning". MBPO's own description of where it comes from: the branched rollout "resembles the scheme proposed in the original Dyna algorithm ... which can be viewed as a special case of a length 1 branched rollouts".

12Why model error compounds, and what to do about it

A model trained to predict one step is used to predict many, by feeding its own output back in. "If there is any error or approximation in the one-step predictions, then it can compound to make the long-term predictions wildly inaccurate. In practice this is often what happens" (Sutton and Barto §17.3). Three reasons, each with its source:

  • Errors feed on themselves. "A flawed sample is given as input to the model, which causes more errors, and so on" (Talvitie 2014). Worse, the model is now asked about states it was never trained on: compounding errors "change the input distribution for future prediction steps, breaking the train-test i.i.d assumption" (Venkatraman et al. 2015), whose bound on the T-step error grows exponentially in T when the dynamics expand errors (Lipschitz constant above 1), and is tight.
  • The planner seeks the errors out. An optimiser maximising return under the model will find wherever the model is too optimistic: the Doom agent that stopped the dream's monsters firing (section 10).
  • One-step accuracy is the wrong target. "The likelihood of one-step ahead predictions is not always correlated with control performance" (Lambert et al. 2020, objective mismatch).

The animation shows the first effect on real numbers. The environment is Gymnasium's Pendulum-v1 (source: θ = 0 upright, g = 10, time step 0.05 s, torque in [−2, 2]). The model is an ensemble of five small networks (two layers of 24 units) that this page fitted, PETS-style on bootstrap resamples, to 2,000 transitions from random torques, about what an agent has early on (src/fit_pendulum.py). Each network's one-step angle error averages 0.0016 rad. Both pendulums below get the same 50 random torques.

Error by rollout length over 200 random starts: five ensemble members, their spread, and branched rollouts

Three things to read off. The error grows from 0.0016 rad after one step to 0.044 after 25 and 0.20 after 50, 125 times the one-step error, and the mean hides a heavy tail: for network 1 the median error at step 50 is 0.024 rad but 11 of the 200 rollouts are off by more than 0.5 rad, the ones that went over the top the wrong way. The spread between the five networks grows with the error (0.18 rad at step 50), which is why ensembles help: disagreement flags where the model should not be trusted. And restarting from real states every 5 steps keeps the mean error over 50 steps at 0.0044 rad, against 0.071 open loop: short branched rollouts (MBPO) and replanning every step (MPC) are the same remedy.

The remedies, in one list. Plan short and replan often (MPC, MBPO). Measure uncertainty and stay where the model is confident (PETS's ensembles). Make the dream harder than reality (World Models' temperature). Learn only what planning needs, so the model spends no capacity on irrelevant detail (MuZero, value equivalence). Ground the model in real targets (EfficientZero's consistency loss and value prefix). Prefer search in a perfect model whenever one exists (AlphaZero). Sutton and Barto's verdict on the general problem: "cases of full model-based reinforcement learning, in which the environment model is learned from data and then used for planning, are rare ... More work is needed before planning with learned models can be effective" (§17.5).

Planning tends to pay when real experience is expensive and computation is not (robots, physical systems, users); when a perfect or good model already exists and the problem is combinatorial (board games, puzzles, theorem proving), so decision-time search is cheap to trust; when rewards are sparse and planning can carry them back faster than real experience (Dyna's maze); and when the same model can be reused for new goals. It tends not to pay when a fast simulator is available, so data is nearly free and model-free methods can be run at scale (Dactyl, OpenAI Five); when the environment is hard to model and the planner will exploit the gaps; and when actions must be chosen faster than a search can run (AlphaGo Zero took about 0.4 s per move at 1,600 simulations). Model-based agents also have more parts to tune: a model, its uncertainty, a planner, and a rule for how much to trust each.

Language models. In text generation the transition model is known exactly (the next state is the prompt plus the chosen token), so the open question is only whether decision-time search pays. Attempts exist: TS-LLM applied AlphaZero-like tree search to decoding and training, and AlphaProof coupled a pretrained language model with "the AlphaZero reinforcement learning algorithm" to prove olympiad problems in Lean (root Milestones tab). DeepSeek-R1's authors tried MCTS and dropped it: the token search space is exponentially larger than chess, capping each node's expansions traps the search in local optima, and a fine-grained value model is hard to train (see the DeepSeek-R1 paper page, Failed attempts). An interpretation from the earlier Deep RL page, not a claim of the papers: long chain-of-thought in o1- and R1-style models can be read as a sequential search in token space, with the policy itself proposing and checking steps, and OpenAI reported that o1 "consistently improves with more reinforcement learning (train-time compute) and with more time spent thinking (test-time compute)". The RL side of this is on RL for LLMs.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Model-based means a learned model."
Dynamic programming, AlphaGo and AlphaZero are model-based with a given model. Of the methods on this page, Dyna, MuZero, EfficientZero, the world-model agents, PETS and MBPO learn one.
"The reward model makes RLHF model-based."
Nothing plans with it; it stands in for the reward. A model in the RL sense predicts transitions (and rewards) so that an agent can simulate.
"MuZero learns a model of the environment."
It learns a model of what planning needs: rewards, values and policies along action sequences. Its hidden state is never trained to reproduce the observation or to match the true state.
"MCTS needs random rollouts."
Classic MCTS and AlphaGo used them; AlphaGo Zero, AlphaZero and MuZero score each new leaf with a value network and do no rollouts at all.
"Planning is free experience."
It is free of real experience, not of computation: n = 50 Dyna does 50 updates per real step, AlphaGo Zero spent about 0.4 s and 1,600 network evaluations per move, AlphaZero used 5,000 TPUs to generate its games.
"A model with lower one-step error will plan better."
Not necessarily: errors compound over long rollouts and planners exploit them, and one-step likelihood "is not always correlated with control performance" (objective mismatch). MBPO's 200-step rollouts were accurate yet trained worse than short ones.
"Planning with a model explores for you."
A pessimistic model can hide a better option forever: plain Dyna-Q never found the shortcut. Planning needs an exploration incentive such as Dyna-Q+'s κ√τ bonus or R-max's optimism.
"AlphaZero beat Stockfish after 4 hours of training."
Four hours on 5,000 TPUs generating games and 64 more training (arXiv v1), against a Stockfish with a 1 GB hash at one minute per move. The Science version replayed the match at championship settings over 1,000 games: 155 wins, 6 losses.
"AlphaGo Zero trained on 4 TPUs."
Four TPUs is the single machine it played its matches on. The paper does not give the self-play training hardware.
"The final move is the one with the highest value."
It can be, but programs usually play the most visited move, which is robust to a value estimate inflated by a few lucky simulations (Sutton and Barto §8.11).

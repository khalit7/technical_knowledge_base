10Rainbow, and what its ablation shows

{{Rainbow (Hessel et al. 2017)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02298}} asked whether the six extensions "are complementary and can be fruitfully combined", putting each where the blue tags of the figure at the top show; they had to be adapted to each other (closed block below: prioritised by the distributional loss, dueling per atom, double on the n-step target).

How the six were adapted to each other (under 1 min)
  • Distributional and multi-step: the target distribution is the n-th next state's, shrunk by γn and shifted by the n-step discounted reward, projected onto the 51 atoms; the loss is the KL divergence.
  • Double: the online network chooses the greedy action at st+n; its distribution comes from the target network.
  • Prioritised: by that KL loss (proportional, exponent 0.5) rather than a TD error, "since this is what the algorithm is minimizing".
  • Dueling: value and advantage streams each output 51 logits, combined per atom as in dueling DQN, then a softmax.
  • Noisy: every linear layer becomes a factorised noisy layer, and ε = 0.

Results. Rainbow matched DQN's final performance after 7M frames (under 10 hours on one GPU) and passed the baselines' best final performance after 44M; a full 200M-frame run took about 10 days. Median human-normalised score of the best agent snapshot (Table 2): 223% with no-op starts and 153% with human starts, against DQN 79% and 68%; the best single extension, distributional DQN, reached 164% and 125%.

The ablation removes one component at a time (57 games, 200M frames each). The text: "Prioritized replay and multi-step learning were the two most crucial components" (removing either dropped the median a lot; multi-step also hurt final performance); distributional Q-learning "ranked immediately below", no different for the first 40 million frames and lagging afterwards, mostly on games at or above human level; removing Noisy Nets made the median worse but helped a few games; and "in aggregate, we did not observe a significant difference when removing the dueling network" or double Q-learning. The authors' hypothesis for double Q-learning: returns often exceeded 10, outside the [−10, 10] support, so "clipping the values to this constrained range counteracts the overestimation bias", though "the importance of double Q-learning may increase if the support of the distributions is expanded".

Figure 4 gives the per-game evidence: the area under each ablated agent's learning curve, normalised so that 0 is as good as Rainbow and 1 as bad as DQN (two games where DQN beats Rainbow are omitted, leaving 55). The PDF draws every bar as a vector rectangle, so all 330 heights (6 components × 55 games) were decoded exactly decoded from the figure. Predict first: the ablation is often quoted as "prioritised replay and multi-step matter, the rest barely do". In how many of the 55 games did removing double Q-learning make Rainbow worse?

11Scaling out: Ape-X, R2D2 and Agent57

After Rainbow the value-based line on Atari moved to distributed agents that separate acting from learning: many actors fill one replay buffer, one learner trains on it. Ape-X ({{Horgan et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.00933}}) used 360 actors and reached a 434% median after 22.8 billion frames. R2D2 ({{Kapturowski et al. 2019|https://openreview.net/forum?id=r1lyTjAqYX}}) added an LSTM and replays sequences, warming the stale hidden state up with a burn-in before training, and replaced reward clipping with value rescaling: 1920.6% median. Agent57 ({{Badia et al. 2020b|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350}}) is "the first deep RL agent that outperforms the standard human benchmark on all 57 Atari games".

How each one works, with its numbers (2 min)
  • Ape-X: 360 actor processes, each with its own environment, write into one shared prioritised replay, and one GPU learner trains on it (Double DQN with dueling and n-step targets). Actors compute initial priorities themselves, so new data needs no learner pass. Actor i explores with εi = ε1 + iα/(N−1) (ε = 0.4, α = 7), a spread from greedy to very exploratory. Median 434% with no-op starts after 22.8 billion frames in 5 days (its Table 1).
  • R2D2 (PDF read via the {{Wayback Machine|http://web.archive.org/web/20190211025644id_/https://openreview.net/pdf?id=r1lyTjAqYX}}): an LSTM after the convolutions; replays fixed-length sequences of 80 steps (overlapping by 40); stores the actor's hidden state and spends a burn-in of 40 steps unrolling the network before training on the rest. 256 actors, n = 5 double Q-learning, γ = 0.997, priorities mixing the max and mean TD error over the sequence (η = 0.9); a lost life is not an episode end, and rewards are not clipped: values are rescaled with h(x) = sign(x)(√(|x| + 1) − 1) + εx, ε = 10−3 (from Pohlen et al. 2018), which compresses a return of 1,000 to about 31.6 derived. Its Table 1: median 1920.6% and mean 4024.9% over 57 games, final score averaged over 3 seeds; the feed-forward variant reached 589.2%.
  • NGU and Agent57 ({{Badia et al. 2020a|https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06038}}; 2020b): R2D2 still scored near zero on the hardest exploration games. Never Give Up adds an intrinsic novelty reward and learns a family of policies, each with its own weight on that reward and its own discount. Agent57 separates intrinsic and extrinsic value heads, trains 32 such policies, and lets a sliding-window UCB bandit choose which one each actor runs; Skiing, the last game, fell after 78 billion frames. The bandit is on {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}.

Scores for Ape-X, R2D2, MuZero and Agent57, with protocols, frame counts and the per-game recomputation, are on the parent's {{Milestones and benchmarks|https://app.notion.com/p/3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}} tab. That tab found that Agent57's per-game table prints two of MuZero's scores with an extra digit (Asteroids and Beam Rider), which reorders the medians, and notes that R2D2's paper could not be fetched there; it is quoted here from the archived PDF, and its printed 1920.6% matches the figure MuZero and NGU reprint. Agent57 is above the human benchmark on all 57 games, which MuZero is not (51); MuZero has the higher mean and median, so which is "better" depends on the aggregate.

12Reading Atari numbers: protocols and pitfalls

Every score on this page is a human-normalised score: (agent − random) / (human − random) per game ({{van Hasselt et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461}}, equation 5), aggregated by a median or a mean. The formula hides a dozen choices, and two papers rarely make them alike. The same agent, DQN, has at least four published medians: 93.5%, 79.1%, 68.5% and 47.5%.

Nine printed medians for three agents: the same agent moves by a factor of two with the protocol

Each bar is a printed median from the table named, with its protocol. They share an axis only to show the spread; reading any two as a comparison of agents is the mistake this section is about. Compare only within one table, or recompute from per-game scores under one protocol.

The choices that move the number most: no-op starts (up to 30 random "do nothing" actions) against human starts (100 start points from expert play), whose gap Rainbow reads as "the extent to which the agent has over-fit to its own trajectories" (223% against 153%); the episode cap (5 or 30 minutes); a deterministic emulator against sticky actions ({{Machado et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06009}}); the best snapshot during training against the final agent; and the number of seeds. {{Agarwal et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13264}} find "substantial uncertainty in median scores even with 50 runs" and recommend stratified bootstrap confidence intervals and the interquartile mean (IQM).

13When to use a value-based deep method

  • Discrete actions, with data that is precious or a simulator that is cheap to replay from: the DQN family. Replay makes it sample-efficient and off-policy learning reuses old data. The price: the deadly triad, many interacting hyperparameters, and no natural handling of continuous actions, where the max over actions is itself an optimisation (DDPG, TD3 and SAC learn an actor to do that max; {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}). Note that off-policy is not offline: DQN and SAC interact online and learn off-policy from a buffer.
  • Stability over sample efficiency, or any action space: PPO-style on-policy methods, same page; they cannot replay old data without importance ratios.
  • Planning matters: MuZero's search uses a value network trained with the same n-step value targets and, on Atari, R2D2's value rescaling ({{Model-based RL and planning|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8116be5fd39e10bd94c5}}).
  • No interaction at all: offline Q-learning (CQL and others) must keep Q-values down on actions the data never shows ({{Offline RL and imitation|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81d4995ec47e428de9b3}}).
  • Language models: post-training uses policy gradients (PPO, GRPO), not Q-learning over a 100,000-token action space ({{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}).

Libraries implement DQN and a few descendants rather than full Rainbow: {{Stable-Baselines3|https://github.com/DLR-RM/stable-baselines3}} lists DQN for discrete actions only, with QR-DQN in its contrib package; {{CleanRL|https://github.com/vwxyzjn/cleanrl}} has single-file DQN and C51; Google's {{Dopamine|https://github.com/google/dopamine}} implements DQN, C51, Rainbow and IQN (with SAC and PPO). Their defaults have drifted from the Nature paper (CleanRL: Adam at 0.0001 and plain MSE loss).

Misconceptions worth checking yourself against (1 min)
  • "The target network solves the deadly triad." It cuts soft divergence a great deal (14% against 61%), but fitting a frozen target exactly can still diverge when the features cannot represent it (§3's counterexample).
  • "Overestimation needs biased estimates." Every estimate can be unbiased, and with a network it appears even with exact, noise-free samples (§4).
  • "The dueling head's V is the state value." Only with the max-subtracting form; the mean form every experiment used shifts V and A by a constant (§5).
  • "Multi-step returns are free." Uncorrected n-step targets from a buffer assume the intermediate actions were greedy (§7).
  • "Rainbow shows double Q-learning and dueling do not matter." No significant aggregate difference inside Rainbow, where the [−10, 10] support itself limits overestimation; per game both helped some and hurt others (§10).

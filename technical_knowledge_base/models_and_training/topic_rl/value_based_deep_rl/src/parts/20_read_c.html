9Distributional RL: C51, QR-DQN and IQN

A Q-value is the mean of a random return Z(s, a). {{Bellemare, Dabney and Munos (2017)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06887}} learn the whole distribution instead, using the distributional Bellman equation: Z(s, a) has the same distribution as R + γZ(S′, A′). For a fixed policy the distributional Bellman operator is a contraction in a maximal form of the Wasserstein metric, so evaluation converges as usual. For control it is not: their Proposition 1 shows the optimality operator "is not a contraction in any metric over distributions", although the means still converge to Q* exponentially fast.

C51: a fixed grid of atoms

The categorical algorithm represents each Z(s, a) as probabilities on N fixed atoms zi = VMIN + iΔz, with Δz = (VMAX − VMIN)/(N − 1); the Atari agent uses N = 51 and VMAX = −VMIN = 10, hence the name. The network outputs a softmax over atoms for each action, and acts greedily on the means. The target for a transition takes the next state's distribution under the greedy action, shrinks every atom by γ, shifts it by r, and clips it to [VMIN, VMAX]. The shifted atoms no longer sit on the grid, so each one's probability is split between its two neighbouring atoms in proportion to closeness (equation 7):

(ΦT̂Z)i = Σj [ 1 − | [r + γzj]VMINVMAX − zi | / Δz ]01 pj(s′, a*)
Φ
projection onto the fixed atoms; [·] clips its argument to the given range
loss
the cross-entropy between the projected target and the predicted distribution, −Σi mi log pi(s, a): the KL divergence up to a constant, so "the Bellman update" becomes "multiclass classification"
a*
the greedy action at s′ by the means; the target uses a target network, as in DQN

The next-state distribution is illustrative (two bumps, a losing outcome and a surviving one, on [−10, 10]); the projection is computed exactly. The dashed line is DQN's scalar target r + γ𝔼[Z]: the distributional target has the same mean whenever nothing is clipped, and keeps the two outcomes apart instead of averaging them.

Algorithm 1 as printed loses probability
The paper's pseudocode computes bj = (T̂zj − VMIN)/Δz, l = ⌊bj⌋, u = ⌈bj⌉ and adds pj(u − bj) to atom l and pj(bj − l) to atom u. When bj is a whole number, l = u and both amounts are zero, so that atom's probability vanishes. With r = 0 and γ = 1 every atom lands on the grid and the whole target is lost; at the default settings the two end atoms land exactly on the grid (b = 1 and b = 10 at N = 11) and their mass disappears derived, from the printed pseudocode. Equation 7 itself has no such gap, and implementations special-case the tie. Switch the toggle above to see it.

Evidence. On five training games, more atoms always helped, and the 51-atom agent beat DQN on all five (the two-atom "Bernoulli" variant on 3 of 5). Across 57 games, best evaluation score during training: C51 701% mean and 178% median human-normalised, against DQN 228% and 79%, Double DQN 307% and 118%, dueling 373% and 151%, prioritised 434% and 124%, prioritised dueling 592% and 172% (their Figure 6, a table). The authors' explanation: the learned distributions keep "the low-value, 'losing' event" separate from "the high-value, 'survival' event, rather than average them into one (unrealizable) expectation". The 51-atom network trains at about 75% of DQN's speed.

QR-DQN and IQN: move the atoms instead

  • QR-DQN ({{Dabney et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10044}}) fixes the probabilities and learns the locations: N quantiles of the return at the midpoints τ̂i = (2i − 1)/(2N), trained by quantile regression with an asymmetric Huber loss (κ = 1). This minimises a Wasserstein distance directly, closing C51's gap between the theory (Wasserstein contraction) and the algorithm (a KL loss after a projection), and removes the need to choose VMIN and VMAX. With N = 200: 915% mean and 211% median over 57 games (QR-DQN-1, best scores; QR-DQN-0 without the Huber smoothing 881% and 199%).
  • IQN ({{Dabney et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.06923}}) samples τ uniformly from [0, 1] and feeds it to the network through a cosine embedding, φj(τ) = ReLU(Σi=0n−1 cos(πiτ) wij + bj) with n = 64, so one network represents the whole quantile function. Eight samples of τ per update were enough; K = 32 samples choose actions. Distorting the sampling of τ gives risk-sensitive policies (CVaR, for example, acts on the worst η of outcomes). Results, 57 games, 30 no-op starts (their Table 1, averaged over seeds): IQN 1019% mean and 218% median (5 seeds), against QR-DQN 864% and 193% (3 seeds) and Rainbow 1189% and 230% (2 seeds); under human starts IQN's median is 162% against Rainbow's 153%.

Note that IQN's table prints QR-DQN at 193% median and Rainbow at 230%, while their own papers print 211% and 223%: different seeds, averaging and protocol, the subject of section 13.

10NoisyNet: exploration in the weights

ε-greedy explores by occasionally pressing a random button, the same way in every state. {{NoisyNet (Fortunato et al. 2017)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.10295}} replaces a linear layer y = wx + b with a noisy one whose noise scale is learned by gradient descent along with the weights:

y = (μw + σw ⊙ εw) x + μb + σb ⊙ εb
μ, σ
learnable means and noise scales of the weights and biases
ε
noise. Independent: one Gaussian per weight, pq + q variables for p inputs and q outputs. Factorised: p + q unit Gaussians combined as εi,j = f(εi)f(εj) with f(x) = sgn(x)√|x|, used for DQN and dueling to save random-number generation
initialisation
factorised: μ uniform in [−1/√p, 1/√p], σ = σ0/√p with σ0 = 0.5; independent: μ uniform in [−√(3/p), √(3/p)], σ = 0.017

NoisyNet-DQN drops ε-greedy and acts greedily on the noisy network, with the noise resampled before every action and held fixed across each replayed minibatch; independent noise samples are used for the online network, the target network and acting, "to avoid bias due to the correlation". Because σ is learned, the network can reduce its noise where it is sure and keep it where it is not, giving state-dependent, temporally consistent exploration. Median and mean human-normalised scores, no-op starts (their Table 1): DQN 83% and 319% to NoisyNet-DQN 123% and 379%; dueling 132% and 524% to 172% and 633%; A3C 80% and 293% to 94% and 347%.

48% of what?
The paper's text says NoisyNet adds "at least 18 (in the case of A3C) and at most 48 (in the case of DQN) percentage points to the median". Its Table 1 says otherwise: the median gains are 40 points for DQN (83 to 123) and 14 for A3C (80 to 94); the table's 48% and 18% are relative improvements (123/83 and 94/80) derived. The same finding is recorded on the parent's Milestones tab.

How NoisyNet sits among exploration methods (count bonuses, curiosity, Thompson-style randomised value functions) is on {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}.

11Rainbow, and what its ablation shows

{{Rainbow (Hessel et al. 2017)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02298}} asked whether the six extensions above "are complementary and can be fruitfully combined". They are not simply stacked: each had to be adapted to the others.

  • Distributional and multi-step: the target distribution is the next-n-th state's distribution shrunk by γn and shifted by the n-step discounted reward, projected onto the 51 atoms; the loss is the KL divergence.
  • Double: the greedy action at st+n is chosen by the online network and its distribution taken from the target network.
  • Prioritised: transitions are prioritised by that KL loss (proportional variant, exponent 0.5) rather than by a TD error, "since this is what the algorithm is minimizing".
  • Dueling: value and advantage streams each output 51 logits per atom, combined per atom as in dueling DQN, then a softmax.
  • Noisy: every linear layer is replaced by a factorised noisy layer, and ε = 0.

Results. Rainbow matched DQN's final performance after 7M frames (under 10 hours on one GPU) and surpassed the baselines' best final performance after 44M; a full 200M-frame run took about 10 days. Median human-normalised score of the best agent snapshot (Table 2): 223% with no-op starts and 153% with human starts, against DQN 79% and 68%, Double DQN 117% and 110%, prioritised Double DQN 140% and 128%, dueling Double DQN 151% and 117%, A3C 116% (human starts), Noisy DQN 118% and 102%, distributional DQN 164% and 125%.

The ablation

Removing one component at a time, the paper reports: "Prioritized replay and multi-step learning were the two most crucial components", removing either causing a large drop in median performance (and multi-step also hurt final performance); distributional Q-learning "ranked immediately below", with no difference for the first 40 million frames and a lag afterwards, mostly on games at or above human level; removing Noisy Nets made the median worse but helped a few games; and "in aggregate, we did not observe a significant difference when removing the dueling network" or double Q-learning. The authors' hypothesis for double Q-learning: the actual returns often exceeded 10, outside the [−10, 10] support, so "clipping the values to this constrained range counteracts the overestimation bias", though "the importance of double Q-learning may increase if the support of the distributions is expanded".

The ablation's median curves (their Figure 3) are curves and cannot be read off reliably. Its per-game bars (Figure 4: area under the learning curve, 0 = as good as Rainbow, 1 = as bad as DQN) are drawn as vector rectangles in the PDF, so their heights can be decoded exactly. The {{Rainbow ablation, game by game|#t-abl}} tab does that for all six ablations and 55 games:

Median drop over 55 games when each component is removed decoded from the figure: the rows agree with the text's ranking. Removing double Q-learning hurt 30 games and helped 25; it was never the strongest drop. The paper's text says Rainbow "performed better than either ablation in 53 games out of 57" for prioritisation and multi-step; in Figure 4 both bars are positive in 46 of 55 games and at least one is in 49 derived, so the 53 likely counts something other than these bars (final scores, perhaps); the sentence does not say.

Later work re-ran the ablation cheaply: {{Revisiting Rainbow (Obando-Ceron and Castro 2021)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.14826}} revisits Rainbow's components on small-scale environments, arguing that these "can still yield valuable scientific insights" at a fraction of the compute; its findings are not quoted here because they were not checked for this page.

12Scaling out: Ape-X, R2D2 and Agent57

After Rainbow, the value-based line on Atari moved to distributed agents that separate acting from learning.

  • Ape-X ({{Horgan et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.00933}}): 360 actor processes, each with its own copy of the environment, write into one shared prioritised replay, and one GPU learner trains on it (Double DQN with dueling and n-step targets). Actors compute the initial priorities themselves, so new data needs no learner pass. Each actor i explores with its own εi = ε1 + iα/(N−1) (ε = 0.4, α = 7), a spread from greedy to very exploratory. Median human-normalised score 434% with no-op starts after 22.8 billion frames in 5 days (its Table 1).
  • R2D2 ({{Kapturowski et al. 2019|https://openreview.net/forum?id=r1lyTjAqYX}}; PDF read via the {{Wayback Machine|http://web.archive.org/web/20190211025644id_/https://openreview.net/pdf?id=r1lyTjAqYX}}) adds an LSTM after the convolutions and replays fixed-length sequences of 80 steps (overlapping by 40). The problem with replaying a recurrent network is the stale hidden state; R2D2 stores the state the actor had and spends a burn-in of 40 steps unrolling the network before training on the rest. It uses 256 actors, n = 5 double Q-learning, γ = 0.997, priorities mixing the max and mean TD error over the sequence (η = 0.9), does not treat a lost life as an episode end, and does not clip rewards: instead it rescales values with h(x) = sign(x)(√(|x| + 1) − 1) + εx, ε = 10−3 (from Pohlen et al. 2018), which compresses a return of 1,000 to about 31.6 derived. Its Table 1: median 1920.6% and mean 4024.9% over 57 games, final score averaged over 3 seeds; the feed-forward variant reached 589.2%.
  • NGU and Agent57 ({{Badia et al. 2020a|https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06038}}; {{2020b|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350}}): R2D2 still scored near zero on the hardest exploration games. Never Give Up adds an intrinsic novelty reward and learns a family of policies, each with its own weight on that reward and its own discount. Agent57 separates the intrinsic and extrinsic value heads, trains 32 such policies, and lets a sliding-window UCB bandit choose which policy each actor runs. It is "the first deep RL agent that outperforms the standard human benchmark on all 57 Atari games"; Skiing, the last, fell after 78 billion frames. Its bandit is described on {{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}.

Scores for Ape-X, R2D2, MuZero and Agent57, with their protocols, frame counts and the per-game recomputation, are on the parent's {{Milestones and benchmarks|https://app.notion.com/p/3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}} tab. That tab found that Agent57's per-game table prints two of MuZero's scores with an extra digit (Asteroids and Beam Rider); with MuZero's own values the medians reorder. The tab also notes R2D2's paper could not be fetched there; it is quoted here from the archived PDF, and its printed 1920.6% matches the figure MuZero and NGU reprint.

Rainbow ablation, game by game

Rainbow's authors removed one of its six components at a time and trained each ablated agent on all 57 Atari games for 200M frames ({{Hessel et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02298}}). Their text ranks the components; their Figure 4 shows the per-game evidence: for each game, the area under the ablated agent's learning curve, normalised so that 0 means as good as full Rainbow and 1 means as bad as DQN. Two games where DQN beats Rainbow are omitted, leaving 55. This tab reads the Reading tab's section 11 at full resolution.

How these numbers were obtained
The figure exists only as a drawing, but the PDF draws every bar as a vector rectangle. Each bar's height was read from the rectangle's coordinates relative to the row's two reference lines (Rainbow at 0, DQN at 1), giving 330 values (6 rows × 55 games) decoded from the figure. Two checks: the figure highlights the strongest drop per game with an opaque overlay, and in all 55 games the highlighted bar is the largest decoded value (one game, Montezuma's Revenge, has two highlighted bars, both at the clip); and the medians rank the components in the order the paper's text gives. The drawing clips bars at −1 and 1, so a bar at the edge may be larger; means are affected, medians and counts are not. Script: src/checks/decode_rainbow_fig4.py.
Predict first: Rainbow's ablation is often quoted as "prioritised replay and multi-step matter, the rest barely do". In how many of the 55 games did removing double Q-learning make Rainbow worse?

One game, six removals

What the figure cannot say: it averages over the whole learning run, so a component that only speeds up early learning (as the paper says prioritisation and multi-step both do) and one that only raises the final score look alike here. The paper's median curves (Figure 3) separate the two but are curves, which this page does not read.

Value-based deep RL in one screen

Q-learning stores one number per state and action and moves it toward r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′) after every step. Value-based deep RL replaces the table with a neural network Q(s, a; θ) and acts greedily on it. That one change breaks the guarantees the table had: an update for one state now moves the estimates of other states, including the one the target was computed from. The history of the family from 2013 to 2020 is a list of specific repairs, each aimed at one failure, then the question of which repairs matter together.

1Why tables stop working: function approximation

A table needs one entry per state, and real problems have too many states to visit even once: Go has about 2 × 10170 legal positions ({{Tromp|https://tromp.github.io/go/legal.html}}), and an Atari screen is 210 × 160 pixels with a 128-colour palette ({{Mnih et al. 2015, Methods|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf}}). Two screens that differ in one pixel share nothing in a table. The fix is to approximate the value function with a parameterised function, q̂(s, a, w) ≈ Q(s, a), so that an update at one state also moves the estimates of similar states, including ones never visited. That generalisation is the point, and also the problem.

Three kinds of approximator

  • Linear in features: v̂(s, w) = w⊤x(s), where x(s) is a hand-made feature vector: polynomials, Fourier bases, coarse coding, tile coding (several overlapping grids, one active tile per grid), or radial basis functions ({{Sutton and Barto §9.4 to 9.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=226}}). The gradient is simply x(s), and the theory is clean.
  • Linear with a table as the special case: one-hot features recover the table exactly, so tabular learning is linear function approximation with no generalisation at all.
  • Neural networks: the features are learned too. DQN's network reads the last four 84 × 84 greyscale frames and outputs one Q-value per action in a single forward pass (section 4). Nothing in the classical convergence theory covers this case.

Semi-gradient TD

Monte Carlo regression onto returns is ordinary supervised learning: the target Gt does not depend on w, so stochastic gradient descent on the squared error is a true gradient method. TD learning replaces Gt with a bootstrapped target that uses the current weights, and then differentiates only the prediction:

w ← w + α [ Rt+1 + γ v̂(St+1, w) − v̂(St, w) ] ∇w v̂(St, w)
semi-gradient
the update follows the gradient of the prediction at St but treats the target as a constant, ignoring that it also depends on w ({{Sutton and Barto §9.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=225}}). It is not the gradient of any objective, which is why it can fail.
Q-learning version
replace v̂(St+1) with maxa′ q̂(St+1, a′, w) and the gradient with ∇w q̂(St, At, w): this is the update DQN performs on minibatches.

With on-policy data and linear features, semi-gradient TD(0) converges, to the TD fixed point, whose error is within a factor 1/(1 − γ) of the best the features allow: VE(wTD) ≤ minw VE(w) / (1 − γ) ({{Sutton and Barto eq. 9.14|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=229}}, from {{Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1997|https://www.mit.edu/~jnt/Papers/J063-97-bvr-td.pdf}}). At γ = 0.99 that factor is 100 derived, so even the convergent case can be far from the best fit.

What RL data does to a network

Supervised learning assumes the training examples are independent draws from a fixed distribution with fixed labels. Q-learning on a network violates all three assumptions:

  • Correlated data. Consecutive transitions of one trajectory are nearly identical, so gradient steps on them in order are like training a classifier on a thousand pictures of the same cat. Stochastic gradient descent assumes roughly independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples.
  • Moving targets. The target r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ) is computed with the very weights being trained, so every update moves the target it is chasing.
  • Data that depends on the policy. As the greedy policy changes, the states it visits change, so the training distribution shifts under the network. DQN's paper gives the example: "if the maximizing action is to move left then the training samples will be dominated by samples from the left-hand side", and the parameters "could get stuck in a poor local minimum, or even diverge catastrophically" ({{Mnih et al. 2015, Methods|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf}}). A bad update produces bad data, which produces worse updates.

Each method on this page is best read as a defence against one of these failures, or against the divergence the next section shows.

2The deadly triad

Sutton and Barto name the combination that can make value estimates diverge: function approximation (generalising across states), bootstrapping (targets built from current estimates, as in TD and DP rather than Monte Carlo) and off-policy training (updating on a distribution of transitions other than the one the target policy would produce). "If any two elements of the deadly triad are present, but not all three, then instability can be avoided" ({{Sutton and Barto §11.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=286}}). The danger is not caused by control or by learning from samples: it appears even in prediction, and even in dynamic programming with a known model. Q-learning with replay has all three, which is why DQN needed its stabilisers.

The smallest example is two states whose estimates share one weight, w and 2w (features 1 and 2), with a transition from the first to the second paying 0 ({{§11.2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=282}}). Its TD error is γ·2w − w = (2γ − 1)w, so each update multiplies w by 1 + α(2γ − 1): greater than 1 whenever γ > 0.5, whatever the step size. Starting from w = 10 with α = 0.1 and γ near 1, the book's narration goes 10, then about 11, then about 12.1: the update raises the first state, which raises the second state twice as much, which makes the next TD error larger. Off-policy, nothing ever corrects it. On-policy, every visit to the 2w state is followed by an update out of it, which pulls w back: "the promise of future reward must be kept and the system is kept in check" (§11.2).

Baird's counterexample shows the same thing in a complete problem ({{Baird 1995|http://www.leemon.com/papers/1995b.pdf}}; {{Sutton and Barto Figure 11.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=283}}). Seven states, eight weights: the six upper states have value 2wi + w8, the seventh w7 + 2w8. Every reward is 0, so the true values are all 0, which w = 0 represents exactly; the features are linearly independent. The target policy always takes the "solid" action to state 7. The behaviour policy takes "dashed" (to a random upper state) with probability 6/7, so the states are updated uniformly. With γ = 0.99 the weights diverge "for any positive step size, no matter how small", and even with expected, DP-style updates of every state at once (eq. 11.9). Change only the distribution of those updates to the on-policy one, concentrated in state 7, and convergence is guaranteed (§11.2).

Try: in the two-state example set γ below 0.5 (the off-policy update then shrinks w); set α large in the on-policy run (the updates overshoot, a separate step-size problem). In Baird's example compare the two distributions over 1,000 sweeps: same features, same updates, only the weighting of states differs.

Defaults reproduce: the two-state factor 1 + α(2γ − 1) and the book's 10, 11, 12.1 narration (independently, from §11.2's update); Baird's weights over 1,000 sweeps with α = 0.01 and w0 = (1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 10, 1), the setting of Figure 11.2 (right). The book prints only the curves, so the match is in shape (w8 climbing past 300, w1 to w6 past 100, w7 drifting below 10), not in printed values. Both are checked against an independent Python implementation.

Does the triad bite in deep RL?

Less than the theory suggests. Van Hasselt and colleagues ran 336 configurations of DQN variants on all 57 Atari games, three replications each, about 20M frames per run ({{van Hasselt et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02648}}). Since rewards were clipped to [−1, 1] and γ = 0.99, no true value can exceed 1/(1 − γ) = 100; they call estimates beyond that soft divergence. Estimates "frequently grew quickly to over a million" and then came back down, but no run ever became unbounded (none overflowed). The rates they print:

Share of runs with soft divergence by bootstrap target, the labels printed on their Figure 2 transcribed. "Inverse double Q" corrects overestimation but bootstraps on the online network. Their text adds: one-step Q-learning soft-diverged in 94% of runs, with 10-step returns 21%; larger networks diverged more often (67% against 53% for small ones, against their hypothesis); stronger prioritisation diverged more. All three findings return below: target networks (section 4), double estimation (section 5), multi-step targets (section 8).

Fixes that keep all three elements, in the linear case: gradient-TD methods (GTD2, TDC) that descend the projected Bellman error, and emphatic TD, which reweights updates toward the on-policy emphasis ({{Sutton and Barto §11.7 to 11.8|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=300}}). They are provably stable with linear features but, as van Hasselt and colleagues note, "have mostly not been extended to non-linear function approximation, and have not been widely adopted in practice". Deep value methods instead live with the triad and tame it with the engineering below.

3Fitted Q iteration: regression in a loop

Before DQN, the batch answer to the moving target was to stop it moving. Fitted Q iteration (FQI) takes a fixed set of transitions (s, a, r, s′) and alternates two steps ({{Ernst, Geurts and Wehenkel 2005|https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume6/ernst05a/ernst05a.pdf}}, building on fitted value iteration, Gordon 1999):

  1. Freeze the current estimate Q̂N−1 and compute a label for every transition: q = r + γ maxu Q̂N−1(s′, u).
  2. Fit a fresh regressor Q̂N to those labels with any supervised method (Ernst et al. used tree ensembles; Riedmiller's {{neural fitted Q iteration|https://doi.org/10.1007/11564096_32}}, 2005, used a multilayer perceptron trained on the whole batch).

Iteration N approximates the optimal N-step Q-function, and each regression is an ordinary supervised problem with fixed labels. DQN can be read as the online, stochastic version of this loop: the replay buffer is the dataset, the target network is Q̂N−1 held fixed for C updates, and the C minibatch steps are a partial regression. DQN's own derivation says it outright: "we hold the parameters from the previous iteration θi− fixed when optimizing the ith loss function", "resulting in a sequence of well-defined optimization problems" ({{Mnih et al. 2015, Methods|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf}}).

Freezing the target is not a cure for the triad
Fitting the regression all the way at every iteration can still diverge. Tsitsiklis and Van Roy's counterexample extends the w, 2w pair with a transition from the 2w state to the end with probability ε (reward 0 everywhere). Choosing wk+1 as the exact least-squares fit to the expected one-step targets gives wk+1 = γ(6 − 4ε)/5 · wk, which diverges whenever γ > 5/(6 − 4ε) and w0 ≠ 0 ({{Sutton and Barto Example 11.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=285}}). With ε = 0.1, any γ above 0.893 diverges derived. The convergence of FQI "depends on properties of the function approximation algorithm" ({{Sutton and Barto §16.7|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=469}}, citing Gordon 1999). Stability is guaranteed for approximators "that do not extrapolate from the observed targets", called averagers (nearest-neighbour methods, locally weighted regression), "but not popular methods such as tile coding and artificial neural networks" (§11.2).

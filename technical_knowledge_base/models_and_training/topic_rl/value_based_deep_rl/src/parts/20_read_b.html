3DQN: replay and a target network

DQN (deep Q-network) is Q-learning with a convolutional network that reads raw pixels and outputs one Q-value per action, trained by regression on the TD target. One update, with numbers: a stored transition has r = 1, γ = 0.99, and the target network gives Q(s′, ·; θ−) = (2.0, 3.5, 3.0) for three actions. The target is 1 + 0.99 × 3.5 = 4.465 derived. If the online network says Q(s, a; θ) = 4.0, the TD error is 0.465 and the gradient step raises Q(s, a) toward 4.465. Over a minibatch of such transitions:

Two versions. The 2013 arXiv paper used replay only; the target network arrived with the 2015 Nature paper, "the second modification to online Q-learning". Retellings merge them; the parent's atlas keeps two rows.

Experience replay

Store every transition (the Nature version kept the most recent 1 million) and train on minibatches of 32 drawn uniformly. In the paper's words: "each step of experience is potentially used in many weight updates"; "randomizing the samples breaks these correlations and therefore reduces the variance of the updates"; and "the behavior distribution is averaged over many of its previous states, smoothing out learning and avoiding oscillations or divergence". Replay is legitimate only because Q-learning is off-policy: its target does not depend on which policy chose the stored action ("when learning by experience replay, it is necessary to learn off-policy ... which motivates the choice of Q-learning"). It also makes the data more off-policy (a mixture of past policies), strengthening one corner of the triad while weakening correlation. The idea is {{Lin's (1992)|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00992699}} ({{Sutton and Barto §16.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=462}}).

The target network

Every C updates (10,000 in the Nature paper), copy θ into θ− and use θ− for every target in between. The paper's reason is the triad in one sentence: in online Q-learning "an update that increases Q(st, at) often also increases Q(st+1, a) for all a and hence also increases the target", "possibly leading to oscillations or divergence"; older parameters add "a delay between the time an update to Q is made and the time the update affects the targets". DQN is online fitted Q iteration (FQI; {{Ernst, Geurts and Wehenkel 2005|https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume6/ernst05a/ernst05a.pdf}}): FQI labels a fixed dataset with targets from the frozen previous estimate and fits a fresh regressor to them, again and again. In DQN the buffer is the dataset, θ− is the previous estimate, and C minibatch steps are a partial regression.

Freezing the target is not a cure for the triad
Even exact regression onto a frozen target diverges when the features cannot represent it: in Tsitsiklis and Van Roy's counterexample, any γ above 0.893 (with ε = 0.1). Convergence is guaranteed only for approximators that do not extrapolate, "but not popular methods such as tile coding and artificial neural networks" (Sutton and Barto §11.2).
Fitted Q iteration step by step, and the counterexample (1 min)

FQI (after fitted value iteration, Gordon 1999) freezes Q̂N−1, labels every stored transition with q = r + γ maxu Q̂N−1(s′, u), and fits a fresh regressor Q̂N to those labels with any supervised method (Ernst et al. used tree ensembles; Riedmiller's {{neural fitted Q iteration|https://doi.org/10.1007/11564096_32}}, 2005, a multilayer perceptron trained on the whole batch). Iteration N approximates the optimal N-step Q-function, and each regression has fixed labels. DQN's derivation says so: "we hold the parameters from the previous iteration θi− fixed when optimizing the ith loss function", "resulting in a sequence of well-defined optimization problems" ({{Mnih et al. 2015, Methods|https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/dqn/DQNNaturePaper.pdf}}). With two states the animation's features represent the true values, so its FQI is plain value iteration and converges at rate γ.

The counterexample. Extend the two-state w, 2w pair (Model-free prediction and control, section 7) with a transition from the 2w state to the end with probability ε (reward 0 everywhere); the exact least-squares fit gives wk+1 = γ(6 − 4ε)/5 · wk, which diverges whenever γ > 5/(6 − 4ε) and w0 ≠ 0 ({{Sutton and Barto Example 11.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=285}}); with ε = 0.1 that is γ above 0.893 derived. FQI's convergence "depends on properties of the function approximation algorithm" ({{§16.7|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=469}}): guaranteed for approximators "that do not extrapolate from the observed targets", called averagers (nearest neighbours, locally weighted regression).

4Overestimation and Double DQN

The max in the target uses the same noisy estimates to select the best action and to evaluate it, so whichever action happens to be overestimated is picked, and its overestimate enters the target. Every estimate can be unbiased and the max still is not: with three actions truly worth 0 and independent standard normal errors, the expected maximum is about 0.85; with ten, about 1.54 derived. The tabular version (Sutton and Barto's Example 6.7, live) is on {{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}} (its Reading section 5); what changes with a network is that the bias needs no noise at all.

{{Van Hasselt, Guez and Silver (2015)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461}}, Figure 2: 10 actions with the same true value in every state; each action's estimate is a polynomial fitted to exact samples of that value at the integer states from −6 to 6, with two adjacent states left out (a different pair per action). The fits disagree between samples, and their max sits above the truth almost everywhere. The double estimator picks with one fit and values with another, fitted to different samples. Double DQN uses the two networks DQN already has. Continuing the example: the online network gives Q(s′, ·; θ) = (2.5, 3.0, 3.8), so it selects the third action; the target network values that action at 3.0; the target is 1 + 0.99 × 3.0 = 3.97 instead of 4.465 derived.

On Atari, DQN's estimates were "consistently and sometimes vastly overoptimistic", and overestimation "was observed for DQN in all 49 tested Atari games, albeit in varying amounts". With DQN's hyperparameters unchanged, the median human-normalised score over 49 games rose from 93.5% to 114.7% and the mean from 241.1% to 330.3% (5 minutes of play, no-op starts, Table 1).

Theorem 1, game-level effects, the tuned version and descendants (1 min)

Theorem 1 needs no independence and no particular error distribution: if all true action values in a state are equal, Q*(s, a) = V*(s), and the estimates are unbiased on the whole (errors sum to 0) but not all correct (mean squared error C > 0), then maxa Qt(s, a) ≥ V*(s) + √(C / (m − 1)) for m actions, and the bound is tight; the Double Q-learning estimate's lower bound on absolute error is zero. The bound shrinks with m only because it is a worst case; with errors uniform on [−1, 1] the overestimation is (m − 1)/(m + 1), which grows with m (0.82 for 10 actions derived).

On Asterix and Wizard of Wor the estimates grew by orders of magnitude and the scores fell as they did, so the paper reads the instability as overestimation rather than inherent off-policy instability; overestimation does not always hurt (DQN plays Pong optimally while slightly overestimating). Some games changed sharply (Road Runner 233% to 617%, Double Dunk 17% to 397%). A tuned version (target refresh every 30,000 frames instead of 10,000, ε 0.01 in training and 0.001 in evaluation, one shared bias for all action values) reached 116.7% median under human starts, against 47.5% for DQN and 88.4% for untuned Double DQN (Table 2).

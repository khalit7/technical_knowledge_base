4DQN: two versions, two stabilisers

DQN (deep Q-network) is Q-learning with a convolutional network that reads raw pixels and outputs one Q-value per action, trained by regression on the TD target. It exists in two versions that are often merged in retellings, and the parent page's atlas keeps them as two rows for that reason:

In the Nature version a life lost marks the end of an episode during training.

Error clipping. The Nature paper also clipped the TD error to [−1, 1] in the update, which "corresponds to using an absolute value loss function for errors outside of the (−1, 1) interval": a Huber loss with threshold 1, as most implementations now write it.

Experience replay

Store every transition in a buffer (the Nature version kept the most recent 1 million) and train on minibatches of 32 drawn uniformly at random. Three advantages, in the paper's words: "each step of experience is potentially used in many weight updates"; "randomizing the samples breaks these correlations and therefore reduces the variance of the updates"; and "the behavior distribution is averaged over many of its previous states, smoothing out learning and avoiding oscillations or divergence". Replaying old data is legitimate only because Q-learning is off-policy: its target does not depend on which policy chose the stored action ("when learning by experience replay, it is necessary to learn off-policy ... which motivates the choice of Q-learning"). The idea is older than DQN: replay buffers were introduced by {{Lin (1992)|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00992699}}, as Sutton and Barto note in {{§16.5|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=462}}. Replay also makes the data more off-policy (a mixture of past policies), so it strengthens one corner of the triad while weakening the correlation problem.

The target network

Every C updates, copy the online weights into θ− and use θ− for every target in between (C = 10,000 updates in the Nature paper). The paper's explanation is the triad's mechanism in one sentence: in standard online Q-learning "an update that increases Q(st, at) often also increases Q(st+1, a) for all a and hence also increases the target", "possibly leading to oscillations or divergence"; an older set of parameters "adds a delay between the time an update to Q is made and the time the update affects the targets". Worked example (from the old page). A stored transition has r = 1, γ = 0.99, and the target network gives Q(s′, ·; θ−) = (2.0, 3.5, 3.0) for three actions. The target is 1 + 0.99 × 3.5 = 4.465 derived. If the online network says Q(s, a; θ) = 4.0, the TD error is 0.465 and the gradient step raises Q(s, a) toward 4.465.

Evaluation in the Nature paper: each game played 30 times for up to 5 minutes, from up to 30 random no-op starts, with ε = 0.05; the professional human tester played "around 20 episodes of each game lasting a maximum of 5 min each, following around 2 h of practice", with no pausing, saving or reloading. The random baseline pressed a random action at 10 Hz. Those choices define "human-normalised score" for every paper since: (agent − random) / (human − random) per game, the formula {{van Hasselt et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461}} write out as their equation 5.

5Overestimation and Double DQN

The max in the Q-learning target uses the same noisy estimates both to select the best action and to evaluate it: whichever action happens to be overestimated gets picked, and its overestimate is what enters the target. Every estimate can be unbiased and the max still is not. With three actions all truly worth 0 and independent standard normal errors, the expected maximum is about 0.85; with ten, about 1.54 derived. That tabular version, with Sutton and Barto's Example 6.7 rerun live, is on {{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}} (its Maximisation bias tab); this section adds what changes with function approximation.

{{Van Hasselt, Guez and Silver (2015)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.06461}} proved that the bias does not need independent errors, or any particular error distribution. Theorem 1: if all true action values in a state are equal, Q*(s, a) = V*(s), and the estimates are unbiased on the whole (their errors sum to 0) but not all correct (mean squared error C > 0), then maxa Qt(s, a) ≥ V*(s) + √(C / (m − 1)) for m actions, and the bound is tight; the Double Q-learning estimate's lower bound on absolute error is zero. The bound shrinks with m only because it is a worst case; with errors uniform on [−1, 1] the overestimation is (m − 1)/(m + 1), which grows with m (0.82 for 10 actions derived).

Their Figure 2 shows that function approximation alone produces the bias, with no noise at all. In each state there are 10 actions, all with the same true value. Each action's estimate is a polynomial fitted to exact samples of the true value at integer states from −6 to 6, with two adjacent states left out (a different pair for each action). The fits disagree between the samples, and the max over ten of them sits above the truth almost everywhere. The double estimator picks the best action with one fit and values it with another, fitted to different samples.

Defaults reproduce the paper's printed average errors independently: computed here from the stated construction (least-squares fits, the samples of action ai+5 as the second set for ai, errors averaged over a fine grid on [−6, 6]), the three rows give +0.612 and −0.021, +0.473 and +0.016, +3.349 and −0.016, against the printed +0.61 and −0.02, +0.47 and +0.02, +3.35 and −0.02. The bottom row is the instructive one: the more flexible fit passes through every sample exactly and overestimates most, because it swings wildly between them.

Double DQN uses the two networks DQN already has: the online network selects, the target network evaluates.

Worked example continued: the online network gives Q(s′, ·; θ) = (2.5, 3.0, 3.8), so it selects the third action; the target network values that action at 3.0; the target is 1 + 0.99 × 3.0 = 3.97 instead of 4.465 derived.

What it changed on Atari. DQN's value estimates were "consistently and sometimes vastly overoptimistic", and overestimation "was observed for DQN in all 49 tested Atari games, albeit in varying amounts". On Asterix and Wizard of Wor the estimates grew by orders of magnitude and the scores fell as they did, so the paper reads the instability as overestimation rather than inherent off-policy instability; overestimation does not always hurt (DQN plays Pong optimally while slightly overestimating). With DQN's hyperparameters unchanged, the median human-normalised score over 49 games rose from 93.5% to 114.7% and the mean from 241.1% to 330.3% (5 minutes of play, no-op starts, Table 1); some games changed sharply (Road Runner 233% to 617%, Double Dunk 17% to 397%). A tuned version (target refresh every 30,000 frames instead of 10,000, ε 0.01 in training and 0.001 in evaluation, one shared bias for all action values) reached 116.7% median under human starts, against 47.5% for DQN and 88.4% for untuned Double DQN (Table 2).

Double Q-learning survives in the descendants: Rainbow and R2D2 use Double DQN's target, and in actor-critic methods TD3 fights the same bias with twin critics and a minimum (on {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}). Offline RL meets the bias in its worst form, on actions the data never tried (on {{Offline RL and imitation|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81d4995ec47e428de9b3}}).

6The dueling network: value plus advantage

The {{dueling architecture (Wang et al. 2015)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06581}} changes only the network's head. After the shared convolutional layers it splits into a state-value stream V(s) and an advantage stream A(s, a), then recombines them. The insight: "for many states, it is unnecessary to estimate the value of each action choice" (in Enduro, left or right "only matters when a collision is eminent"), but bootstrapping needs a good state value in every state. With a separate V stream, every update improves V(s), whichever action was taken.

Q(s, a) = V(s) + ( A(s, a) − (1/|𝒜|) Σa′ A(s, a′) )
|𝒜|
the number of actions
why subtract
Q = V + A alone is unidentifiable: add a constant to V and subtract it from every A and Q is unchanged, "mirrored by poor practical performance when this equation is used directly"

The paper first forces the best action's advantage to zero, Q = V + (A − maxa′ A), so that V is exactly the state value. It then uses the mean instead, as above, for every reported experiment: this "loses the original semantics of V and A" (they are off by a constant) but "increases the stability of the optimization", because the advantages "only need to change as fast as the mean". Either way the ranking of actions, and so the greedy policy, is unchanged. Example: V = 5 and A = (1, −1, 3) has mean 1, so the mean form gives Q = (5, 3, 7); the max form would give (3, 1, 5) derived, the same ranking.

Evidence. On a corridor task with exact Q-values, both architectures learned equally fast with 5 actions; as no-op actions were added (10, then 20), the dueling network's squared error fell faster, the gap growing with the number of actions. On all 57 Atari games, trained with Double DQN, with the combined gradient into the last convolutional layer rescaled by 1/√2 and gradients clipped to norm 10, the dueling network ("Duel Clip") beat the same single-stream network with the same clipping on 75.4% of games (43 of 57). Mean and median human-normalised scores, 30 no-op starts (their Table 1): Nature DQN 227.9% and 79.1%; single stream 307.3% and 117.8%; single stream with clipping 341.2% and 132.6%; dueling with clipping 373.1% and 151.5%; dueling with prioritised replay 591.9% and 172.1%. Under human starts the dueling gain over the clipped single stream is smaller (343.8% and 117.1% against 302.8% and 114.1%).

7Prioritised experience replay

Uniform replay replays every transition as often as it happened, informative or not. {{Prioritised experience replay (Schaul et al. 2015)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.05952}} replays transitions with a large TD error more often, the way prioritised sweeping orders planning updates. Its motivating example, the "Blind Cliffwalk", has n states and a reward that random actions reach with probability 2−n, so the one useful transition is buried among failures; an oracle that picks the most useful transition to replay learns exponentially faster than uniform replay.

Settings and evidence. The paper's grid search on 8 games chose α = 0.7, β0 = 0.5 (rank-based) and α = 0.6, β0 = 0.4 (proportional), with the step size cut by 4 because high-error transitions give larger gradients. The proportional version samples in O(log N) with a sum-tree (each node holds the sum of its children's priorities). Prioritised replay outperformed uniform replay "on 41 out of 49 games". Median and mean under human starts (their Table 1): DQN 48% and 122%, rank-based on DQN 106% and 355% (49 games); tuned Double DQN 111% and 418%, rank-based 113% and 454%, proportional 128% and 551% (57 games). Prioritisation makes the replay distribution even more off-policy, and van Hasselt et al. (2018, section 2) found stronger prioritisation soft-diverges more often.

8Multi-step targets

A one-step target moves reward information back one state per update. An n-step target uses n real rewards before bootstrapping:

y(n) = Σk=0n−1 γk rt+k+1 + γn maxa′ Q(st+n, a′; θ−)
n
steps of real reward; n = 1 is DQN's target, n = ∞ a Monte Carlo return

It propagates reward faster and leans less on the estimate, at a cost: the n − 1 intermediate actions were chosen by the behaviour policy (ε-greedy, and old for replayed data), not the greedy policy whose value the target claims to be, so the uncorrected target is biased off-policy. Rainbow and R2D2 accept the bias (n = 3 and n = 5); Rainbow found n "a sensitive hyper-parameter", with 3 and 5 both good early and 3 best by the end. Corrected alternatives exist, such as {{Retrace (Munos et al. 2016)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.02647}}, which cuts traces with truncated importance weights; Agent57 uses a Retrace loss. Multi-step targets also bootstrap less, and in the 2018 triad study longer n clearly reduced soft divergence (section 2).

Value-based deep RL in one screen

Q-learning stores one number per state and action and moves it toward r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′) after every step. Value-based deep RL replaces the table with a neural network Q(s, a; θ) and acts greedily on it. That one change breaks the guarantees the table had: an update for one state now moves the estimates of other states, including the one the target was computed from. The history of the family from 2013 to 2020 is a list of specific repairs, each aimed at one failure, then the question of which repairs matter together.

1Why tables stop working: function approximation

A table needs one entry per state, and real problems have too many states to visit even once: Go has about 2 × 10170 legal positions (Tromp), and an Atari screen is 210 × 160 pixels with a 128-colour palette (Mnih et al. 2015, Methods). Two screens that differ in one pixel share nothing in a table. The fix is to approximate the value function with a parameterised function, q̂(s, a, w) ≈ Q(s, a), so that an update at one state also moves the estimates of similar states, including ones never visited. That generalisation is the point, and also the problem.

Three kinds of approximator

  • Linear in features: v̂(s, w) = w⊤x(s), where x(s) is a hand-made feature vector: polynomials, Fourier bases, coarse coding, tile coding (several overlapping grids, one active tile per grid), or radial basis functions (Sutton and Barto §9.4 to 9.5). The gradient is simply x(s), and the theory is clean.
  • Linear with a table as the special case: one-hot features recover the table exactly, so tabular learning is linear function approximation with no generalisation at all.
  • Neural networks: the features are learned too. DQN's network reads the last four 84 × 84 greyscale frames and outputs one Q-value per action in a single forward pass (section 4). Nothing in the classical convergence theory covers this case.

Semi-gradient TD

Monte Carlo regression onto returns is ordinary supervised learning: the target Gt does not depend on w, so stochastic gradient descent on the squared error is a true gradient method. TD learning replaces Gt with a bootstrapped target that uses the current weights, and then differentiates only the prediction:

w ← w + α [ Rt+1 + γ v̂(St+1, w) − v̂(St, w) ] ∇w v̂(St, w)
semi-gradient
the update follows the gradient of the prediction at St but treats the target as a constant, ignoring that it also depends on w (Sutton and Barto §9.3). It is not the gradient of any objective, which is why it can fail.
Q-learning version
replace v̂(St+1) with maxa′ q̂(St+1, a′, w) and the gradient with ∇w q̂(St, At, w): this is the update DQN performs on minibatches.

With on-policy data and linear features, semi-gradient TD(0) converges, to the TD fixed point, whose error is within a factor 1/(1 − γ) of the best the features allow: VE(wTD) ≤ minw VE(w) / (1 − γ) (Sutton and Barto eq. 9.14, from Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1997). At γ = 0.99 that factor is 100 derived, so even the convergent case can be far from the best fit.

What RL data does to a network

Supervised learning assumes the training examples are independent draws from a fixed distribution with fixed labels. Q-learning on a network violates all three assumptions:

  • Correlated data. Consecutive transitions of one trajectory are nearly identical, so gradient steps on them in order are like training a classifier on a thousand pictures of the same cat. Stochastic gradient descent assumes roughly independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples.
  • Moving targets. The target r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ) is computed with the very weights being trained, so every update moves the target it is chasing.
  • Data that depends on the policy. As the greedy policy changes, the states it visits change, so the training distribution shifts under the network. DQN's paper gives the example: "if the maximizing action is to move left then the training samples will be dominated by samples from the left-hand side", and the parameters "could get stuck in a poor local minimum, or even diverge catastrophically" (Mnih et al. 2015, Methods). A bad update produces bad data, which produces worse updates.

Each method on this page is best read as a defence against one of these failures, or against the divergence the next section shows.

2The deadly triad

Sutton and Barto name the combination that can make value estimates diverge: function approximation (generalising across states), bootstrapping (targets built from current estimates, as in TD and DP rather than Monte Carlo) and off-policy training (updating on a distribution of transitions other than the one the target policy would produce). "If any two elements of the deadly triad are present, but not all three, then instability can be avoided" (Sutton and Barto §11.3). The danger is not caused by control or by learning from samples: it appears even in prediction, and even in dynamic programming with a known model. Q-learning with replay has all three, which is why DQN needed its stabilisers.

The smallest example is two states whose estimates share one weight, w and 2w (features 1 and 2), with a transition from the first to the second paying 0 (§11.2). Its TD error is γ·2w − w = (2γ − 1)w, so each update multiplies w by 1 + α(2γ − 1): greater than 1 whenever γ > 0.5, whatever the step size. Starting from w = 10 with α = 0.1 and γ near 1, the book's narration goes 10, then about 11, then about 12.1: the update raises the first state, which raises the second state twice as much, which makes the next TD error larger. Off-policy, nothing ever corrects it. On-policy, every visit to the 2w state is followed by an update out of it, which pulls w back: "the promise of future reward must be kept and the system is kept in check" (§11.2).

Baird's counterexample shows the same thing in a complete problem (Baird 1995; Sutton and Barto Figure 11.1). Seven states, eight weights: the six upper states have value 2wi + w8, the seventh w7 + 2w8. Every reward is 0, so the true values are all 0, which w = 0 represents exactly; the features are linearly independent. The target policy always takes the "solid" action to state 7. The behaviour policy takes "dashed" (to a random upper state) with probability 6/7, so the states are updated uniformly. With γ = 0.99 the weights diverge "for any positive step size, no matter how small", and even with expected, DP-style updates of every state at once (eq. 11.9). Change only the distribution of those updates to the on-policy one, concentrated in state 7, and convergence is guaranteed (§11.2).

Try: in the two-state example set γ below 0.5 (the off-policy update then shrinks w); set α large in the on-policy run (the updates overshoot, a separate step-size problem). In Baird's example compare the two distributions over 1,000 sweeps: same features, same updates, only the weighting of states differs.

Does the triad bite in deep RL?

Less than the theory suggests. Van Hasselt and colleagues ran 336 configurations of DQN variants on all 57 Atari games, three replications each, about 20M frames per run (van Hasselt et al. 2018). Since rewards were clipped to [−1, 1] and γ = 0.99, no true value can exceed 1/(1 − γ) = 100; they call estimates beyond that soft divergence. Estimates "frequently grew quickly to over a million" and then came back down, but no run ever became unbounded (none overflowed). The rates they print:

Share of runs with soft divergence by bootstrap target, the labels printed on their Figure 2 transcribed. "Inverse double Q" corrects overestimation but bootstraps on the online network. Their text adds: one-step Q-learning soft-diverged in 94% of runs, with 10-step returns 21%; larger networks diverged more often (67% against 53% for small ones, against their hypothesis); stronger prioritisation diverged more. All three findings return below: target networks (section 4), double estimation (section 5), multi-step targets (section 8).

Fixes that keep all three elements, in the linear case: gradient-TD methods (GTD2, TDC) that descend the projected Bellman error, and emphatic TD, which reweights updates toward the on-policy emphasis (Sutton and Barto §11.7 to 11.8). They are provably stable with linear features but, as van Hasselt and colleagues note, "have mostly not been extended to non-linear function approximation, and have not been widely adopted in practice". Deep value methods instead live with the triad and tame it with the engineering below.

3Fitted Q iteration: regression in a loop

Before DQN, the batch answer to the moving target was to stop it moving. Fitted Q iteration (FQI) takes a fixed set of transitions (s, a, r, s′) and alternates two steps (Ernst, Geurts and Wehenkel 2005, building on fitted value iteration, Gordon 1999):

  1. Freeze the current estimate Q̂N−1 and compute a label for every transition: q = r + γ maxu Q̂N−1(s′, u).
  2. Fit a fresh regressor Q̂N to those labels with any supervised method (Ernst et al. used tree ensembles; Riedmiller's neural fitted Q iteration, 2005, used a multilayer perceptron trained on the whole batch).

Iteration N approximates the optimal N-step Q-function, and each regression is an ordinary supervised problem with fixed labels. DQN can be read as the online, stochastic version of this loop: the replay buffer is the dataset, the target network is Q̂N−1 held fixed for C updates, and the C minibatch steps are a partial regression. DQN's own derivation says it outright: "we hold the parameters from the previous iteration θi− fixed when optimizing the ith loss function", "resulting in a sequence of well-defined optimization problems" (Mnih et al. 2015, Methods).

Freezing the target is not a cure for the triad
Fitting the regression all the way at every iteration can still diverge. Tsitsiklis and Van Roy's counterexample extends the w, 2w pair with a transition from the 2w state to the end with probability ε (reward 0 everywhere). Choosing wk+1 as the exact least-squares fit to the expected one-step targets gives wk+1 = γ(6 − 4ε)/5 · wk, which diverges whenever γ > 5/(6 − 4ε) and w0 ≠ 0 (Sutton and Barto Example 11.1). With ε = 0.1, any γ above 0.893 diverges derived. The convergence of FQI "depends on properties of the function approximation algorithm" (Sutton and Barto §16.7, citing Gordon 1999). Stability is guaranteed for approximators "that do not extrapolate from the observed targets", called averagers (nearest-neighbour methods, locally weighted regression), "but not popular methods such as tile coding and artificial neural networks" (§11.2).

4DQN: two versions, two stabilisers

DQN (deep Q-network) is Q-learning with a convolutional network that reads raw pixels and outputs one Q-value per action, trained by regression on the TD target. It exists in two versions that are often merged in retellings, and the parent page's atlas keeps them as two rows for that reason:

Experience replay

Store every transition in a buffer (the Nature version kept the most recent 1 million) and train on minibatches of 32 drawn uniformly at random. Three advantages, in the paper's words: "each step of experience is potentially used in many weight updates"; "randomizing the samples breaks these correlations and therefore reduces the variance of the updates"; and "the behavior distribution is averaged over many of its previous states, smoothing out learning and avoiding oscillations or divergence". Replaying old data is legitimate only because Q-learning is off-policy: its target does not depend on which policy chose the stored action ("when learning by experience replay, it is necessary to learn off-policy ... which motivates the choice of Q-learning"). The idea is older than DQN: replay buffers were introduced by Lin (1992), as Sutton and Barto note in §16.5. Replay also makes the data more off-policy (a mixture of past policies), so it strengthens one corner of the triad while weakening the correlation problem.

The target network

Every C updates, copy the online weights into θ− and use θ− for every target in between (C = 10,000 updates in the Nature paper). The paper's explanation is the triad's mechanism in one sentence: in standard online Q-learning "an update that increases Q(st, at) often also increases Q(st+1, a) for all a and hence also increases the target", "possibly leading to oscillations or divergence"; an older set of parameters "adds a delay between the time an update to Q is made and the time the update affects the targets". 5Overestimation and Double DQN

The max in the Q-learning target uses the same noisy estimates both to select the best action and to evaluate it: whichever action happens to be overestimated gets picked, and its overestimate is what enters the target. Every estimate can be unbiased and the max still is not. With three actions all truly worth 0 and independent standard normal errors, the expected maximum is about 0.85; with ten, about 1.54 derived. That tabular version, with Sutton and Barto's Example 6.7 rerun live, is on Model-free prediction and control (its Maximisation bias tab); this section adds what changes with function approximation.

Van Hasselt, Guez and Silver (2015) proved that the bias does not need independent errors, or any particular error distribution. Theorem 1: if all true action values in a state are equal, Q*(s, a) = V*(s), and the estimates are unbiased on the whole (their errors sum to 0) but not all correct (mean squared error C > 0), then maxa Qt(s, a) ≥ V*(s) + √(C / (m − 1)) for m actions, and the bound is tight; the Double Q-learning estimate's lower bound on absolute error is zero. The bound shrinks with m only because it is a worst case; with errors uniform on [−1, 1] the overestimation is (m − 1)/(m + 1), which grows with m (0.82 for 10 actions derived).

Their Figure 2 shows that function approximation alone produces the bias, with no noise at all. In each state there are 10 actions, all with the same true value. Each action's estimate is a polynomial fitted to exact samples of the true value at integer states from −6 to 6, with two adjacent states left out (a different pair for each action). The fits disagree between the samples, and the max over ten of them sits above the truth almost everywhere. The double estimator picks the best action with one fit and values it with another, fitted to different samples.

Defaults reproduce the paper's printed average errors independently: computed here from the stated construction (least-squares fits, the samples of action ai+5 as the second set for ai, errors averaged over a fine grid on [−6, 6]), the three rows give +0.612 and −0.021, +0.473 and +0.016, +3.349 and −0.016, against the printed +0.61 and −0.02, +0.47 and +0.02, +3.35 and −0.02. What it changed on Atari. DQN's value estimates were "consistently and sometimes vastly overoptimistic", and overestimation "was observed for DQN in all 49 tested Atari games, albeit in varying amounts". On Asterix and Wizard of Wor the estimates grew by orders of magnitude and the scores fell as they did, so the paper reads the instability as overestimation rather than inherent off-policy instability; overestimation does not always hurt (DQN plays Pong optimally while slightly overestimating). With DQN's hyperparameters unchanged, the median human-normalised score over 49 games rose from 93.5% to 114.7% and the mean from 241.1% to 330.3% (5 minutes of play, no-op starts, Table 1); some games changed sharply (Road Runner 233% to 617%, Double Dunk 17% to 397%). A tuned version (target refresh every 30,000 frames instead of 10,000, ε 0.01 in training and 0.001 in evaluation, one shared bias for all action values) reached 116.7% median under human starts, against 47.5% for DQN and 88.4% for untuned Double DQN (Table 2).

Double Q-learning survives in the descendants: Rainbow and R2D2 use Double DQN's target, and in actor-critic methods TD3 fights the same bias with twin critics and a minimum (on Policy gradients and actor-critic). Offline RL meets the bias in its worst form, on actions the data never tried (on Offline RL and imitation).

6The dueling network: value plus advantage

The dueling architecture (Wang et al. 2015) changes only the network's head. After the shared convolutional layers it splits into a state-value stream V(s) and an advantage stream A(s, a), then recombines them. The insight: "for many states, it is unnecessary to estimate the value of each action choice" (in Enduro, left or right "only matters when a collision is eminent"), but bootstrapping needs a good state value in every state. With a separate V stream, every update improves V(s), whichever action was taken.

Q = V + A alone is unidentifiable: add a constant to V and subtract it from every A and Q is unchanged, "mirrored by poor practical performance when this equation is used directly"

The paper first forces the best action's advantage to zero, Q = V + (A − maxa′ A), so that V is exactly the state value. It then uses the mean instead, as above, for every reported experiment: this "loses the original semantics of V and A" (they are off by a constant) but "increases the stability of the optimization", because the advantages "only need to change as fast as the mean". Either way the ranking of actions, and so the greedy policy, is unchanged. Example: V = 5 and A = (1, −1, 3) has mean 1, so the mean form gives Q = (5, 3, 7); the max form would give (3, 1, 5) derived, the same ranking.

Evidence. On a corridor task with exact Q-values, both architectures learned equally fast with 5 actions; as no-op actions were added (10, then 20), the dueling network's squared error fell faster, the gap growing with the number of actions. On all 57 Atari games, trained with Double DQN, with the combined gradient into the last convolutional layer rescaled by 1/√2 and gradients clipped to norm 10, the dueling network ("Duel Clip") beat the same single-stream network with the same clipping on 75.4% of games (43 of 57). Mean and median human-normalised scores, 30 no-op starts (their Table 1): Nature DQN 227.9% and 79.1%; single stream 307.3% and 117.8%; single stream with clipping 341.2% and 132.6%; dueling with clipping 373.1% and 151.5%; dueling with prioritised replay 591.9% and 172.1%. Under human starts the dueling gain over the clipped single stream is smaller (343.8% and 117.1% against 302.8% and 114.1%).

7Prioritised experience replay

Uniform replay replays every transition as often as it happened, informative or not. Prioritised experience replay (Schaul et al. 2015) replays transitions with a large TD error more often, the way prioritised sweeping orders planning updates. Its motivating example, the "Blind Cliffwalk", has n states and a reward that random actions reach with probability 2−n, so the one useful transition is buried among failures; an oracle that picks the most useful transition to replay learns exponentially faster than uniform replay.

Settings and evidence. The paper's grid search on 8 games chose α = 0.7, β0 = 0.5 (rank-based) and α = 0.6, β0 = 0.4 (proportional), with the step size cut by 4 because high-error transitions give larger gradients. The proportional version samples in O(log N) with a sum-tree (each node holds the sum of its children's priorities). Prioritised replay outperformed uniform replay "on 41 out of 49 games". Median and mean under human starts (their Table 1): DQN 48% and 122%, rank-based on DQN 106% and 355% (49 games); tuned Double DQN 111% and 418%, rank-based 113% and 454%, proportional 128% and 551% (57 games). Prioritisation makes the replay distribution even more off-policy, and van Hasselt et al. (2018, section 2) found stronger prioritisation soft-diverges more often.

8Multi-step targets

A one-step target moves reward information back one state per update. An n-step target uses n real rewards before bootstrapping:

y(n) = Σk=0n−1 γk rt+k+1 + γn maxa′ Q(st+n, a′; θ−)
n
steps of real reward; n = 1 is DQN's target, n = ∞ a Monte Carlo return

It propagates reward faster and leans less on the estimate, at a cost: the n − 1 intermediate actions were chosen by the behaviour policy (ε-greedy, and old for replayed data), not the greedy policy whose value the target claims to be, so the uncorrected target is biased off-policy. Rainbow and R2D2 accept the bias (n = 3 and n = 5); Rainbow found n "a sensitive hyper-parameter", with 3 and 5 both good early and 3 best by the end. Corrected alternatives exist, such as Retrace (Munos et al. 2016), which cuts traces with truncated importance weights; Agent57 uses a Retrace loss. Multi-step targets also bootstrap less, and in the 2018 triad study longer n clearly reduced soft divergence (section 2). The bias-variance dial behind n, and the tabular n-step and TD(λ) methods, are on Model-free prediction and control.

9Distributional RL: C51, QR-DQN and IQN

A Q-value is the mean of a random return Z(s, a). Bellemare, Dabney and Munos (2017) learn the whole distribution instead, using the distributional Bellman equation: Z(s, a) has the same distribution as R + γZ(S′, A′). For a fixed policy the distributional Bellman operator is a contraction in a maximal form of the Wasserstein metric, so evaluation converges as usual. For control it is not: their Proposition 1 shows the optimality operator "is not a contraction in any metric over distributions", although the means still converge to Q* exponentially fast.

C51: a fixed grid of atoms

The categorical algorithm represents each Z(s, a) as probabilities on N fixed atoms zi = VMIN + iΔz, with Δz = (VMAX − VMIN)/(N − 1); the Atari agent uses N = 51 and VMAX = −VMIN = 10, hence the name. The network outputs a softmax over atoms for each action, and acts greedily on the means. The target for a transition takes the next state's distribution under the greedy action, shrinks every atom by γ, shifts it by r, and clips it to [VMIN, VMAX]. The shifted atoms no longer sit on the grid, so each one's probability is split between its two neighbouring atoms in proportion to closeness (equation 7):

(ΦT̂Z)i = Σj [ 1 − | [r + γzj]VMINVMAX − zi | / Δz ]01 pj(s′, a*)
Φ
projection onto the fixed atoms; [·] clips its argument to the given range
loss
the cross-entropy between the projected target and the predicted distribution, −Σi mi log pi(s, a): the KL divergence up to a constant, so "the Bellman update" becomes "multiclass classification"
a*
the greedy action at s′ by the means; the target uses a target network, as in DQN

The next-state distribution is illustrative (two bumps, a losing outcome and a surviving one, on [−10, 10]); the projection is computed exactly. The dashed line is DQN's scalar target r + γ𝔼[Z]: the distributional target has the same mean whenever nothing is clipped, and keeps the two outcomes apart instead of averaging them.

Algorithm 1 as printed loses probability
The paper's pseudocode computes bj = (T̂zj − VMIN)/Δz, l = ⌊bj⌋, u = ⌈bj⌉ and adds pj(u − bj) to atom l and pj(bj − l) to atom u. When bj is a whole number, l = u and both amounts are zero, so that atom's probability vanishes. With r = 0 and γ = 1 every atom lands on the grid and the whole target is lost; at the default settings the two end atoms land exactly on the grid (b = 1 and b = 10 at N = 11) and their mass disappears derived, from the printed pseudocode. Equation 7 itself has no such gap, and implementations special-case the tie. Switch the toggle above to see it.

Evidence. On five training games, more atoms always helped, and the 51-atom agent beat DQN on all five (the two-atom "Bernoulli" variant on 3 of 5). Across 57 games, best evaluation score during training: C51 701% mean and 178% median human-normalised, against DQN 228% and 79%, Double DQN 307% and 118%, dueling 373% and 151%, prioritised 434% and 124%, prioritised dueling 592% and 172% (their Figure 6, a table). The authors' explanation: the learned distributions keep "the low-value, 'losing' event" separate from "the high-value, 'survival' event, rather than average them into one (unrealizable) expectation". The 51-atom network trains at about 75% of DQN's speed.

QR-DQN and IQN: move the atoms instead

  • QR-DQN (Dabney et al. 2017) fixes the probabilities and learns the locations: N quantiles of the return at the midpoints τ̂i = (2i − 1)/(2N), trained by quantile regression with an asymmetric Huber loss (κ = 1). This minimises a Wasserstein distance directly, closing C51's gap between the theory (Wasserstein contraction) and the algorithm (a KL loss after a projection), and removes the need to choose VMIN and VMAX. With N = 200: 915% mean and 211% median over 57 games (QR-DQN-1, best scores; QR-DQN-0 without the Huber smoothing 881% and 199%).
  • IQN (Dabney et al. 2018) samples τ uniformly from [0, 1] and feeds it to the network through a cosine embedding, φj(τ) = ReLU(Σi=0n−1 cos(πiτ) wij + bj) with n = 64, so one network represents the whole quantile function. Eight samples of τ per update were enough; K = 32 samples choose actions. Distorting the sampling of τ gives risk-sensitive policies (CVaR, for example, acts on the worst η of outcomes). Results, 57 games, 30 no-op starts (their Table 1, averaged over seeds): IQN 1019% mean and 218% median (5 seeds), against QR-DQN 864% and 193% (3 seeds) and Rainbow 1189% and 230% (2 seeds); under human starts IQN's median is 162% against Rainbow's 153%.

Note that IQN's table prints QR-DQN at 193% median and Rainbow at 230%, while their own papers print 211% and 223%: different seeds, averaging and protocol, the subject of section 13.

10NoisyNet: exploration in the weights

ε-greedy explores by occasionally pressing a random button, the same way in every state. NoisyNet (Fortunato et al. 2017) replaces a linear layer y = wx + b with a noisy one whose noise scale is learned by gradient descent along with the weights:

y = (μw + σw ⊙ εw) x + μb + σb ⊙ εb
μ, σ
learnable means and noise scales of the weights and biases
ε
noise. Independent: one Gaussian per weight, pq + q variables for p inputs and q outputs. Factorised: p + q unit Gaussians combined as εi,j = f(εi)f(εj) with f(x) = sgn(x)√|x|, used for DQN and dueling to save random-number generation
initialisation
factorised: μ uniform in [−1/√p, 1/√p], σ = σ0/√p with σ0 = 0.5; independent: μ uniform in [−√(3/p), √(3/p)], σ = 0.017

NoisyNet-DQN drops ε-greedy and acts greedily on the noisy network, with the noise resampled before every action and held fixed across each replayed minibatch; independent noise samples are used for the online network, the target network and acting, "to avoid bias due to the correlation". Because σ is learned, the network can reduce its noise where it is sure and keep it where it is not, giving state-dependent, temporally consistent exploration. Median and mean human-normalised scores, no-op starts (their Table 1): DQN 83% and 319% to NoisyNet-DQN 123% and 379%; dueling 132% and 524% to 172% and 633%; A3C 80% and 293% to 94% and 347%.

48% of what?
The paper's text says NoisyNet adds "at least 18 (in the case of A3C) and at most 48 (in the case of DQN) percentage points to the median". Its Table 1 says otherwise: the median gains are 40 points for DQN (83 to 123) and 14 for A3C (80 to 94); the table's 48% and 18% are relative improvements (123/83 and 94/80) derived. The same finding is recorded on the parent's Milestones tab.

How NoisyNet sits among exploration methods (count bonuses, curiosity, Thompson-style randomised value functions) is on Bandits and exploration.

11Rainbow, and what its ablation shows

Rainbow (Hessel et al. 2017) asked whether the six extensions above "are complementary and can be fruitfully combined". They are not simply stacked: each had to be adapted to the others.

  • Distributional and multi-step: the target distribution is the next-n-th state's distribution shrunk by γn and shifted by the n-step discounted reward, projected onto the 51 atoms; the loss is the KL divergence.
  • Double: the greedy action at st+n is chosen by the online network and its distribution taken from the target network.
  • Prioritised: transitions are prioritised by that KL loss (proportional variant, exponent 0.5) rather than by a TD error, "since this is what the algorithm is minimizing".
  • Dueling: value and advantage streams each output 51 logits per atom, combined per atom as in dueling DQN, then a softmax.
  • Noisy: every linear layer is replaced by a factorised noisy layer, and ε = 0.

The ablation

Removing one component at a time, the paper reports: "Prioritized replay and multi-step learning were the two most crucial components", removing either causing a large drop in median performance (and multi-step also hurt final performance); distributional Q-learning "ranked immediately below", with no difference for the first 40 million frames and a lag afterwards, mostly on games at or above human level; removing Noisy Nets made the median worse but helped a few games; and "in aggregate, we did not observe a significant difference when removing the dueling network" or double Q-learning. The authors' hypothesis for double Q-learning: the actual returns often exceeded 10, outside the [−10, 10] support, so "clipping the values to this constrained range counteracts the overestimation bias", though "the importance of double Q-learning may increase if the support of the distributions is expanded".

The ablation's median curves (their Figure 3) are curves and cannot be read off reliably. Its per-game bars (Figure 4: area under the learning curve, 0 = as good as Rainbow, 1 = as bad as DQN) are drawn as vector rectangles in the PDF, so their heights can be decoded exactly. The Rainbow ablation, game by game tab does that for all six ablations and 55 games:

Later work re-ran the ablation cheaply: Revisiting Rainbow (Obando-Ceron and Castro 2021) revisits Rainbow's components on small-scale environments, arguing that these "can still yield valuable scientific insights" at a fraction of the compute; its findings are not quoted here because they were not checked for this page.

12Scaling out: Ape-X, R2D2 and Agent57

After Rainbow, the value-based line on Atari moved to distributed agents that separate acting from learning.

  • Ape-X (Horgan et al. 2018): 360 actor processes, each with its own copy of the environment, write into one shared prioritised replay, and one GPU learner trains on it (Double DQN with dueling and n-step targets). Actors compute the initial priorities themselves, so new data needs no learner pass. Each actor i explores with its own εi = ε1 + iα/(N−1) (ε = 0.4, α = 7), a spread from greedy to very exploratory. Median human-normalised score 434% with no-op starts after 22.8 billion frames in 5 days (its Table 1).
  • R2D2 (Kapturowski et al. 2019; PDF read via the Wayback Machine) adds an LSTM after the convolutions and replays fixed-length sequences of 80 steps (overlapping by 40). The problem with replaying a recurrent network is the stale hidden state; R2D2 stores the state the actor had and spends a burn-in of 40 steps unrolling the network before training on the rest. It uses 256 actors, n = 5 double Q-learning, γ = 0.997, priorities mixing the max and mean TD error over the sequence (η = 0.9), does not treat a lost life as an episode end, and does not clip rewards: instead it rescales values with h(x) = sign(x)(√(|x| + 1) − 1) + εx, ε = 10−3 (from Pohlen et al. 2018), which compresses a return of 1,000 to about 31.6 derived. Its Table 1: median 1920.6% and mean 4024.9% over 57 games, final score averaged over 3 seeds; the feed-forward variant reached 589.2%.
  • NGU and Agent57 (Badia et al. 2020a; 2020b): R2D2 still scored near zero on the hardest exploration games. Never Give Up adds an intrinsic novelty reward and learns a family of policies, each with its own weight on that reward and its own discount. Agent57 separates the intrinsic and extrinsic value heads, trains 32 such policies, and lets a sliding-window UCB bandit choose which policy each actor runs. It is "the first deep RL agent that outperforms the standard human benchmark on all 57 Atari games"; Skiing, the last, fell after 78 billion frames. Its bandit is described on Bandits and exploration.

Scores for Ape-X, R2D2, MuZero and Agent57, with their protocols, frame counts and the per-game recomputation, are on the parent's Milestones and benchmarks tab. That tab found that Agent57's per-game table prints two of MuZero's scores with an extra digit (Asteroids and Beam Rider); with MuZero's own values the medians reorder. The tab also notes R2D2's paper could not be fetched there; it is quoted here from the archived PDF, and its printed 1920.6% matches the figure MuZero and NGU reprint.

14When to use a value-based deep method, and what people use now

  • Discrete actions, and data that is precious or a simulator that is cheap to replay from: the DQN family. Replay makes it sample-efficient, and off-policy learning lets it reuse old data. The price is sensitivity to the deadly triad, many interacting hyperparameters, and no natural way to handle continuous actions, where the max over actions is itself an optimisation problem (DDPG, TD3 and SAC learn an actor to do that max; on Policy gradients and actor-critic).
  • Stability over sample efficiency, or any action space: PPO-style on-policy methods, same page.
  • Planning matters: MuZero's search uses a value network trained with the same n-step value targets and, on Atari, R2D2's value rescaling; on Model-based RL and planning.
  • No interaction at all: offline variants of Q-learning (CQL and others) must keep Q-values down on actions the data never shows; on Offline RL and imitation.
  • Language models: post-training uses policy-gradient methods (PPO, GRPO), not Q-learning over a 100,000-token action space; on RL for LLMs.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"DQN was stabilised by replay and a target network from the start."
The 2013 version used replay only; the target network arrived with the 2015 Nature version (section 4). The atlas on the parent page keeps them as two rows for this reason.
"Overestimation needs biased estimates."
Every individual estimate can be unbiased, and the max of noisy estimates is still biased upward (Theorem 1 of Double DQN). With function approximation it appears even with exact, noise-free samples (Figure 2, section 5).
"The target network solves the deadly triad."
It reduces divergence a great deal in practice (14% against 61% soft-divergence in the 2018 study), but fitting the frozen target exactly can still diverge when the features cannot represent the target (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy's counterexample, section 3).
"Off-policy means offline."
DQN (and SAC) interact with the environment online and learn off-policy from a replay buffer. Offline RL learns from a fixed dataset with no interaction, a much harder setting with its own page.
"Replay works with any RL algorithm."
Replaying old data requires an off-policy learner: Q-learning's target does not depend on which policy chose the stored action. A policy-gradient method such as A2C needs data from the current policy, or importance ratios for slightly stale data (PPO).
"Multi-step returns are free."
Uncorrected n-step targets in a replay buffer assume the intermediate actions were greedy; they were not. Rainbow and R2D2 accept that bias for faster propagation; Retrace corrects it.
"The dueling head's V is the state value."
Only with the max-subtracting form. The mean-subtracting form every experiment used shifts V and A by a constant; it keeps the action ranking, not the meaning (section 6).
"C51's projection is Algorithm 1 as printed."
The printed pseudocode drops the probability of any atom that lands exactly on the grid; use equation 7 or special-case the tie (section 9).
"Rainbow shows that double Q-learning and dueling do not matter."
It shows no significant aggregate difference when either is removed from Rainbow, where the [−10, 10] support itself limits overestimation. Per game, both helped some games and hurt others (the ablation tab), and the authors expected double Q-learning to matter more with a wider support.
"Agent57 is better than MuZero at Atari."
It is above the human benchmark on all 57 games, which MuZero is not (51); MuZero has the higher mean and median. Which aggregate is "better" is a choice, and Agent57's own comparison table contains two misprinted MuZero scores (parent's Milestones tab).
Comparing medians across papers
DQN's median is 93.5%, 79%, 68% or 47.5% depending on the protocol (section 13). Compare only within one table, or recompute from per-game scores under one protocol.

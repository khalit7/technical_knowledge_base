5Before and after: one real prompt, three updates

The same response, sampled for this page from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on a GSM8K problem, run through what each method computes for every one of its tokens. Before is RLHF with PPO: a reward model's score at the end, a KL penalty on every token, a critic's value at every prefix, GAE. After is GRPO with a verifier, the recipe that replaced it for reasoning: a pass or fail, the group's mean and spread, one number for every token. Dr. GRPO is the 2025 fix of GRPO's two normalisations. Bars are drawn to scale within each step; the counters keep score of what each method needs.

What to take from it: PPO's critic lets credit land on the tokens where the predicted outcome actually changed, and pays for that with a second network that has to be right about every prefix (here we cheated by measuring the value with real continuations, which is what VinePPO does). GRPO gives every token of a response the same push, needs no second network and no reward model when a verifier exists, and pays with a group of rollouts per prompt. Dr. GRPO keeps that and stops long responses from being let off lightly.

6Case study: DeepSeek-R1 and R1-Zero

Released in January 2025 ({{arXiv 2501.12948|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948}}) and published in Nature in September 2025 in a much longer version ({{Nature|https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09422-z}}); the {{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}} rebuilds its tables, decodes its figures and trains a tiny R1-Zero live. What matters for this page:

  • R1-Zero: GRPO plus RLVR (accuracy and format rewards) applied directly to the DeepSeek-V3 base model, with no SFT. Long chains of thought, self-verification and backtracking appeared from RL alone; response length grew over training from about 500 to about 14,200 tokens (28 times, 14,200 / 500 = 28.4), with its steepest step where the length cap was doubled from 32,768 to 65,536 tokens at step 8.2k. AIME 2024 pass@1 (the chance that a single sample solves a problem of the American Invitational Mathematics Examination) climbed from 15.6% to 71.0% in the January 2025 preprint (arXiv v1) and to 77.9% in the later version (arXiv v2 and Nature), which reports the curve to the end of training. Weaknesses: readability and language mixing.
  • R1: the production pipeline: cold-start SFT on curated long chains of thought, reasoning-focused RL, rejection sampling into about 800K new SFT samples, and a final RL stage for helpfulness and harmlessness that used preference reward models only in its last 400 steps. It matched o1-class reasoning at open weights and made GRPO with verifiable rewards the default open recipe through 2025 and 2026.
  • Distillation of R1's traces into small models: cheaper than running RL on them, and better. On Qwen2.5-32B, plain SFT on 800K R1-generated samples reached 72.6 on AIME 2024, against 47.0 for the full R1-Zero RL recipe run directly on the same base model for over 10,000 steps.
  • Cost: R1's RL and data generation took 147K H800 GPU-hours (about $294K at $2 per hour, 147,000 × 2) on top of the base model's 2.788M hours: a post-training bill, not "the cost of R1" (the root's {{Milestones and benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}} tab keeps the dated figures side by side).

7The 2025 to 2026 corrections to GRPO

GRPO's baseline has accumulated well-understood biases and a family of fixes. The DeepSeekMath page's {{Then and now|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} tab animates GRPO's objective term by term through them; here each is derived, with numbers.

Dr. GRPO: two normalisations that bias the update

"GRPO Done Right", from Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training ({{Liu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783}}), finds that both normalisations in GRPO bias what it learns:

  • Length bias from 1/|oi|. Each response's token losses are averaged over its own length, so every token of a long response carries less weight. For a correct response (positive advantage) that gives shorter answers bigger updates; for a wrong one (negative advantage) it penalises long answers less per token, so the policy drifts toward long wrong answers. With the worked example's Â = −0.577, each token of a 100-token wrong response is weighted −0.577 / 100 = −0.00577, and each token of a 1,000-token wrong response only −0.000577: ten times less push-down per token.
  • Difficulty bias from std. Prompts whose rewards barely vary (nearly all correct or nearly all wrong) have a small standard deviation, so dividing by it inflates their advantages and over-weights too-easy and too-hard prompts. In a group of 16 with a single correct answer the mean is 0.0625 and the standard deviation 0.242, so the correct answer gets +3.873; in a group of 16 with 8 correct, the standard deviation is 0.5 and a correct answer gets +1.0.

The fix drops both terms: Âi,t = ri − mean(r1, ..., rG), and the token losses are summed and divided by a constant (the maximum generation length in the authors' implementation) instead of by each response's own length. What is left is an unbiased REINFORCE-style estimator with a group-mean baseline (1 of 16 now gets +0.9375 and 8 of 16 gets +0.5: the gap reflects how surprising the success was, not the group's spread). The paper reports better token efficiency (less length inflation, especially on wrong answers) at equal reasoning performance, and its minimalist recipe reaches 43.3% on AIME 2024 from a 7B base model. The group widget in section 3 switches between the three advantages on real groups.

DAPO: four fixes for long chain-of-thought RL

DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization; ByteDance Seed with Tsinghua University's Institute for AI Industry Research, {{Yu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476}}) is an open, widely adopted recipe that reports 50 points on AIME 2024 from the Qwen2.5-32B base model, above DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B's 47 with 50% of its training steps. Its four changes:

  1. Clip-higher: decouple the clip range into εlow = 0.2 and εhigh = 0.28, clipping the ratio to [1 − εlow, 1 + εhigh]. With a symmetric 0.2, a token the policy gives probability 0.01 can rise only to 0.012 in one update, while a token at 0.9 is effectively unconstrained (0.9 × 1.2 > 1); low-probability exploratory tokens can barely grow, and entropy collapses. With 0.28 the same token can reach 0.0128. (Entropy collapse in general, and its fitted law, is on {{Alignment|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}} "Open problems".)
  2. Dynamic sampling: over-sample and drop groups whose rewards are all identical (all correct or all wrong), since their advantages are zero and they only dilute the batch; keep sampling until the batch is full of groups that carry signal.
  3. Token-level loss: average over all tokens in the batch, (1/Σi|oi|) ΣiΣt, instead of per response and then per group, (1/G) Σi (1/|oi|) Σt, so every token has equal weight and long responses are not under-weighted.
  4. Overlong reward shaping: instead of a flat penalty for truncated responses, a soft length penalty added to the reward:
Rlength(y) = 0 if |y| ≤ Lmax − Lcache;   ((Lmax − Lcache) − |y|) / Lcache if Lmax − Lcache < |y| ≤ Lmax;   −1 if |y| > Lmax
|y|
the response length in tokens; Lmax the maximum length (16,384 in the paper) and Lcache the width of the soft zone before it (4,096). A 14,336-token response sits halfway into the zone: (12,288 − 14,336) / 4,096 = −0.5.

GSPO: the ratio at the sequence level

GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization; Qwen Team, Alibaba, {{Zheng et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18071}}) argues that token-level importance ratios are noisy and misaligned with a reward given to the whole sequence, which destabilises long sequences and mixture-of-experts (MoE) training. It defines one length-normalised ratio per response:

si(θ) = ( πθ(oi | q) / πθold(oi | q) )1/|oi| = exp( (1/|oi|) Σt log ρi,t )
𝒥GSPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ (1/G) Σi min( si(θ) Âi, clip(si(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Âi ) ]
si
the geometric mean of the response's per-token ratios; Âi its group-relative advantage

A 3-token response with per-token ratios 1.1, 0.9 and 1.0 has si = exp((ln 1.1 + ln 0.9 + ln 1.0)/3) = exp(−0.00335) = 0.99666: one number close to 1 in place of three noisy ones. Because the sequence ratio sits so close to 1, the clip ranges are tiny: the paper uses 3e-4 below and 4e-4 above (against 0.2 and 0.27 for its GRPO runs), and reports "a difference of two orders of magnitude in the fractions of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO", GSPO clipping more and still training more efficiently. For MoE models it removes the need for "Routing Replay" (caching which experts were active during rollout so token ratios stay comparable). The paper credits GSPO with contributing to the RL of the Qwen3 models; a GSPO-token variant restores per-token advantages for multi-turn settings. The {{Rollout mismatch|#t-mis}} tab computes the sequence ratio on all 160 real responses.

CISPO: clip the weight, never the gradient

MiniMax-M1's CISPO ({{MiniMax 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13585}}) starts from what PPO's clip does to rare tokens. Reflective tokens such as "However", "Recheck", "Wait" and "Aha" have low probability under the base model, so their ratios grow fast, they are clipped after the first update, and they stop contributing gradient. CISPO keeps every token's gradient and clips only the importance weight, with a stop-gradient:

𝒥CISPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ (1/Σi|oi|) Σi Σt sg( r̂i,t(θ) ) Âi,t log πθ(oi,t | q, oi,<t) ],   r̂ = clip(ρ, 1 − εISlow, 1 + εIShigh)
sg
stop-gradient: the clipped ratio only scales the REINFORCE term, it is never differentiated; "we did not impose a lower bound on the IS weight by setting εISlow to a large value; instead, we only tuned εIShigh"

On Qwen2.5-32B and AIME 2024, "CISPO achieves a 2x speedup compared to DAPO", matching DAPO's results in 50% of the training steps. CISPO is one of the components of ScaleRL, the recipe from a 400,000-GPU-hour study of how RL scales ({{Khatri et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786}}, section 12).

8Off-policy corrections: the rollout engine is not the trainer

There are two sources of off-policyness in real systems: (a) minibatch updates make later steps off-policy within a batch (the clip and the ratio handle this); (b) training/inference mismatch: the vLLM or SGLang rollout engine computes slightly different probabilities than the training stack (kernels, precision, KV cache, batch shapes), so the data is subtly off-policy by design: an implementation gap quietly turns on-policy RL into off-policy RL ({{Yao et al. 2025, "Your Efficient RL Framework Secretly Brings You Off-Policy RL Training"|https://fengyao.notion.site/off-policy-rl}}, which introduced the fix below). The fix that spread is truncated importance sampling (TIS) against the probabilities the engine actually sampled with:

wt = min( πtrain(ot | st) / πrollout(ot | st), C )
πtrain
the probability the training stack computes for the sampled token under the old policy
πrollout
the probability the inference engine actually sampled it with
C
the truncation threshold; verl's documentation suggests "typical threshold 1.5-5.0" for token-level weights and starts from decoupled_token_is(threshold=2.0) ({{verl, Rollout Correction|https://verl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/algo/rollout_corr.html}})

A token the engine sampled at 0.20 that the trainer scores at 0.25 gets weight 1.25; one sampled at 0.05 and scored at 0.25 has ratio 5, truncated to 2. Unlike PPO's two-sided clip, the cap is one-sided: weights below 1 pass through. The sequence-level variant multiplies the per-token ratios over the response (∏ρt), so its thresholds are higher ("typical threshold 2.0-10.0" in verl); rejection sampling (masked importance sampling, MIS) instead drops tokens or sequences whose ratio falls outside set bounds; CISPO's clipped weight (section 7) is the same idea applied to the policy ratio. verl distinguishes a "decoupled" mode with three policies (rollout, old, current) from a "bypass" mode that sets πold = πrollout to skip a forward pass.

How large is the mismatch? On this page's 160 real responses, the same weights in float16 with a KV cache (the "engine") against float32 in one pass (the "trainer") differ by a mean of 0.0015 nats per token; the {{Rollout mismatch|#t-mis}} tab has the full distribution, a bfloat16 comparison, TIS and MIS at any threshold, and GSPO's sequence ratio. In production the gap matters: ScaleRL found that computing the language-model head in FP32 for both generator and trainer "dramatically improves the asymptotic performance A from 0.52 to 0.61" ({{Khatri et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786}}), and the Qwen team's study of a 30B MoE model over "hundreds of thousands of GPU hours" argues that a token-level objective is a valid surrogate for the sequence reward "only when both the training-inference discrepancy and policy staleness are minimized" ({{Zheng et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01374}}).

Theory caught up: Group-Relative REINFORCE Is Secretly an Off-Policy Algorithm ({{Yao et al., ICLR 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24203}}) derives group-relative REINFORCE without assuming where the data came from, reframes GRPO and its friends as regularised REINFORCE, and clarifies "the roles of importance sampling and clipping in GRPO", which makes these corrections principled rather than patches. For a from-scratch loop: log the engine's log-probabilities and compare them with the trainer's; the gap is your mismatch diagnostic.

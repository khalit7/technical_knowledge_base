5RLVR: a program as the reward, and what it gets wrong

RL with verifiable rewards replaces the learned reward model with a program: exact-match answer checking for maths, unit tests for code, format checks, task-success checks for agents. The reward is binary or a small rubric sum. The name and first recipe are Tülu 3's (November 2024: a reward of 10 when the verifier accepts, optimised with PPO and a value model; {{Lambert et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}; its lineage is on {{Alignment|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}} section 7). DeepSeek-R1-Zero's reward is the reference shape: an accuracy reward (the final answer matched against the reference for maths; compilation plus hidden test cases for code) plus a format reward for reasoning inside <think> tags, equally weighted, and deliberately no neural reward model "because of reward hacking" by neural reward models at scale ({{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}). It needs no preference data, cannot be flattered the way a reward model can, and scales with compute; it works only where a checker exists, and an imperfect checker is the new proxy.

6DeepSeek-R1 and R1-Zero: the recipe that made GRPO the default

Released in January 2025 ({{arXiv 2501.12948|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948}}), published in Nature in September 2025 in a much longer version ({{Nature|https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09422-z}}); the {{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}} rebuilds its tables, decodes its figures and trains a tiny R1-Zero live.

  • R1-Zero: GRPO plus verifiable accuracy and format rewards applied directly to the DeepSeek-V3 base model, no SFT. Long chains of thought, self-verification and backtracking appeared from RL alone; response length grew from about 500 to about 14,200 tokens (28 times, 14,200 / 500 = 28.4), with its steepest step where the length cap was doubled from 32,768 to 65,536 tokens at step 8.2k. AIME 2024 pass@1 (the chance that a single sample solves a problem of the American Invitational Mathematics Examination) climbed from 15.6% to 71.0% in the January 2025 preprint (arXiv v1) and to 77.9% in the later version (arXiv v2 and Nature), which reports the curve to the end of training. Weaknesses: readability and language mixing.
  • R1: cold-start SFT on curated long chains of thought, reasoning RL, rejection sampling into about 800K new SFT samples, and a final RL stage for helpfulness and harmlessness that used preference reward models only in its last 400 steps. It matched o1-class reasoning at open weights.
  • Distillation beats RL on small models: on Qwen2.5-32B, plain SFT on 800K R1-generated samples reached 72.6 on AIME 2024, against 47.0 for the full R1-Zero RL recipe run on the same base for over 10,000 steps.
  • Cost: R1's RL and data generation took 147K H800 GPU-hours (about $294K at $2 per hour, 147,000 × 2) on top of the base model's 2.788M hours: a post-training bill, not "the cost of R1" (the root's {{Milestones and benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}} tab keeps the dated figures).

7GRPO's biases and the fixes: Dr. GRPO, DAPO, CISPO, GSPO

Each fix below removes one measured bias of the objective in section 3. The DeepSeekMath page's {{Then and now|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} tab walks GRPO's objective term by term through them; here each is derived with numbers.

Dr. GRPO: two normalisations that bias the update

"GRPO Done Right", from Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training ({{Liu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783}}):

  • Length bias from 1/|oi|. Each response's token losses are averaged over its own length, so each token of a long response carries less weight. For a correct response that favours short answers; for a wrong one it penalises long answers less per token, so the policy drifts toward long wrong answers. With the worked example's Â = −0.577, each token of a 100-token wrong response is weighted −0.577 / 100 = −0.00577, each token of a 1,000-token one only −0.000577: ten times less push-down per token.
  • Difficulty bias from std. Prompts whose rewards barely vary (nearly all right or nearly all wrong) have a small standard deviation, so dividing by it inflates their advantages. In a group of 16 with a single correct answer the mean is 0.0625 and the standard deviation 0.242, so the correct answer gets +3.873; in a group of 16 with 8 correct, the standard deviation is 0.5 and a correct answer gets +1.0 (the group widget in section 3).

The fix drops both: Âi,t = ri − mean(r1, ..., rG), and token losses are summed and divided by a constant (the maximum generation length in the authors' code) instead of each response's own length. What is left is an unbiased REINFORCE-style estimator with a group-mean baseline (1 of 16 now gets +0.9375 and 8 of 16 gets +0.5: the push reflects how surprising the success was, not the group's spread). The paper reports less length inflation, especially on wrong answers, at equal reasoning performance, and its minimalist recipe reaches 43.3% on AIME 2024 from a 7B base model.

DAPO: four fixes for long chain-of-thought RL

DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization; ByteDance Seed with Tsinghua University's Institute for AI Industry Research, {{Yu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476}}), an open, widely adopted recipe, reports 50 points on AIME 2024 from the Qwen2.5-32B base model, above DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B's 47 with 50% of its training steps:

  1. Clip-higher: decouple the clip range into εlow = 0.2 and εhigh = 0.28 (entropy, below).
  2. Dynamic sampling: over-sample and drop groups whose rewards are all identical, since their advantages are zero and they only dilute the batch; keep sampling until the batch is full of groups that carry signal.
  3. Token-level loss: average over all tokens in the batch, (1/Σi|oi|) ΣiΣt, instead of per response and then per group, (1/G) Σi (1/|oi|) Σt, so every token has equal weight and long responses are not under-weighted.
  4. Overlong reward shaping: instead of a flat penalty for truncated responses, a soft length penalty added to the reward:
Rlength(y) = 0 if |y| ≤ Lmax − Lcache;   ((Lmax − Lcache) − |y|) / Lcache if Lmax − Lcache < |y| ≤ Lmax;   −1 if |y| > Lmax
|y|
the response length in tokens; Lmax the maximum length (16,384 in the paper) and Lcache the width of the soft zone before it (4,096). A 14,336-token response sits halfway into the zone: (12,288 − 14,336) / 4,096 = −0.5.

Entropy collapse, and the clip that causes it: clip-higher and CISPO

The symmetric clip treats rare tokens unfairly. With ε = 0.2, a token the policy gives probability 0.01 can rise only to 0.012 in one update, while a token at 0.9 is effectively unconstrained (0.9 × 1.2 > 1). Low-probability exploratory tokens can barely grow, the distribution sharpens, and entropy collapses; with εhigh = 0.28 the same rare token can reach 0.0128. The collapse has a fitted law: in long runs, performance follows R = −a eH + b in the policy's entropy H, so it is capped at −a + b once entropy reaches 0; clip-higher and covariance-based clipping (Clip-Cov and KL-Cov, which restrict updates on the few tokens whose log-probability and advantage covary most) keep entropy alive ({{Cui et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22617}}).

MiniMax-M1's CISPO ({{MiniMax 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.13585}}) names the tokens this hurts: reflective tokens such as "However", "Recheck", "Wait" and "Aha" have low probability under the base model, so their ratios grow fast, they are clipped after the first update, and they stop contributing gradient. CISPO "clips importance sampling weights rather than token updates": every token keeps its gradient, and only the importance weight is clipped, under a stop-gradient:

𝒥CISPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ (1/Σi|oi|) Σi Σt sg( r̂i,t(θ) ) Âi,t log πθ(oi,t | q, oi,<t) ],   r̂ = clip(ρ, 1 − εISlow, 1 + εIShigh)
sg
stop-gradient: the clipped ratio only scales the REINFORCE term, it is never differentiated; "we did not impose a lower bound on the IS weight by setting εISlow to a large value; instead, we only tuned εIShigh"

The rare-token toy, in four stretches

  1. Update 0: one rare token, advantage +1. The rollout sampled a token the policy gives probability 0.010, in a response that scored above its group. Every objective starts with weight 1: ratio 1, nothing clipped yet.
  2. Updates 1 to 3: every objective pushes. Sixteen gradient steps are taken on the same rollout (R1-Zero used 16 minibatches per rollout). Inside the clip range the token's weight is its ratio, so all four curves rise together.
  3. Updates 4 to 6: the clip binds. Once the ratio passes 1 + ε the clipped objective is flat: weight 0, and the token stops learning from this rollout. At the defaults that happens after 4 updates under the 0.2 clip and 5 under clip-higher, near p0 × 1.2 and p0 × 1.28 (the dashed lines).
  4. Updates 7 to 16: CISPO keeps pushing. CISPO never zeroes the gradient: it caps the weight at 1.28, and a rare reflective token keeps learning from every minibatch. With no clip the weight is the raw ratio and grows with every step, on the strength of one advantage estimate: what the clip exists to stop.

On Qwen2.5-32B and AIME 2024, "CISPO achieves a 2x speedup compared to DAPO", matching DAPO's results in 50% of the training steps. It gives up the clip's trust region and relies on the weight cap and small steps. CISPO is a component of ScaleRL, the recipe from a 400,000-GPU-hour study of how RL scales (section 12).

GSPO: the ratio at the sequence level

GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization; Qwen Team, Alibaba, {{Zheng et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18071}}) argues that token-level ratios are noisy and misaligned with a reward given to the whole sequence, which destabilises long sequences and mixture-of-experts (MoE) training. A 3-token response with per-token ratios 1.1, 0.9 and 1.0 gets one ratio, exp((ln 1.1 + ln 0.9 + ln 1.0)/3) = exp(−0.00335) = 0.99666, in place of three noisy ones:

si
the geometric mean of the response's per-token ratios; Âi its group-relative advantage

Because the sequence ratio sits so close to 1, the clip ranges are tiny: 3e-4 below and 4e-4 above (against 0.2 and 0.27 for the paper's GRPO runs), with "a difference of two orders of magnitude in the fractions of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO", GSPO clipping more and still training more efficiently. The price is the per-token view: one outlier token is no longer clipped on its own. For MoE models it removes the need for "Routing Replay" (caching which experts were active during rollout so token ratios stay comparable). The paper credits GSPO with contributing to the RL of the Qwen3 models; a GSPO-token variant restores per-token advantages for multi-turn settings. Section 8 computes si on all 160 real responses.

8Off-policy corrections: the rollout engine is not the trainer

Real systems are off-policy in two ways: minibatch updates make later steps off-policy within a batch (the ratio and the clip handle this), and training/inference mismatch: the vLLM or SGLang engine computes slightly different probabilities than the training stack (kernels, precision, KV cache, batch shapes), so an implementation gap quietly turns on-policy RL into off-policy RL ({{Yao et al. 2025, "Your Efficient RL Framework Secretly Brings You Off-Policy RL Training"|https://fengyao.notion.site/off-policy-rl}}). How large is it? Below, the same weights computed three ways on this page's rollouts.

The fix that spread is truncated importance sampling (TIS), introduced in that post, against the probabilities the engine actually sampled with:

wt = min( πtrain(ot | st) / πrollout(ot | st), C )
πtrain, πrollout
the probability the training stack computes for the sampled token under the old policy; the probability the engine sampled it with
C
the truncation threshold; verl suggests "typical threshold 1.5-5.0" for token-level weights and starts from decoupled_token_is(threshold=2.0) ({{verl, Rollout Correction|https://verl.readthedocs.io/en/latest/algo/rollout_corr.html}})

A token the engine sampled at 0.20 that the trainer scores at 0.25 gets weight 1.25; one sampled at 0.05 and scored at 0.25 has ratio 5, truncated to 2. Unlike PPO's two-sided clip the cap is one-sided: weights below 1 pass through. The sequence-level variant multiplies the per-token ratios over the response (∏ρt), which compounds every token's gap, so its thresholds are higher ("typical threshold 2.0-10.0" in verl); rejection sampling (masked importance sampling, MIS) instead drops tokens or sequences whose ratio falls outside set bounds; CISPO's clipped weight (section 7) is the same idea applied to the policy ratio. verl distinguishes a "decoupled" mode with three policies (rollout, old, current) from a "bypass" mode that sets πold = πrollout to skip a forward pass.

The sequence ratio compounds every token's gap; GSPO's geometric mean stays within a hair of 1

In production the gap matters. ScaleRL found that computing the language-model head in FP32 for both generator and trainer "dramatically improves the asymptotic performance A from 0.52 to 0.61" ({{Khatri et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786}}); the Qwen team's study of a 30B MoE model over "hundreds of thousands of GPU hours" argues that a token-level objective is a valid surrogate for the sequence reward "only when both the training-inference discrepancy and policy staleness are minimized" ({{Zheng et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.01374}}). The weight export opens a quieter gap: casting the fp32 master to bf16 with round-to-nearest drops every update smaller than half a bf16 step, so on Qwen3-1.7B (AdamW, learning rate 10-6) only 0.4 to 0.5% of served weights change per step and the sampler serves a policy 50 to 150 optimizer steps stale, a staleness that importance ratios computed on the trainer's fp32 copy cannot see. Fair Cast exports with stochastic rounding and computes the loss on the served copy, so rollouts are on-policy for the weights that produced them; in GRPO runs on one H100 each with models up to 4B parameters (Qwen3-0.6B to 4B, OLMoE-1B-7B), it ended more than a point ahead on 18 of 25 matched seed-pairs and within a point on 6 ({{Fair Cast|n:40c5c17b0d0d81c39641d49880e39f73}}). MoE models add a discrete version, router drift: a tiny probability difference can flip which experts a token is routed to. And low-precision rollouts widen the gap on purpose, to buy speed: the recipes that work treat the mismatch, not the quantisation error, as the thing to engineer, and reach BF16-level RL with FP4 rollouts (TRACE, up to 5.4x faster rollouts). Both in detail:

Router drift in MoE models, and low-precision rollouts (TRACE, TRIAGE, KV4-RL) (closed)

The trainer computes the gradient through different experts than the engine sampled with (router drift). Routing replay (R3) reuses the rollout's expert choices during training; Router Drift Control argues that replay biases the gradient, because it drops the experts the training router would have picked, and instead rejects whole responses whose routing disagreement is high. In asynchronous RL, Router Drift Control plus TIS raised the average peak validation accuracy on AIME 2024 and 2025 by 5.5 points over TIS alone and 4.5 over R3 ({{Router Drift Control (arXiv 2610.21682)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21682}}).

TRACE guides the trainer's FP4 rounding by the rollout's and reaches RL "comparable to BF16 rollout" with FP4 weights, activations and KV cache on four large MoE models, with up to 5.4x faster rollouts ({{TRACE (arXiv 2610.07767)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07767}}); TRIAGE repairs mismatched updates by their gradient direction and keeps native NVFP4 W4A4 on both sampler and learner, reaching full-precision scores on five math benchmarks with Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-30B-A3B at up to 2.3x BF16 rollout throughput ({{TRIAGE (arXiv 2610.07043)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07043}}); KV4-RL found that plain INT4 KV rollouts "can degrade learning even with token-level truncated importance sampling" and fixed it by keeping attention sinks and recent tokens in INT8 ({{KV4-RL (arXiv 2610.14448)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14448}}). The formats themselves are on {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}.

Theory caught up: Group-Relative REINFORCE Is Secretly an Off-Policy Algorithm ({{Yao et al., ICLR 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24203}}) derives group-relative REINFORCE without assuming where the data came from, reframes GRPO and its relatives as regularised REINFORCE and clarifies "the roles of importance sampling and clipping in GRPO", which makes these corrections principled rather than patches. For a from-scratch loop: log the engine's log-probabilities and compare them with the trainer's; the gap is your mismatch diagnostic.

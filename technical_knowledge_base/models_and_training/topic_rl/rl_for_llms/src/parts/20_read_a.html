The idea in one screen

RL post-trains a language model by sampling its answers, scoring each whole answer with one number, and raising the probability of every token in proportion to how much better than expected its answer scored; the algorithms differ in how they estimate "expected": a learned critic at every token (PPO), or the mean of a group of answers to the same prompt (GRPO, RLOO).

One wrong answer, one score at its end: PPO's critic blames the tokens where the answer went wrong; GRPO blames every token equally

Real data: Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct's answer 7 to GSM8K test problem 3 (242 tokens, wrong; 6 of the 16 answers to this prompt pass the verifier). PPO's critic is measured here, not learned: 16 continuations sampled from each of 10 prefixes give the chance of passing from that prefix (VinePPO's method). It starts at 0.5, falls from 0.56 to 0.19 over the shaded stretch (tokens 48 to 73, where the working goes wrong) and reaches 0 by token 97, so PPO's advantage, the score minus the critic's estimate, blames the early tokens and nothing after the critic knew the answer was lost. GRPO gives all 242 tokens (0 − 0.375) / 0.484 = −0.775; Dr. GRPO gives 0 − 0.375 = −0.375. The KL penalty is left out (β = 0) and GAE's λ = 1, to show the credit alone; section 4 steps through the full computation.

You need: the policy-gradient theorem, baselines, GAE and PPO's clip from {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}} (sections 4, 6 and 9); where RL sits in post-training from {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}} (section 4 draws the PPO and GRPO loops, section 7 the swap of a reward model for a verifier).

Why RL at all: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can only imitate demonstrations; RL can improve on them, because it needs a way to score an answer, not a way to write one. That makes RL the engine behind instruction-following chat models (RL from human feedback, RLHF) and behind reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 (RL with verifiable rewards, RLVR). Three methods carry most of the field, and each removes one component from the one before it:

1Token generation as a Markov decision process

The MDP vocabulary of the root ({{Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}}, "The loop, state and return") maps onto generation with three things unusual: the environment is trivial, the action space is huge, and the reward comes once.

  • State st: the prompt plus the tokens so far, the "state = full history" choice St = Ht, Markov by construction because nothing is hidden. Action at: the next token, one of about 105 (151,936 for the Qwen model on this page, from its {{config.json|https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct/blob/main/config.json}}). Transition: deterministic and known, the token is appended (agentic RL breaks this, section 11). Policy: the LLM, πθ(at | st).
  • Reward: zero at every token except the last, where one scalar lands (a reward-model score, or a verifier's pass or fail): the hero's top row. The horizon is the generation length and γ is typically 1, so the return from every token of a response is the same number, the final score.
J(θ) = 𝔼q ∼ 𝒟, o ∼ πθ(· | q) [ r(q, o) ],    ∇θJ ≈ (1/N) Σn Σt ∇θ log πθ(o(n)t | q(n), o(n)<t) · Â(n)t
q, o, r(q, o)
a prompt from the training set 𝒟; a response (o1, ..., o|o|) sampled token by token, |o| its length; the scalar score of the whole response
N, Ât
the number of sampled responses; the estimated advantage of token t: the score minus a baseline that estimates how well responses to this prompt were expected to score. A baseline that does not depend on the action leaves the gradient unbiased and cuts its variance (the theorem is derived on {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}).

How the advantage is estimated is exactly what separates the algorithms on this page: PPO learns a value model to predict it token by token; GRPO and RLOO estimate it from a group of samples for the same prompt. Three consequences: credit assignment is severe (one scalar for hundreds or thousands of tokens); the environment model is trivially known, so model-based planning buys nothing (the reward model does not make RLHF model-based, the root's common mistakes); and rollouts are expensive, because generating at inference speed dominates wall-clock time (section 12).

2RLHF with PPO: four models, a shaped reward and a critic

The standard recipe since {{InstructGPT|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}} (verified on the {{InstructGPT page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}, which trains the three-step pipeline live in its "Run the three steps" tab) runs four models:

  1. Policy πθ, initialised from the SFT model, and a frozen reference πref, a copy of it.
  2. Reward model rφ: frozen, trained beforehand on human preference pairs, one scalar per full response. InstructGPT's was 6B parameters, trained on rankings of 4 to 9 answers per prompt with the Bradley-Terry pairwise loss (on Alignment, section 3).
  3. Value model Vψ (the critic): trained alongside the policy (InstructGPT initialised it from the RM); it predicts the expected final reward from each prefix, which gives per-token advantages through GAE.

The loop: sample prompts; generate one response each; score it with the RM; subtract a per-token KL penalty so the reward the policy sees already contains it; compute advantages with Vψ and GAE; update the policy with PPO's clipped objective and the value model by regression; repeat.

rt = 1[t = T] · rφ(q, o) − β log( πθ(ot | q, o<t) / πref(ot | q, o<t) )
T
= |o|, the last token; 1[t = T] puts the RM score there only
β
the KL coefficient (0.02 in InstructGPT); the log-ratio is a one-sample estimate of KL(πθ ‖ πref) at that token. Summed over a response it is InstructGPT's sequence-level term β log(πRL(y | x) / πSFT(y | x)) ({{Eq. 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155}})

Why the KL penalty: it keeps the policy where the reward model is trustworthy, near the data it was trained on (the leash against over-optimisation, drawn on {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}} section 2); it preserves general capabilities and fluency; and it ties the policy to a fixed anchor, a trust region relative to the start rather than to last iteration's policy.

GAE over tokens. GAE ({{Schulman et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02438}}; the λ dial is explained on Policy gradients and actor-critic, section 6) blends one-step and multi-step estimates of how much better a token turned out than the critic expected:

δt = rt + γVψ(st+1) − Vψ(st),    Ât = Σl=0T−t (γλ)l δt+l
δt
the temporal-difference (TD) error at token t; Vψ(st) is zero after the last token; γ = 1 here
λ
in [0, 1]: λ = 0 trusts the critic completely (low variance, biased when it is wrong); λ = 1 uses the actual final reward minus the baseline (unbiased, high variance). The critic is regressed onto the returns Ât + Vψ(st).

With γ = λ = 1 the sum telescopes: Ât = (final score − β × the sum of the remaining log-ratios) − V(st), and the critic serves only as a per-prefix baseline (the hero's PPO row). With λ < 1 a token's credit is mostly the jump in predicted value it caused: the point of a critic, and its weakness, because it must be right about every prefix of every response.

The clipped update is PPO's, unchanged: maximise min(ρtÂt, clip(ρt, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Ât) with the per-token ratio ρt = πθ(ot | st) / πθold(ot | st) and ε typically 0.2 ({{Schulman et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347}}). Once a token's ratio has moved more than ε the way its advantage wants, its gradient is zero; a wrong-way move keeps its gradient, which makes several minibatch epochs on one batch of rollouts safe. The four cases, the derivation from TRPO and the clip animated over epochs are on {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}, section 9; what the clip does to rare tokens in an LLM is section 7 here.

Pain points: four large models in memory; a value model as big as the policy, trained from scratch during RL, asked to value every prefix when the true reward is one scalar at the end. How hard that is was measured: on MATH and GSM8K, PPO's value networks "often produce poor estimate of expected return and barely outperform a random baseline when comparing alternative steps"; replacing them with Monte Carlo estimates from extra rollouts at intermediate states (VinePPO, the method this page's hero uses) beat PPO in up to 3.0 times less wall-clock time ({{Kazemnejad et al., ICML 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.01679}}). GRPO removes the pain differently: it gives up per-token credit altogether.

Where RLHF came from, and what InstructGPT achieved and cost (closed)

RLHF began with simulated robots and Atari learning from preferences between clips ({{Christiano et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741}}); PPO with a KL-penalised learned reward arrived with fine-tuning language models from preferences ({{Ziegler et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08593}}), moved to summarisation ({{Stiennon et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.01325}}) and became the standard recipe with InstructGPT, which also mixed in pretraining gradients ("PPO-ptx") to limit regressions on public benchmarks.

Results (verified on the InstructGPT page): outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT model were preferred to those of 175B GPT-3; 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 85 ± 3% of the time; the RL stage cost 60 petaflop/s-days against 3,640 for pretraining GPT-3, about 1.6% (60 / 3,640 = 0.0165).

Corrections carried from the InstructGPT page
InstructGPT is "about 1.4 to 1.5 times as truthful" by human labels, not "twice"; it is 16 to 20% less toxic at 175B when asked to be respectful, not "25%", and no less biased on Winogender or CrowS-Pairs ({{InstructGPT page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}).

3GRPO and RLOO: a group of samples instead of a critic

Group Relative Policy Optimization is PPO minus the value model ({{DeepSeekMath, Shao et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300}}; the {{DeepSeekMath page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} animates PPO against GRPO on one question and trains six methods live). For each prompt, sample a group of G responses, score each, and give every token of a response the same advantage: its score's distance from the group mean, in units of the group's spread.

A group of four, worked. A maths prompt, G = 4, a binary verifier; one response is correct and three are wrong, r = (1, 0, 0, 0). Computed by the page's engine and checked against src/recompute.py:

  1. Baseline: the group mean is (1 + 0 + 0 + 0) / 4 = 0.25.
  2. Spread: the deviations are 0.75, −0.25, −0.25 and −0.25; their squares average to (0.5625 + 3 × 0.0625) / 4 = 0.1875, so the standard deviation is √0.1875 = 0.433.
  3. Advantages: the correct response gets 0.75 / 0.433 = +1.732 on every one of its tokens; each wrong response gets −0.25 / 0.433 = −0.577. They sum to zero: the correct answer is pushed up by as much as the three wrong ones together are pushed down.

If all four had been correct, or all wrong, every advantage would be zero and the group would teach nothing but cost four rollouts; DAPO's dynamic sampling (section 7) filters such groups. On this page's real data, 4 of 10 GSM8K groups of 16 from the 0.5B model are uniform under the strict verifier (all four all-wrong).

The KL is estimated per token with Schulman's k3 estimator ({{Approximating KL divergence|http://joschu.net/blog/kl-approx.html}}): 𝔻KL ≈ x − log x − 1 with x = πref(token) / πθ(token). It is never negative (x − log x − 1 ≥ 0 for every x > 0, zero only at 1) and unbiased for KL(πθ ‖ πref) when tokens are sampled from πθ, because the expectation of x under πθ is exactly 1. On a token the reference gives half the policy's probability, x = 0.5 and k3 = 0.5 + 0.693 − 1 = 0.193.

Corrections carried from the DeepSeekMath page
The paper never trains PPO: GRPO's memory saving is asserted, not measured, and the first head-to-head is in DeepSeek-R1's supplementary material. And differentiating k3 in the loss gives, in expectation, the gradient of KL(πref ‖ πθ), not of KL(πθ ‖ πref): the value is an unbiased estimate of one divergence, its gradient pushes on the other ({{DeepSeekMath page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}}, "k3 gradient" widget).

RLOO, REINFORCE leave-one-out, is the older member of the family: response i's baseline is the mean of the other G − 1 rewards ({{Kool et al. 2019|https://openreview.net/forum?id=r1lgTGL5DE}}). That baseline does not use ri, so the estimator is exactly unbiased, and algebra shows it is the mean-centred reward scaled by G/(G − 1): ri − (Σj≠i rj)/(G − 1) = (G/(G − 1)) (ri − mean). For (1, 0, 0, 0) that is +1.000 and −0.333. GRPO's mean-centring includes ri in its own baseline, which only shrinks the advantage by (G − 1)/G; the bias that matters is the standard-deviation division (section 7). {{Ahmadian et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740}} ("Back to Basics") argued that such "simpler REINFORCE-style optimization variants outperform both PPO and newly proposed 'RL-free' methods such as DPO and RAFT" for RLHF.

What GRPO gains and loses. No value model: roughly half the memory and no critic warm-up. The DeepSeek-R1 team report that PPO can match GRPO, but only with careful GAE tuning (λ = 1.0; "the default value in most open-source PPO implementations", 0.95, is much worse), and still at the cost of a full value model ({{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}). GRPO is group-baselined REINFORCE with PPO-style clipping, policy-based rather than actor-critic because nothing bootstraps from the baseline (the root's common mistakes). It pays with equal credit for every token, noisier on long agentic tasks, and with G rollouts per prompt (typically 8 to 64), shifting cost from memory to inference compute.

The clip on the worked example, published settings, and the std convention (closed)

The clip, on one token of the correct response: suppose that after a few minibatch steps its ratio is ρ = 1.3, with ε = 0.2. The unclipped term is 1.3 × 1.732 = 2.252 and the clipped term 1.2 × 1.732 = 2.078; the min takes 2.078, which does not depend on θ, so this token contributes no more gradient. The same ratio on a token of a wrong response: unclipped 1.3 × (−0.577) = −0.751, clipped 1.2 × (−0.577) = −0.693; the min takes −0.751, the unclipped term, so the gradient still pushes that token's probability back down. With one gradient step per batch the ratio stays at 1 (up to the rollout mismatch of section 8) and the clip never binds: the update is plain REINFORCE with a group baseline, and the clip matters only when a batch is reused for several steps.

Published settings. DeepSeekMath-RL 7B used G = 64 samples per question, β = 0.04, a policy learning rate of 1e-6 and a single policy update per sampling batch, so its clip barely binds; it lifted the 7B instruct model from 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH and 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K. DeepSeek-R1-Zero used G = 16, β = 0.001, a learning rate of 3e-6, a reference replaced every 400 steps, and 16 minibatches per rollout trained once each with an unusually large clip ratio of 10 ({{DeepSeekMath page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}}, {{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}). The β values are not comparable with InstructGPT's 0.02, which sits in the reward rather than the loss.

The standard deviation. Implementations differ on dividing by G or G − 1 (for (1, 0, 0, 0): 0.433 and +1.732 / −0.577 with G; 0.5 and +1.5 / −0.5 with G − 1), and add a small constant so that a uniform group does not divide by zero (without it the loss becomes NaN); the examples on this page divide by G.

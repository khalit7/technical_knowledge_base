9pass@1 against pass@k: does RLVR teach, or elicit?

RLVR reliably raises pass@1. The question is whether it adds abilities the base model lacked or makes the base model's best answers its usual ones (sharpening). The tool is pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)k for a problem solved with probability p per sample, averaged over problems: sharpening raises pass@1 and can lower pass@k at large k, because probability taken from rarely solved problems is coverage lost. Predict: if RL only sharpens, which model solves more problems with 1,024 tries?

Sharpening doubles pass@1 yet loses at large k; the curves cross

Sharpening only: pass@1 10.4% (base) to 21.1% (after RL); pass@1024 80.0% to 77.9%. With expansion, pass@1024 rises to 82.9%. illustrative 40 problems with per-sample success probabilities spread from 0 to 0.9 for the base model; "sharpening" raises every probability above 0.15 toward 1 and pushes the rest toward 0; "expansion" also gives a quarter of the unsolved problems a small chance. pass@k computed exactly per problem and averaged; the shape, not the numbers, is the point.

What the evidence says. {{Yue et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837}} (NeurIPS 2025 oral): RLVR-trained models beat their base at small k, but "the base models achieve a higher pass@k score when k is large"; RLVR operates "within the base model's capabilities rather than expanding beyond them", six popular RLVR algorithms "perform similarly and remain far from optimal", and distillation introduces genuinely new reasoning patterns. Against: prolonged RL with KL control and reference resets (ProRL) "consistently outperform[s] base models across a wide range of pass@k evaluations", including problems the base cannot solve at any sampling budget ({{Liu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24864}}). Agentic tool use pays the same tax: base models with a light harness "often surpass their post-trained counterparts in solution coverage (pass@K)", and the loss of retry value, a Sharpening Tax estimable from a few rollouts, is positive in 36 of 42 base and post-trained pairs, while the base model reaches more tasks at pass@128 in only 26 (clearly in 18, mostly the larger models): the two counts differ. Posterior-tempered group sampling (PTGS), which sets each prompt's sampling temperature from its estimated difficulty during RL, lowered the tax only within run-to-run noise (five runs per arm, two grid-world games, one 7B model), and a plain fixed temperature of 0.5 paid a smaller tax in both games: promising, not shown ({{Sharpening Tax in Post-Training|n:3fe5c17b0d0d81efa2a9f532c14c87a3}}, paper page; {{arXiv 2610.01509|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01509}}). And a warning about reading any gain: spurious rewards, random ones, lifted Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500 by 21.4 points against 29.1 for true rewards, traced to "clipping bias" in GRPO amplifying a pretraining behaviour (code-style reasoning, 65% to over 90% of answers), an effect that "often fail[s] to produce gains for other model families, such as Llama3 or OLMo2" ({{Shao et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947}}).

Where the gain lives. Comparing Qwen3-4B-Base with its RL-trained model on 120 AIME problems, taking the RL model's token only at the positions where the two models' greedy choices differ (about 4% of positions) and the base model's everywhere else recovered 90.5% of the pass@1 gain; forcing just the RL model's opening word, "Alright", recovered 35%, and power sampling the base model (sharpening its own distribution, at 8,192 tokens) gave nothing over greedy decoding ({{What RL Brings to Reasoning (arXiv 2610.24663)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24663}}). So RL changes a few decisions the base model would not make, and sharpening the base model's own distribution does not find them. The markers people read as RL-made reasoning are mostly inherited: RL from a base model almost never coins "wait"; it arrives with the data (mid-training, reasoning-trace SFT) and some RL recipes multiply it where present, so a marker's frequency is not evidence that RL induced the reasoning ({{Inherited, Not Invented (arXiv 2610.30882)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30882}}). The RL itself does matter against a filtered-data baseline: in a pre-registered comparison (Qwen3-4B-Base, LoRA, 3 seeds, one synthetic task), GRPO beat a rejection-sampling fine-tune tuned over 27 runs on every prompt set ({{Is it RL or Data? (arXiv 2610.25812)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.25812}}).

The reading the sources support: RLVR reliably converts pass@k into pass@1 and improves reasoning validity, through a small number of changed decisions that plain sharpening of the base model does not reproduce and that training on the base model's own filtered samples matches only partly; the coverage post-training gives up shows in agentic tool use as in maths and code, and how much depends partly on how rollouts are sampled; whether it creates capability outside the base model's support remains contested, with prolonged multi-stage RL the strongest claim for expansion. Measure pass@k at large k, on more than one base family, before claiming a new capability. The same question seen from inference (a measured chart where pass@1 climbs and pass@256 does not, and what that means for spending samples) is on {{Test-time compute and reasoning|n:4035c17b0d0d813f9fb2d62cff1f09ef}}, section 8.

The rest of the evidence, in the order it arrived (closed)
  • DeepSeekMath already saw the shape in 2024: RL raised majority-vote accuracy (Maj@K) but not Pass@K; the page's decoding of its Figure 7 finds Pass@64 slightly lower after RL ({{DeepSeekMath page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}}).
  • The invisible leash: RLVR as "a support-constrained optimization mechanism"; token-level entropy can rise while answer-level entropy falls ({{Wu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14843}}).
  • Reasoning quality: judging the chain and not just the answer (CoT-Pass@k), RLVR "can encourage sound reasoning processes even when rewards depend solely on answer correctness" ({{Wen et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14245}}).
  • Agentic detail: base models "equipped with a light inference harness, can serve as capable agents" despite far lower pass@1; post-training pushes tasks to always solved or never solved; the 42 cases are 14 base and post-trained pairs from four families on three agentic benchmarks.
  • On-policy distillation shows the same shape: it "improves performance without expanding the student's capabilities" ({{Cui et al. (arXiv 2610.03185)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03185}}).

10Reward hacking, specifically in LLM RL

Every RL method optimises the reward it is given, not the one intended. The general picture, the over-optimisation curve of {{Gao et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760}} (proxy reward keeps rising while the true reward peaks and falls as the policy moves away from its start) and how the KL leash decides where the policy stops on it, is {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}} section 2; chat models are its section 3, coding agents section 4, and its Case gallery tab lists every documented case. What is specific to the RL step of a language model, measured where this page can:

The real reward model ranks right above wrong 85% of the time, and prefers shorter answers

Every one of the 160 answers as a dot: reward-model score across, length up; filled when the strict verifier passes it. A reward model a policy could not exploit would put every filled dot right of every hollow one. Here it ranks a correct answer above a wrong one in 85% of pairs (AUC), scores 10 of 123 wrong answers above the average correct one, and its score falls with length (r = −0.54: long answers here are mostly truncated or confused ones, so the sign of a length preference depends on the reward model and the data, which is why it has to be measured).

  • Length. Reward models in chat tend to prefer longer answers (Reward Hacking section 3), and GRPO's 1/|o| makes long wrong answers cheap (section 7): two independent pushes toward verbosity. On this page's data the reward model's scores fall with length instead (r = −0.54), which is why the preference must be measured, not assumed.
  • The reward model's blind spots. A learned scorer ranks some wrong answers above right ones and the policy finds those regions: the real reward model here misranks 15% of right-wrong pairs, and on GSM8K problem 3 it scores a wrong answer above a right one (section 4).
  • Lenient verifiers. An extractor that accepts the reference number anywhere rewards a policy that lists many numbers: here such a verifier passes 29 answers the strict checker fails (section 5). Model-based judges are fooled by "master keys" and hacked "particularly after fine-tuning". A judge's accuracy on a fixed set does not rank it as a reward: thinking judges were more accurate than answer-only judges, yet the answer-only judges trained better policies (online DPO) in 7 of 8 matched settings ({{Scaling Laws for Generative Reward Models (arXiv 2610.30765)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30765}}).
  • Tests and format. Special-cased outputs, edited tests and early exits pass a suite without solving the task; a <think> block satisfies a format reward while empty.

Defences that belong to the RL loop: the KL penalty or a refreshed reference; rule rewards for long runs and learned rewards only briefly (R1's last 400 steps); held-out verifiers; logging reward components separately; and reading samples, especially the highest-reward ones. Monitoring the chain of thought helps, but training against the monitor teaches the model to hide it (Reward Hacking, section 6); routing a flagged rollout's update into an adapter deleted after training, instead of penalising its reward, keeps the monitor out of the objective ({{GRAFT|n:4055c17b0d0d8139ba07f0ddc44d3092}}, paper page; {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}, section 7).

11Agentic and multi-turn RL

When the policy calls tools, the MDP of section 1 changes in three ways. The transition is no longer trivial: after a tool call the environment appends tokens the policy did not generate (a search result, a test log, a shell's output). Those tokens must be masked out of the loss, because the policy cannot be credited or blamed for them; Search-R1 introduced "retrieved token masking for stable RL training" ({{Jin et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.09516}}). And episodes span many turns with one reward at the end, so credit assignment is harder than in single-turn reasoning; GiGPO adds step-level advantages computed from states that recur across a group, and reports over 12% better than GRPO on ALFWorld and 9% on WebShop ({{arXiv 2505.10978|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10978}}, authors' measurement).

  • Exact token accounting. If the trainer re-tokenises the engine's text instead of using the engine's token ids, the same text can tokenise differently, the trainer scores tokens the engine never sampled, and the data is silently off-policy (section 8). Mercor's 397B run calls the discipline "token-in-token-out" ({{Mercor, 1 September 2026|https://www.mercor.com/blog/training-frontier-knowledge-work-agents-a-397b-rl-training-guide-with-skyrl/}}).
  • Cost as a term in the objective. Cognition trained SWE-2's whole effort ladder in one RL run with the reward R = S − λeC, where S ∈ {0, 1} is success, C the rollout's cost, and λe "tuned to match the slope of the Pareto curve of the base model at effort level e", instead of one checkpoint per effort tier ({{Cognition, 10 September 2026|https://cognition.com/blog/swe-2}}).
Agentic recipes in detail: SWE-2's numbers, several rewards at once, Mercor's 397B run (closed)
  • SWE-2's saving showed up in turns: on FrontierCode 1.1 Main, SWE-2 medium used 53 steps against SWE-1.7's 127 (58% fewer, 1 − 53/127) at 81% lower average cost ({{Cognition, 10 September 2026|https://cognition.com/blog/swe-2}}). For agents, turn count is where the money goes once per-token price is competitive.
  • Several objectives at once. When a run optimises several rewards, gradient keeps flowing into the ones already mastered. Learn What's Left, Not What's Mastered normalises each objective per group and down-weights it by (1 − saturation)γ, the multi-objective cousin of DAPO's dynamic sampling (no disagreement, no signal); its {{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e38b0fe72f3daf44bc}} trains it live and finds the gains unproven: single runs, and the obvious fixed-weight rival never run.
  • The largest published recipe in this shape: Mercor with SkyRL trained Qwen3.5-397B-A17B on 1,928 expert knowledge-work tasks (consulting, banking, law) and "improved Pass@1 by 70% relative, from 16.11% to 27.29%" on APEX-Agents (27.29 / 16.11 = 1.69). Most of its write-up is about exact token accounting, fully asynchronous RL with in-flight weight updates, timeouts and error classes for robust environments, and the harness: fixing its tool servers "raised the base ... from 22.74% to 28.69% mean reward, with zero training". Its authors' summary: "algorithm choices mattered less than the data". The recipe in full is on {{Topic: llm-training-and-post-training|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}}.

12Infrastructure: rollouts, weights and environments

An RL step for an LLM is four jobs on one or two pools of GPUs: generate rollouts with an inference engine (vLLM, SGLang), score them (a verifier, a reward model, an environment), train on them, and sync the new weights back to the engine. verl's HybridFlow colocates trainer and engine on the same GPUs and switches between them, which makes weight sync fast; disaggregated designs give each its own pool and ship weights over the network. The frameworks (verl, OpenRLHF, TRL, SkyRL, NeMo RL) are listed on {{Alignment|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}; few methods reach them: of 1,598 papers proposing general RL methods from January 2025 to June 2026, 37 reached any of seven open frameworks, and the included papers more often trained 30B+ policies (41% against 10%) ({{From RL Papers to Framework Code (arXiv 2610.28027)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28027}}); checkpoints, failures and goodput on {{Fault tolerance at scale|n:3fc5c17b0d0d815786d1c1dddb311a9b}}.

Synchronous or asynchronous. A synchronous step waits for the longest response in the batch, and reasoning lengths have a long tail, so most of the engine idles at the end of every generation phase. Asynchronous systems let generation run ahead, and pay with staleness, the off-policy problem of section 8. Predict how much of the engine's time the synchronous schedule wastes on this page's real lengths, then step:

The same 160 real responses finish 1.49 times sooner when the engine never waits, at the cost of stale samples

On this model the synchronous schedule takes 5,915 ticks with the engine busy 59% of slot time; the asynchronous one 3,982 ticks, 87% busy, and 121 of the 160 responses are trained on after the weights moved on, by at most 2 versions.

  • AReaL's rollout workers "continuously generate new outputs without waiting" while trainers update "whenever a batch of data is collected", with "a staleness-enhanced PPO variant" for the older samples, reaching "up to 2.77× training speedup" ({{Fu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24298}}); PipelineRL updates weights in flight, pausing generation only briefly so sequences already in progress continue on the new weights, about 2× faster learning on 128 H100s ({{Piché et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.19128}}).

How RL scales. A 400,000-GPU-hour study fits reward against compute with a sigmoid, RC − R0 = (A − R0) / (1 + (Cmid/C)B), and finds that most recipe details (loss aggregation, normalisation, curriculum, the off-policy algorithm) change the efficiency B more than the ceiling A ({{Khatri et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13786}}).

Environments at scale and the ScaleRL recipe (closed)
  • Environments are now the expensive part for agents. DeepSeek's sandbox layer runs about 3 million sandboxes a day, over 5,000 created per second, on about 160 nodes, co-designed so stateful rollouts survive preemptible GPU training; the paper page judges the production scale self-reported ({{DSec page|n:3e95c17b0d0d81bd927be047bb0d7b0b}}, which places a burst of sandboxes live).
  • ScaleRL, the study's recipe, combines PipelineRL, forced length interruptions, CISPO, prompt-level loss averaging, batch-level advantage normalisation, FP32 logits, zero-variance filtering and "No-Positive-Resampling" (prompts whose historical pass rate reaches 0.9 or more leave the pool).

14Depth: mistakes, a checklist, and an unverified claim

Mistakes that cost real runs (closed)

Letting the engine's sampling defaults change the policy. A model's shipped generation_config.json can carry sampling settings the trainer knows nothing about; Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct's sets repetition_penalty 1.1, temperature 0.7, top_p 0.8 and top_k 20 ({{file|https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct/blob/main/generation_config.json}}). Building this page, a first run that overrode temperature, top_p and top_k but not the repetition penalty sampled from a different distribution than πθ: its stored log-probabilities differed from the model's by 0.08 to 0.45 nats per token on average across 32 responses (up to 3.2 on one token), against about 0.0015 for a pure precision gap (section 8's third set-up). Sample at temperature 1 with every processor off, or record and correct for what you change.

Assuming the rollout policy is the old policy. Computing ρ against the trainer's recomputed log-probabilities alone hides the engine's systematic gap; log both and correct with TIS or rejection sampling.

Counting the KL penalty twice. RLHF-PPO puts it in the reward, GRPO in the loss. Doing both, or comparing β across the two placements (0.02 in InstructGPT's reward, 0.04 and 0.001 in DeepSeek's losses), mixes up two different regularisers.

Expecting PPO's behaviour from GRPO. With one gradient step per batch the clip never binds (section 3's closed block); the clip matters only when a batch is reused, as in R1-Zero's 16 minibatches.

Training on environment tokens. In multi-turn RL, tool outputs are part of the state, not the policy's actions; mask them from the loss, and pass token ids end to end.

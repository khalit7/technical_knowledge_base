Verifier gallery: what a checker accepts, and what it misses

RLVR's reward is only as good as its checker. Three answer checkers, from strict to lenient, ran on this page's 160 real answers; where they disagree, one of them is wrong, and a policy trained against it would learn the wrong thing. Below them, failures documented in the literature.

Strict: the boxed answer

Take the last \boxed{...}, strip "$" and commas, compare the last number in it with the reference. No box, no reward. The reward this page uses everywhere else.

Last number

Take the last number anywhere in the answer. Forgives a missing box; rewards a stray trailing number.

Lenient: the number anywhere

Pass if the reference number appears anywhere in the answer. A buggy extractor of this kind rewards listing many numbers: an easy hack.

Documented failures

Equivalent answers rejected

Rule-based verifiers "fail to recognize equivalent answers in different formats, leading to substantial false negatives that increasingly hinder RL performance as the policy model gets stronger" ({{Huang et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22203}}). A false negative is a correct answer punished: the policy learns the checker's format, not maths.

Model verifiers hacked

Model-based verifiers "substantially improve static accuracy" but are "highly susceptible to reward hacking during RL, where they misclassify certain patterns in responses as correct, particularly after fine-tuning" (Huang et al.).

One token fools a judge

"Master keys", a lone ":" or "." or a generic opener such as "Thought process:" or "Let's solve this step by step", "can consistently elicit false positive rewards without any substantive reasoning" from LLM judges and generative reward models; the authors' fix is training the judge on truncated answers as negatives ({{Zhao et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08794}}).

Tests that can be passed without solving

Special-cased outputs, edited tests and exits before the assertions; measured on impossible tasks and in coding agents. The cases, with transcripts, are in {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}} ("Case gallery" tab).

Why R1 used no neural reward

DeepSeek-R1-Zero used rule-based accuracy and format rewards only, "because of reward hacking" by neural reward models in large-scale RL; R1 used preference reward models only for its last 400 steps ({{DeepSeek-R1 page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}).

A random reward still "works"

Not a verifier failure but a warning about reading verifier gains: random rewards lifted Qwen2.5-Math-7B by 21.4 points on MATH-500 ({{Shao et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10947}}). Ablate the reward before crediting it.

In one screen

Post-training a language model with reinforcement learning (RL) treats the model as a policy: let it generate, score what it generated, and shift its probabilities toward the higher-scoring outputs. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can only imitate demonstrations; RL can improve on them, because it needs a way to score an answer, not a way to write one. That makes RL the engine behind instruction-following chat models (RL from human feedback, RLHF) and behind reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 (RL with verifiable rewards, RLVR).

Three methods carry most of the field, and each removes one component from the one before it:

RLHF with PPO (InstructGPT)A learned reward modelA learned value model (critic) and GAE, token by tokenInto the per-token reward4 (policy, reference, reward, value)usually 1
GRPO (DeepSeekMath)Any scorerThe response's score minus its group's mean, divided by the group's spread; the same for every tokenInto the loss (k3 estimator)3, or 2 with a verifiera group, 8 to 64

Read as a lineage, these are two deletions: GRPO deletes RLHF's critic, RLVR deletes its learned reward model. GRPO with verifiable rewards is the default open recipe for reasoning models, and most research since 2025 is about its biases and their fixes: Dr. GRPO, DAPO, GSPO, CISPO and the off-policy corrections below.

1Token generation as a Markov decision process

Autoregressive generation maps onto the MDP vocabulary of the root page (Topic: rl, "The loop, state and return"): an agent in a state picks an action, the environment moves to a new state and may emit a reward, and the agent maximises the total reward it collects.

  • State st: the prompt plus the tokens generated so far. This is the "state = full history" choice, St = Ht, which is Markov by construction because nothing is hidden: the environment is fully observable and deterministic.
  • Action at: the next token; the action space is the vocabulary, of the order of 105 discrete actions (151,936 for the Qwen model used on this page, from its config.json).
  • Transition: deterministic and known; the chosen token is appended to the state. (Agentic RL breaks this: a tool's reply is appended by the environment, section 11.)
  • Reward: usually zero at every token except the last; a single scalar (a reward-model score, or a verifier's pass or fail) lands at the end of the sequence. The reward is sparse and delayed, the horizon is the generation length, and the discount γ is typically 1.
  • Policy: the LLM itself, πθ(at | st), the probability it gives the next token after the prefix.

With γ = 1 and one reward at the end, the return from every token of a response is the same number: the final score. The objective is the expected score of the policy's responses:

J(θ) = 𝔼q ∼ 𝒟, o ∼ πθ(· | q) [ r(q, o) ]
q
a prompt drawn from the training prompt set 𝒟
o
a response (o1, ..., o|o|) sampled token by token from the policy; |o| is its length in tokens
r(q, o)
the scalar score of the whole response; θ are the model's weights

The policy-gradient theorem (derived on Policy gradients and actor-critic) turns this into an update: raise the log-probability of every token of a response in proportion to how much better than expected the response scored.

∇θJ ≈ (1/N) Σn Σt ∇θ log πθ(o(n)t | q(n), o(n)<t) · Â(n)t
N
the number of sampled responses; o(n)<t the tokens of response n before position t
Ât
the estimated advantage of token t: the score minus a baseline that estimates how well responses to this prompt were expected to score. A baseline that does not depend on the action leaves the gradient unbiased and cuts its variance.

How the advantage is estimated is exactly what separates the algorithms on this page: PPO learns a value model to predict it token by token; GRPO and RLOO estimate it from a group of samples for the same prompt.

Consequences: credit assignment is severe (one scalar for hundreds or thousands of tokens); the environment model is trivially known, so model-based planning machinery buys nothing (the root's "common mistakes": the reward model does not make RLHF model-based); and rollouts are expensive, because generating the responses at inference speed dominates wall-clock time (section 12).

2RLHF with PPO, in full (the InstructGPT recipe)

Reinforcement learning from human feedback began with simulated robots and Atari learning from preferences between clips (Christiano et al. 2017); PPO with a KL-penalised learned reward arrived with fine-tuning language models from preferences (Ziegler et al. 2019), moved to summarisation (Stiennon et al. 2020) and became the standard recipe with InstructGPT (verified on the InstructGPT page, which trains the whole three-step pipeline live in its "Run the three steps" tab). Four models take part:

  1. Policy πθ: the model being trained, initialised from the SFT model.
  2. Reference model πref: a frozen copy of the SFT model.
  3. Reward model (RM) rφ: frozen, trained beforehand on human preference pairs; it scores a full response with one scalar. InstructGPT's was 6B parameters, trained on rankings of 4 to 9 answers per prompt with the Bradley-Terry pairwise loss, which is on Alignment (section 2).
  4. Value model Vψ: trained alongside the policy (InstructGPT initialised it from the RM); it predicts the expected final reward from each prefix, which gives per-token advantages through GAE (generalised advantage estimation).

The loop: sample prompts; generate responses from πθ; score each with the RM; subtract a per-token KL penalty so the reward the policy sees already contains it; compute advantages with Vψ and GAE; update the policy with the PPO clipped objective and the value model with a regression loss; repeat.

The shaped reward. The Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty to the reference is folded into the reward, token by token:

rt = 1[t = T] · rφ(q, o) − β log( πθ(ot | q, o<t) / πref(ot | q, o<t) )
T
= |o|, the position of the last token; 1[t = T] is 1 there and 0 elsewhere, so the RM score lands only at the end
β
the KL coefficient (0.02 in InstructGPT); the log-ratio is a one-sample estimate of KL(πθ ‖ πref) at that token

Summed over a response, the penalties give InstructGPT's sequence-level term β log(πRL(y | x) / πSFT(y | x)) (InstructGPT, Eq. 2), which also adds a pretraining-gradient mix ("PPO-ptx") to limit regressions on public benchmarks.

Advantages with GAE over tokens. The value model supplies a baseline at every prefix, and GAE (Schulman et al. 2015) blends one-step and multi-step estimates of how much better a token turned out than the value model expected:

δt = rt + γVψ(st+1) − Vψ(st),    Ât = Σl=0T−t (γλ)l δt+l
δt
the temporal-difference (TD) error at token t; Vψ(st) is the value model's prediction for the prefix st (zero after the last token); γ is 1 here
λ
in [0, 1]: λ = 0 trusts the value model completely (low variance, biased when it is wrong); λ = 1 uses the actual final reward minus the baseline (unbiased, high variance). The value model is regressed onto the returns Ât + Vψ(st).

With γ = λ = 1 the sum telescopes: Ât = (final score − β × the sum of the remaining log-ratios) − V(st). The critic then serves only as a per-prefix baseline. With λ < 1 a token's credit is mostly the jump in predicted value it caused, which is the point of a critic and also its weakness: the value model must be right about every prefix of every response. Section 5 runs this on a real response with the value taken from real continuations.

The clipped update. PPO maximises

LCLIP(θ) = 𝔼t [ min( ρt(θ) Ât, clip(ρt(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Ât ) ],   ρt(θ) = πθ(ot | st) / πθold(ot | st)
ρt
the importance ratio between the policy being updated and the policy that generated the data, πθold
ε
the clip range, typically 0.2 (Schulman et al. 2017)

Once the ratio has moved more than ε in the direction the advantage wants, the clipped term is the smaller one, it does not depend on θ, and the gradient is zero; if the ratio has moved the wrong way, the unclipped term is the smaller one and the gradient still pulls it back. That one-sided flatness is what makes several epochs of minibatch updates on one batch of rollouts safe. The derivation from TRPO, and the clip animated over ten epochs on a small policy, are on Policy gradients and actor-critic.

Why the KL penalty: (a) it keeps the policy on the distribution where the reward model is trustworthy, which is near the data it was trained on (anti-reward-hacking); (b) it preserves general capabilities and fluency; (c) it acts as a trust region tying the policy to a fixed anchor rather than merely to last iteration's policy.

What it achieved, and what it cost (InstructGPT, verified on its page): outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT model were preferred to those of 175B GPT-3; 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 85 ± 3% of the time; the RL stage cost 60 petaflop/s-days against 3,640 for pretraining GPT-3, about 1.6% (60 / 3,640 = 0.0165).

Pain points: four large models in memory; the value model is as big as the policy, must be trained from scratch during RL, and per-token value estimation is hard when the true reward is one scalar at the end. How hard was measured later: on MATH and GSM8K, PPO's value networks "often produce poor estimate of expected return and barely outperform a random baseline when comparing alternative steps"; replacing them with Monte Carlo estimates from extra rollouts at intermediate states (VinePPO) beat PPO in up to 3.0 times less wall-clock time (Kazemnejad et al., ICML 2025). GRPO removes exactly this pain, differently: it gives up per-token credit altogether.

Corrections carried from the InstructGPT page
InstructGPT is "about 1.4 to 1.5 times as truthful" by human labels, not "twice"; it is 16 to 20% less toxic at 175B when asked to be respectful, not "25%", and no less biased on Winogender or CrowS-Pairs (InstructGPT page).

3GRPO and RLOO: a group of samples instead of a critic

Group Relative Policy Optimization is PPO minus the value model (DeepSeekMath, Shao et al. 2024; the DeepSeekMath page animates PPO against GRPO on one question and trains all six of its methods live). For each prompt q, sample a group of G responses {o1, ..., oG} from the current policy and score each, giving rewards r1, ..., rG. Every token of response i receives the same group-relative advantage:

Âi,t = ( ri − mean(r1, ..., rG) ) / std(r1, ..., rG)
mean, std
over the G rewards of this prompt's group only; t indexes the tokens of response i

The empirical group mean is the baseline: a Monte Carlo estimate of the prompt's value V(q), computed from samples instead of predicted by a network. Dividing by the standard deviation rescales each prompt's advantages to unit spread. Implementations differ on whether the standard deviation divides by G or G − 1 (for rewards (1, 0, 0, 0): 0.433 and +1.732 / −0.577 with G; 0.5 and +1.5 / −0.5 with G − 1), and add a small constant so that a group with identical rewards does not divide by zero; the examples on this page divide by G.

Then the familiar clipped objective is applied with per-token importance ratios, plus an explicit KL term to πref added to the loss rather than folded into the reward, so that the penalty does not contaminate the advantages:

𝒥GRPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ (1/G) Σi (1/|oi|) Σt { min( ρi,t Âi,t, clip(ρi,t, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Âi,t ) − β 𝔻KL[πθ ‖ πref] } ]
ρi,t
πθ(oi,t | q, oi,<t) / πθold(oi,t | q, oi,<t), the per-token importance ratio
|oi|
the length of response i in tokens: each response's tokens are averaged first, then the G responses (section 7 shows why this is biased)
ε, β
the clip range and the KL coefficient

DeepSeek estimates the per-token KL with Schulman's low-variance k3 estimator (Approximating KL divergence):

𝔻KL ≈ x − log x − 1,   x = πref(oi,t | ·) / πθ(oi,t | ·)
x
the ratio of the reference's probability to the policy's for the sampled token. k3 is never negative, because x − log x − 1 ≥ 0 for every x > 0 with equality only at x = 1, and it is an unbiased estimate of KL(πθ ‖ πref) when tokens are sampled from πθ, because the expectation of x under πθ is exactly 1.

Corrections carried from the DeepSeekMath page
The paper never trains PPO: GRPO's memory saving is asserted, not measured, and the first head-to-head is in DeepSeek-R1's supplementary material. And differentiating k3 in the loss gives, in expectation, the gradient of KL(πref ‖ πθ), not of KL(πθ ‖ πref): the value is an unbiased estimate of one divergence, its gradient pushes on the other (DeepSeekMath page, "k3 gradient" widget).

Published settings. DeepSeekMath-RL 7B used G = 64 samples per question, β = 0.04, a policy learning rate of 1e-6 and a single policy update per sampling batch, so its clip barely binds; it lifted the 7B instruct model from 46.8% to 51.7% on MATH and 82.9% to 88.2% on GSM8K. DeepSeek-R1-Zero used G = 16, β = 0.001, a learning rate of 3e-6, a reference replaced every 400 steps, and 16 minibatches per rollout trained once each with an unusually large clip ratio of 10 (DeepSeekMath page, DeepSeek-R1 page).

Worked example: one group of four

A maths prompt, G = 4 samples, a binary verifier. One response is correct and three are wrong, so r = (1, 0, 0, 0). Computed in your browser by the page's engine and checked against src/recompute.py:

  1. Baseline: the group mean is (1 + 0 + 0 + 0) / 4 = 0.25.
  2. Spread: the deviations from the mean are 0.75, −0.25, −0.25 and −0.25; their squares average to (0.5625 + 3 × 0.0625) / 4 = 0.1875, so the standard deviation is √0.1875 = 0.433.
  3. Advantages: the correct response gets 0.75 / 0.433 = +1.732 on every one of its tokens; each wrong response gets −0.25 / 0.433 = −0.577. The four advantages sum to zero: the correct answer is pushed up by as much as the three wrong answers together are pushed down.
  4. The clip, on one token of the correct response: suppose that after a few minibatch steps its ratio is ρ = 1.3, with ε = 0.2. The unclipped term is 1.3 × 1.732 = 2.252 and the clipped term 1.2 × 1.732 = 2.078. The min takes 2.078, which does not depend on θ, so this token contributes no more gradient: it has already moved far enough.
  5. The same ratio on a token of a wrong response: unclipped 1.3 × (−0.577) = −0.751, clipped 1.2 × (−0.577) = −0.693. The min takes −0.751, the unclipped term, so the gradient still flows and pushes the probability of that token back down.
  6. The KL term, on a token to which the reference gives half the policy's probability: x = 0.5, so k3 = 0.5 − ln 0.5 − 1 = 0.5 + 0.693 − 1 = 0.193.

If all four responses had been correct, r = (1, 1, 1, 1), every deviation would be zero, every advantage zero, and the group would contribute no policy gradient at all, only the KL term. The same holds when all four are wrong. Such groups cost four rollouts and teach nothing, which is why DAPO's dynamic sampling (section 7) filters them out. On this page's real data, 3 of 10 GSM8K groups of 16 from the 0.5B model are uniform under the strict verifier.

One group, step by step: GRPO, Dr. GRPO and RLOO

Click a response to flip its score. The "real" groups are this page's Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct samples on GSM8K, with their real lengths in tokens and the strict verifier's verdicts; the others are the old page's worked examples with illustrative lengths. Dr. GRPO removes the division by the standard deviation and replaces 1/|oi| by a constant (here the longest length in the group); RLOO subtracts the mean of the other responses.

RLOO, REINFORCE leave-one-out, is the older member of the family: the baseline for response i is the mean reward of the other G − 1 responses (Kool et al. 2019). Because that baseline does not use ri, the estimator is exactly unbiased, and algebra shows it is the mean-centred reward scaled by G/(G − 1): ri − (Σj≠i rj)/(G − 1) = (G/(G − 1)) (ri − mean). For (1, 0, 0, 0) that is +1.000 and −0.333. Ahmadian et al. 2024 ("Back to Basics") argued that such "simpler REINFORCE-style optimization variants outperform both PPO and newly proposed 'RL-free' methods such as DPO and RAFT" for RLHF. GRPO's mean-centring includes ri in its own baseline, which only shrinks the advantage by (G − 1)/G; the bias that matters is the standard-deviation division (section 7).

What is gained and lost:

  • No value model: roughly half the memory, no critic warm-up, no per-token value estimation problem. The DeepSeek-R1 team report that PPO can match GRPO, but only with careful GAE tuning (λ = 1.0; "the default value in most open-source PPO implementations", 0.95, is much worse), and it still costs a full value model (DeepSeek-R1 page).
  • The baseline is the REINFORCE-with-baseline idea (Policy gradients and actor-critic); GRPO is closer to group-baselined REINFORCE with PPO-style clipping than to true PPO. It is policy-based, not actor-critic, because nothing bootstraps from the baseline (the root's common mistakes).
  • Pure Monte Carlo credit assignment: every token in a response is credited equally; no per-token shaping. Fine for verifiable single-turn tasks, noisier for long-horizon agentic ones.
  • It needs G rollouts per prompt (typically 8 to 64), which shifts cost from memory to inference compute.

4RLVR: verifiable rewards

RL with verifiable rewards replaces the learned reward model with a programmatic verifier: exact-match answer checking for maths, unit tests for code, format checks, task-success checks for agents. The reward is typically binary or a small rubric sum. The name and first recipe are Tülu 3's (November 2024, a reward of 10 when the verifier accepts, optimised with PPO and a value model; Lambert et al.); its lineage (AlphaCode's execution filtering, Qwen3, Olmo 3 RL-Zero) is on Alignment section 6. DeepSeek-R1-Zero's reward is the reference shape: an accuracy reward (the final answer matched against the reference for maths; compilation plus hidden test cases for code) plus a format reward for putting the reasoning inside <think> tags, equally weighted, and deliberately no neural reward model at all (DeepSeek-R1 page).

Benefits: no reward-model bias to hack, no preference-data collection, scales with compute. Limits: it works only where a verifier exists; imperfect verifiers reintroduce hacking. Extending RLVR beyond maths and code (rubric-based and generative verifiers) is a main 2026 research front; rubric rewards are on Alignment ("Open problems"). 5Before and after: one real prompt, three updates

The same response, sampled for this page from Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on a GSM8K problem, run through what each method computes for every one of its tokens. Before is RLHF with PPO: a reward model's score at the end, a KL penalty on every token, a critic's value at every prefix, GAE. After is GRPO with a verifier, the recipe that replaced it for reasoning: a pass or fail, the group's mean and spread, one number for every token. What to take from it: PPO's critic lets credit land on the tokens where the predicted outcome actually changed, and pays for that with a second network that has to be right about every prefix (here we cheated by measuring the value with real continuations, which is what VinePPO does). GRPO gives every token of a response the same push, needs no second network and no reward model when a verifier exists, and pays with a group of rollouts per prompt. Dr. GRPO keeps that and stops long responses from being let off lightly.

6Case study: DeepSeek-R1 and R1-Zero

Released in January 2025 (arXiv 2501.12948) and published in Nature in September 2025 in a much longer version (Nature); the DeepSeek-R1 page rebuilds its tables, decodes its figures and trains a tiny R1-Zero live. What matters for this page:

  • R1-Zero: GRPO plus RLVR (accuracy and format rewards) applied directly to the DeepSeek-V3 base model, with no SFT. Long chains of thought, self-verification and backtracking appeared from RL alone; response length grew over training from about 500 to about 14,200 tokens (28 times, 14,200 / 500 = 28.4), with its steepest step where the length cap was doubled from 32,768 to 65,536 tokens at step 8.2k. AIME 2024 pass@1 (the chance that a single sample solves a problem of the American Invitational Mathematics Examination) climbed from 15.6% to 71.0% in the January 2025 preprint (arXiv v1) and to 77.9% in the later version (arXiv v2 and Nature), which reports the curve to the end of training. Weaknesses: readability and language mixing.
  • R1: the production pipeline: cold-start SFT on curated long chains of thought, reasoning-focused RL, rejection sampling into about 800K new SFT samples, and a final RL stage for helpfulness and harmlessness that used preference reward models only in its last 400 steps. It matched o1-class reasoning at open weights and made GRPO with verifiable rewards the default open recipe through 2025 and 2026.
  • Distillation of R1's traces into small models: cheaper than running RL on them, and better. On Qwen2.5-32B, plain SFT on 800K R1-generated samples reached 72.6 on AIME 2024, against 47.0 for the full R1-Zero RL recipe run directly on the same base model for over 10,000 steps.
  • Cost: R1's RL and data generation took 147K H800 GPU-hours (about $294K at $2 per hour, 147,000 × 2) on top of the base model's 2.788M hours: a post-training bill, not "the cost of R1" (the root's Milestones and benchmarks tab keeps the dated figures side by side).
Correction: R1-Zero's AIME figure has two versions
The old page said "climbed from 15.6% to 77.9%" without a version. 71.0% is the January 2025 preprint (v1); 77.9% is v2 and the Nature version; the R1 page's decoding of Figure 1 finds the curve's peak at 77.7% and v1's 71.0% equal to the curve at step 8,400. Do not mix them. And the "aha moment" was not created by RL: the base model already produces reflective words, and RL changed how often and how long (DeepSeek-R1 page).

7The 2025 to 2026 corrections to GRPO

GRPO's baseline has accumulated well-understood biases and a family of fixes. The DeepSeekMath page's Then and now tab animates GRPO's objective term by term through them; here each is derived, with numbers.

Dr. GRPO: two normalisations that bias the update

"GRPO Done Right", from Understanding R1-Zero-Like Training (Liu et al. 2025), finds that both normalisations in GRPO bias what it learns:

  • Length bias from 1/|oi|. Each response's token losses are averaged over its own length, so every token of a long response carries less weight. For a correct response (positive advantage) that gives shorter answers bigger updates; for a wrong one (negative advantage) it penalises long answers less per token, so the policy drifts toward long wrong answers. With the worked example's Â = −0.577, each token of a 100-token wrong response is weighted −0.577 / 100 = −0.00577, and each token of a 1,000-token wrong response only −0.000577: ten times less push-down per token.
  • Difficulty bias from std. Prompts whose rewards barely vary (nearly all correct or nearly all wrong) have a small standard deviation, so dividing by it inflates their advantages and over-weights too-easy and too-hard prompts. In a group of 16 with a single correct answer the mean is 0.0625 and the standard deviation 0.242, so the correct answer gets +3.873; in a group of 16 with 8 correct, the standard deviation is 0.5 and a correct answer gets +1.0.

The fix drops both terms: Âi,t = ri − mean(r1, ..., rG), and the token losses are summed and divided by a constant (the maximum generation length in the authors' implementation) instead of by each response's own length. What is left is an unbiased REINFORCE-style estimator with a group-mean baseline (1 of 16 now gets +0.9375 and 8 of 16 gets +0.5: the gap reflects how surprising the success was, not the group's spread). The paper reports better token efficiency (less length inflation, especially on wrong answers) at equal reasoning performance, and its minimalist recipe reaches 43.3% on AIME 2024 from a 7B base model. The group widget in section 3 switches between the three advantages on real groups.

DAPO: four fixes for long chain-of-thought RL

DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization; ByteDance Seed with Tsinghua University's Institute for AI Industry Research, Yu et al. 2025) is an open, widely adopted recipe that reports 50 points on AIME 2024 from the Qwen2.5-32B base model, above DeepSeek-R1-Zero-Qwen-32B's 47 with 50% of its training steps. Its four changes:

  1. Clip-higher: decouple the clip range into εlow = 0.2 and εhigh = 0.28, clipping the ratio to [1 − εlow, 1 + εhigh]. With a symmetric 0.2, a token the policy gives probability 0.01 can rise only to 0.012 in one update, while a token at 0.9 is effectively unconstrained (0.9 × 1.2 > 1); low-probability exploratory tokens can barely grow, and entropy collapses. With 0.28 the same token can reach 0.0128. (Entropy collapse in general, and its fitted law, is on Alignment "Open problems".)
  2. Dynamic sampling: over-sample and drop groups whose rewards are all identical (all correct or all wrong), since their advantages are zero and they only dilute the batch; keep sampling until the batch is full of groups that carry signal.
  3. Token-level loss: average over all tokens in the batch, (1/Σi|oi|) ΣiΣt, instead of per response and then per group, (1/G) Σi (1/|oi|) Σt, so every token has equal weight and long responses are not under-weighted.
  4. Overlong reward shaping: instead of a flat penalty for truncated responses, a soft length penalty added to the reward:
Rlength(y) = 0 if |y| ≤ Lmax − Lcache;   ((Lmax − Lcache) − |y|) / Lcache if Lmax − Lcache < |y| ≤ Lmax;   −1 if |y| > Lmax
|y|
the response length in tokens; Lmax the maximum length (16,384 in the paper) and Lcache the width of the soft zone before it (4,096). A 14,336-token response sits halfway into the zone: (12,288 − 14,336) / 4,096 = −0.5.

GSPO: the ratio at the sequence level

GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization; Qwen Team, Alibaba, Zheng et al. 2025) argues that token-level importance ratios are noisy and misaligned with a reward given to the whole sequence, which destabilises long sequences and mixture-of-experts (MoE) training. It defines one length-normalised ratio per response:

si(θ) = ( πθ(oi | q) / πθold(oi | q) )1/|oi| = exp( (1/|oi|) Σt log ρi,t )
𝒥GSPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ (1/G) Σi min( si(θ) Âi, clip(si(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Âi ) ]
si
the geometric mean of the response's per-token ratios; Âi its group-relative advantage

A 3-token response with per-token ratios 1.1, 0.9 and 1.0 has si = exp((ln 1.1 + ln 0.9 + ln 1.0)/3) = exp(−0.00335) = 0.99666: one number close to 1 in place of three noisy ones. Because the sequence ratio sits so close to 1, the clip ranges are tiny: the paper uses 3e-4 below and 4e-4 above (against 0.2 and 0.27 for its GRPO runs), and reports "a difference of two orders of magnitude in the fractions of clipped tokens between GSPO and GRPO", GSPO clipping more and still training more efficiently. For MoE models it removes the need for "Routing Replay" (caching which experts were active during rollout so token ratios stay comparable). The paper credits GSPO with contributing to the RL of the Qwen3 models; a GSPO-token variant restores per-token advantages for multi-turn settings. The Rollout mismatch tab computes the sequence ratio on all 160 real responses.

CISPO: clip the weight, never the gradient

MiniMax-M1's CISPO (MiniMax 2025) starts from what PPO's clip does to rare tokens. Reflective tokens such as "However", "Recheck", "Wait" and "Aha" have low probability under the base model, so their ratios grow fast, they are clipped after the first update, and they stop contributing gradient. CISPO keeps every token's gradient and clips only the importance weight, with a stop-gradient:

𝒥CISPO(θ) = 𝔼 [ (1/Σi|oi|) Σi Σt sg( r̂i,t(θ) ) Âi,t log πθ(oi,t | q, oi,<t) ],   r̂ = clip(ρ, 1 − εISlow, 1 + εIShigh)
sg
stop-gradient: the clipped ratio only scales the REINFORCE term, it is never differentiated; "we did not impose a lower bound on the IS weight by setting εISlow to a large value; instead, we only tuned εIShigh"

On Qwen2.5-32B and AIME 2024, "CISPO achieves a 2x speedup compared to DAPO", matching DAPO's results in 50% of the training steps. CISPO is one of the components of ScaleRL, the recipe from a 400,000-GPU-hour study of how RL scales (Khatri et al. 2025, section 12).

8Off-policy corrections: the rollout engine is not the trainer

There are two sources of off-policyness in real systems: (a) minibatch updates make later steps off-policy within a batch (the clip and the ratio handle this); (b) training/inference mismatch: the vLLM or SGLang rollout engine computes slightly different probabilities than the training stack (kernels, precision, KV cache, batch shapes), so the data is subtly off-policy by design: an implementation gap quietly turns on-policy RL into off-policy RL (Yao et al. 2025, "Your Efficient RL Framework Secretly Brings You Off-Policy RL Training", which introduced the fix below). The fix that spread is truncated importance sampling (TIS) against the probabilities the engine actually sampled with:

wt = min( πtrain(ot | st) / πrollout(ot | st), C )
πtrain
the probability the training stack computes for the sampled token under the old policy
πrollout
the probability the inference engine actually sampled it with
C
the truncation threshold; verl's documentation suggests "typical threshold 1.5-5.0" for token-level weights and starts from decoupled_token_is(threshold=2.0) (verl, Rollout Correction)

A token the engine sampled at 0.20 that the trainer scores at 0.25 gets weight 1.25; one sampled at 0.05 and scored at 0.25 has ratio 5, truncated to 2. Unlike PPO's two-sided clip, the cap is one-sided: weights below 1 pass through. The sequence-level variant multiplies the per-token ratios over the response (∏ρt), so its thresholds are higher ("typical threshold 2.0-10.0" in verl); rejection sampling (masked importance sampling, MIS) instead drops tokens or sequences whose ratio falls outside set bounds; CISPO's clipped weight (section 7) is the same idea applied to the policy ratio. verl distinguishes a "decoupled" mode with three policies (rollout, old, current) from a "bypass" mode that sets πold = πrollout to skip a forward pass.

How large is the mismatch? On this page's 160 real responses, the same weights in float16 with a KV cache (the "engine") against float32 in one pass (the "trainer") differ by a mean of 0.0015 nats per token; the Rollout mismatch tab has the full distribution, a bfloat16 comparison, TIS and MIS at any threshold, and GSPO's sequence ratio. In production the gap matters: ScaleRL found that computing the language-model head in FP32 for both generator and trainer "dramatically improves the asymptotic performance A from 0.52 to 0.61" (Khatri et al. 2025), and the Qwen team's study of a 30B MoE model over "hundreds of thousands of GPU hours" argues that a token-level objective is a valid surrogate for the sequence reward "only when both the training-inference discrepancy and policy staleness are minimized" (Zheng et al. 2025).

Theory caught up: Group-Relative REINFORCE Is Secretly an Off-Policy Algorithm (Yao et al., ICLR 2026) derives group-relative REINFORCE without assuming where the data came from, reframes GRPO and its friends as regularised REINFORCE, and clarifies "the roles of importance sampling and clipping in GRPO", which makes these corrections principled rather than patches. For a from-scratch loop: log the engine's log-probabilities and compare them with the trainer's; the gap is your mismatch diagnostic.

9Does RLVR teach, or elicit?

RLVR reliably raises pass@1. The question is whether it adds abilities the base model lacked or makes the base model's best answers its usual ones (sharpening). The evidence, in the order it arrived:

  • DeepSeekMath already saw the shape in 2024: RL raised majority-vote accuracy (Maj@K) but not Pass@K; the page's decoding of its Figure 7 finds Pass@64 slightly lower after RL (DeepSeekMath page).
  • Yue et al. (NeurIPS 2025 oral): RLVR-trained models beat their base at small k, but "the base models achieve a higher pass@k score when k is large"; RLVR operates "within the base model's capabilities rather than expanding beyond them", six popular RLVR algorithms "perform similarly and remain far from optimal", and distillation introduces genuinely new reasoning patterns (Yue et al. 2025).
  • The invisible leash: RLVR as "a support-constrained optimization mechanism"; token-level entropy can rise while answer-level entropy falls (Wu et al. 2025).
  • Spurious rewards: random rewards lifted Qwen2.5-Math-7B on MATH-500 by 21.4 points against 29.1 for true rewards, traced to "clipping bias" in GRPO amplifying a pretraining behaviour (code-style reasoning, 65% to over 90% of answers); the effect "often fail[s] to produce gains for other model families, such as Llama3 or OLMo2". Lesson: ablate on more than one base family (Shao et al. 2025).
  • Against: judging the reasoning chain and not just the answer (CoT-Pass@k), RLVR "can encourage sound reasoning processes even when rewards depend solely on answer correctness" (Wen et al. 2025); prolonged RL with KL control and reference resets (ProRL) "consistently outperform[s] base models across a wide range of pass@k evaluations", including problems the base cannot solve at any sampling budget (Liu et al. 2025).

The reading the old page gave, and which the sources above still support: RLVR reliably converts pass@k into pass@1 and improves reasoning validity; whether it creates capability outside the base model's support remains contested, and prolonged multi-stage RL is the strongest claim for genuine expansion. Measure pass@k at large k before claiming a new capability.

Why the curves cross: sharpening against expansion

illustrative 40 problems with per-sample success probabilities spread from 0 to 0.9 for the base model; "sharpening" raises every probability above 0.15 toward 1 and pushes the rest toward 0; "expansion" also gives a quarter of the unsolved problems a small chance. pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)k per problem, averaged, computed exactly; the shape, not the numbers, is the point.

10Reward hacking, specifically in LLM RL

Every RL method optimises the reward it is given, not the one intended. The general picture, the over-optimisation curves of Gao et al. 2022 (proxy reward keeps rising while the true reward rises, peaks and falls as the policy moves away from its start), a case gallery and the defences are on Reward Hacking; the KL leash is animated on the training topic's Axis 4. What is specific to the RL step of a language model:

  • Length. Reward models tend to prefer longer answers (Reward Hacking, "Verbosity: length is the easiest feature to buy"), and GRPO's 1/|o| makes long wrong answers cheap (section 7): two independent pushes toward verbosity. On this page's data the real reward model's scores correlate with length.
  • The reward model's blind spots. A learned scorer ranks wrong answers above right ones some of the time; the policy finds those regions. On this page's data the real reward model separates correct from wrong answers imperfectly, which is exactly the gap a policy optimising against it would exploit (Real rollouts tab).
  • Lenient verifiers. An answer extractor that accepts the reference number anywhere in the response rewards a policy that lists many numbers. On this page's data such a verifier accepts many wrong answers (Verifier gallery).
  • Model-based verifiers and judges are fooled by surface patterns ("master keys" such as ":" or "Thought process:"; Zhao et al. 2025) and get hacked during RL "particularly after fine-tuning" (Huang et al. 2025).
  • Tests. In code RL, special-cased outputs, edited tests and early exits pass a test suite without solving the task; measured cases are in the Reward Hacking page's case gallery.
  • Format rewards are satisfied in form: a <think> block can be empty or decorative.

Defences that belong to the RL loop: the KL penalty or a refreshed reference; rule rewards for long runs and learned rewards only briefly (R1's 400 final steps); held-out verifiers; logging reward components separately; and reading samples, especially the highest-reward ones. Monitoring the chain of thought helps, but training against the monitor teaches the model to hide (Reward Hacking, "Read the chain of thought, but do not train against it").

11Agentic and multi-turn RL

When the policy calls tools, the MDP of section 1 changes in three ways. The transition is no longer trivial: after a tool call the environment appends tokens the policy did not generate (a search result, a test log, a shell's output). Those tokens must be masked out of the loss, because the policy cannot be credited or blamed for them; Search-R1 introduced "retrieved token masking for stable RL training" (Jin et al. 2025). And episodes span many turns with one reward at the end of the task, so credit assignment is harder than in single-turn reasoning; step-level advantages from states that recur across a group (GiGPO) are on Alignment ("Credit over many turns").

  • Exact token accounting. If the trainer re-tokenises the engine's text instead of using the engine's own token ids, the log-probabilities no longer line up and the data is silently off-policy (section 8). Mercor's 397B run calls the discipline "token-in-token-out" (Mercor, 1 September 2026).
  • Cost as a term in the objective. Selectable reasoning-effort tiers have normally been separate checkpoints, or a budget bolted onto a finished model. Cognition trained SWE-2's whole effort ladder in one RL run with the reward R = S − λeC, where S ∈ {0, 1} is success, C the rollout's cost, and λe "tuned to match the slope of the Pareto curve of the base model at effort level e". The saving showed up in turns: on FrontierCode 1.1 Main, SWE-2 medium used 53 steps against SWE-1.7's 127 (58% fewer, 1 − 53/127) at 81% lower average cost (Cognition, 10 September 2026). For agents, turn count is where the money goes once per-token price is competitive.
  • Several objectives at once. When a run optimises several rewards, gradient keeps flowing into the ones already mastered. Learn What's Left, Not What's Mastered normalises each objective per group and down-weights it by (1 − saturation)γ; it is the multi-objective cousin of DAPO's dynamic sampling, for the same reason (no disagreement, no signal). Its paper page trains it live and finds the gains unproven: single runs, and the obvious fixed-weight rival never run.
  • The largest published recipe in this shape: Mercor with SkyRL trained Qwen3.5-397B-A17B on 1,928 expert knowledge-work tasks (consulting, banking, law) and "improved Pass@1 by 70% relative, from 16.11% to 27.29%" on APEX-Agents (27.29 / 16.11 = 1.69). Most of its write-up is about exact token accounting, fully asynchronous RL with in-flight weight updates, timeouts and error classes for robust environments, and the harness: fixing its tool servers "raised the base ... from 22.74% to 28.69% mean reward, with zero training". Its authors' summary: "algorithm choices mattered less than the data". The recipe in full is on Topic: llm-training-and-post-training.

12Infrastructure: rollouts, weights and environments

An RL step for an LLM is four jobs on one or two pools of GPUs: generate rollouts with an inference engine (vLLM, SGLang), score them (a verifier, a reward model, an environment), train on them, and sync the new weights back to the engine. The frameworks (verl, OpenRLHF, TRL, SkyRL, NeMo RL) are listed on Alignment section 6; checkpoints, failures and goodput are on Training Infrastructure.

  • Colocated or disaggregated. verl's HybridFlow colocates trainer and engine on the same GPUs and switches between them, which makes weight sync fast; disaggregated designs give each its own pool and ship weights over the network.
  • Synchronous or asynchronous. A synchronous step waits for the longest response in the batch, and reasoning lengths have a long tail, so most GPUs idle at the end of every generation phase. Asynchronous systems let generation run ahead: AReaL's rollout workers "continuously generate new outputs without waiting" while trainers update "whenever a batch of data is collected", with "a staleness-enhanced PPO variant" for the older samples, reaching "up to 2.77× training speedup" (Fu et al. 2025); PipelineRL updates weights in flight, pausing generation only briefly so sequences already in progress continue on the new weights, about 2× faster learning on 128 H100s (Piché et al. 2025). The price is staleness, which is the off-policy problem of section 8.
  • Environments are now the expensive part for agents (the training topic's Axis 3 has the numbers). DeepSeek's sandbox layer runs about 3 million sandboxes a day, over 5,000 created per second, on about 160 nodes, co-designed so stateful rollouts survive preemptible GPU training; the paper page judges the production scale self-reported (DSec page, which lets you place a burst of sandboxes live).
  • How RL scales. A 400,000-GPU-hour study fits reward against compute with a sigmoid, RC − R0 = (A − R0) / (1 + (Cmid/C)B), and finds that most recipe details (loss aggregation, normalisation, curriculum, the off-policy algorithm) change the efficiency B more than the ceiling A. Its recipe, ScaleRL, combines PipelineRL, forced length interruptions, CISPO, prompt-level loss averaging, batch-level advantage normalisation, FP32 logits, zero-variance filtering and "No-Positive-Resampling" (prompts whose historical pass rate reaches 0.9 or more leave the pool) (Khatri et al. 2025).

14Common mistakes and misconceptions

Assuming the rollout policy is the old policy.
The inference engine's probabilities differ from the trainer's; computing ρ against the trainer's recomputed log-probabilities alone hides a systematic off-policy gap. Log both and correct with TIS or rejection sampling (section 8).
Letting the engine's sampling defaults change the policy.
A model's shipped generation_config.json can carry sampling settings the trainer knows nothing about; Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct's sets repetition_penalty 1.1, temperature 0.7, top_p 0.8 and top_k 20 (file). Building this page, a first run that overrode temperature, top_p and top_k but not the repetition penalty sampled from a different distribution than πθ: its stored log-probabilities differed from the model's by 0.08 to 0.45 nats per token on average across 32 responses (up to 3.2 on one token), against about 0.0015 for a pure precision gap (the Rollout mismatch tab's third set-up). Sample at temperature 1 with every processor off, or record and correct for what you change.
Forgetting that uniform groups teach nothing.
A group where every answer is right, or every answer is wrong, has zero advantage everywhere. On a prompt set that is too easy or too hard most of the batch is wasted; filter or resample, and track the fraction of zero-signal groups.
Dividing by a zero standard deviation.
The same uniform groups give 0 / 0 in the advantage; without a small constant in the denominator the loss becomes NaN.
Letting length normalisation happen by default.
Averaging each response over its own length quietly rewards long wrong answers. Choose token-mean, sequence-mean or a constant denominator deliberately, and watch response length on wrong answers.
Calling GRPO "PPO without a critic" and expecting PPO's behaviour.
With one gradient step per batch the ratio stays at 1 (up to the rollout mismatch) and the clip never binds, so the update is plain REINFORCE with a group baseline; the clip only matters when a batch is reused for several steps (R1-Zero's 16 minibatches).
Counting the KL penalty twice.
RLHF-PPO puts it in the reward, GRPO in the loss. Doing both, or comparing β values across the two placements (0.02 in InstructGPT's reward, 0.04 and 0.001 in DeepSeek's losses), mixes up two different regularisers. And k3's gradient in the loss is not the gradient of the KL it estimates (section 3).
Re-tokenising text between the engine and the trainer.
The same text can tokenise differently; the trainer then scores tokens the engine never sampled. Pass token ids end to end ("token-in-token-out").
Training on environment tokens.
In multi-turn RL, tool outputs are part of the state, not the policy's actions; mask them from the loss (section 11).
Reading a pass@1 gain as new capability.
RLVR reliably turns pass@k into pass@1; whether it expands what the base model can solve at all is open (section 9). Measure pass@k at large k, on more than one base family, before claiming it.
Trusting the verifier.
An answer extractor that accepts malformed output, or a test suite with gaps, is a reward to hack; the policy will find it. Read the highest-reward samples.

Documented failures

Equivalent answers rejected

Rule-based verifiers "fail to recognize equivalent answers in different formats, leading to substantial false negatives that increasingly hinder RL performance as the policy model gets stronger" (Huang et al. 2025). A false negative is a correct answer punished: the policy learns the checker's format, not maths.

Model verifiers hacked

Model-based verifiers "substantially improve static accuracy" but are "highly susceptible to reward hacking during RL, where they misclassify certain patterns in responses as correct, particularly after fine-tuning" (Huang et al.).

One token fools a judge

"Master keys", a lone ":" or "." or a generic opener such as "Thought process:" or "Let's solve this step by step", "can consistently elicit false positive rewards without any substantive reasoning" from LLM judges and generative reward models; the authors' fix is training the judge on truncated answers as negatives (Zhao et al. 2025).

Tests that can be passed without solving

Special-cased outputs, edited tests and exits before the assertions; measured on impossible tasks and in coding agents. The cases, with transcripts, are in Reward Hacking ("Case gallery" tab).

Why R1 used no neural reward

DeepSeek-R1-Zero used rule-based accuracy and format rewards only, "because of reward hacking" by neural reward models in large-scale RL; R1 used preference reward models only for its last 400 steps (DeepSeek-R1 page).

A random reward still "works"

Not a verifier failure but a warning about reading verifier gains: random rewards lifted Qwen2.5-Math-7B by 21.4 points on MATH-500 (Shao et al. 2025). Ablate the reward before crediting it.

2The dial between them: n-step returns, TD(λ) and eligibility traces

Between one step and the whole episode lie the n-step returns: take n real rewards, then bootstrap ({{Sutton and Barto, §7.1|https://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=164}}).     Error reduction, and the three kinds of trace (depth)

    Error-reduction property: the worst error of the expected n-step return is at most γn times the worst error of V ({{§7.1, equation 7.3|https://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=166}}), so each extra real reward shrinks the bias the estimate can pass on. n-step TD updates V(St) only n steps later.

    Traces. The accumulating trace adds 1 on every visit. The replacing trace resets it to 1, so a state revisited in a loop never builds a trace above 1 ({{Singh and Sutton 1996|https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00114726}}). The dutch trace makes the online backward view exactly equal to the online λ-return with linear approximation: true online TD(λ) ({{van Seijen and Sutton 2014|https://proceedings.mlr.press/v32/seijen14.html}}; {{§12.5|https://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=321}}). Sutton and Barto now see replacing traces as "crude approximations to dutch traces, which largely supersede them" ({{§12.6|https://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=323}}).

    3Control from samples: learn Q, and keep exploring

    Control runs DP's loop, evaluate then improve (generalised policy iteration, {{§5.3|https://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=119}}; taught on {{Dynamic programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81baada7e1c05ce69d01}}), and sampling breaks both halves. Improvement greedy on V needs the model, π′(s) = argmaxa [R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) V(s′)], so model-free control learns Q(s, a), where greedy is a plain argmax. And evaluation only covers what the agent visits, so a greedy policy can lock onto a wrong action it never retries: the policy must keep exploring.

    ε-greedy: with probability 1 − ε the greedy action, otherwise one of the m actions uniformly, so the greedy action has probability 1 − ε + ε/m and every other ε/m. The ε-greedy policy with respect to Qπ is at least as good as any ε-soft π (every action at least ε/m), because its value puts the 1 − ε mass on the largest Q ({{§5.4, equation 5.2|https://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=123}}). The catch: improvement then finds only the best ε-soft policy. For the optimum, exploration must fade: a schedule is GLIE (greedy in the limit with infinite exploration) when every pair is visited infinitely often and the policy converges to greedy; εk = 1/k on the k-th episode is GLIE ({{Silver, Lecture 5|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-5-model-free-control-.pdf}}).

    Monte Carlo control: exploring starts, GLIE MC control, blackjack (depth)

    Monte Carlo ES starts every episode from a random state-action pair, every pair possible, so all pairs are visited however greedy the policy is; after each episode it averages the returns that followed each first visit into Q and makes the policy greedy at the visited states ({{§5.3, box|https://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=121}}). On blackjack it finds a policy matching Thorp's (1966) basic strategy except one notch in the usable-ace policy, which the authors are "confident" is optimal for their version (Example 5.3, Figure 5.2). It cannot settle on a suboptimal policy, but "convergence to this optimal fixed point ... has not yet been formally proved", "one of the most fundamental open theoretical questions in reinforcement learning" (partial solution: {{Tsitsiklis 2002|https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v3/tsitsiklis02a.html}}). Exploring starts are easy in a simulator and rarely possible with real interaction.

    GLIE Monte Carlo control: sample the k-th episode with the ε-greedy policy; for each visited pair increment N(St, At) and move Q(St, At) by (1/N)(Gt − Q); set ε ← 1/k. It converges to Q* (Silver, Lecture 5).

Offline RL and imitation in one screen

Everything else in reinforcement learning assumes the agent can act and see what happens. Here it cannot: a fixed dataset of someone else's behaviour is all there is. Two families learn from it. Imitation learning copies the behaviour and needs no reward; its central failure is compounding error, because the copy drifts into states the expert never showed it. Offline RL uses the rewards logged in the data to do better than the behaviour; its central failure is extrapolation error, because maximising a learned value function picks exactly the actions the data never tried. Each method below is a different answer to one of those two failures.

1The setting: a fixed dataset, no interaction

An offline (or batch) learner receives a dataset D = {(si, ai, ri, s′i)} collected by some behaviour policy πβ (one policy, many people, a mixture of old controllers, often unknown) and must output a policy without collecting anything more (Levine, Kumar, Tucker and Fu 2020; the older name "batch RL" and fitted Q iteration go back to Ernst, Geurts and Wehenkel 2005). Why bother: in medicine, driving, robotics on expensive hardware and recommendation, trying random actions is dangerous, slow or costly, while logs of past decisions are plentiful. And deep learning's successes come from large fixed datasets; offline RL is the attempt to give decision-making the same recipe.

Three distinctions are easy to blur. Off-policy means learning about one policy from data produced by another; Q-learning and DQN are off-policy but still online, because they keep collecting data with their current policy (Model-free prediction and control, Value-based deep RL). Offline removes the collection entirely: the policy that would fix its own mistakes by trying them never gets to. And imitation differs from offline RL in what the data must contain: imitation needs good behaviour and no reward; offline RL needs rewards and can use poor behaviour, in principle combining pieces of mediocre trajectories into a better one ("stitching", which D4RL's maze datasets were built to test; Fu et al. 2020).

The two failures, in one line each

Imitation: the learner is trained on the expert's states but tested on its own, and a small error moves it to states where it has never been taught, so errors feed on themselves (sections 2 and 3).

Offline RL: the learner's value estimates are only checked by data where the data has actions; maximising over actions finds the places where an unchecked estimate happens to be high (section 7).

2Behaviour cloning, and why its errors compound

Behaviour cloning (BC) is supervised learning from states to the expert's actions: maximise Σi log π(ai | si) over expert pairs, or minimise squared error for continuous actions. It is the oldest method here: ALVINN steered a vehicle from camera images with a small network trained on a person's driving (Pomerleau 1988). It is also the most used: supervised fine-tuning of a language model on demonstrations is behaviour cloning on tokens (Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR), and large robot policies are mostly BC with expressive models: RT-1 was trained on about 130,000 demonstration episodes collected by 13 robots over 17 months and performs over 700 training instructions at 97% success (Brohan et al. 2022); Diffusion Policy reports an average improvement of 46.9% over prior methods across 12 manipulation tasks by modelling multimodal action distributions (Chi et al. 2023).

The problem is that supervised learning assumes test inputs come from the training distribution, and here they do not. The training states come from the expert's state distribution dπ*; at test time the learner visits dπ, the states its own actions lead to. One small error takes it somewhere the expert never went, where it has no data and errs more, which takes it further away. NVIDIA's end-to-end driving paper says it plainly: "Training with data from only the human driver is not sufficient. The network must learn how to recover from mistakes. Otherwise the car will slowly drift off the road" (Bojarski et al. 2016, §5). Their fix was data augmentation: images from left and right cameras, and synthetic shifts and rotations, labelled with the steering that would return to the lane.

That is Ross and Bagnell's bound for behaviour cloning, as restated in Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011, Theorem 2.1 (first proved in Ross and Bagnell 2010), and it is tight: they give a problem where J(π̂) = (1 − εT) J(π*) + T²ε. The intuition is a sum: a first mistake at step t happens with probability about ε and can cost up to T − t afterwards, so the total is about Σt ε (T − t) ≈ εT²/2. A learner whose errors did not change its inputs (ordinary classification) would pay only Tε.

The same failure in language models is called exposure bias: a model trained to predict the next token given the true previous tokens must, at generation time, condition on its own outputs, "as errors may accumulate along the way" (Ranzato et al. 2015). On-policy distillation is the DAgger answer: the student generates, the teacher labels the student's own sequences (Agarwal et al. 2023, GKD). RL fine-tuning after SFT is another, since the model is then trained on its own samples (RL for LLMs).

3DAgger: label the states the learner actually reaches

If the trouble is that training states come from the expert and test states from the learner, train on the learner's states. DAgger (Dataset Aggregation; Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011) does this iteratively:

D ← expert demonstrations; π̂₁ ← train on D for i = 1 … N: π_i ← β_i π* + (1 − β_i) π̂_i (β₁ = 1, then usually 0) run π_i, record the states it visits ask the expert for its action at every one of those states D ← D ∪ {(s, π*(s))}; π̂_{i+1} ← train on all of D return the best π̂ on validation

The guarantee changes from quadratic to linear. With enough rounds (N of order T up to log factors), some policy in the sequence has error εN on its own state distribution, the best achievable by the policy class on the aggregated data, and therefore J(π̂) ≤ J(π*) + uTεN + O(1), where u bounds how much worse the cost-to-go gets after one non-expert action (the cost of recovering; Theorems 3.1 and 3.2). The analysis reduces imitation to no-regret online learning: each round is a new loss function (the new states), and training on all data so far is Follow-The-Leader. In Super Tux Kart, behaviour cloning did not improve with more expert laps, "because most of the training laps are all very similar and do not help the learner to learn how to recover from mistakes", while DAgger's policy "never falls off the track after 15 iterations of training" (§5.1).

Its price is the expert. DAgger needs an expert who can be asked "what would you do here?" in states the expert would never have reached, and needs the environment to run the learner. That is cheap for a planner, a search procedure or a stronger model (teacher-student distillation), and awkward for people: labelling while not in control is unnatural, so variants let the human take over only when needed and learn from the takeovers (HG-DAgger, Kelly et al. 2018). When neither the expert nor the environment is available again, the remedies are the offline ones: augment the data with perturbed states labelled by recovery actions (NVIDIA's shifted cameras), collect demonstrations with noise injected into the expert's actions so the data shows recoveries (DART, Laskey et al. 2017), or move to methods that reason about the whole trajectory (sections 4 and 5).

4Inverse RL: recover the reward, then plan

Behaviour cloning copies actions one step at a time. Inverse reinforcement learning asks a different question: what reward is the expert optimising? Given that reward, planning or RL produces a policy that judges whole trajectories, so a learner that has drifted still knows where it should be heading. The reward is also often the more transferable thing: it describes the task, while the policy describes one way of doing it in one environment.

The problem is ill-posed. Ng and Russell (2000) set it up for a known MDP and observed optimal behaviour and noted the degenerate answer first: R = 0, under which every policy is optimal, explains any behaviour. Their fix was a heuristic: among the rewards that make the expert optimal, pick the one that makes the expert's action beat the next-best action by the largest margin, with a penalty favouring simple rewards. Every IRL method since is a choice of which reward to prefer.

  • Feature matching (apprenticeship learning). Assume the reward is linear in known features, R(s) = w·φ(s) with ‖w‖ ≤ 1. A policy's value is then w·μ(π), where μ(π) = E[Σt γt φ(st)] are its feature expectations. If a policy's feature expectations are within ε of the expert's, its value is within ε of the expert's for every such reward, so the true reward need never be identified. The algorithm alternates a max-margin step (find the w under which the expert most beats the policies so far) with an RL step (find the best policy for that w) (Abbeel and Ng 2004). Maximum margin planning (Ratliff, Bagnell and Zinkevich 2006) casts the same idea as structured prediction: the expert's path must beat every other path by a margin that grows with how different that path is.
  • Maximum entropy IRL. Matching feature counts still leaves many policies, and many distributions over paths. Ziebart et al. (2008) resolve the ambiguity with the principle of maximum entropy: the distribution over paths that matches the expert's feature counts and is otherwise as uncommitted as possible is P(ζ) ∝ exp(θ·fζ), so better paths are exponentially more likely but every path has some probability. The likelihood gradient is the expert's feature counts minus the model's expected feature counts, which needs a soft planning pass (a soft-max version of value iteration) per gradient step. They learned the route preferences of 25 Yellow Cab drivers from over 100,000 miles of GPS traces. The causal-entropy version handles stochastic dynamics and is the form later deep methods build on.
  • Deep IRL. With neural rewards and unknown dynamics, the planning pass is replaced by sampling with a policy that is itself being optimised: guided cost learning (Finn, Levine and Abbeel 2016), which leads directly to the adversarial methods of the next section.

The cost is an RL problem inside every update, and access to the environment (or a model of it) to solve it. That is why inverse RL is mostly used where the dynamics are known or cheap to simulate (route planning, robot footstep planning, driver modelling), and why it matters here mainly through its descendants: GAIL and learning from preferences.

5GAIL and adversarial imitation

Ho and Ermon (2016) showed that running RL on the reward found by maximum-causal-entropy IRL is the same as finding the policy whose occupancy measure ρπ(s, a) = π(a|s) Σt γt P(st = s | π), the discounted distribution of state-action pairs it produces, is closest to the expert's, up to an entropy term (their Proposition 3.2; the occupancy measure is the same object as in the linear-programming view of MDPs on Dynamic programming). The reward never needs to be written down: matching distributions is a two-sample problem, which a GAN-style discriminator solves. GAIL finds a saddle point of

With the Jensen-Shannon form of the regulariser, the objective is the JS divergence between the two occupancy measures minus λH(π) (their Equation 15). Because it matches distributions over whole trajectories rather than single actions, GAIL does not compound errors the way behaviour cloning does. In the paper it was "generally quite sample efficient in terms of expert data": on Humanoid, behaviour cloning never exceeded 60% of expert performance while GAIL matched the expert at every dataset size tested; on Reacher, behaviour cloning was the more data-efficient. But GAIL is not offline: its environment interaction "was comparable to the number needed for TRPO to train the expert policies from reinforcement signals", against behaviour cloning's zero (§6 and §7) (the Method atlas on Topic: rl lists GAIL's data as "On-policy (TRPO steps) plus fixed demonstrations").

Successors fix one weakness each. AIRL structures the discriminator so that it recovers a reward that still works when the dynamics change (Fu, Luo and Levine 2017). ValueDICE rewrites the distribution-matching objective so that it can be optimised off-policy, from replay data (Kostrikov, Nachum and Tompson 2019). IQ-Learn learns a single soft Q-function from which both the reward and the policy follow, avoiding the adversarial game (Garg et al. 2021).

6Learning from preferences: the bridge to RLHF

Inverse RL infers a reward from what an expert does; preference learning infers it from what a person prefers between two behaviours. Comparisons are easier to give than demonstrations, and possible for tasks the rater cannot perform. Christiano et al. (2017) showed two short video clips (segments σ¹ and σ²) to a non-expert, asked which was better, and fitted a reward model r̂ with the Bradley-Terry model: the probability that σ¹ is preferred is exp Σ r̂ over σ¹ divided by the sum of that and exp Σ r̂ over σ², trained by cross-entropy on the human's choices. The policy is trained by RL on r̂ while more comparisons are collected on its newest behaviour. It solved Atari games and MuJoCo locomotion "while providing feedback on less than one percent of our agent's interactions with the environment", and taught the Hopper to backflip from 900 queries in less than an hour, a behaviour no one had a reward function for.

That recipe, with a language model as the policy, is RLHF: a reward model fitted to human rankings of answers, then PPO against it with a KL penalty to the supervised model (InstructGPT). DPO then removed the RL loop by training the policy directly on the preference pairs, which makes it an offline preference method in exactly this page's sense (DPO). This page stops at the bridge. The reward model's loss and its failure modes are on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR (section 2, Reward models), PPO, GRPO and verifiable rewards on RL for LLMs, and what goes wrong when a policy optimises a learned reward too hard on Reward Hacking.

Where each LLM training stage sits on this page's map

Supervised fine-tuning is behaviour cloning (section 2), with exposure bias as its compounding error. A reward model fitted to preferences is preference-based reward learning (this section). DPO is offline learning from fixed preference pairs; online RLHF and RLVR are online RL. Rejection sampling and best-of-n fine-tuning (train on the model's own highest-scoring samples) are filtered behaviour cloning, the same idea as the "10% BC" baseline in section 11.

7Offline RL's problem: distributional shift and extrapolation error

Offline RL keeps the rewards and asks for more than imitation: a policy better than the one that collected the data. The natural tool is off-policy Q-learning, which in principle learns the optimal Q from any data. In practice it fails, and the reason is in its target:

Q(s, a) ← r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′)
maxa′
queries Q at actions a′ that may never appear with s′ in the data
Q(s′, a′)
for such actions, whatever the function approximator extrapolates; no sample ever corrects it

Online, a wrongly high value gets tried, disappoints and is corrected. Offline it never gets tried. The max picks out exactly the actions whose errors happen to be positive, bootstrapping copies those values into every state that leads there, and the policy that maximises Q heads for actions nobody has tested. Fujimoto, Meger and Precup (2019) called this extrapolation error, "introduced by the mismatch between the dataset and true state-action visitation of the current policy", and showed how bad it is: an off-policy actor-critic (DDPG) trained on a million transitions collected by a trained expert "quickly learns to take non-expert actions, under the guise of optimistic extrapolation. As a result, the value estimates rapidly diverge and the agent fails to learn"; even an agent trained on exactly the data of a behavioural agent learning alongside it ("concurrent") did much worse than that agent. Kumar et al. (2019) named the propagated version bootstrapping error, which "accumulates via the Bellman backup operator". It is overestimation bias (Value-based deep RL has Double DQN's version) in its worst form: online, the noise is around actions that are tried; offline, around actions that never will be.

8Policy constraints: stay on the data's actions (BCQ, BEAR, TD3+BC)

The first family attacks the max directly: if the policy only ever chooses actions the data contains, Q is only ever queried where data can correct it. Methods differ in how they define "contains".

  • BCQ (batch-constrained Q-learning; Fujimoto, Meger and Precup 2019) fits a generative model Gω of the data's actions (a conditional VAE), samples n actions from it in each state, lets a small perturbation network nudge each within [−Φ, Φ], and takes the one with the highest Q (their Equation 11). The two knots interpolate between imitation and RL: "If Φ = 0, and the number of sampled actions n = 1, then the policy resembles behavioral cloning and as Φ → amax − amin and n → ∞, then the algorithm approaches Q-learning". The BCQ-style learner in the animation above is this idea with a Gaussian in place of the VAE.
  • BEAR (Kumar et al. 2019) argues that matching the behaviour policy's distribution is too strict when the data comes from a poor, noisy policy, and constrains only its support: "restrict the support of the learned policy, but not the probabilities of the actions lying within the support", enforced with a sampled maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) penalty. BRAC (Wu, Tucker and Nachum 2019) puts the divergence to the behaviour policy either into the value target or into the policy loss and compares the choices.
  • TD3+BC (Fujimoto and Gu 2021) asks how little needs to change. Its whole method is one line in the actor's loss of the online algorithm TD3, plus normalising the states:
π = argmaxπ E(s, a)∼D [ λ Q(s, π(s)) − (π(s) − a)² ], λ = α / meani |Q(si, ai)|, α = 2.5
λ Q(s, π(s))
TD3's usual objective, rescaled by the average size of Q so that α means the same thing on every task
(π(s) − a)²
a behaviour-cloning term pulling the action toward the data's action in that state

It "compares favorably against many offline RL algorithms, while being significantly easier to implement and more than halving the required computation cost", and its Table 1 lists the many changes other offline methods make to their base algorithms, each with its own hyperparameters. The lesson the field took: much of offline RL's progress was tuning and implementation, and a strong simple baseline is required in any comparison. ReBRAC (Tarasov et al. 2023) later revisited this minimalist approach with a handful of further design choices and is the strongest average performer in CORL's reruns (see the D4RL results tab).

The limit of every constraint is that it ties the policy to the behaviour: the tighter it is, the safer and the less improvement. When the data has to be stitched (pieces of different trajectories joined into one route that none of them took), a policy kept near each state's logged actions cannot follow the joined route. In CORL's reruns on AntMaze's medium and large mazes, TD3+BC scores between 0 and 0.25, against 20.5 to 67.25 for CQL and 30.25 to 73.75 for IQL (normalised score of the last policy, 4 seeds; Tarasov et al., Table 1).

9Conservative values: CQL

Instead of restricting the policy, Conservative Q-learning changes what Q means: it learns a Q-function whose values are a lower bound on the true ones, so maximising it is safe because nothing is overestimated (Kumar, Zhou, Tucker and Levine 2020). The paper proves two bounds. With only the push-down term (its Equation 1), the learned Q is below the true Qπ at every state and action, for α large enough relative to the sampling error (Theorem 3.1). With both terms (Equation 2), Q is no longer a pointwise lower bound (values of the data's own actions can be too high) but the policy's value Eπ[Q̂] is a lower bound on its true value when μ = π, and a tighter one (Theorem 3.2). CQL reported "2-5 times higher final return" than prior offline methods, especially on datasets mixing several policies.

In the animation, CQL at the default α = 0.1 ends with a believed value of 5.2 against a true 6.4 on seed 1 (a lower bound, as Theorem 3.2 says for μ = π), above the behaviour policy's 5.5: a real improvement found without trusting any value off the data. At α = 10 the policy collapses onto the behaviour and both numbers settle at its value: over-conservatism is CQL's failure mode, and α is a hyperparameter that cannot be tuned without evaluating policies somewhere. CORL's reruns found "CQL is extremely sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters and implementation details", needing, for example, five hidden layers in the critic on AntMaze but not elsewhere (Tarasov et al. 2022, Observation 3). The idea scales: Q-Transformer trains a Transformer Q-function offline on real robot data with a conservative regulariser that "pushes down the Q-values Q(s, a) for any action a outside of the dataset" (Chebotar et al. 2023).

A model-based relative uses the same pessimism with a learned dynamics model: MOPO subtracts an uncertainty penalty from the model's rewards, and MOReL builds a pessimistic MDP that ends episodes where the model is unsure (Yu et al. 2020; Kidambi et al. 2020). Learned models and their exploitation are covered on Model-based RL and planning.

10In-sample learning: IQL and advantage-weighted regression

The third family never asks about an action outside the data at all. The simplest version is SARSA on the dataset: bootstrap from the action that was actually taken next, Q(s, a) ← r + γQ(s′, a′), which evaluates the behaviour policy without extrapolating; one step of policy improvement on top of that value is already a strong method (Brandfonbrener et al. 2021, "Offline RL Without Off-Policy Evaluation"). The question is how to get more than one step of improvement without leaving the data.

Implicit Q-learning (Kostrikov, Nair and Levine 2021) answers with expectile regression. The τ-expectile of a random variable minimises an asymmetric squared loss, L2τ(u) = |τ − 1(u < 0)| u²: at τ = 0.5 it is the mean, and as τ → 1 it approaches the maximum. IQL fits a state value V(s) to an upper expectile of Q(s, a) over the dataset's actions in that state, then fits Q(s, a) to r + γV(s′). At τ = 0.5 this is SARSA; as τ → 1 it approaches Q-learning's max, but restricted to actions the data contains (their Equation 4), so it "never needs to evaluate actions outside of the dataset". The policy is extracted afterwards by advantage-weighted regression: behaviour cloning in which each logged action is weighted by exp(β(Q(s, a) − V(s))), so better-than-average actions are copied more; small β is plain cloning, large β approaches the best in-data action. The paper's settings: τ = 0.7 and β = 3 on locomotion, τ = 0.9 and β = 10 on AntMaze, where the larger expectile is "crucial" for stitching.

The weighting comes from AWR (Peng et al. 2019), two supervised regressions (a value function, then weighted action regression), and AWAC (Nair et al. 2020), which uses a Q-function critic and was designed for fine-tuning online after offline training. These methods inherit the stability of supervised learning: every loss is a regression on dataset samples. In the animation, IQL's policy lives on the 30 data actions; with τ = 0.9 its believed value 7.6 is close to its true 6.9, and as τ rises toward 0.99 the believed value climbs toward the highest Q among the data's actions (8.8 on seed 1), while the policy still never leaves the data. In CORL's reruns IQL, AWAC and ReBRAC are "the strongest offline baselines on average" (Observation 1).

11Sequence modelling: Decision Transformer, and when it works

Decision Transformer (Chen et al. 2021) drops value functions entirely. Each trajectory becomes a sequence of triples (R̂t, st, at), where R̂t = Σt′ ≥ t rt′ is the return-to-go, and a causal GPT-style Transformer is trained to predict each action from the last K triples (K = 30 on Atari). At test time you ask for a return: feed the desired R̂ first, act, subtract each reward received, and continue. It "matches or exceeds the performance of state-of-the-art model-free offline RL baselines on Atari, OpenAI Gym, and Key-to-Door tasks", and longer contexts helped (K = 1 was "significantly worse"). The same idea, conditioning an action model on an outcome, came earlier as upside-down RL and reward-conditioned policies, and Trajectory Transformer (Janner, Li and Levine 2021) modelled whole trajectories and planned with beam search.

The debate. Four results narrowed what return conditioning buys.

  • Filtered cloning does as well on D4RL. The Decision Transformer paper itself introduced "Percentile Behavior Cloning": clone only the top X% of timesteps by episode return. With plentiful data, as in D4RL, "we find %BC can match or beat other offline RL methods", with DT "competitive with the performance of the best %BC" (§5.1); DT's advantage showed in the low-data Atari setting. The 10% BC column in the D4RL results tab is this baseline.
  • A small MLP is enough. RvS (Emmons et al. 2022): "simply maximizing likelihood with a two-layer feedforward MLP is competitive with state-of-the-art results of substantially more complex methods based on TD learning or sequence modeling with Transformers"; what matters is model capacity and what to condition on (a goal or a return).
  • It needs near-determinism and return coverage, and it cannot stitch. Brandfonbrener et al. (2022) prove that return-conditioned supervised learning recovers a near-optimal policy only under assumptions "stronger than those needed for the more traditional dynamic programming-based algorithms" (near-deterministic dynamics, and data that actually reaches the return being asked for), and that such methods "cannot perform stitching in general, even with infinite data".
  • It confuses luck with skill. In a stochastic environment a high-return trajectory may be high-return because of chance; conditioning on its return asks the policy to repeat the actions that were lucky (Paster, McIlraith and Ba 2022, "You Can't Count on Luck"). Their fix, ESPER, conditions on the average return of clusters of trajectories instead.

The working summary: sequence models are a strong way to imitate selectively from large, mostly good, near-deterministic data, and a weak way to improve on data that must be recombined, where dynamic programming (CQL, IQL) still wins. Kumar et al. (2022) make the same distinction between offline RL and behaviour cloning generally: offline RL wins with sparse rewards, "critical" states where a few decisions matter, long horizons and noisy data, and they show "policies trained on sufficiently noisy suboptimal data can attain better performance than even BC algorithms with expert data".

12Offline to online: start from the data, then interact

In practice the purest offline setting is rare; more often a dataset exists and some interaction is allowed later. The natural recipe, pretrain offline then fine-tune online, is less natural than it looks. Nakamoto et al. (2023) note the "counter-intuitive trend: policy initializations obtained from more effective offline RL methods tend to exhibit worse online fine-tuning performance", and identify an unlearning dip: a conservative Q-function's values are too low, so the first online updates spend themselves raising them and the policy gets worse before it gets better. Cal-QL keeps CQL's lower bound on the learned policy but stops values from falling below a reference policy's (the behaviour policy's) value: "calibrated", "a one-line code change" to CQL, better than prior methods on 9 of 11 fine-tuning tasks.

The opposite route also works: run an ordinary online off-policy method with the offline data mixed in. RLPD (Ball, Smith, Kostrikov and Levine 2023) trains SAC from scratch with "symmetric sampling", each batch half from the offline data and half from the online replay buffer, plus layer normalisation in the critic (which "can bound the extrapolation of networks" without constraining the policy to the data) and large ensembles, reporting "a 2.5× improvement over existing approaches" with no offline pretraining at all. In CORL's reruns on AntMaze (6 datasets, 1 million offline then 1 million online steps), the average normalised score moved from 48.4 to 95.6 for CQL, 56.3 to 78.5 for IQL, 53.0 to 97.3 for Cal-QL and 18.1 to 16.5 for AWAC (Tarasov et al., Table 3; per-dataset values in the D4RL results tab).

13Evaluation: D4RL, its pitfalls, and off-policy evaluation

D4RL (Fu et al. 2020) is the benchmark nearly every paper above reports. Its datasets were built to probe specific difficulties: Gym-MuJoCo locomotion with "medium" (1 million samples from a SAC policy stopped early), "medium-replay" (the replay buffer up to that point) and "medium-expert" (medium and expert data mixed) for narrow and mixed data; Maze2D and AntMaze for stitching undirected data with sparse rewards; Adroit's 24-degree-of-freedom hand with a small number of human demonstrations; Franka Kitchen for multitask data. Scores are normalised so that 0 is a uniformly random policy and 100 a domain expert (a SAC agent on Gym-MuJoCo):

The D4RL results tab shows five printed tables as they are. Reading them needs care, because several things that look like the same number are not:

  • Dataset versions. The Gym-MuJoCo datasets were reissued as v2 in December 2020, which "fixed Hopper's performance and the qpos/qvel fields" (D4RL README). The IQL paper points out that the Decision Transformer and one-step papers "incorrectly report results for some prior methods, such as CQL, using the '-v0' environments", which "generally produce lower scores than the '-v2' environments that these papers use for their own methods" (§5.2). CQL on halfcheetah-medium-expert is 62.4 in its own paper (v0) and 91.6 in IQL's table (v2). RvS's AntMaze table, labelled v2, includes CQL, TD3+BC and one-step numbers from v0, flagged by an asterisk.
  • Last policy or best policy. CORL reports both: the score of the policy at the end of training and the best score seen during training. Across Gym-MuJoCo, BC averages 50.4 last and 70.2 best; CQL on AntMaze 50.7 last and 61.8 best (Tables 1 and 2). "Best during training" was found by evaluating in the environment, which an offline method cannot do.
  • Hyperparameters chosen online. The same applies to tuning: "existing hyperparameter selection methods for offline RL break the offline assumption by evaluating policies corresponding to each hyperparameter setting in the environment" (Paine et al. 2020), and offline RL algorithms "are not robust to hyperparameter choices".
  • Few seeds, and two printings of the same run. Most tables average 3 to 5 seeds. The CQL paper's Table 1 and the D4RL paper's Table 2 both print CQL on the same v0 datasets, and disagree: CQL is 111.0 on hopper-medium-expert and 98.7 on walker2d-medium-expert in the CQL paper, and the reverse in D4RL's; the CQL paper's walker2d-medium-expert row also repeats the baselines of its walker2d-mixed row. Which printing is right is unconfirmed; the tab shows both.
  • Saturation and succession. On Gym-MuJoCo the best averages in CORL's reruns are 89.7 (ReBRAC) and 92.9 (EDAC) on a scale where 100 is the reference SAC agent; the hard remaining suites are AntMaze and Adroit. D4RL itself is no longer maintained: its environments moved to Gymnasium and its datasets to Minari.

Off-policy evaluation (OPE) is the offline answer to "how good is this policy?", needed for model selection and for deployment decisions. Importance sampling reweights logged returns by the product of π/πβ along each trajectory: unbiased, with variance that grows exponentially with the horizon (the off-policy Monte Carlo section of Model-free prediction and control shows the blow-up). Doubly robust estimators add a learned value model as a control variate, "guaranteed to be unbiased and can have a much lower variance" (Jiang and Li 2016). Fitted Q evaluation (FQE) runs fitted Q iteration for the fixed policy and reads off its value (Le, Voloshin and Yue 2019); it is biased by the same extrapolation as section 7 when the policy leaves the data. A benchmark of these estimators on D4RL-style tasks is DOPE (Fu et al. 2021); Paine et al. found that, controlled carefully, offline estimates "can reliably rank policies across hyperparameter choices". The healthcare literature shows what goes wrong when they are not: see the next section.

14Where it is used, and its limits

  • Robotics. QT-Opt learned closed-loop grasping from "over 580k real-world grasp attempts" of logged off-policy data; "even fully off-policy training can outperform strong baselines", and about 28,000 additional on-policy grasps raised success to 96% on unseen objects (Kalashnikov et al. 2018). Q-Transformer trains offline on demonstrations plus autonomously collected data across a multi-task real-robot suite (Chebotar et al. 2023). Most deployed robot policies, though, are behaviour cloning at scale (RT-1, Diffusion Policy; section 2), because demonstrations are easier to collect than reward labels.
  • Recommendation. YouTube scaled REINFORCE to "an action space on the orders of millions" learning from logged user feedback, with an off-policy correction for the fact that the logs only show items earlier recommenders chose, and a top-K version of the correction for recommending several items at once; it was tested in live experiments (Chen et al. 2019). Learning from logs is the norm in recommendation, usually as contextual bandits (Bandits and exploration).
  • Healthcare is where the setting fits best and evaluation is hardest. The AI Clinician learned vasopressor and fluid dosing for sepsis from intensive-care records and reported that "mortality was lowest in patients for whom clinicians' actual doses matched the AI decisions" (Komorowski et al. 2018). A reproduction found that, under the same importance-sampling evaluation, a policy giving no drugs at all often scored better than the clinicians (Jeter et al. 2019), and methodological work showed how the summary of a patient's history, the variance of the estimators and confounders "can result in unreliable, even misleading estimates of the quality of a treatment policy" (Gottesman et al. 2018; guidelines in Nature Medicine 2019).
  • Language models. SFT is behaviour cloning; DPO is offline preference learning (section 6); value-based offline RL has been applied to text generation (ILQL, Snell et al. 2022). Most current post-training is online RL on the model's own samples (RL for LLMs), partly because sampling a language model is cheap and its "environment" (a verifier or reward model) is free to query.
Limits that no algorithm removes

Coverage: no method can learn the value of something the data never does; offline RL can only recombine and reweight what is there, and the best policy within the data's support (dashed band in the animation) is the ceiling. Confounding: if the people who produced the data acted on information the data does not record (a clinician's bedside impression), the logged actions are correlated with outcomes for reasons the learner cannot see, and every estimate inherits the bias. Evaluation: without interaction, the policy's quality is itself an estimate, with the problems of section 13. Non-stationarity: the world that produced the logs may not be the one the policy is deployed in, and deploying the policy changes it.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Off-policy means offline."
Off-policy is about which policy the values describe; offline is about whether the agent may collect data. DQN and SAC are off-policy and online; naive off-policy algorithms fail offline for the reason in section 7, even on expert data (Fujimoto et al.'s "imitation" batch).
"More expert demonstrations will fix behaviour cloning."
More data from the expert's state distribution lowers ε but does not cover the states the learner drifts into; Ross et al.'s Super Tux Kart learner did not improve with more expert laps. Coverage of recovery states (DAgger, noise injection, augmentation) is what helps.
"Offline RL beats behaviour cloning."
Not on clean expert data, and not always on D4RL: in CORL's reruns 10% BC averages 69.3 on Gym-MuJoCo against CQL's 78.3, and BC beats both on pen-human (71.0 against −3.9 for TD3+BC and 13.7 for CQL). Offline RL wins when rewards are sparse, data is noisy or must be stitched (Kumar et al. 2022).
"A conservative value is a pessimistic estimate of every action."
CQL's practical form (Equation 2) bounds the policy's value, not every Q-value: actions that are frequent in the data can be overestimated. Only the push-down-only form (Equation 1) is a pointwise lower bound.
Comparing D4RL scores across papers
Check the dataset version (v0 against v2), last against best checkpoint, the number of seeds, and whether hyperparameters were chosen by online evaluation. Compare within one table where possible, as the D4RL results tab does.
Asking a Decision Transformer for a return far above the data
Return conditioning interpolates among returns the data reached; asking for more does not make the policy better, and in stochastic environments the returns it was trained on may reflect luck rather than skill.
"GAIL is offline imitation."
GAIL needs on-policy rollouts in the environment, comparable in number to training the expert with RL. Its demonstrations are fixed; its own data is not.
Trusting an importance-sampled value of a learned treatment policy
With long horizons and a deterministic learned policy, few logged trajectories match it, the weights explode, and the estimate rests on a handful of patients. Report the effective sample size and use several estimators.

What the tables agree on

Behaviour cloning is strong wherever the data is good (medium-expert, Adroit expert), and filtering the data helps it: in CORL's runs 10% BC lifts BC's Gym-MuJoCo average from 50.4 to 69.3. Only methods that do dynamic programming (CQL, IQL, ReBRAC) score more than a few points on AntMaze's medium and large mazes, where trajectories must be stitched. The ensemble methods are, in CORL's words, "the strongest baselines for Gym-MuJoCo and Maze2d, but they perform poorly on both AntMaze and Adroit domains" (SAC-N and EDAC score 0 on every AntMaze dataset with their last policy). And with its last policy no method reaches 10 on Adroit's human and cloned door, hammer and relocate datasets.

6Offline RL's problem: distributional shift and extrapolation error

Offline RL keeps the rewards and asks for more than imitation: a policy better than the one that collected the data. The natural tool is off-policy Q-learning, which in principle learns the optimal Q from any data. In practice it fails, and the reason is in its target:

Q(s, a) ← r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′)
maxa′
queries Q at actions a′ that may never appear with s′ in the data
Q(s′, a′)
for such actions, whatever the function approximator extrapolates; no sample ever corrects it

Online, a wrongly high value gets tried, disappoints and is corrected. Offline it never gets tried. The max picks out exactly the actions whose errors happen to be positive, bootstrapping copies those values into every state that leads there, and the policy heads for actions nobody has tested. {{Fujimoto, Meger and Precup (2019)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02900}} called this extrapolation error, "introduced by the mismatch between the dataset and true state-action visitation of the current policy": an off-policy actor-critic (DDPG) trained on a million transitions collected by a trained expert "quickly learns to take non-expert actions, under the guise of optimistic extrapolation. As a result, the value estimates rapidly diverge and the agent fails to learn". {{Kumar et al. (2019)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00949}} named the propagated version bootstrapping error, which "accumulates via the Bellman backup operator". It is overestimation bias ({{Value-based deep RL|n:3ee5c17b0d0d818689b2c0dbce434ece}} has Double DQN's version) in its worst form: online, the noise is around actions that are tried; offline, around actions that never will be.

How the four learners are built, and the original paper's experiments 1 min

The rewards in the data carry noise of s.d. 0.1. Q is a ReLU network with its first layer frozen (21 fixed features, ridge 10⁻³), so every fit is solved exactly and the extrapolation outside the data is linear, as a ReLU network's is. Each iteration fits targets ri + γ·(value of the next state). The value of the next state is the policy's expected Q, where the policy is ∝ exp(β Q) over all actions (naive, CQL), over actions within two standard deviations of the data's mean (BCQ-style, the shaded band; a fitted Gaussian stands in for BCQ's generative model), or, for IQL, the τ-expectile of Q over the dataset's own actions, with the policy extracted by advantage weights exp(β(Q − V)) on those actions. CQL adds α·((1/β) log Σ exp(β Q) − mean of Q on the data) to every fit: CQL(H), with its soft maximum taken at the policy's temperature so that the distribution pushed down is the policy itself (μ = π, the case of CQL's Theorem 3.2). Checked against an independent Python implementation (src/recompute.py).

In Fujimoto et al.'s experiments, even an agent trained on exactly the data of a behavioural agent learning alongside it ("concurrent") did much worse than that agent: the same data, without the ability to try its own actions, is not enough.

7Policy constraints: stay on the data's actions (BCQ, TD3+BC)

The first family attacks the max directly: if the policy only ever chooses actions the data contains, Q is only ever queried where data can correct it. BCQ (batch-constrained Q-learning; {{Fujimoto, Meger and Precup 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02900}}) fits a generative model of the data's actions (a conditional VAE), samples n actions from it in each state, lets a small perturbation network nudge each within [−Φ, Φ], and takes the one with the highest Q (their Equation 11). The two knobs interpolate between imitation and RL: "If Φ = 0, and the number of sampled actions n = 1, then the policy resembles behavioral cloning and as Φ → amax − amin and n → ∞, then the algorithm approaches Q-learning". The BCQ-style learner in the animation is this idea with a Gaussian in place of the VAE.

TD3+BC ({{Fujimoto and Gu 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06860}}) asks how little needs to change. Its whole method is one line in the actor's loss of the online algorithm TD3, plus normalising the states:

π = argmaxπ E(s, a)∼D [ λ Q(s, π(s)) − (π(s) − a)² ], λ = α / meani |Q(si, ai)|, α = 2.5
λ Q(s, π(s))
TD3's usual objective, rescaled by the average size of Q so that α means the same thing on every task
(π(s) − a)²
a behaviour-cloning term pulling the action toward the data's action in that state

It "compares favorably against many offline RL algorithms, while being significantly easier to implement and more than halving the required computation cost". The lesson the field took: much of offline RL's progress was tuning and implementation, and a strong simple baseline is required in any comparison. {{ReBRAC|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09836}} (Tarasov et al. 2023) revisited this minimalist approach with a handful of further design choices and is the strongest average performer in CORL's reruns ({{D4RL results|#t-d4rl}} tab).

The limit of every constraint is that it ties the policy to the behaviour: the tighter, the safer and the less improvement. When the data has to be stitched, a policy kept near each state's logged actions cannot follow the joined route. In CORL's reruns on AntMaze's medium and large mazes, TD3+BC scores between 0 and 0.25, against 20.5 to 67.25 for CQL and 30.25 to 73.75 for IQL (normalised score of the last policy, 4 seeds; {{Tarasov et al., Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07105}}).

Support against distribution: BEAR and BRAC, and TD3+BC's table of changes under 1 min

BEAR ({{Kumar et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00949}}) argues that matching the behaviour policy's distribution is too strict when the data comes from a poor, noisy policy, and constrains only its support: "restrict the support of the learned policy, but not the probabilities of the actions lying within the support", enforced with a sampled maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) penalty. BRAC ({{Wu, Tucker and Nachum 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.11361}}) puts the divergence to the behaviour policy either into the value target or into the policy loss and compares the choices. TD3+BC's Table 1 lists the many changes other offline methods make to their base algorithms, each with its own hyperparameters.

8Conservative values: CQL

Instead of restricting the policy, Conservative Q-learning changes what Q means: it learns a Q-function whose values are a lower bound on the true ones, so maximising it is safe because nothing is overestimated ({{Kumar, Zhou, Tucker and Levine 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04779}}). In the animation, CQL at the default α = 0.1 ends with a believed value of 5.2 against a true 6.4 on seed 1, a lower bound, and above the behaviour policy's 5.5: a real improvement found without trusting any value off the data. The regulariser is added to the ordinary Bellman error:

With both terms, Q is not a pointwise lower bound (values of the data's own actions can be too high), but the policy's value Eπ[Q̂] is a lower bound on its true value when μ = π (Theorem 3.2). CQL reported "2-5 times higher final return" than prior offline methods, especially on datasets mixing several policies. Set α to 10 in the animation and the policy collapses onto the behaviour, both numbers settling at its 5.5: over-conservatism is CQL's failure mode, and α cannot be tuned without evaluating policies somewhere. CORL's reruns found "CQL is extremely sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters and implementation details", needing, for example, five hidden layers in the critic on AntMaze but not elsewhere ({{Tarasov et al. 2022, Observation 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07105}}).

The two theorems, Q-Transformer, and model-based pessimism (MOPO, MOReL) 1 min

The paper proves two bounds. With only the push-down term (its Equation 1), the learned Q is below the true Qπ at every state and action, for α large enough relative to the sampling error (Theorem 3.1). With both terms (Equation 2), the bound holds for the policy's value only, and is tighter (Theorem 3.2). In the animation the Theorem 3.2 bound holds in every run at β = 1 and at the defaults, by construction of the setting (the soft maximum is taken at the policy's temperature); at α = 10 it is within the noise of 30 rewards (up to about 0.3 above).

The idea scales: Q-Transformer trains a Transformer Q-function offline on real robot data with a conservative regulariser that "pushes down the Q-values Q(s, a) for any action a outside of the dataset" ({{Chebotar et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10150}}). A model-based relative uses the same pessimism with a learned dynamics model: MOPO subtracts an uncertainty penalty from the model's rewards, and MOReL builds a pessimistic MDP that ends episodes where the model is unsure ({{Yu et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.13239}}; {{Kidambi et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05951}}). Learned models and their exploitation are on {{Model-based RL and planning|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8116be5fd39e10bd94c5}}.

9In-sample learning: IQL and advantage-weighted regression

The third family never asks about an action outside the data at all. The simplest version is SARSA on the dataset: bootstrap from the action actually taken next, Q(s, a) ← r + γQ(s′, a′), which evaluates the behaviour policy without extrapolating; one step of policy improvement on top of that value is already a strong method ({{Brandfonbrener et al. 2021, "Offline RL Without Off-Policy Evaluation"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08909}}). The question is how to get more than one step of improvement without leaving the data.

Implicit Q-learning ({{Kostrikov, Nair and Levine 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06169}}) answers with expectile regression. The τ-expectile of a set of numbers minimises an asymmetric squared loss, L2τ(u) = |τ − 1(u < 0)| u²: at τ = 0.5 it is the mean, and as τ → 1 it approaches the maximum.

The expectile slides from the mean of the data's action values to their maximum, without ever looking at an action outside the data

Dots: Q (vertical) at each of the 30 data actions (horizontal) of the animation above, from the IQL learner's final fit (seed 1, τ = 0.9, β = 3), each drawn at its action. The line is the τ-expectile of those 30 numbers, computed exactly. Predict before dragging: where is the line at τ = 0.5, and can any τ lift it to the true optimum of 10, which sits at an action off the data?

IQL fits a state value V(s) to that upper expectile of Q(s, a) over the dataset's actions, then fits Q(s, a) to r + γV(s′). At τ = 0.5 this is SARSA; as τ → 1 it approaches Q-learning's max restricted to actions the data contains (their Equation 4), so it "never needs to evaluate actions outside of the dataset". The policy is extracted afterwards by advantage-weighted regression: behaviour cloning in which each logged action is weighted by exp(β(Q(s, a) − V(s))), so better-than-average actions are copied more; small β is plain cloning, large β approaches the best in-data action. The paper's settings: τ = 0.7 and β = 3 on locomotion, τ = 0.9 and β = 10 on AntMaze, where the larger expectile is "crucial" for stitching.

In the animation, IQL's policy lives on the 30 data actions; with τ = 0.9 its believed value 7.6 is close to its true 6.9, and as τ rises toward 0.99 the believed value climbs toward the highest Q among the data's actions (8.8 on seed 1), while the policy still never leaves the data. Every loss is a regression on dataset samples, so these methods inherit the stability of supervised learning; in CORL's reruns IQL, AWAC and ReBRAC are "the strongest offline baselines on average" (Observation 1).

Where the weighting comes from: AWR and AWAC under 1 min

The weighting comes from {{AWR (Peng et al. 2019)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00177}}, two supervised regressions (a value function, then weighted action regression), and {{AWAC (Nair et al. 2020)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09359}}, which uses a Q-function critic and was designed for fine-tuning online after offline training.

10Sequence modelling: Decision Transformer, and when it works

{{Decision Transformer (Chen et al. 2021)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01345}} drops value functions entirely. Each trajectory becomes a sequence of triples (R̂t, st, at), where R̂t = Σt′ ≥ t rt′ is the return-to-go, and a causal GPT-style Transformer predicts each action from the last K triples (K = 30 on Atari). At test time you ask for a return: feed the desired R̂ first, act, subtract each reward received, and continue.

Training reads returns off the data; at test time the return is a request, reduced by each reward received

Top: one logged trajectory as tokens; the Transformer sees everything to the left of each action token and is trained to predict that action. Bottom: at test time the first token is the return asked for (90 here, illustrative), and after each reward the next token is what is still owed. The model only interpolates among returns the data reached: asking for more than the data contains does not make the policy better.

It "matches or exceeds the performance of state-of-the-art model-free offline RL baselines on Atari, OpenAI Gym, and Key-to-Door tasks", and longer contexts helped (K = 1 was "significantly worse"). Four results then narrowed what return conditioning buys:

  • Filtered cloning does as well on D4RL. The paper's own "Percentile Behavior Cloning" clones only the top X% of timesteps by episode return; with plentiful data "we find %BC can match or beat other offline RL methods", with DT "competitive with the performance of the best %BC" (§5.1). DT's advantage showed in low-data Atari. The 10% BC column in the {{D4RL results|#t-d4rl}} tab is this baseline.
  • A small MLP is enough. {{RvS (Emmons et al. 2022)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10751}}: "simply maximizing likelihood with a two-layer feedforward MLP is competitive with state-of-the-art results of substantially more complex methods based on TD learning or sequence modeling with Transformers".
  • It needs near-determinism and return coverage, and cannot stitch. {{Brandfonbrener et al. (2022)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01079}} prove that return-conditioned supervised learning recovers a near-optimal policy only under assumptions "stronger than those needed for the more traditional dynamic programming-based algorithms", and that such methods "cannot perform stitching in general, even with infinite data".
  • It confuses luck with skill. In a stochastic environment a high-return trajectory may be high-return by chance; conditioning on its return asks the policy to repeat the lucky actions ({{Paster, McIlraith and Ba 2022, "You Can't Count on Luck"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15967}}). Their fix, ESPER, conditions on the average return of clusters of trajectories instead.

The working summary: sequence models are a strong way to imitate selectively from large, mostly good, near-deterministic data, and a weak way to improve on data that must be recombined, where dynamic programming (CQL, IQL) still wins. {{Kumar et al. (2022)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05618}} draw the same line between offline RL and behaviour cloning generally: offline RL wins with sparse rewards, "critical" states where a few decisions matter, long horizons and noisy data, and "policies trained on sufficiently noisy suboptimal data can attain better performance than even BC algorithms with expert data".

Precursors, and what RvS found matters under 1 min

Conditioning an action model on an outcome came earlier as upside-down RL and reward-conditioned policies, and Trajectory Transformer ({{Janner, Li and Levine 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02039}}) modelled whole trajectories and planned with beam search. RvS found that what matters is model capacity and what to condition on (a goal or a return), not the Transformer. Brandfonbrener et al.'s assumptions are near-deterministic dynamics and data that actually reaches the return being asked for.

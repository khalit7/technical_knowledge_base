8Policy constraints: stay on the data's actions (BCQ, BEAR, TD3+BC)

The first family attacks the max directly: if the policy only ever chooses actions the data contains, Q is only ever queried where data can correct it. Methods differ in how they define "contains".

  • BCQ (batch-constrained Q-learning; {{Fujimoto, Meger and Precup 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02900}}) fits a generative model Gω of the data's actions (a conditional VAE), samples n actions from it in each state, lets a small perturbation network nudge each within [−Φ, Φ], and takes the one with the highest Q (their Equation 11). The two knots interpolate between imitation and RL: "If Φ = 0, and the number of sampled actions n = 1, then the policy resembles behavioral cloning and as Φ → amax − amin and n → ∞, then the algorithm approaches Q-learning". The BCQ-style learner in the animation above is this idea with a Gaussian in place of the VAE.
  • BEAR ({{Kumar et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00949}}) argues that matching the behaviour policy's distribution is too strict when the data comes from a poor, noisy policy, and constrains only its support: "restrict the support of the learned policy, but not the probabilities of the actions lying within the support", enforced with a sampled maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) penalty. BRAC ({{Wu, Tucker and Nachum 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.11361}}) puts the divergence to the behaviour policy either into the value target or into the policy loss and compares the choices.
  • TD3+BC ({{Fujimoto and Gu 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.06860}}) asks how little needs to change. Its whole method is one line in the actor's loss of the online algorithm TD3, plus normalising the states:
π = argmaxπ E(s, a)∼D [ λ Q(s, π(s)) − (π(s) − a)² ], λ = α / meani |Q(si, ai)|, α = 2.5

a behaviour-cloning term pulling the action toward the data's action in that state

It "compares favorably against many offline RL algorithms, while being significantly easier to implement and more than halving the required computation cost", and its Table 1 lists the many changes other offline methods make to their base algorithms, each with its own hyperparameters. The lesson the field took: much of offline RL's progress was tuning and implementation, and a strong simple baseline is required in any comparison. {{ReBRAC|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09836}} (Tarasov et al. 2023) later revisited this minimalist approach with a handful of further design choices and is the strongest average performer in CORL's reruns (see the {{D4RL results|#t-d4rl}} tab).

The limit of every constraint is that it ties the policy to the behaviour: the tighter it is, the safer and the less improvement. When the data has to be stitched (pieces of different trajectories joined into one route that none of them took), a policy kept near each state's logged actions cannot follow the joined route. In CORL's reruns on AntMaze's medium and large mazes, TD3+BC scores between 0 and 0.25, against 20.5 to 67.25 for CQL and 30.25 to 73.75 for IQL (normalised score of the last policy, 4 seeds; {{Tarasov et al., Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07105}}).

9Conservative values: CQL

Instead of restricting the policy, Conservative Q-learning changes what Q means: it learns a Q-function whose values are a lower bound on the true ones, so maximising it is safe because nothing is overestimated ({{Kumar, Zhou, Tucker and Levine 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.04779}}). The regulariser is added to the ordinary Bellman error:

The paper proves two bounds. With only the push-down term (its Equation 1), the learned Q is below the true Qπ at every state and action, for α large enough relative to the sampling error (Theorem 3.1). With both terms (Equation 2), Q is no longer a pointwise lower bound (values of the data's own actions can be too high) but the policy's value Eπ[Q̂] is a lower bound on its true value when μ = π, and a tighter one (Theorem 3.2). CQL reported "2-5 times higher final return" than prior offline methods, especially on datasets mixing several policies.

In the animation, CQL at the default α = 0.1 ends with a believed value of 5.2 against a true 6.4 on seed 1 (a lower bound, as Theorem 3.2 says for μ = π), above the behaviour policy's 5.5: a real improvement found without trusting any value off the data. At α = 10 the policy collapses onto the behaviour and both numbers settle at its value: over-conservatism is CQL's failure mode, and α is a hyperparameter that cannot be tuned without evaluating policies somewhere. CORL's reruns found "CQL is extremely sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters and implementation details", needing, for example, five hidden layers in the critic on AntMaze but not elsewhere ({{Tarasov et al. 2022, Observation 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07105}}). The idea scales: Q-Transformer trains a Transformer Q-function offline on real robot data with a conservative regulariser that "pushes down the Q-values Q(s, a) for any action a outside of the dataset" ({{Chebotar et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10150}}).

A model-based relative uses the same pessimism with a learned dynamics model: MOPO subtracts an uncertainty penalty from the model's rewards, and MOReL builds a pessimistic MDP that ends episodes where the model is unsure ({{Yu et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.13239}}; {{Kidambi et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05951}}). Learned models and their exploitation are covered on {{Model-based RL and planning|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8116be5fd39e10bd94c5}}.

10In-sample learning: IQL and advantage-weighted regression

The third family never asks about an action outside the data at all. The simplest version is SARSA on the dataset: bootstrap from the action that was actually taken next, Q(s, a) ← r + γQ(s′, a′), which evaluates the behaviour policy without extrapolating; one step of policy improvement on top of that value is already a strong method ({{Brandfonbrener et al. 2021, "Offline RL Without Off-Policy Evaluation"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08909}}). The question is how to get more than one step of improvement without leaving the data.

Implicit Q-learning ({{Kostrikov, Nair and Levine 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.06169}}) answers with expectile regression. The τ-expectile of a random variable minimises an asymmetric squared loss, L2τ(u) = |τ − 1(u < 0)| u²: at τ = 0.5 it is the mean, and as τ → 1 it approaches the maximum. IQL fits a state value V(s) to an upper expectile of Q(s, a) over the dataset's actions in that state, then fits Q(s, a) to r + γV(s′). At τ = 0.5 this is SARSA; as τ → 1 it approaches Q-learning's max, but restricted to actions the data contains (their Equation 4), so it "never needs to evaluate actions outside of the dataset". The policy is extracted afterwards by advantage-weighted regression: behaviour cloning in which each logged action is weighted by exp(β(Q(s, a) − V(s))), so better-than-average actions are copied more; small β is plain cloning, large β approaches the best in-data action. The paper's settings: τ = 0.7 and β = 3 on locomotion, τ = 0.9 and β = 10 on AntMaze, where the larger expectile is "crucial" for stitching.

The weighting comes from {{AWR (Peng et al. 2019)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00177}}, two supervised regressions (a value function, then weighted action regression), and {{AWAC (Nair et al. 2020)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09359}}, which uses a Q-function critic and was designed for fine-tuning online after offline training. These methods inherit the stability of supervised learning: every loss is a regression on dataset samples. In the animation, IQL's policy lives on the 30 data actions; with τ = 0.9 its believed value 7.6 is close to its true 6.9, and as τ rises toward 0.99 the believed value climbs toward the highest Q among the data's actions (8.8 on seed 1), while the policy still never leaves the data. In CORL's reruns IQL, AWAC and ReBRAC are "the strongest offline baselines on average" (Observation 1).

11Sequence modelling: Decision Transformer, and when it works

{{Decision Transformer (Chen et al. 2021)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01345}} drops value functions entirely. Each trajectory becomes a sequence of triples (R̂t, st, at), where R̂t = Σt′ ≥ t rt′ is the return-to-go, and a causal GPT-style Transformer is trained to predict each action from the last K triples (K = 30 on Atari). At test time you ask for a return: feed the desired R̂ first, act, subtract each reward received, and continue. It "matches or exceeds the performance of state-of-the-art model-free offline RL baselines on Atari, OpenAI Gym, and Key-to-Door tasks", and longer contexts helped (K = 1 was "significantly worse"). The same idea, conditioning an action model on an outcome, came earlier as upside-down RL and reward-conditioned policies, and Trajectory Transformer ({{Janner, Li and Levine 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.02039}}) modelled whole trajectories and planned with beam search.

The debate. Four results narrowed what return conditioning buys.

  • Filtered cloning does as well on D4RL. The Decision Transformer paper itself introduced "Percentile Behavior Cloning": clone only the top X% of timesteps by episode return. With plentiful data, as in D4RL, "we find %BC can match or beat other offline RL methods", with DT "competitive with the performance of the best %BC" (§5.1); DT's advantage showed in the low-data Atari setting. The 10% BC column in the {{D4RL results|#t-d4rl}} tab is this baseline.
  • A small MLP is enough. {{RvS (Emmons et al. 2022)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.10751}}: "simply maximizing likelihood with a two-layer feedforward MLP is competitive with state-of-the-art results of substantially more complex methods based on TD learning or sequence modeling with Transformers"; what matters is model capacity and what to condition on (a goal or a return).
  • It needs near-determinism and return coverage, and it cannot stitch. {{Brandfonbrener et al. (2022)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01079}} prove that return-conditioned supervised learning recovers a near-optimal policy only under assumptions "stronger than those needed for the more traditional dynamic programming-based algorithms" (near-deterministic dynamics, and data that actually reaches the return being asked for), and that such methods "cannot perform stitching in general, even with infinite data".
  • It confuses luck with skill. In a stochastic environment a high-return trajectory may be high-return because of chance; conditioning on its return asks the policy to repeat the actions that were lucky ({{Paster, McIlraith and Ba 2022, "You Can't Count on Luck"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.15967}}). Their fix, ESPER, conditions on the average return of clusters of trajectories instead.

The working summary: sequence models are a strong way to imitate selectively from large, mostly good, near-deterministic data, and a weak way to improve on data that must be recombined, where dynamic programming (CQL, IQL) still wins. {{Kumar et al. (2022)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05618}} make the same distinction between offline RL and behaviour cloning generally: offline RL wins with sparse rewards, "critical" states where a few decisions matter, long horizons and noisy data, and they show "policies trained on sufficiently noisy suboptimal data can attain better performance than even BC algorithms with expert data".

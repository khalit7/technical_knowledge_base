The idea in one screen

A learner trained on a fixed dataset fails exactly where it leaves the data: a copy of the expert drifts into states the data never showed (compounding error), and a value maximiser picks actions the data never tried (extrapolation error). Every method on this page is a way of keeping the learner where the data can vouch for it, or of getting new data where it cannot.

Two ways off the data: along the state axis (imitation) and along the action axis (offline RL)

The shaded band is the dataset: the states the behaviour visited and, in each, the actions it took. ① A cloned policy makes a small mistake, lands in a state just outside the band, has no label there and errs again: the states drift away (sections 2 and 3). ② Q-learning asks for maxa Q(s, a) in a state inside the band; the max lands on an action nobody tried, where the network's value is an unchecked guess (section 6). The fixes are labelled where they act: DAgger extends the band to the learner's states; BCQ and TD3+BC keep the policy inside it; CQL pushes values down outside it; IQL never queries outside it. 1The setting: a fixed dataset, no interaction

An offline (or batch) learner receives a dataset D = {(si, ai, ri, s′i)} collected by a behaviour policy πβ and must output a policy without collecting anything more ({{Levine, Kumar, Tucker and Fu 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01643}}; the older name "batch RL" and fitted Q iteration go back to {{Ernst, Geurts and Wehenkel 2005|https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v6/ernst05a.html}}). Why bother: in medicine, driving, robotics on expensive hardware and recommendation, trying random actions is dangerous, slow or costly, while logs of past decisions are plentiful; offline RL is the attempt to give decision-making deep learning's recipe of large fixed datasets.

Three distinctions are easy to blur. Off-policy means learning about one policy from data produced by another; Q-learning and DQN are off-policy but still online, because they keep collecting data with their current policy ({{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}}, {{Value-based deep RL|n:3ee5c17b0d0d818689b2c0dbce434ece}}). Offline removes the collection entirely: the policy that would fix its own mistakes by trying them never gets to. And imitation differs from offline RL in what the data must contain: imitation needs good behaviour and no reward; offline RL needs rewards and can use poor behaviour, in principle stitching pieces of mediocre trajectories into a better one (which D4RL's maze datasets were built to test; {{Fu et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07219}}).

2Behaviour cloning, and why its errors compound

Behaviour cloning maximises Σi log π(ai | si) over expert pairs (or minimises squared error for continuous actions). It is the oldest method here: ALVINN steered a vehicle from camera images with a small network trained on a person's driving ({{Pomerleau 1988|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1988/hash/812b4ba287f5ee0bc9d43bbf5bbe87fb-Abstract.html}}). It is also the most used: supervised fine-tuning of a language model on demonstrations is behaviour cloning on tokens ({{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}), and large robot policies are mostly BC with expressive models: RT-1 was trained on about 130,000 demonstration episodes collected by 13 robots over 17 months and performs over 700 training instructions at 97% success ({{Brohan et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06817}}); Diffusion Policy reports an average improvement of 46.9% over prior methods across 12 manipulation tasks by modelling multimodal action distributions ({{Chi et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04137}}).

The trouble: supervised learning assumes test inputs come from the training distribution. Training states come from the expert's distribution dπ*; at test time the learner visits dπ, the states its own actions lead to. NVIDIA's end-to-end driving paper says it plainly: "Training with data from only the human driver is not sufficient. The network must learn how to recover from mistakes. Otherwise the car will slowly drift off the road" ({{Bojarski et al. 2016, §5|https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07316}}). Their fix was data augmentation: images from left and right cameras, and synthetic shifts and rotations, labelled with the steering that would return to the lane.

A number first. A learner wrong on 2% of the expert's own states (ε = 0.02) over T = 60 steps: if its errors did not change its inputs, it would expect 60 × 0.02 = 1.2 bad steps. Because each slip moves it off the data, the bound is T²ε = 72, and the exact cost on the task below is 25.5 steps out of lane.

J(π) ≤ J(π*) + T² ε
J(π)
expected total cost of the learner over T steps, with each step's cost in [0, 1]
ε
the learner's error rate (0-1 loss) on the expert's own state distribution: what supervised learning controls
T²
the price of distribution shift: a mistake at step t can cost up to every remaining step

That is Ross and Bagnell's bound for behaviour cloning ({{Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011, Theorem 2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686}}; first proved in {{Ross and Bagnell 2010|https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/ross10a.html}}). The intuition is a sum: a first mistake at step t happens with probability about ε and can cost up to T − t afterwards, so the total is about Σt ε (T − t) ≈ εT²/2. A learner whose errors did not change its inputs (ordinary classification) would pay only Tε.

How the driving task and the two learners are built 1 min

Nine lanes, the car's own lane in the middle; each step the action turns the heading left, keeps it, or turns it right, and the car moves one lane per step of heading. The expert steers back to its lane and straightens up, so its own data never leaves the centre state and its cost is 0. Cost: 1 for every step outside the car's lane. The learner predicts the expert's label at the nearest state it has data for and is wrong with probability ε at every step (ε is exactly its error on the expert's states). DAgger: round 1 is the expert's data (so its policy is behaviour cloning); each later round rolls out the current learner 5 times, has the expert label every state visited, and retrains on everything ({{Ross et al. 2011, Algorithm 3.1 with βi = I(i = 1)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686}}). The state distribution is propagated exactly (seed fixed for the sampled car and the DAgger rollouts); the page's engine equals an independent Python implementation on 32,948 values (src/recompute.py).

Behaviour cloning's cost grows like T² until a slip is near-certain; DAgger's grows like T

Exact expected cost J at ε = 0.02 (log-log) of behaviour cloning and of DAgger after 8 rounds (rollouts of length T), against the lines εT² (Ross and Bagnell's bound, with J* = 0 here) and εT. From T = 5 to T = 40 behaviour cloning's cost rises 44 times for 8 times the horizon, then bends back to slope 1 once almost every remaining step is out of the lane. DAgger stays at about 2εT: each slip costs about two steps out of lane before the correction (Ross et al.'s recovery constant u ≈ 2).

The same failure in language models is exposure bias: a model trained to predict the next token given the true previous tokens must, at generation time, condition on its own outputs, "as errors may accumulate along the way" ({{Ranzato et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06732}}). On-policy distillation is the DAgger answer: the student generates, the teacher labels the student's own sequences ({{Agarwal et al. 2023, GKD|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649}}). RL fine-tuning after SFT is another, since the model is then trained on its own samples ({{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}).

The bound is tight under 1 min

Ross, Gordon and Bagnell give a problem where J(π̂) = (1 − εT) J(π*) + T²ε, so no better guarantee is possible for behaviour cloning in general: the quadratic is the real price of training on the expert's states and testing on the learner's.

3DAgger: label the states the learner actually reaches

If training states come from the expert and test states from the learner, train on the learner's states. DAgger (Dataset Aggregation; {{Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011|https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686}}) does this iteratively; the animation above runs it:

D ← expert demonstrations; π̂₁ ← train on D for i = 1 … N: π_i ← β_i π* + (1 − β_i) π̂_i (β₁ = 1, then usually 0) run π_i, record the states it visits ask the expert for its action at every one of those states D ← D ∪ {(s, π*(s))}; π̂_{i+1} ← train on all of D return the best π̂ on validation

The guarantee changes from quadratic to linear: J(π̂) ≤ J(π*) + uTεN + O(1), where εN is the error on the learner's own state distribution and u bounds how much worse the cost-to-go gets after one non-expert action (the cost of recovering; Theorems 3.1 and 3.2). In Super Tux Kart, behaviour cloning did not improve with more expert laps, "because most of the training laps are all very similar and do not help the learner to learn how to recover from mistakes", while DAgger's policy "never falls off the track after 15 iterations of training" (§5.1).

Its price is the expert. DAgger needs an expert who can be asked "what would you do here?" in states the expert would never have reached, and needs the environment to run the learner. That is cheap for a planner, a search procedure or a stronger model (teacher-student distillation), and awkward for people: labelling while not in control is unnatural, so variants let the human take over only when needed and learn from the takeovers ({{HG-DAgger, Kelly et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02890}}). When neither the expert nor the environment is available again, the remedies are offline: augment the data with perturbed states labelled by recovery actions (NVIDIA's shifted cameras), inject noise into the expert's actions while collecting so the data shows recoveries ({{DART, Laskey et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09327}}), or reason about the whole trajectory (section 4).

Why the analysis works: a reduction to no-regret online learning under 1 min

With enough rounds (N of order T up to log factors), some policy in the sequence has error εN on its own state distribution, the best achievable by the policy class on the aggregated data. Each round is a new loss function (the new states), and training on all data so far is Follow-The-Leader, a no-regret algorithm; no-regret over the rounds is what turns the per-round errors into the linear bound.

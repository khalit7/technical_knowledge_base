Offline RL and imitation in one screen

Everything else in reinforcement learning assumes the agent can act and see what happens. Here it cannot: a fixed dataset of someone else's behaviour is all there is. Two families learn from it. Imitation learning copies the behaviour and needs no reward; its central failure is compounding error, because the copy drifts into states the expert never showed it. Offline RL uses the rewards logged in the data to do better than the behaviour; its central failure is extrapolation error, because maximising a learned value function picks exactly the actions the data never tried. Each method below is a different answer to one of those two failures.

1The setting: a fixed dataset, no interaction

An offline (or batch) learner receives a dataset D = {(si, ai, ri, s′i)} collected by some behaviour policy πβ (one policy, many people, a mixture of old controllers, often unknown) and must output a policy without collecting anything more ({{Levine, Kumar, Tucker and Fu 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01643}}; the older name "batch RL" and fitted Q iteration go back to {{Ernst, Geurts and Wehenkel 2005|https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v6/ernst05a.html}}). Why bother: in medicine, driving, robotics on expensive hardware and recommendation, trying random actions is dangerous, slow or costly, while logs of past decisions are plentiful. And deep learning's successes come from large fixed datasets; offline RL is the attempt to give decision-making the same recipe.

Three distinctions are easy to blur. Off-policy means learning about one policy from data produced by another; Q-learning and DQN are off-policy but still online, because they keep collecting data with their current policy ({{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}}, {{Value-based deep RL|n:3ee5c17b0d0d818689b2c0dbce434ece}}). Offline removes the collection entirely: the policy that would fix its own mistakes by trying them never gets to. And imitation differs from offline RL in what the data must contain: imitation needs good behaviour and no reward; offline RL needs rewards and can use poor behaviour, in principle combining pieces of mediocre trajectories into a better one ("stitching", which D4RL's maze datasets were built to test; {{Fu et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07219}}).

The two failures, in one line each

Imitation: the learner is trained on the expert's states but tested on its own, and a small error moves it to states where it has never been taught, so errors feed on themselves (sections 2 and 3).

Offline RL: the learner's value estimates are only checked by data where the data has actions; maximising over actions finds the places where an unchecked estimate happens to be high (section 7).

2Behaviour cloning, and why its errors compound

Behaviour cloning (BC) is supervised learning from states to the expert's actions: maximise Σi log π(ai | si) over expert pairs, or minimise squared error for continuous actions. It is the oldest method here: ALVINN steered a vehicle from camera images with a small network trained on a person's driving ({{Pomerleau 1988|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/1988/hash/812b4ba287f5ee0bc9d43bbf5bbe87fb-Abstract.html}}). It is also the most used: supervised fine-tuning of a language model on demonstrations is behaviour cloning on tokens ({{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}), and large robot policies are mostly BC with expressive models: RT-1 was trained on about 130,000 demonstration episodes collected by 13 robots over 17 months and performs over 700 training instructions at 97% success ({{Brohan et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06817}}); Diffusion Policy reports an average improvement of 46.9% over prior methods across 12 manipulation tasks by modelling multimodal action distributions ({{Chi et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04137}}).

The problem is that supervised learning assumes test inputs come from the training distribution, and here they do not. The training states come from the expert's state distribution dπ*; at test time the learner visits dπ, the states its own actions lead to. One small error takes it somewhere the expert never went, where it has no data and errs more, which takes it further away. NVIDIA's end-to-end driving paper says it plainly: "Training with data from only the human driver is not sufficient. The network must learn how to recover from mistakes. Otherwise the car will slowly drift off the road" ({{Bojarski et al. 2016, §5|https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07316}}). Their fix was data augmentation: images from left and right cameras, and synthetic shifts and rotations, labelled with the steering that would return to the lane.

J(π) ≤ J(π*) + T² ε
J(π)
expected total cost of the learner over T steps, with each step's cost in [0, 1]
ε
the learner's error rate (0-1 loss) on the expert's own state distribution: what supervised learning controls
T²
the price of distribution shift: a mistake at step t can cost up to every remaining step

That is Ross and Bagnell's bound for behaviour cloning, as restated in {{Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011, Theorem 2.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686}} (first proved in {{Ross and Bagnell 2010|https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/ross10a.html}}), and it is tight: they give a problem where J(π̂) = (1 − εT) J(π*) + T²ε. The intuition is a sum: a first mistake at step t happens with probability about ε and can cost up to T − t afterwards, so the total is about Σt ε (T − t) ≈ εT²/2. A learner whose errors did not change its inputs (ordinary classification) would pay only Tε.

Exact expected cost J of behaviour cloning and of DAgger after 8 rounds (rollouts of length T), against the two lines εT² (Ross and Bagnell's bound, with J* = 0 here) and εT. Behaviour cloning grows like T² while εT is small (from T = 5 to T = 40 its cost rises 44 times for 8 times the horizon), then bends back to slope 1 once a slip is near-certain and almost every remaining step is out of the lane. DAgger stays at about 2εT: each slip costs about two steps out of lane before the correction (Ross et al.'s recovery constant u ≈ 2).

The same failure in language models is called exposure bias: a model trained to predict the next token given the true previous tokens must, at generation time, condition on its own outputs, "as errors may accumulate along the way" ({{Ranzato et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06732}}). On-policy distillation is the DAgger answer: the student generates, the teacher labels the student's own sequences ({{Agarwal et al. 2023, GKD|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649}}). RL fine-tuning after SFT is another, since the model is then trained on its own samples ({{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}).

3DAgger: label the states the learner actually reaches

If the trouble is that training states come from the expert and test states from the learner, train on the learner's states. DAgger (Dataset Aggregation; {{Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011|https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686}}) does this iteratively:

D ← expert demonstrations; π̂₁ ← train on D for i = 1 … N: π_i ← β_i π* + (1 − β_i) π̂_i (β₁ = 1, then usually 0) run π_i, record the states it visits ask the expert for its action at every one of those states D ← D ∪ {(s, π*(s))}; π̂_{i+1} ← train on all of D return the best π̂ on validation

The guarantee changes from quadratic to linear. With enough rounds (N of order T up to log factors), some policy in the sequence has error εN on its own state distribution, the best achievable by the policy class on the aggregated data, and therefore J(π̂) ≤ J(π*) + uTεN + O(1), where u bounds how much worse the cost-to-go gets after one non-expert action (the cost of recovering; Theorems 3.1 and 3.2). The analysis reduces imitation to no-regret online learning: each round is a new loss function (the new states), and training on all data so far is Follow-The-Leader. In Super Tux Kart, behaviour cloning did not improve with more expert laps, "because most of the training laps are all very similar and do not help the learner to learn how to recover from mistakes", while DAgger's policy "never falls off the track after 15 iterations of training" (§5.1).

Its price is the expert. DAgger needs an expert who can be asked "what would you do here?" in states the expert would never have reached, and needs the environment to run the learner. That is cheap for a planner, a search procedure or a stronger model (teacher-student distillation), and awkward for people: labelling while not in control is unnatural, so variants let the human take over only when needed and learn from the takeovers ({{HG-DAgger, Kelly et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02890}}). When neither the expert nor the environment is available again, the remedies are the offline ones: augment the data with perturbed states labelled by recovery actions (NVIDIA's shifted cameras), collect demonstrations with noise injected into the expert's actions so the data shows recoveries ({{DART, Laskey et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.09327}}), or move to methods that reason about the whole trajectory (sections 4 and 5).

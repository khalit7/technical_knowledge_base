4Inverse RL: recover the reward, then plan

Behaviour cloning copies actions one step at a time. Inverse reinforcement learning asks a different question: what reward is the expert optimising? Given that reward, planning or RL produces a policy that judges whole trajectories, so a learner that has drifted still knows where it should be heading. The reward is also often the more transferable thing: it describes the task, while the policy describes one way of doing it in one environment.

The problem is ill-posed. {{Ng and Russell (2000)|https://ai.stanford.edu/~ang/papers/icml00-irl.pdf}} set it up for a known MDP and observed optimal behaviour and noted the degenerate answer first: R = 0, under which every policy is optimal, explains any behaviour. Their fix was a heuristic: among the rewards that make the expert optimal, pick the one that makes the expert's action beat the next-best action by the largest margin, with a penalty favouring simple rewards. Every IRL method since is a choice of which reward to prefer.

  • Feature matching (apprenticeship learning). Assume the reward is linear in known features, R(s) = w·φ(s) with ‖w‖ ≤ 1. A policy's value is then w·μ(π), where μ(π) = E[Σt γt φ(st)] are its feature expectations. If a policy's feature expectations are within ε of the expert's, its value is within ε of the expert's for every such reward, so the true reward need never be identified. The algorithm alternates a max-margin step (find the w under which the expert most beats the policies so far) with an RL step (find the best policy for that w) ({{Abbeel and Ng 2004|https://ai.stanford.edu/~ang/papers/icml04-apprentice.pdf}}). {{Maximum margin planning|https://www.ri.cmu.edu/pub_files/pub4/ratliff_nathan_2006_1/ratliff_nathan_2006_1.pdf}} (Ratliff, Bagnell and Zinkevich 2006) casts the same idea as structured prediction: the expert's path must beat every other path by a margin that grows with how different that path is.
  • Maximum entropy IRL. Matching feature counts still leaves many policies, and many distributions over paths. {{Ziebart et al. (2008)|https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~bziebart/publications/maxentirl-bziebart.pdf}} resolve the ambiguity with the principle of maximum entropy: the distribution over paths that matches the expert's feature counts and is otherwise as uncommitted as possible is P(ζ) ∝ exp(θ·fζ), so better paths are exponentially more likely but every path has some probability. The likelihood gradient is the expert's feature counts minus the model's expected feature counts, which needs a soft planning pass (a soft-max version of value iteration) per gradient step. They learned the route preferences of 25 Yellow Cab drivers from over 100,000 miles of GPS traces. The causal-entropy version handles stochastic dynamics and is the form later deep methods build on.
  • Deep IRL. With neural rewards and unknown dynamics, the planning pass is replaced by sampling with a policy that is itself being optimised: guided cost learning ({{Finn, Levine and Abbeel 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.00448}}), which leads directly to the adversarial methods of the next section.

The cost is an RL problem inside every update, and access to the environment (or a model of it) to solve it. That is why inverse RL is mostly used where the dynamics are known or cheap to simulate (route planning, robot footstep planning, driver modelling), and why it matters here mainly through its descendants: GAIL and learning from preferences.

5GAIL and adversarial imitation

{{Ho and Ermon (2016)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03476}} showed that running RL on the reward found by maximum-causal-entropy IRL is the same as finding the policy whose occupancy measure ρπ(s, a) = π(a|s) Σt γt P(st = s | π), the discounted distribution of state-action pairs it produces, is closest to the expert's, up to an entropy term (their Proposition 3.2; the occupancy measure is the same object as in the linear-programming view of MDPs on {{Dynamic programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81baada7e1c05ce69d01}}). The reward never needs to be written down: matching distributions is a two-sample problem, which a GAN-style discriminator solves. GAIL finds a saddle point of

Eπ[log D(s, a)] + EπE[log(1 − D(s, a))] − λ H(π)

discriminator: trained to tell the policy's pairs (high) from the expert's (low)
π
policy: updated with TRPO steps using log D(s, a) as its cost, so it is pushed toward pairs the discriminator mistakes for expert ones

causal entropy bonus, from the maximum-entropy derivation

With the Jensen-Shannon form of the regulariser, the objective is the JS divergence between the two occupancy measures minus λH(π) (their Equation 15). Because it matches distributions over whole trajectories rather than single actions, GAIL does not compound errors the way behaviour cloning does. In the paper it was "generally quite sample efficient in terms of expert data": on Humanoid, behaviour cloning never exceeded 60% of expert performance while GAIL matched the expert at every dataset size tested; on Reacher, behaviour cloning was the more data-efficient. But GAIL is not offline: its environment interaction "was comparable to the number needed for TRPO to train the expert policies from reinforcement signals", against behaviour cloning's zero (§6 and §7) (the Method atlas on {{Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}} lists GAIL's data as "On-policy (TRPO steps) plus fixed demonstrations").

Successors fix one weakness each. AIRL structures the discriminator so that it recovers a reward that still works when the dynamics change ({{Fu, Luo and Levine 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.11248}}). ValueDICE rewrites the distribution-matching objective so that it can be optimised off-policy, from replay data ({{Kostrikov, Nachum and Tompson 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05032}}). IQ-Learn learns a single soft Q-function from which both the reward and the policy follow, avoiding the adversarial game ({{Garg et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.12142}}).

6Learning from preferences: the bridge to RLHF

Inverse RL infers a reward from what an expert does; preference learning infers it from what a person prefers between two behaviours. Comparisons are easier to give than demonstrations, and possible for tasks the rater cannot perform. {{Christiano et al. (2017)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741}} showed two short video clips (segments σ¹ and σ²) to a non-expert, asked which was better, and fitted a reward model r̂ with the Bradley-Terry model: the probability that σ¹ is preferred is exp Σ r̂ over σ¹ divided by the sum of that and exp Σ r̂ over σ², trained by cross-entropy on the human's choices. The policy is trained by RL on r̂ while more comparisons are collected on its newest behaviour. It solved Atari games and MuJoCo locomotion "while providing feedback on less than one percent of our agent's interactions with the environment", and taught the Hopper to backflip from 900 queries in less than an hour, a behaviour no one had a reward function for.

That recipe, with a language model as the policy, is RLHF: a reward model fitted to human rankings of answers, then PPO against it with a KL penalty to the supervised model ({{InstructGPT|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}). DPO then removed the RL loop by training the policy directly on the preference pairs, which makes it an offline preference method in exactly this page's sense ({{DPO|n:3c65c17b0d0d818bb1d8cafd30e20f9e}}). This page stops at the bridge. The reward model's loss and its failure modes are on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}} (section 2, Reward models), PPO, GRPO and verifiable rewards on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, and what goes wrong when a policy optimises a learned reward too hard on {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}.

Where each LLM training stage sits on this page's map

Supervised fine-tuning is behaviour cloning (section 2), with exposure bias as its compounding error. A reward model fitted to preferences is preference-based reward learning (this section). DPO is offline learning from fixed preference pairs; online RLHF and RLVR are online RL. Rejection sampling and best-of-n fine-tuning (train on the model's own highest-scoring samples) are filtered behaviour cloning, the same idea as the "10% BC" baseline in section 11.

7Offline RL's problem: distributional shift and extrapolation error

Offline RL keeps the rewards and asks for more than imitation: a policy better than the one that collected the data. The natural tool is off-policy Q-learning, which in principle learns the optimal Q from any data. In practice it fails, and the reason is in its target:

Q(s, a) ← r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′)
maxa′
queries Q at actions a′ that may never appear with s′ in the data
Q(s′, a′)
for such actions, whatever the function approximator extrapolates; no sample ever corrects it

Online, a wrongly high value gets tried, disappoints and is corrected. Offline it never gets tried. The max picks out exactly the actions whose errors happen to be positive, bootstrapping copies those values into every state that leads there, and the policy that maximises Q heads for actions nobody has tested. {{Fujimoto, Meger and Precup (2019)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02900}} called this extrapolation error, "introduced by the mismatch between the dataset and true state-action visitation of the current policy", and showed how bad it is: an off-policy actor-critic (DDPG) trained on a million transitions collected by a trained expert "quickly learns to take non-expert actions, under the guise of optimistic extrapolation. As a result, the value estimates rapidly diverge and the agent fails to learn"; even an agent trained on exactly the data of a behavioural agent learning alongside it ("concurrent") did much worse than that agent. {{Kumar et al. (2019)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00949}} named the propagated version bootstrapping error, which "accumulates via the Bellman backup operator". It is overestimation bias ({{Value-based deep RL|n:3ee5c17b0d0d818689b2c0dbce434ece}} has Double DQN's version) in its worst form: online, the noise is around actions that are tried; offline, around actions that never will be.

One dataset, four offline learners, fitted Q iteration run exactly

The task illustrative: one state, an action a anywhere in [−1, 1], reward r(a) = 1 − 2(a − 0.15)², and the next state is the same state, with γ = 0.9, so always taking a is worth r(a)/(1 − γ) (dashed curve; best 10 at a = 0.15). The data: 30 actions from a narrow behaviour policy N(−0.35, 0.12²) (ticks at the bottom) with noisy rewards (noise s.d. 0.1). Q is a ReLU network with its first layer frozen (21 fixed features, ridge 10⁻³), so every fit is solved exactly and the extrapolation outside the data is linear, as a ReLU network's is. Each step is one iteration of fitted Q iteration: targets ri + γ·(value of the next state), refit. The value of the next state is the policy's expected Q, where the policy is ∝ exp(β Q) over all actions (naive, CQL), over actions within two standard deviations of the data's mean (BCQ-style, the shaded band; a fitted Gaussian stands in for BCQ's generative model), or, for IQL, the τ-expectile of Q over the dataset's own actions, with the policy extracted by advantage weights exp(β(Q − V)) on those actions. CQL adds α·((1/β) log Σ exp(β Q) − mean of Q on the data) to every fit: CQL(H), with its soft maximum taken at the policy's temperature so that the distribution pushed down is the policy itself (μ = π, the case of CQL's Theorem 3.2). The shaded area under the axis is the policy. The faint grey curve is the naive learner at the same iteration, drawn to the same scale. Checked against an independent Python implementation (src/recompute.py).

Try: play Naive Q and watch the right edge: the fitted line keeps rising past the data, the policy runs to a = 1, and the believed value climbs past 25 while the true value of what it does is below 0. Then press CQL at the last step: the same data, the values pulled down off the data, the believed value now a lower bound on the true one. Set CQL α to 10: the policy collapses onto the data and both values settle at the behaviour policy's own (5.5 on seed 1): safe, and no better than the data. IQL never looks outside the ticks.

4Inverse RL and GAIL: judge whole trajectories, at the price of an RL loop

Behaviour cloning copies actions one step at a time. Inverse reinforcement learning asks what reward the expert is optimising. Given that reward, planning or RL produces a policy that judges whole trajectories, so a learner that has drifted still knows where it should be heading; the reward is also the more transferable thing, describing the task rather than one way of doing it in one environment.

The problem is ill-posed. {{Ng and Russell (2000)|https://ai.stanford.edu/~ang/papers/icml00-irl.pdf}} set it up for a known MDP and observed optimal behaviour and noted the degenerate answer first: R = 0, under which every policy is optimal, explains any behaviour. Their fix was a heuristic: among the rewards that make the expert optimal, pick the one that makes the expert's action beat the next-best action by the largest margin, with a penalty favouring simple rewards. Every IRL method since is a choice of which reward to prefer, run inside the same loop:

Every method here runs an RL problem inside each reward update; that inner loop is why none of them is offline

The expert's demonstrations are fixed. Each outer step fits a reward (or a discriminator) that separates the expert from the current policy, then improves the policy against it, which needs the environment or a model of it. The methods differ in what is compared: feature expectations (apprenticeship learning), the likelihood of paths under a maximum-entropy model (MaxEnt IRL), or a classifier's output (GAIL).

  • Feature matching ({{Abbeel and Ng 2004|https://ai.stanford.edu/~ang/papers/icml04-apprentice.pdf}}). With a reward linear in known features, R(s) = w·φ(s), a policy's value is w·μ(π), where μ(π) = E[Σt γt φ(st)] are its feature expectations. Match the expert's feature expectations within ε and the value is within ε for every such reward, so the true reward need never be identified.
  • Maximum entropy IRL ({{Ziebart et al. 2008|https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~bziebart/publications/maxentirl-bziebart.pdf}}). Among path distributions that match the expert's feature counts, take the least committed: P(ζ) ∝ exp(θ·fζ), so better paths are exponentially more likely but every path has some probability. They learned the route preferences of 25 Yellow Cab drivers from over 100,000 miles of GPS traces.
  • GAIL ({{Ho and Ermon 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03476}}). RL on the reward found by maximum-causal-entropy IRL is the same as finding the policy whose occupancy measure ρπ(s, a) is closest to the expert's, up to an entropy term (their Proposition 3.2; the same object as the linear-programming view of MDPs on {{Dynamic programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81baada7e1c05ce69d01}}). Matching distributions is a two-sample problem, which a GAN-style discriminator solves, so the reward never needs to be written down.

Because GAIL matches distributions over whole trajectories rather than single actions, it does not compound errors the way behaviour cloning does: on Humanoid, behaviour cloning never exceeded 60% of expert performance while GAIL matched the expert at every dataset size tested; on Reacher, behaviour cloning was the more data-efficient. But GAIL is not offline: its environment interaction "was comparable to the number needed for TRPO to train the expert policies from reinforcement signals", against behaviour cloning's zero (§6 and §7). The cost of the whole family is that inner RL problem, which is why inverse RL is mostly used where dynamics are known or cheap to simulate (route planning, robot footstep planning, driver modelling).

Details of each method, and GAIL's successors 2 min

Apprenticeship learning alternates a max-margin step (find the w with ‖w‖ ≤ 1 under which the expert most beats the policies so far) with an RL step (find the best policy for that w). {{Maximum margin planning|https://www.ri.cmu.edu/pub_files/pub4/ratliff_nathan_2006_1/ratliff_nathan_2006_1.pdf}} (Ratliff, Bagnell and Zinkevich 2006) casts the same idea as structured prediction: the expert's path must beat every other path by a margin that grows with how different that path is.

MaxEnt IRL's likelihood gradient is the expert's feature counts minus the model's expected feature counts, which needs a soft planning pass (a soft-max version of value iteration) per gradient step. The causal-entropy version handles stochastic dynamics and is the form later deep methods build on. Deep IRL replaces the planning pass with sampling from a policy that is itself being optimised: guided cost learning ({{Finn, Levine and Abbeel 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.00448}}), which leads directly to the adversarial methods.

GAIL's objective, with the Jensen-Shannon form of the regulariser, is the JS divergence between the two occupancy measures minus λH(π) (their Equation 15); ρπ(s, a) = π(a|s) Σt γt P(st = s | π). The paper found GAIL "generally quite sample efficient in terms of expert data"; the parent's Method atlas lists its data as "On-policy (TRPO steps) plus fixed demonstrations".

Successors fix one weakness each. AIRL structures the discriminator so that it recovers a reward that still works when the dynamics change ({{Fu, Luo and Levine 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.11248}}). ValueDICE rewrites the distribution-matching objective so that it can be optimised off-policy, from replay data ({{Kostrikov, Nachum and Tompson 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.05032}}). IQ-Learn learns a single soft Q-function from which both the reward and the policy follow, avoiding the adversarial game ({{Garg et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.12142}}).

5Learning from preferences: the bridge to RLHF

Inverse RL infers a reward from what an expert does; preference learning infers it from what a person prefers between two behaviours, which is easier to give and possible for tasks the rater cannot perform. {{Christiano et al. (2017)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741}} showed a non-expert two short clips, asked which was better, fitted a reward model with the Bradley-Terry model, and trained the policy by RL on it while collecting more comparisons on its newest behaviour. It solved Atari games and MuJoCo locomotion "while providing feedback on less than one percent of our agent's interactions with the environment", and taught the Hopper to backflip from 900 queries in less than an hour, a behaviour no one had a reward function for.

That recipe with a language model as the policy is RLHF ({{InstructGPT|n:3c65c17b0d0d8180b958d8299996a063}}); DPO removed the RL loop by training on the preference pairs directly, which makes it an offline preference method in exactly this page's sense ({{DPO|n:3c65c17b0d0d818bb1d8cafd30e20f9e}}). This page stops at the bridge: the reward model's loss and its failure modes are on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}} (section 2, Reward models), PPO, GRPO and verifiable rewards on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, and over-optimising a learned reward on {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}.

12Offline to online: start from the data, then interact

In practice the purest offline setting is rare; more often a dataset exists and some interaction is allowed later. The natural recipe, pretrain offline then fine-tune online, is less natural than it looks. {{Nakamoto et al. (2023)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05479}} note the "counter-intuitive trend: policy initializations obtained from more effective offline RL methods tend to exhibit worse online fine-tuning performance", and identify an unlearning dip: a conservative Q-function's values are too low, so the first online updates spend themselves raising them and the policy gets worse before it gets better. Cal-QL keeps CQL's lower bound on the learned policy but stops values from falling below a reference policy's (the behaviour policy's) value: "calibrated", "a one-line code change" to CQL, better than prior methods on 9 of 11 fine-tuning tasks.

The opposite route also works: run an ordinary online off-policy method with the offline data mixed in. RLPD ({{Ball, Smith, Kostrikov and Levine 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02948}}) trains SAC from scratch with "symmetric sampling", each batch half from the offline data and half from the online replay buffer, plus layer normalisation in the critic (which "can bound the extrapolation of networks" without constraining the policy to the data) and large ensembles, reporting "a 2.5× improvement over existing approaches" with no offline pretraining at all. In CORL's reruns on AntMaze (6 datasets, 1 million offline then 1 million online steps), the average normalised score moved from 48.4 to 95.6 for CQL, 56.3 to 78.5 for IQL, 53.0 to 97.3 for Cal-QL and 18.1 to 16.5 for AWAC ({{Tarasov et al., Table 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.07105}}; per-dataset values in the {{D4RL results|#t-d4rl}} tab).

13Evaluation: D4RL, its pitfalls, and off-policy evaluation

D4RL ({{Fu et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.07219}}) is the benchmark nearly every paper above reports. Its datasets were built to probe specific difficulties: Gym-MuJoCo locomotion with "medium" (1 million samples from a SAC policy stopped early), "medium-replay" (the replay buffer up to that point) and "medium-expert" (medium and expert data mixed) for narrow and mixed data; Maze2D and AntMaze for stitching undirected data with sparse rewards; Adroit's 24-degree-of-freedom hand with a small number of human demonstrations; Franka Kitchen for multitask data. Scores are normalised so that 0 is a uniformly random policy and 100 a domain expert (a SAC agent on Gym-MuJoCo):

normalised score = 100 × (score − random score) / (expert score − random score)

The {{D4RL results|#t-d4rl}} tab shows five printed tables as they are. Reading them needs care, because several things that look like the same number are not:

  • Dataset versions. The Gym-MuJoCo datasets were reissued as v2 in December 2020, which "fixed Hopper's performance and the qpos/qvel fields" ({{D4RL README|https://github.com/Farama-Foundation/D4RL}}). The IQL paper points out that the Decision Transformer and one-step papers "incorrectly report results for some prior methods, such as CQL, using the '-v0' environments", which "generally produce lower scores than the '-v2' environments that these papers use for their own methods" (§5.2). CQL on halfcheetah-medium-expert is 62.4 in its own paper (v0) and 91.6 in IQL's table (v2). RvS's AntMaze table, labelled v2, includes CQL, TD3+BC and one-step numbers from v0, flagged by an asterisk.
  • Last policy or best policy. CORL reports both: the score of the policy at the end of training and the best score seen during training. Across Gym-MuJoCo, BC averages 50.4 last and 70.2 best; CQL on AntMaze 50.7 last and 61.8 best (Tables 1 and 2). "Best during training" was found by evaluating in the environment, which an offline method cannot do.
  • Hyperparameters chosen online. The same applies to tuning: "existing hyperparameter selection methods for offline RL break the offline assumption by evaluating policies corresponding to each hyperparameter setting in the environment" ({{Paine et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.09055}}), and offline RL algorithms "are not robust to hyperparameter choices".
  • Few seeds, and two printings of the same run. Most tables average 3 to 5 seeds. The CQL paper's Table 1 and the D4RL paper's Table 2 both print CQL on the same v0 datasets, and disagree: CQL is 111.0 on hopper-medium-expert and 98.7 on walker2d-medium-expert in the CQL paper, and the reverse in D4RL's; the CQL paper's walker2d-medium-expert row also repeats the baselines of its walker2d-mixed row. Which printing is right is unconfirmed; the tab shows both.
  • Saturation and succession. On Gym-MuJoCo the best averages in CORL's reruns are 89.7 (ReBRAC) and 92.9 (EDAC) on a scale where 100 is the reference SAC agent; the hard remaining suites are AntMaze and Adroit. D4RL itself is no longer maintained: its environments moved to Gymnasium and its datasets to {{Minari|https://minari.farama.org/}}.

Off-policy evaluation (OPE) is the offline answer to "how good is this policy?", needed for model selection and for deployment decisions. Importance sampling reweights logged returns by the product of π/πβ along each trajectory: unbiased, with variance that grows exponentially with the horizon (the off-policy Monte Carlo section of {{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}} shows the blow-up). Doubly robust estimators add a learned value model as a control variate, "guaranteed to be unbiased and can have a much lower variance" ({{Jiang and Li 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.03722}}). Fitted Q evaluation (FQE) runs fitted Q iteration for the fixed policy and reads off its value ({{Le, Voloshin and Yue 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.08738}}); it is biased by the same extrapolation as section 7 when the policy leaves the data. A benchmark of these estimators on D4RL-style tasks is {{DOPE (Fu et al. 2021)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.16596}}; Paine et al. found that, controlled carefully, offline estimates "can reliably rank policies across hyperparameter choices". The healthcare literature shows what goes wrong when they are not: see the next section.

14Where it is used, and its limits

  • Robotics. QT-Opt learned closed-loop grasping from "over 580k real-world grasp attempts" of logged off-policy data; "even fully off-policy training can outperform strong baselines", and about 28,000 additional on-policy grasps raised success to 96% on unseen objects ({{Kalashnikov et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.10293}}). Q-Transformer trains offline on demonstrations plus autonomously collected data across a multi-task real-robot suite ({{Chebotar et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10150}}). Most deployed robot policies, though, are behaviour cloning at scale (RT-1, Diffusion Policy; section 2), because demonstrations are easier to collect than reward labels.
  • Recommendation. YouTube scaled REINFORCE to "an action space on the orders of millions" learning from logged user feedback, with an off-policy correction for the fact that the logs only show items earlier recommenders chose, and a top-K version of the correction for recommending several items at once; it was tested in live experiments ({{Chen et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.02353}}). Learning from logs is the norm in recommendation, usually as contextual bandits ({{Bandits and exploration|n:3ee5c17b0d0d81acba54d70ac51c601c}}).
  • Healthcare is where the setting fits best and evaluation is hardest. The AI Clinician learned vasopressor and fluid dosing for sepsis from intensive-care records and reported that "mortality was lowest in patients for whom clinicians' actual doses matched the AI decisions" ({{Komorowski et al. 2018|https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-018-0213-5}}). A reproduction found that, under the same importance-sampling evaluation, a policy giving no drugs at all often scored better than the clinicians ({{Jeter et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.03271}}), and methodological work showed how the summary of a patient's history, the variance of the estimators and confounders "can result in unreliable, even misleading estimates of the quality of a treatment policy" ({{Gottesman et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.12298}}; guidelines in {{Nature Medicine 2019|https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-018-0310-5}}).
  • Language models. SFT is behaviour cloning; DPO is offline preference learning (section 6); value-based offline RL has been applied to text generation ({{ILQL, Snell et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11871}}). Most current post-training is online RL on the model's own samples ({{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}), partly because sampling a language model is cheap and its "environment" (a verifier or reward model) is free to query.
Limits that no algorithm removes

Coverage: no method can learn the value of something the data never does; offline RL can only recombine and reweight what is there, and the best policy within the data's support (dashed band in the animation) is the ceiling. Confounding: if the people who produced the data acted on information the data does not record (a clinician's bedside impression), the logged actions are correlated with outcomes for reasons the learner cannot see, and every estimate inherits the bias. Evaluation: without interaction, the policy's quality is itself an estimate, with the problems of section 13. Non-stationarity: the world that produced the logs may not be the one the policy is deployed in, and deploying the policy changes it.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Off-policy means offline."
Off-policy is about which policy the values describe; offline is about whether the agent may collect data. DQN and SAC are off-policy and online; naive off-policy algorithms fail offline for the reason in section 7, even on expert data (Fujimoto et al.'s "imitation" batch).
"More expert demonstrations will fix behaviour cloning."
More data from the expert's state distribution lowers ε but does not cover the states the learner drifts into; Ross et al.'s Super Tux Kart learner did not improve with more expert laps. Coverage of recovery states (DAgger, noise injection, augmentation) is what helps.
"Offline RL beats behaviour cloning."
Not on clean expert data, and not always on D4RL: in CORL's reruns 10% BC averages 69.3 on Gym-MuJoCo against CQL's 78.3, and BC beats both on pen-human (71.0 against −3.9 for TD3+BC and 13.7 for CQL). Offline RL wins when rewards are sparse, data is noisy or must be stitched (Kumar et al. 2022).
"A conservative value is a pessimistic estimate of every action."
CQL's practical form (Equation 2) bounds the policy's value, not every Q-value: actions that are frequent in the data can be overestimated. Only the push-down-only form (Equation 1) is a pointwise lower bound.
Comparing D4RL scores across papers
Check the dataset version (v0 against v2), last against best checkpoint, the number of seeds, and whether hyperparameters were chosen by online evaluation. Compare within one table where possible, as the D4RL results tab does.
Asking a Decision Transformer for a return far above the data
Return conditioning interpolates among returns the data reached; asking for more does not make the policy better, and in stochastic environments the returns it was trained on may reflect luck rather than skill.
"GAIL is offline imitation."
GAIL needs on-policy rollouts in the environment, comparable in number to training the expert with RL. Its demonstrations are fixed; its own data is not.
Trusting an importance-sampled value of a learned treatment policy
With long horizons and a deterministic learned policy, few logged trajectories match it, the weights explode, and the estimate rests on a handful of patients. Report the effective sample size and use several estimators.

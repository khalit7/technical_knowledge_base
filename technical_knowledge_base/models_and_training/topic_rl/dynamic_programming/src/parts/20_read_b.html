3Value functions and the Bellman equations

The value is the expected return, which removes the randomness of single episodes. In an MRP, V(s) = 𝔼[Gt | St = s]. In an MDP the return depends on how you act, so values are defined for a policy π, and the two kinds are one step apart:

Vπ(s) = 𝔼π[Gt | St = s] = Σa π(a | s) Qπ(s, a),    Qπ(s, a) = 𝔼π[Gt | St = s, At = a] = R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Vπ(s′)
Vπ(s)
the state value: expected return from s following π; it averages the action values under the policy
Qπ(s, a)
the action value: take a in s, then follow π. Its right-hand side is the one-step lookahead, the step that needs the model, P and R
𝔼π
an expectation over the randomness of both the policy and the environment

That asymmetry is why Q is what you want for model-free control: acting greedily on Q is argmaxa Q(s, a), a lookup, while acting greedily on V needs the lookahead and so the dynamics (a model-free agent that stores only V cannot pick actions from it). DP has the model, so it works with V and computes Q when it needs it. Optimal values: V*(s) = maxπ Vπ(s) and Q*(s, a) = maxπ Qπ(s, a). An MDP is solved once Q* is known: picking argmaxa Q*(s, a) everywhere is optimal. For any finite MDP an optimal policy π* is at least as good as every other policy in every state, all optimal policies share V* and Q*, and there is always a deterministic one ({{Silver, Lecture 2|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-2-mdp.pdf}}).

The Bellman equation cuts a long-horizon problem into one-step pieces, and follows from the return's own recursion: V(s) = 𝔼[Rt+1 + γGt+1 | St = s] = 𝔼[Rt+1 + γV(St+1) | St = s], where the last step replaces the random tail by its expectation, which the Markov property allows. The value of a state is the immediate reward plus the discounted value of the successor. For a known MRP it reads V(s) = Rs + γ Σs′ Pss′ V(s′). Four value functions give four equations:

Expectation (fixed π):   Vπ(s) = Σa π(a | s) [ R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Vπ(s′) ]
Qπ(s, a) = R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Σa′ π(a′ | s′) Qπ(s′, a′)
Optimality:   V*(s) = maxa [ R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) V*(s′) ]
Q*(s, a) = R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) maxa′ Q*(s′, a′)
s, a, s′, a′
the current state, an action, a possible next state, the action taken there
bracket
the one-step lookahead value of action a

The expectation equations are linear: one equation per state, as many unknowns as states, so Vπ = Rπ + γPπVπ has the direct solution Vπ = (I − γPπ)−1 Rπ, with column vectors over states and Pπ the MRP's matrix from section 1. Inversion costs O(n³) for n states, so it is only for small problems. The max makes the optimality equations nonlinear: matrix inversion does not solve them, there is no closed form in general, and they are solved iteratively, by value and policy iteration when the model is known (this page) and from samples by Q-learning and SARSA when it is not ({{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}}). Everything downstream, from value iteration to DQN's TD target to the value model in PPO-based RLHF, is a way of approximately solving one of these equations; the DP algorithms are "little more than these equations turned into assignment statements" ({{Sutton and Barto §4.8|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=110}}).

Prediction and control are the two problems these equations pose. Prediction: given a fixed π, compute Vπ or Qπ. Control: find π*, equivalently V* or Q*. You usually solve prediction to solve control: evaluate, improve greedily, repeat. With a model, prediction is policy evaluation (section 4) and control is policy iteration (section 6) or value iteration (section 7).

4Backups, sweeps and policy evaluation

Four words recur ({{Sutton and Barto §4.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=96}}). A backup (Sutton and Barto: an expected update) is one application of the right-hand side of a Bellman equation to one state, as an assignment; "expected" because it averages over every possible successor with its probability rather than one sampled successor, which is what a full-width backup means and why it needs the model. A sweep backs up every state once. A synchronous sweep computes every new value from the previous sweep's values, keeping two arrays (Jacobi-style, after Jacobi's method for linear systems). An in-place sweep overwrites as it goes, so later states already see this sweep's new values (Gauss–Seidel-style): one array, usually faster, and then the order in which states are swept matters (section 8); Sutton and Barto "usually have the in-place version in mind" ({{chapter 4 notes|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=112}}).

Policy evaluation estimates Vπ of a given, fixed policy by turning the expectation equation into that assignment:

Vk+1(s) = Σa π(a | s) [ R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Vk(s′) ]
Vk
the estimate after k sweeps. Start from any guess, usually zeros, with terminal states at 0; sweep until the largest change, maxs |Vk+1(s) − Vk(s)|, falls below a small threshold θ

Why it converges. Vπ is a fixed point of the update and the backup is a γ-contraction (section 9), so the iteration reaches the unique Vπ from any start. Sutton and Barto's condition is wider: Vπ exists, is unique and is the limit "as long as either γ < 1 or eventual termination is guaranteed from all states under the policy π" ({{§4.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=96}}). Or solve it directly: (I − γPπ)−1Rπ is one linear solve of order n³. Iterative evaluation is "a classical successive approximation algorithm for solving a system of linear equations" ({{Sutton and Barto, chapter 4 notes|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=111}}); it wins when n is large, when transitions are sparse (a sweep then costs about n times the number of successors), and when a good starting guess exists, as inside policy iteration.

The standard example is Sutton and Barto's 4 × 4 gridworld (Example 4.1): two shaded corners end the episode, every move costs −1, a move off the grid leaves you in place, γ = 1, and the policy picks each move with probability ¼, so Vπ is minus the expected number of steps to a corner. The {{Sweep lab|#t-lab}} opens on it, and the greedy policy on its values is optimal from k = 3 on, as Figure 4.1's caption says ({{book p. 77|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=99}}), long before the values converge.

5Policy improvement and its theorem

Once you know Vπ, act greedily on it: compute every action's lookahead value and pick the best, π′(s) = argmaxa [ R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Vπ(s′) ] = argmaxa Qπ(s, a), a new deterministic policy. The student MDP did exactly this in section 3 and got the optimal policy in one step.

The policy improvement theorem ({{Sutton and Barto §4.2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=100}}): if deterministic π and π′ satisfy Qπ(s, π′(s)) ≥ Vπ(s) in every state, then Vπ′(s) ≥ Vπ(s) in every state, strictly wherever the premise is strict. In words: taking the better action once and then following π is at least as good as following π, and doing so at every later step only adds improvement. The greedy policy satisfies the premise automatically (the best action's value is at least the policy-weighted average, which is Vπ(s)). And if greedy improvement changes nothing, then Vπ(s) = maxa Qπ(s, a) everywhere, which is the Bellman optimality equation: π is already optimal. So improvement "must give us a strictly better policy except when the original policy is already optimal".

The proof in one line, and ties (depth, 1 min)

Keep expanding the Q side and reapplying the premise:

Vπ(s) ≤ Qπ(s, π′(s)) = 𝔼π′[Rt+1 + γVπ(St+1) | St = s] ≤ 𝔼π′[Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ²Vπ(St+2) | St = s] ≤ ... ≤ 𝔼π′[Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ²Rt+3 + ... | St = s] = Vπ′(s)

Stochastic policies and ties. The theorem carries over to stochastic policies. When several actions tie for the max, the new policy may split its probability among them in any way, as long as every non-maximising action gets zero; Figure 4.1's right column draws an arrow for every tied action for this reason.

6Policy iteration: control with an explicit policy

Alternate the two steps until the policy stops changing: evaluate the current policy (sweeps until the values settle, or the linear solve), then improve it greedily. The chain π0 → Vπ0 → π1 → Vπ1 → ... → π* → V* has each policy strictly better than the last unless already optimal ({{Sutton and Barto §4.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=102}}). Start each evaluation from the previous policy's values: this "typically results in a great increase in the speed of convergence of policy evaluation (presumably because the value function changes little from one policy to the next)".

Why it terminates. A finite MDP has mn deterministic policies for m actions and n states; each improvement is at least as good everywhere and strictly better somewhere unless optimal, so no policy repeats and the loop ends at a policy that satisfies the optimality equation. In practice it needs very few outer iterations, because the policy changes in whole jumps and often becomes optimal long before its values converge: on Figure 4.1's gridworld it "would find the optimal policy after just one iteration"; on Jack's car rental (Example 4.2: two locations with up to 20 cars each, Poisson rentals and returns, γ = 0.9) π0, ..., π4 reach the optimum in four improvements (Figure 4.2). On the {{Sweep lab|#t-lab}}'s worlds it stops after 2 improvements on both 4 × 4 grids, 3 on the Dyna maze and 5 on Russell and Norvig's 4 × 3 world.

The tie bug
As written, the stopping test "the policy did not change" may never fire if ties in the argmax are broken arbitrarily: the policy can flip forever between equally good actions while the values stay put. Sutton and Barto leave this as Exercise 4.4 ("a subtle bug ... okay for pedagogy, but not for actual use"; {{Exercise 4.4|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=104}}). Fix: keep the current action unless another is strictly better, or stop when the values stop changing. The Sweep lab keeps the current action among ties.

Evaluation does not have to converge. Stopping evaluation after k sweeps and improving anyway still reaches the optimal policy: modified policy iteration (Puterman and Shin 1978), which contains policy iteration (k → ∞) and value iteration (k = 1) as special cases. In Silver's 4 × 4 gridworld (Figure 4.1's) k = 3 evaluation sweeps were already enough for the greedy policy to be optimal ({{Silver, Lecture 3|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-3-planning-by-dynamic-programming-.pdf}}). One catch, found while building the old version of this page: with truncated evaluation, "the policy did not change" no longer proves optimality, because the values the policy was greedy on were not yet its own. Stop only when the policy is unchanged and the values have stopped changing.

Generalised policy iteration (GPI) is the general pattern: any interleaving of some evaluation with some improvement, at any granularity, down to a single state. "As long as both processes continue to update all states, the ultimate result is typically the same, convergence to the optimal value function and an optimal policy" ({{Sutton and Barto §4.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=108}}). The two compete (making the policy greedy makes the values wrong for it; making the values right makes the policy no longer greedy) and cooperate: both stabilise only when the policy is greedy on its own value function, which is the optimality equation. "Almost all reinforcement learning methods are well described as GPI", PPO included (section 13).

5Backups, sweeps, synchronous and in place

Four words recur below ({{Sutton and Barto §4.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=96}}):

  • A backup (Sutton and Barto: an expected update) is one application of the right-hand side of a Bellman equation to one state, as an assignment. "Expected" because it averages over every possible successor with its probability, rather than one sampled successor: that is what a full-width backup means, and why it needs the model.
  • A sweep backs up every state once.
  • A synchronous sweep computes every new value from the previous sweep's values, so it keeps two arrays (old and new). It is also called Jacobi-style, after Jacobi's classical method for linear systems.
  • An in-place sweep overwrites values as it goes, so states later in the sweep already see this sweep's new values (Gauss–Seidel-style). It needs one array, usually converges faster, and then the order in which states are swept matters; Sutton and Barto "usually have the in-place version in mind" ({{chapter 4 notes|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=112}}). On the racetrack below (section 14), Gauss–Seidel value iteration was about twice as fast as the Jacobi version.

6Policy evaluation: the prediction problem

Estimate Vπ, the value function of a given, fixed policy, by turning the Bellman expectation equation into an update rule:

Vk+1(s) = Σa π(a | s) [ R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Vk(s′) ]
Vk
the estimate after k sweeps; Vk+1 the estimate after one more; every other symbol as in section 3
  1. Start with an arbitrary value function, usually all zeros. Terminal states have value 0 and stay there.
  2. For every state, look one step ahead: for each action the policy might take, the immediate reward plus the discounted value of each possible successor, weighted by its probability.
  3. Set the state's new value to the policy-weighted average of those lookahead values.
  4. Repeat the sweep until the largest change in any state, maxs |Vk+1(s) − Vk(s)|, falls below a small threshold θ.

Why it converges. Vπ is a fixed point of the update, and the backup is a γ-contraction (section 11), so the iteration converges to the unique Vπ from any start. Sutton and Barto's condition is wider: Vπ exists, is unique and is the limit "as long as either γ < 1 or eventual termination is guaranteed from all states under the policy π" ({{§4.1|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=96}}). With γ = 1 the contraction argument does not apply, and convergence needs every state to reach a terminal state eventually under π.

Or solve it directly. Because the equations are linear (section 3), Vπ = (I − γPπ)−1Rπ is one linear solve, of the order of n³ operations. Iterative policy evaluation is "a classical successive approximation algorithm for solving a system of linear equations" ({{Sutton and Barto, chapter 4 notes|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=111}}); it wins when n is large, when transitions are sparse (each sweep then costs about n times the number of successors), and when a good starting guess exists, as it does inside policy iteration.

The standard example is Sutton and Barto's 4 × 4 gridworld (Example 4.1): two shaded corners end the episode, every move costs −1, a move off the grid leaves you in place, γ = 1, and the policy picks each of the four moves with probability ¼. Its Vπ is minus the expected number of steps to a corner. The {{Sweep lab|#t-lab}} runs it sweep by sweep: the computed values reproduce Figure 4.1 independently, 92 of the 96 printed values at two significant digits. The other four are the exact value −1.75 at k = 2, printed −1.7 while the same figure prints −2.875 as −2.9: a rounding slip in the figure, not a different computation. Figure 4.1's numbers also require synchronous sweeps; an in-place sweep gives different intermediate values. The greedy policy on those values is optimal from k = 3 on, as the figure's caption says ({{book p. 77|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=99}}).

7Policy improvement and its theorem

Once you know Vπ, you can do better than π by acting greedily with respect to it. Compute the lookahead value Qπ(s, a) of every action (section 3) and pick the best:

π′(s) = argmaxa [ R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Vπ(s′) ] = argmaxa Qπ(s, a)
π′
the new, deterministic policy
argmaxa
returns the action with the largest bracket

The policy improvement theorem ({{Sutton and Barto §4.2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=100}}): if deterministic policies π and π′ satisfy Qπ(s, π′(s)) ≥ Vπ(s) in every state, then Vπ′(s) ≥ Vπ(s) in every state, strictly wherever the premise is strict. The proof keeps expanding the Q side and reapplying the premise:

Vπ(s) ≤ Qπ(s, π′(s)) = 𝔼π′[Rt+1 + γVπ(St+1) | St = s] ≤ 𝔼π′[Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ²Vπ(St+2) | St = s] ≤ ... ≤ 𝔼π′[Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ²Rt+3 + ... | St = s] = Vπ′(s)

In words: taking the better action once and then following π is at least as good as following π, and doing the same at every later step only adds improvement. The greedy policy satisfies the premise automatically, because the best action's value is at least the policy-weighted average of all actions' values, which is Vπ(s). And if greedy improvement changes nothing, then Vπ(s) = maxa Qπ(s, a) in every state, which is exactly the Bellman optimality equation: π is already optimal. So policy improvement "must give us a strictly better policy except when the original policy is already optimal".

Stochastic policies and ties. The theorem carries over to stochastic policies. When several actions tie for the max, the new policy may split its probability among them in any way, as long as every non-maximising action gets zero. Figure 4.1's right column draws an arrow for every tied action for this reason. The student MDP in section 3 shows a full improvement in one step: greedy on the uniform policy's Qπ is already optimal.

8Policy iteration: control with an explicit policy

Alternate the two steps until the policy stops changing: evaluate the current policy, then improve it greedily. This yields a chain π0 → Vπ0 → π1 → Vπ1 → ... → π* → V*, each policy strictly better than the last unless already optimal ({{Sutton and Barto §4.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=102}}).

  1. Start with an arbitrary policy, for example uniformly random, and arbitrary values.
  2. Evaluate: run policy evaluation sweeps for the current policy until the values settle, or solve the linear system directly. Start each evaluation from the previous policy's values: this "typically results in a great increase in the speed of convergence of policy evaluation (presumably because the value function changes little from one policy to the next)".
  3. Improve: replace the policy with the greedy policy on those values.
  4. If the policy did not change, stop: it is optimal. Otherwise go back to step 2.

Why it terminates. A finite MDP has finitely many deterministic policies, mn for m actions and n states. Each improvement gives a policy at least as good everywhere and strictly better somewhere unless already optimal, so no policy repeats, and the loop must end at a policy satisfying the Bellman optimality equation. In practice it needs very few outer iterations, because the policy changes in whole jumps and often becomes optimal long before its values have converged: on Figure 4.1's gridworld it "would find the optimal policy after just one iteration", and on Jack's car rental (Example 4.2, two locations with up to 20 cars each, Poisson rentals and returns, γ = 0.9) the sequence π0, ..., π4 reaches the optimum in four improvements (Figure 4.2). On the {{Sweep lab|#t-lab}}'s worlds it stops after 2 improvements on both 4 × 4 grids, 3 on the Dyna maze and 5 on Russell and Norvig's 4 × 3 world.

The tie bug
As written, the stopping test "the policy did not change" may never fire if ties in the argmax are broken arbitrarily: the policy can flip forever between equally good actions while the values stay put. Sutton and Barto leave this as Exercise 4.4 ("a subtle bug ... okay for pedagogy, but not for actual use"). Fix: keep the current action unless another is strictly better, or stop when the values stop changing. The Sweep lab keeps the current action among ties.

Evaluation does not have to converge. Stopping evaluation after k sweeps and improving anyway still converges to the optimal policy. This is modified policy iteration (Puterman and Shin 1978), which contains policy iteration (k → ∞) and value iteration (k = 1) as special cases. In Silver's 4 × 4 gridworld (Figure 4.1's) k = 3 evaluation sweeps were already enough for the greedy policy to be optimal ({{Silver, Lecture 3|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-3-planning-by-dynamic-programming-.pdf}}). One catch, found while building the old version of this page: with truncated evaluation, "the policy did not change" no longer proves optimality, because the values the policy was greedy on were not yet its own. Stop only when the policy is unchanged and the values have stopped changing.

Generalised policy iteration (GPI) is the general pattern: any interleaving of some evaluation with some improvement, at any granularity, down to a single state. "As long as both processes continue to update all states, the ultimate result is typically the same, convergence to the optimal value function and an optimal policy" ({{Sutton and Barto §4.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=108}}). The two processes compete (making the policy greedy makes the values wrong for it; making the values right makes the policy no longer greedy) and cooperate: both stabilise only when the policy is greedy with respect to its own value function, which is the Bellman optimality equation. "Almost all reinforcement learning methods are well described as GPI" (section 16).

9Value iteration: control with no explicit policy

Turn the Bellman optimality equation itself into the update, with no policy stored anywhere:

Vk+1(s) = maxa [ R(s, a) + γ Σs′ P(s′ | s, a) Vk(s′) ]
maxa
over every action available in s; Vk is the estimate after k sweeps
  1. Start with an arbitrary value function, usually all zeros.
  2. For every state, compute the lookahead value of every action and keep the best.
  3. Repeat until the largest change in a sweep falls below θ.
  4. Read off the policy once at the end, greedily: π(s) = argmaxa of the same bracket.

It looks like policy evaluation with one crucial difference: no policy is being followed; each sweep takes a max over all actions rather than an average under some π. Equivalently, each sweep is one evaluation sweep of the policy that is greedy on the current values, so value iteration is policy iteration with evaluation cut to a single sweep ({{Sutton and Barto §4.4|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=104}}). Silver's intuition: "start with final rewards and work backwards"; it "still works with loopy, stochastic MDPs". A consequence: the intermediate value functions need not be the value of any policy; only the limit is, and it is the optimal policy's ({{Silver, Lecture 3|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-3-planning-by-dynamic-programming-.pdf}}). Silver's shortest-path slide shows it: a 4 × 4 grid with one goal in the corner, −1 per step; synchronous value iteration from zeros gives V2 = −1 everywhere but the goal, V3 = −2 in most cells, and only V7 holds the true distances, 0 to −6 (reproduced exactly in the {{Sweep lab|#t-lab}}).

Formally value iteration converges only in the limit, but the greedy policy becomes optimal after finitely many sweeps (Bertsekas 1987, cited in {{Sutton and Barto's notes|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=111}}). Between the two extremes, "faster convergence is often achieved by interposing multiple policy evaluation sweeps between each policy improvement sweep" (§4.4): modified policy iteration again.

A worked showcase: the gambler's problem (Sutton and Barto Example 4.3). A gambler with capital 1 to 99 stakes any whole number of dollars up to what he has (and what he needs to reach 100) on a coin that comes up heads with probability ph; reaching 100 pays +1, everything else 0, γ = 1, so the value is the probability of winning. With ph = 0.4, value iteration's optimal policy is famously curious: at capital 50 bet everything, at 51 bet a little. It is optimal but not unique; a whole family of optimal policies corresponds to ties in the argmax. The {{Sweep lab|#t-lab}} runs it live for any ph.

10Worked example: a three-state corridor

Three states in a row, A, B and C, with a terminal goal G to the right of C. Two actions, left and right, both deterministic. Moving right from C enters G, earns a reward of 10 and ends the episode; every other move earns 0; moving left from A bumps into the wall and stays in A. The discount is γ = 0.9 and V(G) = 0 always.

Value iteration, synchronous, from all zeros. The backups are V(A) ← max(0.9 V(A), 0.9 V(B)), V(B) ← max(0.9 V(A), 0.9 V(C)) and V(C) ← max(0.9 V(B), 10 + 0.9 × 0).

SweepV(A)V(B)V(C)What happened
0000Initial guess
10010C: right gives 10 + 0.9 × 0 = 10, left gives 0.9 × 0 = 0

38.1910A: right gives 0.9 × 9 = 8.1
48.1910No change: converged

So V* = (8.1, 9, 10) and the greedy policy is right in every state. The reward travelled back one state per sweep, which is the general picture: after k sweeps, synchronous value iteration has only seen rewards up to k steps away. An in-place sweep in the order C, B, A converges in a single sweep instead (C becomes 10, then B sees the new C and becomes 9, then A becomes 8.1), which is why sweep order matters for in-place DP; in the order A, B, C it is no faster than synchronous here.

Policy iteration, from the uniformly random policy (left and right with probability 0.5 each). Evaluation backups: V(A) ← 0.45 V(A) + 0.45 V(B), V(B) ← 0.45 V(A) + 0.45 V(C) and V(C) ← 0.45 V(B) + 5, where 0.45 is 0.5 × 0.9 and 5 is 0.5 × 10. From zeros the sweeps give (0, 0, 5), then (0, 2.25, 5), then (1.0125, 2.25, 6.0125), then (1.4681, 3.1612, 6.0125), converging to the exact solution of the three linear equations, Vπ ≈ (4.29, 5.24, 7.36). Improvement on those values compares the lookahead value of each action: in A, left is 0.9 × 4.29 = 3.86 and right 0.9 × 5.24 = 4.72; in B, left 3.86 and right 0.9 × 7.36 = 6.62; in C, left 0.9 × 5.24 = 4.72 and right 10. Right wins everywhere, so one improvement already gives the optimal policy. Evaluating it gives (8.1, 9, 10), the next improvement changes nothing, and policy iteration stops after two outer iterations.

Evaluation did not need to converge: after only three sweeps, with values (1.0125, 2.25, 6.0125), the greedy comparison is already right everywhere (in A, right is 0.9 × 2.25 = 2.025 against left 0.9 × 1.0125 = 0.91; in B, 5.41 against 0.91; in C, 10 against 2.025). That is modified policy iteration paying off.

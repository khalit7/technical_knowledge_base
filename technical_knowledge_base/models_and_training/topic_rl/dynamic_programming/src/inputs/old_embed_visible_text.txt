What it is and why it matters

Dynamic programming (DP) in reinforcement learning computes value functions and optimal policies when the full model of the environment is known: the transition probabilities P(s′ | s, a) and the expected reward R(s, a) for every state and action. Nothing is learned from interaction; the algorithms compute. That makes DP model-based planning, not learning.

The idea is Bellman's: a long-horizon problem breaks into one-step pieces. A state's value is the reward now plus the discounted value of where you land. Start from a guess everywhere and apply that one-line computation, a backup, to every state, again and again. Each sweep pushes reward information one step further back, and the guesses converge to the true values.

One backup: look at every action, every successor it can lead to, weight by probability, and combine. DP does this for every state in every sweep, using the known model.
Why it matters, 1: the ideal
Monte Carlo, TD, SARSA and Q-learning replace DP's exact expectation over all successors with one sampled successor. DP is what they approximate.
Why it matters, 2: the template
A TD target, a DQN target and PPO's value target are all reward plus discounted value of the next state: a DP backup.

Two problems: prediction (how good is a given policy? policy evaluation) and control (find the best policy: policy iteration and value iteration, both iterative solutions of the Bellman optimality equation). Their model-free counterparts, SARSA and Q-learning, are on Model-free methods.

Definitions

An MDP (Markov decision process) is (𝒮, 𝒜, P, R, γ): states, actions, a transition model P(s′ | s, a), an expected immediate reward R(s, a), and a discount γ ∈ [0, 1]. The Markov property (the next state depends only on the current state and action) makes one-step recursion valid. Full vocabulary: RL foundations.

A policy π(a | s) is the probability of action a in state s; a deterministic policy is written π(s).
The return Gt = Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ2Rt+3 + ... is the discounted sum of future rewards.
State value Vπ(s): expected return from s following π. Action value Qπ(s, a): expected return taking a in s, then following π.
Optimal value V∗(s) = max over policies of Vπ(s); an optimal policy π∗ achieves it in every state. Every finite MDP has at least one deterministic optimal policy.

Start with arbitrary values, usually zeros. Terminal states have value 0 and stay there.
For every state, look one step ahead: for each action the policy may take, reward plus discounted successor values, weighted by probability.
Set the new value to the policy-weighted average of those lookahead values.
Repeat until the largest change in any state, maxs |Vk+1(s) − Vk(s)|, is below a small threshold θ.
Convergence
The backup is a γ-contraction (see Why it converges), so it converges to the unique Vπ from any start. With γ = 1 that argument is gone; convergence then needs every state to reach a terminal state eventually under π.

Policy improvement theorem
If Qπ(s, π′(s)) ≥ Vπ(s) in every state, then Vπ′(s) ≥ Vπ(s) in every state: taking the better action once and then following π is at least as good as π, and repeating the argument at every later step only adds improvement. The greedy policy meets the premise automatically, since the best action's value is at least the policy-weighted average, which is Vπ(s). If greedy improvement changes nothing, Vπ(s) = maxa Qπ(s, a) everywhere: the Bellman optimality equation. π is already optimal.
Policy iteration (control, with an explicit policy)

Alternate evaluation (values of the current policy) and improvement (greedy on those values) until the policy stops changing.

Start with an arbitrary policy (for example uniformly random) and arbitrary values.
Evaluate: policy evaluation sweeps until the values settle (or a direct linear solve).
Improve: replace the policy by the greedy policy on those values.
If the policy did not change, stop: it is optimal. Otherwise evaluate again.

Why it terminates. There are finitely many deterministic policies (actions to the power of states). Each improvement is at least as good everywhere and strictly better somewhere unless already optimal, so no policy repeats and the loop ends at a policy satisfying the Bellman optimality equation. It needs few outer iterations: the policy changes in whole jumps and often becomes optimal long before its values converge. There is an explicit policy throughout: the policy-flavoured member of the DP pair.

Evaluation does not have to converge
Stop evaluation after k sweeps and improve anyway: modified policy iteration, still convergent. Interleaving any amount of evaluation with any amount of improvement is generalised policy iteration (GPI). In Silver's 4 by 4 gridworld (reward of minus 1 per step, uniform random start, no discount) k = 3 sweeps already gave the optimal greedy policy. k = 1 is exactly value iteration. One catch: after a truncated evaluation, an unchanged policy is no longer proof of optimality (the values were not yet its own), so stop only when the values have also stopped changing.

Policy iteration vs value iteration
	Policy iteration	Value iteration
Flavour	Policy-based (explicit policy stored and improved)	Value-based (no explicit policy until the end)
Model needed	Yes: model-based	Yes: model-based
Update rule	Bellman expectation equation + greedy improvement	Bellman optimality equation directly
Intermediate values	At every step, V corresponds to some actual policy	Intermediate V may correspond to no policy; only the final V corresponds to (the optimal) one
Per-sweep cost	An evaluation sweep under a deterministic policy looks at one action per state: cheaper by a factor of the number of actions. Only improvement looks at all actions. But evaluation is a loop inside the outer loop	Every sweep maxes over all actions; single loop
Typical behaviour	Few outer iterations; policy often stabilises before values fully converge	More sweeps, each simple
Stops when	The policy does not change: exact, finite	Largest change below a threshold: approximate

Both converge to the same V∗ and π∗. Neither dominates: policy iteration wins when evaluation is cheap (few actions, or an affordable linear solve); value iteration wins when states have many actions or the policy would change many times. Modified policy iteration, a handful of evaluation sweeps per improvement, sits between and is often the practical choice.

Cost, scale and asynchronous DP

Each backup looks at every action and every successor: full-width backups. With n states and m actions:

a sweep over state values costs O(m n2);
a sweep over action values Q(s, a) costs O(m2 n2): mn entries, each looking at mn successor pairs.

Fine for medium-sized problems (Silver's slides put the limit at millions of states), but the number of states grows exponentially with the number of state variables: Bellman's curse of dimensionality. Ten variables with ten values each is already ten billion states, and even one backup can be too expensive.

The curse of dimensionality

States grow as values per variable to the power of the number of variables; one sweep over state values costs about m n² operations.

Asynchronous DP

Back up states in any order rather than in full sweeps; it still converges as long as every state keeps being backed up.

In-place DP: one array; each backup is immediately visible (the corridor converges in one sweep in the right order).
Prioritised sweeping: back up the state with the largest Bellman error (how much its backup would change it) first, from a priority queue; then update its predecessors' errors, which needs the reverse dynamics.
Real-time DP: back up only the states an agent actually visits.
Drop the model: sample backups
One sampled transition replaces the sum over successors; cost per backup no longer depends on the number of states. That is Model-free methods.
Drop the table: approximate DP
A function approximator, backed up on sampled states (fitted value iteration), leads to DQN on Deep RL.
Common mistakes and misconceptions
Calling DP learning. It computes from a given model; without one you need sampling methods.
Acting greedily on V without a model. The greedy step needs the lookahead, so P and R. Model-free control uses Q(s, a) so the greedy step is a plain arg max.
Reading intermediate value iteration values as a policy's value. They may belong to no policy.
Trusting a small last change. A change of θ can hide an error up to γθ/(1 − γ): a hundred times θ at γ = 0.99.
Letting policy iteration flip between equally good actions. Break ties consistently (keep the current action unless another is strictly better) or stop when the values stop changing.
Assuming full evaluation is needed before improving. A few sweeps are usually enough; one sweep is value iteration.
Stopping modified policy iteration on an unchanged policy alone. After a truncated evaluation the values are not yet the policy's own; also require the values to have converged.
Using γ = 1 carelessly. No contraction; evaluation converges only if every evaluated policy eventually reaches a terminal state.
Treating DP as obsolete. Evaluate then improve, and reward plus discounted next value, are inside every modern RL algorithm.
How it connects, and why it still matters for LLM work
Up the tree: Topic: rl places DP as the model-based solution of the Bellman equations whose vocabulary is on RL foundations.
GPI is the shape of nearly every RL algorithm, including PPO for RLHF: the critic update is approximate policy evaluation, the policy-gradient step approximate policy improvement. AlphaZero is GPI with Monte Carlo tree search as the improvement operator.
The one-step backup is the template for every target: V(s) ← r + γV(s′) is a DP backup with one sample in place of the expectation. It is the TD target, Q-learning is sampled value iteration, DQN regresses onto the same target, and GAE (generalised advantage estimation) is built from the TD errors r + γV(s′) − V(s) that PPO's critic produces.
Token generation is an MDP with a trivial, known transition (append the token), so DP's model is available; the obstacle is the size of the state space, every possible prefix, which is why LLM RL samples instead (RL for LLMs).
Key takeaways
DP plans with a known model; it computes, it does not learn.
Policy evaluation = Bellman expectation equation as an update; value iteration = Bellman optimality equation as an update.
Policy iteration alternates evaluation and greedy improvement; the policy improvement theorem guarantees each step is no worse, and it terminates in finitely many steps.
Evaluation can be truncated (modified policy iteration, GPI); one sweep per improvement is value iteration.
Both backups are γ-contractions: error shrinks by at least γ per sweep, so sweeps needed grow with 1/(1 − γ), and a small last change can hide a much larger error.
A sweep costs O(m n²): fine up to millions of states, killed by the curse of dimensionality. Sampling (model-free) and function approximation (deep RL) are the ways out.
Every TD, DQN and PPO value target is a sampled DP backup.

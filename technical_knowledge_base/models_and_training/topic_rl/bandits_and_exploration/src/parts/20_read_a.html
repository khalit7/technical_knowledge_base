Bandits and exploration: the idea

An agent only sees the reward of the action it takes, so it must spend some reward finding out; random exploration pays the same price every step forever (linear regret), while exploring where its estimates are uncertain makes the price shrink, growing only like the logarithm of time, which is the proven floor.

Arm
one of the k actions of a bandit; its rewards come from a fixed, unknown distribution with mean μa (or q*(a)).
Regret
reward lost against always pulling the best arm: the pulls of each worse arm times its gap Δa = μ* − μa.
Exploration bonus
an addition to an action's estimate that is large where the agent knows little (UCB's √(ln t / N), count bonuses, RND).
Posterior
the agent's belief about an arm's mean after the rewards it has seen; Thompson sampling acts on one draw from it.
Contextual bandit
a bandit that sees features (a user, a page) before each choice and learns a policy from contexts to actions.
Intrinsic reward
reward the agent pays itself for novelty or surprise, added to the task's reward to drive exploration in large state spaces.

1The exploration-exploitation trade-off

Exploitation takes the action believed best, maximising reward now; exploration takes one that looks worse, to buy information that may pay later (Silver's examples: your favourite restaurant or a new one, the best-known advert or a different one; {{Silver, Lecture 9|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-9-exploration-and-exploitation.pdf}}). Pure exploitation gets stuck on the first decent option, because it never collects the evidence that something else is better; pure exploration never cashes in.

What makes this special to reinforcement learning: in supervised learning the labels arrive whatever the model predicts, but here the agent's own choices decide which data it sees, so a wrong belief about an action is never corrected if the agent stops taking it. A multi-armed bandit isolates exactly this: one situation repeated, k actions with unknown mean payoffs, no state and no delay, so nothing is left but exploration ({{Sutton and Barto, chapter 2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=47}}).

The trade-off is "solved" in theory: Bayes-optimal play and the Gittins index (1 min)

With a prior over the arms and a known horizon, the best possible policy is the Bayes-optimal one, computed by planning over all future beliefs. It is almost never computed, because the tree of possible futures explodes: Sutton and Barto note that for 1,000 steps of a two-armed bandit with two possible rewards the tree would have 22000 leaves ({{§2.10|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=65}}). For the discounted, infinite-horizon case, {{Gittins (1979)|https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1979.tb01068.x}} showed that the Bayes-optimal policy reduces to an index computed per arm. Everything else on this page is a cheaper rule that gets close.

2The k-armed bandit and action values

At each step t the agent picks an action At from k options and receives a reward Rt from that action's distribution. An action's value is its expected reward, q*(a) = E[Rt | At = a]; the agent keeps an estimate Qt(a), usually the sample average, updated without storing past rewards:

Qn+1 = Qn + αn [Rn − Qn],   αn = 1/n (sample average) or α (constant)
Rn − Qn
the error: how surprising the new reward is. The form NewEstimate ← OldEstimate + StepSize [Target − OldEstimate] recurs through all of RL, from TD learning to the deep methods ({{Sutton and Barto §2.4, eq. 2.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=52}})
constant α
unrolls to an exponential recency-weighted average, Qn+1 = (1 − α)n Q1 + Σi α (1 − α)n−i Ri ({{§2.5, eq. 2.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=54}}): weights decay geometrically with age and sum to 1

When a step-size schedule converges: the stochastic approximation conditions (under 1 min)

A schedule αn converges to the true value when Σ αn = ∞ (steps large enough to overcome any start) and Σ αn2 < ∞ (small enough to settle). The sample average's 1/n meets both; a constant α fails the second, which is exactly what lets it keep tracking a moving target ({{§2.5, eq. 2.7|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=55}}). The initial estimate Q1 is a bias: with sample averages it disappears the first time an arm is pulled; with a constant α it is permanent, though decreasing.

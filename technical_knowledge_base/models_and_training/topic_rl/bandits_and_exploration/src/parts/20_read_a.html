Bandits and exploration in one screen

An agent that learns from its own actions has to keep choosing between exploiting the action it currently believes is best and exploring an action that might be better. The cleanest form of the problem is the multi-armed bandit: one state, k actions with unknown average payoffs, and a score for the total reward collected. Bandits have a precise yardstick, regret, and a proven floor on it: any good strategy must lose reward growing at least like the logarithm of time. The strategies below differ in how they spend that loss.

1The exploration-exploitation trade-off

Exploitation uses current knowledge to take the action believed best, maximising reward now. Exploration takes an action that looks worse, to gather information that may pay off later. Silver's everyday examples ({{Silver, Lecture 9|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-9-exploration-and-exploitation.pdf}}): go to your favourite restaurant or try a new one; show the most successful advert or a different one; drill for oil at the best known site or at a new one; play the move you believe is best or an experimental one. Both extremes fail. Pure exploitation gets stuck on the first decent option it finds, because it never collects the evidence that something else is better. Pure exploration never cashes in what it has learned.

Why this is special to reinforcement learning: in supervised learning the training labels arrive whatever the model predicts. Here the agent's own choices decide which data it sees, so a wrong belief about an action can never be corrected if the agent stops taking it. The value of trying an action is therefore partly the reward it pays now and partly the information it buys. Bandits isolate this: one situation repeated, no state, no delay between action and reward, so nothing is left except exploration ({{Sutton and Barto, chapter 2|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=47}}).

There is a sense in which the trade-off is "solved": if you have a prior over the arms and a known horizon, the best possible policy is the Bayes-optimal one, computed by planning over all future beliefs. It is almost never computed, because the tree of possible futures explodes; Sutton and Barto note that for 1,000 steps of a two-armed bandit with two possible rewards the tree would have 22000 leaves ({{§2.10|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=65}}). For the discounted, infinite-horizon case, {{Gittins (1979)|https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1979.tb01068.x}} showed that the Bayes-optimal policy reduces to an index computed per arm. Everything else on this page is a cheaper rule that gets close.

2The k-armed bandit and action values

At each step t the agent picks an action At from k options and receives a reward Rt drawn from that action's distribution. The value of an action is its expected reward, q*(a) = E[Rt | At = a]; if the agent knew it, the problem would be trivial. It keeps an estimate Qt(a) instead.

Qn+1 = Qn + (1/n) [Rn − Qn]
Qn
the average of the first n − 1 rewards from this action
Rn − Qn
the error: how surprising the new reward is
1/n
the step size; with 1/n the estimate is exactly the sample average

The sample average can be updated without storing past rewards, with the incremental rule above; its general form, NewEstimate ← OldEstimate + StepSize [Target − OldEstimate], recurs through all of RL, from TD learning to the deep methods ({{Sutton and Barto §2.4, eq. 2.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=52}}).

When the arms' values drift over time (a nonstationary problem), the sample average is a poor tracker: it weights a reward from step 1 as much as one from yesterday. A constant step size α ∈ (0, 1] fixes this. Unrolling the update gives an exponential recency-weighted average ({{§2.5, eq. 2.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=54}}):

Qn+1 = (1 − α)n Q1 + Σi=1..n α (1 − α)n−i Ri
(1 − α)n−i
a reward's weight decays geometrically with the number of later rewards; the weights sum to 1
(1 − α)n Q1
the initial estimate never fully disappears: a constant step size keeps a (shrinking) bias

Stochastic approximation says when a schedule of step sizes αn converges to the true value: Σ αn = ∞ (steps large enough to overcome any start) and Σ αn2 < ∞ (small enough to settle). The sample average's 1/n meets both; a constant α fails the second, which is exactly what lets it keep tracking a moving target ({{§2.5, eq. 2.7|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=55}}). The testbed tab's Nonstationary preset shows the difference: {{10-armed testbed|#t-test}}.

The initial estimate Q1 is a bias: with sample averages it disappears the first time an arm is pulled; with a constant α it is permanent, though decreasing ({{§2.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=56}}). That bias is the lever behind optimistic initial values, below.

3The strategies

Greedy and ε-greedy

Greedy always takes argmaxa Qt(a) (ties broken at random). It locks onto whichever arm looked good first. ε-greedy takes the greedy action with probability 1 − ε and otherwise picks uniformly among all m actions, so the greedy action has probability 1 − ε + ε/m and each other action ε/m; with ε = 0.1 and m = 4 that is 0.9 + 0.025 = 0.925 and 0.025 ({{Silver, Lecture 5|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-5-model-free-control-.pdf}}). Every action is tried infinitely often, so every estimate converges; the price is that ε-greedy keeps picking bad arms at the same rate forever, so its regret grows linearly. Decaying ε towards 0 while still trying every action infinitely often is called GLIE (greedy in the limit with infinite exploration); it is the condition under which Monte Carlo control and SARSA converge to the optimal policy ({{Silver, Lecture 5|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-5-model-free-control-.pdf}}; {{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}}).

Optimistic initial values

Set every initial estimate far above any plausible reward (Sutton and Barto use +5 when the true values are drawn from N(0, 1)). Whatever the greedy agent tries disappoints it, so it moves on to the next arm, and every arm is tried several times before the estimates settle ({{§2.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=56}}). It needs a constant step size: with sample averages the optimism is erased by the first pull of each arm. The book calls it "a simple trick that can be quite effective on stationary problems, but it is far from being a generally useful approach": its drive to explore is spent at the start, so it cannot respond when the task changes. The same idea, made principled, is R-MAX in full RL (section 6).

Upper confidence bound (UCB)

ε-greedy explores indiscriminately. Better: prefer arms that could plausibly be the best, given how uncertain their estimates are ({{§2.7, eq. 2.10|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=57}}):

At = argmaxa [ Qt(a) + c √( ln t / Nt(a) ) ]
Nt(a)
how often a has been pulled before t; an unpulled arm counts as maximising
√(ln t / N)
shrinks each time a is pulled, grows slowly (logarithmically) while it is not, so every arm is eventually retried, ever more rarely
c
the confidence level; {{UCB1 (Auer et al. 2002)|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=3}} uses x̄j + √(2 ln n / nj), that is c = √2, for rewards in [0, 1]

This is optimism in the face of uncertainty: act as if each arm is as good as it plausibly could be. Either the optimism was right and you collect, or it was wrong and the pull shrinks the bonus. The book adds a caution: UCB "is more difficult than ε-greedy to extend beyond bandits", to nonstationary problems and to large state spaces with function approximation, where counting visits is not practical; section 6 is about how deep RL works around that.

Gradient bandits

Instead of estimating values, learn a preference Ht(a) per action and act by softmax, πt(a) = eHt(a) / Σb eHt(b). After each reward ({{§2.8, eq. 2.12|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=59}}):

H(At) ← H(At) + α (Rt − R̄t)(1 − πt(At)),   H(a) ← H(a) − α (Rt − R̄t) πt(a) for a ≠ At
R̄t
the baseline: the average reward so far (the book's experiments include Rt itself, per its footnote on p. 37)
Rt − R̄t
better than usual raises the chosen action's probability; worse lowers it; the others move the opposite way

The box in §2.8 proves that the expected update equals the gradient of expected reward, so this is stochastic gradient ascent: the one-state case of the policy gradient (REINFORCE with a baseline). The baseline does not change the expected update, only its variance; Figure 2.5 shows what happens without it when all rewards are shifted by +4 ({{10-armed testbed|#t-test}}, Fig. 2.5). The full derivation and the general case live on {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}.

Thompson sampling

Keep a posterior distribution over each arm's mean. At every step draw one sample from each posterior and pull the arm whose sample is highest; update that arm's posterior with the reward. An arm is pulled with exactly the probability that it is the best arm under the current belief ("probability matching"). For 0/1 rewards with a uniform prior the posterior is Beta(1 + successes, 1 + failures), so the whole algorithm is two counters per arm and one Beta draw. It is the oldest method on the page: {{Thompson (1933)|https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/25.3-4.285}} proposed it "for allocating experimental effort in two-armed bandit problems arising in clinical trials" ({{Russo et al., tutorial|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.02038}}). Uncertain arms have wide posteriors, so they sometimes produce a high draw and get tried; as evidence accumulates a bad arm's posterior narrows below the best arm's and it stops being chosen. Sutton and Barto note that it "often performs similarly to the best of the distribution-free methods" ({{§2.10|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=65}}).

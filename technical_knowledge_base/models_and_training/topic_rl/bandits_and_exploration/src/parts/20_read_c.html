6Exploration in full RL

With states, an exploratory action's payoff may only show up many steps later, after a specific sequence of further actions. That changes the problem in three ways.

  • Dithering is exponentially slow. ε-greedy and softmax noise explore one step at a time. If a reward sits at the end of a chain of N decisions where only one of two actions keeps you on track, a uniformly random policy reaches it with probability 2−N per attempt, so it needs about 2N attempts derived. What is needed is deep (temporally extended) exploration: commit to a plan for finding out, across many steps. Osband et al. put it this way: "Unlike dithering strategies such as epsilon-greedy exploration, bootstrapped DQN carries out temporally-extended (or deep) exploration; this can lead to exponentially faster learning" ({{Osband et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.04621}}).
  • Sparse rewards give no gradient. If almost every episode returns 0, every action looks the same and value estimates have nothing to learn from until something succeeds by chance.
  • Distractors trap exploiters. A small nearby reward is found first, and a greedy-ish learner stops looking for the larger one far away: greedy on the testbed, scaled up.

The families of answers, roughly in the order they appeared:

Optimism with counts (tabular, with guarantees)

E3 ({{Kearns and Singh 2002|https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1017984413808}}) and R-MAX ({{Brafman and Tennenholtz 2002|https://jmlr.org/papers/v3/brafman02a.html}}) gave the first polynomial-time guarantees for exploring an MDP. R-MAX's model "is initialized in an optimistic fashion: all actions in all states return the maximal possible reward (hence the name)"; once a state-action pair has been tried enough times its true model replaces the optimistic one. MBIE-EB ({{Strehl and Littman 2008|https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcss.2007.08.009}}) turns this into a bonus β/√N(s, a) added to the reward in the Bellman equation (as restated by {{Bellemare et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01868}}), the direct MDP analogue of UCB. UCRL2 ({{Jaksch, Ortner and Auer 2010|https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/jaksch10a.html}}) plans optimistically within confidence sets on the transition model and proves regret Õ(DS√(AT)) against a lower bound Ω(√(DSAT)) (D the MDP's diameter, S states, A actions). All of them need to count visits to each state, which is impossible when states are images.

Pseudo-counts and hashing (counts for large state spaces)

{{Bellemare et al. (2016)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01868}} derive a pseudo-count N̂(x) from a density model of observations: how much the model's probability of x rises after one more look at x says how familiar x is. The bonus is β (N̂ + 0.01)−1/2 with β = 0.05. On Montezuma's Revenge their agent "consistently navigates through 15 rooms" of the first level's 24 within 50 million frames, where the agent without the bonus saw two, and scored 3,439 on average by 100 million frames. {{Tang et al. (2017)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.04717}} count hashed states instead (SimHash of pixels or of learned features), bonus β/√n(φ(s)).

Curiosity and prediction error

Reward the agent where its model of the world is wrong. The Intrinsic Curiosity Module uses "the error in an agent's ability to predict the consequence of its own actions in a visual feature space learned by a self-supervised inverse dynamics model" ({{Pathak et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363}}); learning features through an inverse model is meant to ignore what the agent cannot affect. A {{large-scale study|https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355}} trained agents with curiosity alone, no extrinsic reward, on 54 environments, and documented the main failure, the noisy TV: add a TV with a channel-changing action to a maze and "the presence of the TV drastically slows down learning", because unpredictable noise stays surprising forever. Prediction error mixes up "new" with "random".

Random Network Distillation (RND)

{{Burda et al. (2018)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12894}} fix the target: a randomly initialised network that is never trained maps each observation to a feature vector, and a second network is trained to predict those features. Its error is high on observations unlike those seen before and falls with familiarity, but because the target is a deterministic function of the observation, noise does not keep it high. RND was the "first method that achieves better than average human performance on this game without using demonstrations or having access to the underlying state": a mean of 8,152 on Montezuma's Revenge against an average human 4,753 (Table 1), with the best agent often finding "22 out of the 24 rooms on the first level".

Remember and return: NGU, Agent57, Go-Explore

Never Give Up combines a per-episode novelty bonus with RND's lifelong one and was the "first algorithm to achieve non-zero rewards (with a mean score of 8,400) in the game of Pitfall! without using demonstrations" ({{Badia et al. 2020a|https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06038}}); Agent57 adds the bandit meta-controller of section 4 to choose how much to explore, and is "the first deep RL agent that outperforms the standard human benchmark on all 57 Atari games" ({{Badia et al. 2020b|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350}}; see the {{Milestones and benchmarks tab of Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}} for its scores, including two mistyped MuZero entries found there). Go-Explore ({{Ecoffet et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10995}}) splits the problem: keep an archive of distinct states ("cells") reached so far, pick a promising one, return to it (by restoring the emulator's state, which needs a resettable simulator), explore from there, and add new cells; then train a robust policy from the best trajectories. Without domain knowledge its policies averaged 43,763 on Montezuma's Revenge, "higher than the human expert mean of 34,900", and solved level 1 in 99.8% of attempts. Its journal version, {{"First return, then explore"|https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-03157-9}}, adds a policy that learns to return without resets and reports that it "solves all heretofore unsolved Atari games"; with easy-to-provide domain knowledge it reaches 102,571 on Pitfall and 1,731,645 on Montezuma's Revenge (figures from the {{preprint|https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919}}).

Uncertainty about values: posterior sampling in deep RL

Thompson sampling's deep-RL form keeps several plausible value functions and acts greedily with respect to one of them for a whole episode, which gives the temporally extended exploration that per-step noise lacks: Bootstrapped DQN trains several Q heads on bootstrapped data ({{Osband et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.04621}}); the idea is developed in {{Osband, Van Roy, Russo and Wen (2019)|https://jmlr.org/papers/v20/18-339.html}}. NoisyNet instead learns noise in the network's weights, replacing ε-greedy and entropy bonuses, with initial noise scale σ0 = 0.5 ({{Fortunato et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.10295}}); it is one of the six extensions Rainbow combines (Method atlas tab of {{Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}}; details on {{Value-based deep RL|n:3ee5c17b0d0d818689b2c0dbce434ece}}).

Entropy bonuses (policy methods)

Policy-gradient methods explore by sampling from their own stochastic policy, and the danger is that it collapses onto one action too early. A3C adds the policy's entropy to the objective, "with a weight β = 0.01 for all Atari and TORCS experiments" ({{Mnih et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.01783}}); SAC makes it the objective itself, J(π) = Σt E[r(st, at) + α H(π(·|st))], "to succeed at the task while acting as randomly as possible", with the temperature α weighing entropy against reward ({{Haarnoja et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01290}}). Both are local: they keep the policy from collapsing, but do not by themselves send the agent anywhere new. The mechanics are on {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}.

One idea under all of these
Every method on this page either adds a bonus where knowledge is thin (UCB, counts, pseudo-counts, RND), starts optimistic and lets evidence correct it (optimistic values, R-MAX), or acts on a plausible guess drawn from its uncertainty (Thompson sampling, bootstrapped and noisy networks). Randomness alone (ε-greedy, entropy) is the cheap fallback that works when rewards are dense.

7Exploration when training language models

RL on a pretrained language model starts from a policy that already produces sensible answers, so exploration is mostly inherited from pretraining and controlled through sampling: several responses per prompt at a temperature around 1, so that some are right and some wrong and the advantage has something to compare (GRPO's group). Three findings frame what this exploration can and cannot do:

  • RL sharpens more than it discovers. {{Yue et al. (2025)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837}} find that "while RLVR-trained models outperform their base models at small k (e.g., k = 1), the base models achieve a higher pass@k score when k is large", and conclude that the reasoning abilities they measured "originate from and are bounded by the base model". In bandit terms: RL concentrates probability on arms the base model already pulls sometimes; it rarely finds arms the base model never pulls.
  • Entropy collapses, and that caps performance. {{Cui et al. (2025)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22617}} fit "a transformation equation R = −a eH + b between entropy H and downstream performance R", so that when entropy runs out "the ceiling is fully predictable H = 0, R = −a + b"; they trace the entropy change to "the covariance between action probability and the change in logits" and propose Clip-Cov and KL-Cov to slow it.
  • Clip asymmetrically to keep exploring. DAPO's "Clip-Higher ... promotes the diversity of the system and avoids entropy collapse" by letting low-probability tokens grow more than PPO's symmetric clip allows: εlow = 0.2, εhigh = 0.28 ({{Yu et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476}}).

The algorithms (PPO for RLHF, GRPO, RLVR, DAPO) are on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}; the pipeline around them on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}; the paper pages {{DeepSeekMath|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} (GRPO) and {{DeepSeek-R1|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}} (RL from a base model without supervised warm-up) show what this exploration produced at scale.

Common mistakes

Breaking ties by taking the first arm
With all estimates equal at the start, "argmax" in most libraries returns the first index, so a greedy agent always starts on arm 1 and the results depend on arm order. Sutton and Barto break ties at random; so does every simulation on this page except the Reading animation, where the fixed tie rule is visible and stated.
Reading one run, or a few, as a result
A single bandit run is dominated by luck (try the five seeds in section 3). The book averages 2,000 tasks; on the testbed tab, compare 200 with 2,000 to see how much of a curve's wiggle is noise.
Using UCB's constant outside its scale
UCB1's √(2 ln n / nj) assumes rewards in [0, 1]. With rewards in the hundreds the bonus is negligible and UCB is greedy; with rewards in [0, 0.01] it explores almost uniformly. Rescale the rewards or tune c (Figure 2.6 shows how much c matters).
Treating optimistic initial values as a general method
Optimism explores only at the start. With sample averages it vanishes after one pull of each arm, and in a nonstationary task it never comes back (§2.6). R-MAX makes it principled by tying optimism to counts.
Using sample averages on a problem that drifts
The 1/n step gives yesterday's reward the same weight as last year's, so the estimate follows a moving arm ever more slowly. Use a constant step size (the testbed tab's Nonstationary preset shows the gap).
Reading the Lai and Robbins bound as a finite-time floor
It is a statement about the slope against ln T in the limit. A good algorithm can sit below the line at any T you can afford to run (the regret chart in section 4 shows Thompson sampling doing exactly that).
Crediting Thompson sampling for a matched prior
On the 10-armed testbed Thompson sampling with a N(0, 1) prior uses the exact distribution the tasks are drawn from. With a wrong or overconfident prior it can under-explore; report the prior with the result.
Evaluating a new bandit policy on logs from the old one
Logged clicks only show the arm the old policy chose. Li et al.'s replay estimator is unbiased because the logging policy chose uniformly at random; with a non-random logging policy you need its propensities (inverse propensity weighting) or a randomised slice of traffic.
Expecting a novelty bonus to ignore noise
Prediction-error curiosity is drawn to anything unpredictable (the noisy TV). RND's fixed random target and counts of states avoid that particular trap, but any bonus can distract from the task if it is not scaled down as the task reward appears.
Comparing hard-exploration scores across papers
Human baselines, frame budgets, sticky actions, emulator resets and domain knowledge all differ (see the Montezuma's Revenge table). Compare within one paper's table, or re-run.
Thinking ε-greedy is always the wrong choice
In Auer et al.'s experiments "an optimally tuned εn-GREEDY performs almost always best" ({{Auer et al. 2002|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf}}); its weakness is that the tuning depends on the unknown gap, and in full RL that its exploration is shallow. With dense rewards (most of Atari) it is still the default.

5Contextual bandits, A/B tests, and where bandits run in production

A contextual bandit sees a context first (a user's features, the page being viewed), picks an action, and sees the reward of that action only; the goal is a policy from contexts to actions. Sutton and Barto call it associative search and place it exactly between the bandit and full RL ({{§2.9|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=63}}). It is the most deployed form of RL, because many products face exactly this loop: show something, see a click or not, never see what the other options would have earned.

What each step up adds: a situation to recognise, then consequences that last

Bandit
pull arm a→reward of a↺ same situation

One situation, repeated. Only exploration is left.

Contextual bandit
context xt→action a→reward of a only

The next context arrives whatever the agent did. Learn which action suits which context.

Full RL (an MDP)
state st→action a→reward+next state depends on a

Actions have delayed consequences: credit assignment and planning ({{Dynamic programming|n:3c65c17b0d0d81baada7e1c05ce69d01}}).

What a bandit does not do
A bandit's action does not change the next context. If showing an article changes what the user does next week, or a treatment changes the patient's later state, the problem is full RL with delayed consequences, and a bandit optimising the immediate click can be confidently wrong about the long run.

On the Yahoo! Front Page Today Module, with "over 33 million events", LinUCB gave "a 12.5% click lift compared to a standard context-free bandit algorithm, and the advantage becomes even greater when data gets more scarce". Their second contribution is still the standard way to test a policy without shipping it: replay logged traffic in which articles were chosen uniformly at random, keep only the events where the logged article matches the one the new policy would choose, and average their clicks; because the logging was random, this "can provide provably unbiased evaluations" ({{Li, Chu, Langford and Wang 2011|https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.5956}}).

A/B tests are a bandit strategy, and usually not the cheapest

The classic product experiment splits users into two groups (Lattimore and Szepesvári's example: two groups of 10,000), shows each a version, and ships the winner. In bandit terms that is explore-then-commit (ETC): pull each arm m times, then commit to the one that looked best. Its regret has exactly two parts, the test's cost and the risk of shipping the wrong version ({{Lattimore and Szepesvári, Theorem 6.1 and eq. 6.4|https://tor-lattimore.com/downloads/book/book.pdf#page=101}}):

Rn ≤ m Δ + (n − 2m) Δ · P(wrong winner),   P(wrong winner) ≤ exp(−m Δ2 / 4)
m Δ
the visitors sent to the worse version during the test, times the gap: paid in full however obvious the difference becomes
(n − 2m) Δ P(wrong)
everyone after the test, if the test picked the worse version; a longer test lowers this and raises the first term
tuning m
needs the gap Δ, "seldom reasonable to assume" known; with m tuned the regret is at most Δ + C√n, and with m chosen from n alone it is O(n2/3) (the book's eqs. 6.5 to 6.7 and Exercise 6.5)

The A/B test loses 45 conversions, 40 of them (2,000 × 0.02) during the test, and ships the worse version with probability 2.15% (exact, from two binomials; simulated 1.7%; the formula above then gives 46.9). Thompson sampling with Beta(1, 1) priors loses 29, because it shifts traffic as soon as the evidence leans. The price: at 4,000 visitors it has shown the worse version 828 times instead of 2,000, so its 95% interval on the difference is ±2.3 points against ±1.9. A bandit maximises reward during the experiment; an A/B test maximises what you learn at its end (src/make_data.mjs).

Lattimore and Szepesvári put the difference in purpose plainly: "In traditional A/B testing, the objective of the statistician is to decide which website is better. When using a bandit algorithm, there is no need to end the trial" (§1.2). The same tension is sharpest in clinical trials, below.

Where bandits are used

  • Recommendation and content: Yahoo! news (above). Microsoft's Decision Service, a general contextual-bandit system, reported "two live production deployments for content recommendation that achieved click-through improvements of 25-30%, another with 18% revenue lift in the landing page" ({{Agarwal et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03966}}).
  • Advertising: Bing's adPredictor paper proposes that instead of always feeding the expected click rate to the auction, "the system can sample from the posterior weight distribution", an idea it traces to Thompson, which has "the effect of bubbling up ads about whose CTR the system has a high degree of uncertainty left" ({{Graepel et al. 2010, §5|https://icml.cc/Conferences/2010/papers/901.pdf#page=7}}; the paper says "can", not that this was the deployed default). Yahoo!'s {{Chapelle and Li (2011)|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2011/hash/e53a0a2978c28872a4505bdb51db06dc-Abstract.html}} found on display ads and news that "Thompson sampling achieves state-of-the-art results, and in some cases significantly outperforms other alternatives like UCB", which revived the method.
  • Clinical trials: the field's origin (Thompson 1933) and its sharpest tension. Assigning more patients to the arm that looks better is an ethical gain, but it weakens the trial's statistical power (the A/B card's interval, in medicine): {{Villar, Bowden and Wason (2015)|https://doi.org/10.1214/14-STS504}} find bandit designs "offer significant advantages, in terms of assigning more patients to better treatments, and severe limitations, in terms of their resulting statistical power", and propose a rule that keeps power. The cautionary example is the 1985 ECMO trial, which used randomised play-the-winner: one infant was assigned to conventional treatment and died, eleven to ECMO and all survived ({{Bartlett et al. 1985|https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3900904/}}), a result too lopsided for many to accept as evidence.
  • Inside other learners: Agent57's policy chooser and UCT's tree search (section 4).

6Exploration in full RL, in brief

With states, an exploratory action's payoff may only show up many steps later, after a specific sequence of further actions. Three things get harder:

  • Dithering is exponentially slow. If a reward sits at the end of a chain of N decisions where only one of two actions keeps you on track, a uniformly random policy reaches it with probability 2−N per attempt, so it needs about 2N attempts derived. What is needed is deep (temporally extended) exploration: "Unlike dithering strategies such as epsilon-greedy exploration, bootstrapped DQN carries out temporally-extended (or deep) exploration; this can lead to exponentially faster learning" ({{Osband et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.04621}}).
  • Sparse rewards give no gradient. If almost every episode returns 0, every action looks the same until something succeeds by chance.
  • Distractors trap exploiters. A small nearby reward is found first, and a greedy-ish learner stops looking for the larger one far away: greedy on the testbed, scaled up.

The families in detail: sources, quotes and numbers (5 min)

Optimism with counts. E3 ({{Kearns and Singh 2002|https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1017984413808}}) and R-MAX ({{Brafman and Tennenholtz 2002|https://jmlr.org/papers/v3/brafman02a.html}}) gave the first polynomial-time guarantees for exploring an MDP. R-MAX's model "is initialized in an optimistic fashion: all actions in all states return the maximal possible reward (hence the name)"; once a state-action pair has been tried enough times its true model replaces the optimistic one. MBIE-EB ({{Strehl and Littman 2008|https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcss.2007.08.009}}) turns this into a bonus β/√N(s, a) added to the reward in the Bellman equation (as restated by {{Bellemare et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01868}}). UCRL2 ({{Jaksch, Ortner and Auer 2010|https://jmlr.org/papers/v11/jaksch10a.html}}) plans optimistically within confidence sets on the transition model and proves regret Õ(DS√(AT)) against a lower bound Ω(√(DSAT)) (D the MDP's diameter, S states, A actions).

Pseudo-counts and hashing. {{Bellemare et al. (2016)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01868}} derive a pseudo-count N̂(x) from a density model of observations: how much the model's probability of x rises after one more look at x says how familiar x is. The bonus is β (N̂ + 0.01)−1/2 with β = 0.05. On Montezuma's Revenge their agent "consistently navigates through 15 rooms" of the first level's 24 within 50 million frames, where the agent without the bonus saw two, and scored 3,439 on average by 100 million frames. {{Tang et al. (2017)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.04717}} count hashed states instead (SimHash of pixels or of learned features), bonus β/√n(φ(s)).

Curiosity. The Intrinsic Curiosity Module uses "the error in an agent's ability to predict the consequence of its own actions in a visual feature space learned by a self-supervised inverse dynamics model" ({{Pathak et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.05363}}); learning features through an inverse model is meant to ignore what the agent cannot affect. A {{large-scale study|https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.04355}} trained agents with curiosity alone, no extrinsic reward, on 54 environments, and documented the noisy TV: add a TV with a channel-changing action to a maze and "the presence of the TV drastically slows down learning". Prediction error mixes up "new" with "random".

RND. {{Burda et al. (2018)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12894}} fix the target: a randomly initialised network that is never trained maps each observation to a feature vector, and a second network is trained to predict those features; because the target is a deterministic function of the observation, noise does not keep the error high. RND was the "first method that achieves better than average human performance on this game without using demonstrations or having access to the underlying state": a mean of 8,152 on Montezuma's Revenge against an average human 4,753 (Table 1), the best agent often finding "22 out of the 24 rooms on the first level".

Remember and return. Never Give Up combines a per-episode novelty bonus with RND's lifelong one and was the "first algorithm to achieve non-zero rewards (with a mean score of 8,400) in the game of Pitfall! without using demonstrations" ({{Badia et al. 2020a|https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06038}}); Agent57 adds the bandit meta-controller of section 4 and is "the first deep RL agent that outperforms the standard human benchmark on all 57 Atari games" ({{Badia et al. 2020b|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350}}; the {{Milestones and benchmarks tab of Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}} has its scores, including two mistyped MuZero entries found there). Go-Explore ({{Ecoffet et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.10995}}) keeps an archive of distinct states ("cells") reached so far, picks a promising one, returns to it (by restoring the emulator's state), explores from there, then trains a robust policy from the best trajectories. Without domain knowledge its policies averaged 43,763 on Montezuma's Revenge, "higher than the human expert mean of 34,900", and solved level 1 in 99.8% of attempts. The journal version, {{"First return, then explore"|https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-03157-9}}, adds a policy that learns to return without resets and reports that it "solves all heretofore unsolved Atari games"; with easy-to-provide domain knowledge it reaches 102,571 on Pitfall and 1,731,645 on Montezuma's Revenge (figures from the {{preprint|https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12919}}).

Posterior sampling. Thompson sampling's deep-RL form keeps several plausible value functions and acts greedily on one of them for a whole episode, the temporally extended exploration per-step noise lacks: Bootstrapped DQN trains several Q heads on bootstrapped data ({{Osband et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.04621}}), developed in {{Osband, Van Roy, Russo and Wen (2019)|https://jmlr.org/papers/v20/18-339.html}}. NoisyNet learns noise in the network's weights, replacing ε-greedy and entropy bonuses, with initial noise scale σ0 = 0.5 ({{Fortunato et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.10295}}); it is one of the six extensions Rainbow combines (Method atlas tab of {{Topic: rl|n:3c65c17b0d0d8195a6c6c11ad093c16e}}).

Entropy. A3C adds the policy's entropy to the objective, "with a weight β = 0.01 for all Atari and TORCS experiments" ({{Mnih et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.01783}}); SAC makes it the objective itself, J(π) = Σt E[r(st, at) + α H(π(·|st))], "to succeed at the task while acting as randomly as possible", with the temperature α weighing entropy against reward ({{Haarnoja et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.01290}}).

7Exploration when training language models

RL on a pretrained language model starts from a policy that already produces sensible answers, so exploration is inherited from pretraining and controlled through sampling: several responses per prompt at a temperature around 1, so that some are right and some wrong and the advantage has something to compare (GRPO's group). In bandit terms, RL concentrates probability on arms the base model already pulls sometimes; it rarely finds arms the base model never pulls. Three results frame this, each taught on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}: base models overtake their RL-trained versions on pass@k at large k (Yue et al. 2025); performance follows R = −a eH + b in the policy's entropy H, capped at −a + b when entropy runs out (Cui et al. 2025, with Clip-Cov and KL-Cov to slow the collapse); and DAPO's Clip-Higher (εlow = 0.2, εhigh = 0.28) lets rare tokens grow to keep exploring. The pipeline around them is on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}; {{DeepSeekMath|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} (GRPO) and {{DeepSeek-R1|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}} (RL from a base model without supervised warm-up) show what this exploration produced at scale.

Common mistakes

Breaking ties by taking the first arm
With all estimates equal at the start, "argmax" in most libraries returns the first index, so a greedy agent always starts on arm 1 and the results depend on arm order. Sutton and Barto break ties at random; so does every simulation on this page except the strategies animation, where the fixed tie rule is visible and stated.
Reading one run, or a few, as a result
A single bandit run is dominated by luck (try the five seeds in section 3). The book averages 2,000 tasks; on the testbed tab, compare 200 with 2,000 to see how much of a curve's wiggle is noise.
Using UCB's constant outside its scale
UCB1's √(2 ln n / nj) assumes rewards in [0, 1]. With rewards in the hundreds the bonus is negligible and UCB is greedy; with rewards in [0, 0.01] it explores almost uniformly. Rescale the rewards or tune c (Figure 2.6 shows how much c matters).
Treating optimistic initial values as a general method
Optimism explores only at the start. With sample averages it vanishes after one pull of each arm, and in a nonstationary task it never comes back (§2.6). R-MAX makes it principled by tying optimism to counts.
Reading the Lai and Robbins bound as a finite-time floor
It is a statement about the slope against ln T in the limit. A good algorithm can sit below the line at any T you can afford to run (the regret chart in section 4 shows Thompson sampling doing exactly that).
Crediting Thompson sampling for a matched prior
On the 10-armed testbed Thompson sampling with a N(0, 1) prior uses the exact distribution the tasks are drawn from. With a wrong or overconfident prior it can under-explore; report the prior with the result.
Stopping an A/B test the first time it looks significant
The test's p-values and intervals assume its length was fixed in advance; they "are wholly unreliable if users endogenously choose samples sizes by continuously monitoring their tests" ({{Johari, Pekelis and Walsh, Always valid inference|https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.04922}}). To stop early, use a sequential test built for it (their always-valid p-values) or a bandit, which needs no stopping rule.
Expecting a novelty bonus to ignore noise
Prediction-error curiosity is drawn to anything unpredictable (the noisy TV). RND's fixed random target and counts of states avoid that particular trap, but any bonus can distract from the task if it is not scaled down as the task reward appears.
Comparing hard-exploration scores across papers
Human baselines, frame budgets, sticky actions, emulator resets and domain knowledge all differ (see the Montezuma's Revenge table). Compare within one paper's table, or re-run.
Thinking ε-greedy is always the wrong choice
In Auer et al.'s experiments "an optimally tuned εn-GREEDY performs almost always best" ({{Auer et al. 2002|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf}}); its weakness is that the tuning depends on the unknown gap, and in full RL that its exploration is shallow. With dense rewards (most of Atari) it is still the default.

Greedy and ε-greedy

Greedy always takes argmaxa Qt(a) and locks onto whichever arm looked good first. ε-greedy takes the greedy action with probability 1 − ε and otherwise picks uniformly among all m actions, so the greedy action has probability 1 − ε + ε/m: with ε = 0.1 and m = 4 that is 0.9 + 0.025 = 0.925, and each other action 0.025 ({{Silver, Lecture 5|https://davidstarsilver.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/lecture-5-model-free-control-.pdf}}). Every estimate converges, but ε-greedy keeps picking bad arms at the same rate forever, so its regret grows linearly. Decaying ε towards 0 while still trying every action infinitely often is GLIE (greedy in the limit with infinite exploration), the condition under which SARSA and Monte Carlo control converge; it is used on {{Model-free prediction and control|n:3c65c17b0d0d81898a0cd4c05c744969}}.

Optimistic initial values

Start every estimate far above any plausible reward (Sutton and Barto use +5 when true values are drawn from N(0, 1)). Whatever greedy tries disappoints it, so it moves on, and every arm is tried several times before the estimates settle ({{§2.6|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=56}}). It needs a constant step size (a sample average erases the optimism on the first pull), and the book calls it "a simple trick that can be quite effective on stationary problems, but it is far from being a generally useful approach": its drive is spent at the start. Made principled, it is R-MAX in full RL (section 6).

Upper confidence bound (UCB)

ε-greedy explores indiscriminately; UCB prefers arms that could plausibly be the best, given how uncertain their estimates are ({{§2.7, eq. 2.10|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=57}}):

At = argmaxa [ Qt(a) + c √( ln t / Nt(a) ) ]
Nt(a)
how often a has been pulled before t; an unpulled arm counts as maximising
√(ln t / N)
shrinks each time a is pulled and grows slowly while it is not, so every arm is eventually retried, ever more rarely
c
the confidence level; {{UCB1 (Auer et al. 2002)|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=3}} uses x̄j + √(2 ln n / nj), that is c = √2, for rewards in [0, 1] (the hero's left panel)

This is optimism in the face of uncertainty: act as if each arm is as good as it plausibly could be; either the optimism was right and you collect, or the pull shrinks the bonus. The book's caution: UCB "is more difficult than ε-greedy to extend beyond bandits", to nonstationary problems and to large state spaces where counting visits is not practical; section 6 is about how deep RL works around that.

Thompson sampling

Keep a posterior distribution over each arm's mean; at every step draw one sample from each and pull the arm whose sample is highest, then update that arm's posterior. An arm is pulled with exactly the probability that it is the best under the current belief ("probability matching"). For 0/1 rewards with a uniform prior the posterior is Beta(1 + successes, 1 + failures), so the algorithm is two counters per arm and one Beta draw. Uncertain arms have wide posteriors and sometimes draw high; a bad arm's posterior narrows below the best arm's and it stops being chosen. It is the oldest method here: {{Thompson (1933)|https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/25.3-4.285}} proposed it "for allocating experimental effort in two-armed bandit problems arising in clinical trials" ({{Russo et al., tutorial|https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.02038}}), and Sutton and Barto note that it "often performs similarly to the best of the distribution-free methods" ({{§2.10|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=65}}).

Gradient bandits: the one-state policy gradient (1 min)

Instead of estimating values, learn a preference Ht(a) per action and act by softmax, πt(a) = eHt(a) / Σb eHt(b). After each reward ({{§2.8, eq. 2.12|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=59}}):

H(At) ← H(At) + α (Rt − R̄t)(1 − πt(At)),   H(a) ← H(a) − α (Rt − R̄t) πt(a) for a ≠ At
R̄t
the baseline: the average reward so far (the book's experiments include Rt itself, per its footnote on p. 37)
Rt − R̄t
better than usual raises the chosen action's probability; worse lowers it; the others move the opposite way

The box in §2.8 proves that the expected update equals the gradient of expected reward, so this is stochastic gradient ascent: the one-state case of the policy gradient (REINFORCE with a baseline). The baseline does not change the expected update, only its variance; Figure 2.5 shows what happens without it when all rewards are shifted by +4 ({{10-armed testbed|#t-test}}, Fig. 2.5). The general case lives on {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}} (section 2 for the theorem, section 4 for baselines).

Across 2,000 tasks. The {{10-armed testbed|#t-test}} tab reruns Sutton and Barto's Figures 2.2 to 2.6 live, with Thompson sampling added and the nonstationary exercise. Its defaults reproduce the book's printed numbers independently: the best possible average reward is the expected maximum of ten standard normals, 1.539 by integration (book: "about 1.54"); greedy settles near 1.0 per step and finds the best arm in about 35% of tasks (book: "only about 1" and "approximately one-third"); ε = 0.1 stays below its 91% ceiling (src/recompute.py, 2,000 tasks).

4Regret, and the floor under it

Testbed curves average over a family of tasks; theory asks how much a strategy loses on any task against an oracle that always pulls the best arm. That loss is the regret, and it splits into pulls wasted on each bad arm times its gap:

Regret(T) = T μ* − E[ Σt=1..T μAt ] = Σa Δa E[Na(T)]
μ*
the best arm's mean; Δa = μ* − μa is arm a's gap
Na(T)
how often arm a is pulled in T steps (the regret decomposition, {{Lattimore and Szepesvári, Lemma 4.5|https://tor-lattimore.com/downloads/book/book.pdf#page=71}})

A strategy that pulls a bad arm a fixed fraction of the time (greedy stuck on it, ε-greedy with fixed ε) has regret growing linearly in T. How slowly can it grow? The Lai and Robbins lower bound ({{Lai and Robbins 1985|https://doi.org/10.1016/0196-8858(85)90002-8}}): any strategy that is good on every instance (regret o(Ta) for every a > 0) must pull each suboptimal arm at least about ln T / KL(pa, p*) times, so

lim infT→∞ Regret(T) / ln T ≥ Σa: Δa > 0 Δa / KL(pa, p*)
KL(pa, p*)
the Kullback-Leibler divergence between arm a's reward distribution and the best arm's: how much each pull tells them apart. For 0/1 rewards KL(p, q) = p ln(p/q) + (1 − p) ln((1 − p)/(1 − q))

To be sure an arm is worse you must pull it until the evidence rules it out, which takes more pulls the surer you need to be (ln T) and fewer the more distinguishable the arms (KL). The bound is asymptotic: it fixes the slope against ln T, not the regret at any finite T. (The original is paywalled; the statement follows {{Auer et al. 2002, p. 236|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=2}}: "IE[Tj(n)] ≥ (ln n)/D(pj||p*) asymptotically".)

At 10,000 pulls, 200 runs, close arms: greedy 607, ε-greedy 193, UCB1 261, Thompson 55; far arms: 650, 213, 170 and 20 (src/make_data.mjs, the page's engine). The Lai and Robbins constant is 12.28 per unit of ln T on close arms and 4.00 on far ones, from the formula above. On close arms UCB1 trails ε-greedy at this horizon: its constant is large when gaps are small.

Who reaches the floor. UCB1 is logarithmic after any number of plays for rewards in [0, 1], but the authors note its leading constant "8/Δi2 is worse than the corresponding constant 1/D(pj||p*) in Lai and Robbins' result" ({{Auer et al. 2002, Theorem 1|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=3}}). Thompson sampling had no regret analysis for almost eighty years; {{Kaufmann, Korda and Munos (2012)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.4217}} gave "the first finite-time analysis that matches the asymptotic rate given in the Lai and Robbins lower bound" for 0/1 rewards, so it is asymptotically optimal there. KL-UCB builds its confidence bound from the KL divergence itself and is the UCB-family method that closes the gap ({{Lattimore and Szepesvári, §10.2|https://tor-lattimore.com/downloads/book/book.pdf#page=146}}).

The finite-time bounds, and two other ways to score a bandit (2 min)
  • UCB1's bound: regret at most 8 ΣΔi>0 (ln n)/Δi + (1 + π²/3) Σ Δj after any number of plays n ({{Auer et al. 2002, Theorem 1|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=3}}).
  • εn-greedy: ε-greedy with εn = min(1, cK/(d²n)), where d is a lower bound on the gap, is also logarithmic ({{Auer et al., Theorem 3|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=5}}); it needs d, which you do not know in practice.
  • Thompson sampling's first proof: {{Agrawal and Goyal (2012)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.1797}} proved logarithmic regret "for the first time".
  • Thompson below the line is not a contradiction. The bound constrains the slope as T grows without limit; on two arms 0.6 and 0.5 the same engine's Thompson slope rises from 3.2 (103 to 104 pulls) to 4.2 (105 to 106), 20 runs, towards the bound's 4.9 (src/README.md).
  • Worst case. The bound above is instance-dependent: it blows up as a gap shrinks, even though tiny gaps cost little per pull. Over all instances, for any policy there is one with means in [0, 1] whose regret is at least (1/27) √((k − 1)n) ({{Lattimore and Szepesvári, Theorem 15.2|https://tor-lattimore.com/downloads/book/book.pdf#page=208}}): a square root of time, because an adversary can choose gaps of about √(k/n), too small to detect yet still costly every pull.
  • Bayesian regret (expected regret under a prior, where the Gittins index is optimal for discounted problems) is a third yardstick. Which one a paper optimises changes which algorithm "wins".

Where UCB went next. The same index applied at every node of a search tree is UCT ({{Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006|https://doi.org/10.1007/11871842_29}}), the selection rule of Monte Carlo tree search and, with a learned prior, of AlphaGo's search: see {{Model-based RL and planning|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8116be5fd39e10bd94c5}}. Agent57 chooses among its exploration policies with "a simplified sliding-window UCB with εUCB-greedy exploration" over the last 160 episodes ({{Badia et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350}}), because a plain UCB "will not be able to adapt to the changes of the reward through time".

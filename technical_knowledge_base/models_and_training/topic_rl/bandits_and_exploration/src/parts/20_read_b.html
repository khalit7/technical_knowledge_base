4Regret, and the floor under it

Testbed curves average over a family of tasks. The theory asks a sharper question: on any task, how much reward does a strategy lose against an oracle that always pulls the best arm? That loss is the regret:

Regret(T) = T μ* − E[ Σt=1..T μAt ] = Σa Δa E[Na(T)]
μ*
the best arm's mean; Δa = μ* − μa is arm a's gap
Na(T)
how often arm a is pulled in T steps; the second form (the regret decomposition, {{Lattimore and Szepesvári, Lemma 4.5|https://tor-lattimore.com/downloads/book/book.pdf#page=71}}) says regret is just the pulls wasted on each bad arm times its gap

A strategy that pulls a bad arm a fixed fraction of the time (greedy stuck on it, ε-greedy with fixed ε) has regret growing linearly in T. The question is how slowly it can grow.

The Lai and Robbins lower bound ({{Lai and Robbins 1985|https://doi.org/10.1016/0196-8858(85)90002-8}}): for any strategy that is good on every instance (its regret is o(Ta) for every a > 0), each suboptimal arm must be pulled at least about ln T / KL(pa, p*) times, so

lim infT→∞ Regret(T) / ln T ≥ Σa: Δa > 0 Δa / KL(pa, p*)
KL(pa, p*)
the Kullback-Leibler divergence between arm a's reward distribution and the best arm's: how many bits each pull gives towards telling them apart. For 0/1 rewards KL(p, q) = p ln(p/q) + (1 − p) ln((1 − p)/(1 − q))

The intuition: to be sure an arm is worse, you must pull it until the evidence rules out that it is the best one, and the number of pulls that takes grows with how sure you need to be (ln T) and shrinks with how distinguishable the arms are (KL). Arms that are close (small Δ, small KL) are both cheap to confuse and expensive to separate. The bound is asymptotic: it fixes the slope against ln T as T grows without limit, not the regret at any finite T. (The original paper is behind a paywall; the statement here follows the restatement in {{Auer et al. 2002, p. 236|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=2}}: "IE[Tj(n)] ≥ (ln n)/D(pj||p*) asymptotically".)

Strategies that reach logarithmic regret:

  • UCB1, for any rewards in [0, 1], has regret at most 8 ΣΔi>0 (ln n)/Δi + (1 + π²/3) Σ Δj after any number of plays n ({{Auer et al. 2002, Theorem 1|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=3}}). Logarithmic and finite-time, but the authors note its leading constant "8/Δi2 is worse than the corresponding constant 1/D(pj||p*) in Lai and Robbins' result".
  • εn-greedy: ε-greedy with εn = min(1, cK/(d²n)), where d is a lower bound on the gap, is also logarithmic ({{Auer et al., Theorem 3|https://homes.di.unimi.it/~cesabian/Pubblicazioni/ml-02.pdf#page=5}}); it needs d, which you do not know in practice.
  • Thompson sampling had no regret analysis for almost eighty years. {{Agrawal and Goyal (2012)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.1797}} proved logarithmic regret "for the first time", and {{Kaufmann, Korda and Munos (2012)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.4217}} gave "the first finite-time analysis that matches the asymptotic rate given in the Lai and Robbins lower bound" for 0/1 rewards. Thompson sampling is asymptotically optimal there; UCB1 is not (KL-UCB, which builds its confidence bound from the KL divergence itself, is the UCB-family method that closes the gap; {{Lattimore and Szepesvári, §10.2|https://tor-lattimore.com/downloads/book/book.pdf#page=146}}).

Try: switch to far arms: the bound's constant drops from 12.3 to 4.0 because well-separated arms are cheap to tell apart, and every method's regret falls. Change the number of runs to see which differences are noise.

Two other ways to score a bandit. The bound above is instance-dependent: it depends on the gaps, and blows up as a gap shrinks towards 0, even though tiny gaps cost little per pull. The worst case over all instances is a different floor: for any policy there is an instance with means in [0, 1] whose regret is at least (1/27) √((k − 1)n) ({{Lattimore and Szepesvári, Theorem 15.2|https://tor-lattimore.com/downloads/book/book.pdf#page=208}}), a square root of time rather than a logarithm, because the adversary can choose gaps of about √(k/n) that are too small to detect yet still cost something every pull. The Bayesian view (expected regret under a prior, where the Gittins index is optimal for discounted problems) is a third. Which one a paper optimises changes which algorithm "wins".

Where UCB went next. The same index applied at every node of a search tree is UCT ({{Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006|https://doi.org/10.1007/11871842_29}}), the selection rule behind Monte Carlo tree search, and through it AlphaGo's search (with a learned prior added): see {{Model-based RL and planning|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8116be5fd39e10bd94c5}}. And Agent57, the first deep RL agent above the human benchmark on all 57 Atari games, chooses among its exploration policies with a bandit: "a simplified sliding-window UCB with εUCB-greedy exploration" over the last 160 episodes ({{Badia et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.13350}}), because a plain UCB "will not be able to adapt to the changes of the reward through time".

5Contextual bandits, and where bandits run in production

A plain bandit has one situation. A contextual bandit sees a context first (a user's features, a patient's history, the page being viewed), then picks an action, then sees the reward of that action only. The goal is a policy from contexts to actions. Sutton and Barto call it associative search and place it exactly between the bandit and full RL: the action affects the reward, as in a bandit, but there is a situation to recognise; if actions also changed the next situation, it would be full RL ({{§2.9|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=63}}). It is the most deployed form of RL by far, because many products face exactly this loop: show something, see a click or not, never see what the other options would have earned.

LinUCB

{{Li, Chu, Langford and Schapire (2010)|https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.0146}} model each article's click probability as linear in a feature vector xt,a of the user and article, fit by ridge regression, and add an upper-confidence width (their eq. 5):

at = argmaxa ( xt,a⊤ θ̂a + α √( xt,a⊤ Aa−1 xt,a ) )
Aa
= I + Σ x x⊤ over the contexts where a was shown: how much evidence there is in each feature direction
θ̂a
= Aa−1 ba, with ba = Σ (reward) x: the ridge-regression estimate
√(x⊤A−1x)
the standard deviation of the prediction in this context's direction; it plays the part of √(ln t / N) in UCB

On the Yahoo! Front Page Today Module, with "over 33 million events", LinUCB gave "a 12.5% click lift compared to a standard context-free bandit algorithm, and the advantage becomes even greater when data gets more scarce". They evaluated offline, which is the second contribution people still use: replay logged traffic in which articles were chosen uniformly at random, keep only the events where the logged article matches the one the new policy would have chosen, and average their clicks. Because the logging was random, this "can provide provably unbiased evaluations" ({{Li, Chu, Langford and Wang 2011|https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.5956}}).

Where bandits are used

  • Recommendation and content: Yahoo! news (above). Microsoft's Decision Service, a general contextual-bandit system, reported "two live production deployments for content recommendation that achieved click-through improvements of 25-30%, another with 18% revenue lift in the landing page" ({{Agarwal et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.03966}}).
  • Advertising: Bing's adPredictor paper proposes that instead of always feeding the expected click rate to the auction, "the system can sample from the posterior weight distribution", an idea it traces to Thompson, which has "the effect of bubbling up ads about whose CTR the system has a high degree of uncertainty left" ({{Graepel et al. 2010, §5|https://icml.cc/Conferences/2010/papers/901.pdf#page=7}}; the paper says "can", not that this was the deployed default). Yahoo!'s {{Chapelle and Li (2011)|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2011/hash/e53a0a2978c28872a4505bdb51db06dc-Abstract.html}} found on display-ad selection and news recommendation that "Thompson sampling achieves state-of-the-art results, and in some cases significantly outperforms other alternatives like UCB", which is what revived the method.
  • Clinical trials: the field's origin (Thompson 1933) and its sharpest tension. Assigning more patients to the arm that looks better is the bandit's point and an ethical gain, but it leaves fewer patients on the other arm and weakens the trial's statistical power: {{Villar, Bowden and Wason (2015)|https://doi.org/10.1214/14-STS504}} find bandit designs "offer significant advantages, in terms of assigning more patients to better treatments, and severe limitations, in terms of their resulting statistical power", and propose a rule that keeps power. The cautionary example is the 1985 ECMO trial, which used randomised play-the-winner: one infant was assigned to conventional treatment and died, eleven to ECMO and all survived ({{Bartlett et al. 1985|https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3900904/}}), a result too lopsided for many to accept as evidence.
  • Inside other learners: Agent57's policy chooser and UCT's tree search (section 4).
What a bandit does not do
A bandit's action does not change the next context. If showing an article changes what the user does next week, or a treatment changes the patient's later state, the problem is full RL with delayed consequences, and a bandit optimising the immediate click can be confidently wrong about the long run.

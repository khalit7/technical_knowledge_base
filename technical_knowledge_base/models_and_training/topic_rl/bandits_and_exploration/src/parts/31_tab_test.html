The 10-armed testbed, run live

Sutton and Barto's testbed ({{§2.3|http://incompleteideas.net/book/RLbook2020.pdf#page=50}}): each task is a 10-armed bandit whose true values q*(a) are drawn from a standard normal; pulling arm a pays q*(a) plus standard normal noise. One run is 1,000 pulls on one task; a curve is the average over 2,000 tasks. Everything below is computed in your browser when you open a figure, in slices so the page stays responsive, with seeded random numbers: task i is the same task for every method, and the reward noise is drawn from its own stream, so methods differ only in the arms they choose. The engine is checked against an independent Python implementation (every value equal on 40 tasks of every setting).

Try: switch to 200 tasks and back to see how much of a curve's wiggle is noise; on Fig. 2.4, find the spike at step 11; on Fig. 2.6, compare each method's best setting and how wide its plateau is (the book: all are "fairly insensitive, performing well over a range of parameter values varying by about an order of magnitude"); on Nonstationary, watch sample averages fall behind.

What the book's figures show, and what they do not

  • Greedy is not a strawman. It is ahead for the first few steps on Fig. 2.2, and its failure is specific: in about two thirds of tasks the first sample of the best arm happened to be disappointing and it never looked again.
  • The ranking depends on the task. With reward variance 10 instead of 1 the book expects ε-greedy to gain on greedy; with zero variance greedy would find each arm's value after one pull and could do best (§2.3). Optimistic starts and sample averages both treat "the beginning of time" specially and cannot help when the task changes (§2.6), which the nonstationary run shows.
  • A testbed is not a guarantee. These are averages over one distribution of tasks; the regret bounds in the Reading tab are what hold for every task. On this testbed Thompson sampling also knows the exact prior the tasks come from.

Reproduction notes: ties between equal estimates are broken at random (as in the book); UCB treats an unpulled arm as maximising (eq. 2.10); the gradient baseline is the average of all rewards including the current one (the book's footnote on p. 37); "best possible" is the mean, over the tasks run, of each task's largest true value. Figure 2.6's parameter ranges (ε from 1/128 to 1/4, α from 1/32 to 2, c from 1/16 to 4, Q₀ from 1/4 to 4) follow {{Shangtong Zhang's reproduction|https://github.com/ShangtongZhang/reinforcement-learning-an-introduction/blob/master/chapter02/ten_armed_testbed.py}}, since the book's figure gives only the shared axis (1/128 to 4).

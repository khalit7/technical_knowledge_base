Bandits and exploration in one screen

An agent that learns from its own actions has to keep choosing between exploiting the action it currently believes is best and exploring an action that might be better. The cleanest form of the problem is the multi-armed bandit: one state, k actions with unknown average payoffs, and a score for the total reward collected. Bandits have a precise yardstick, regret, and a proven floor on it: any good strategy must lose reward growing at least like the logarithm of time. The strategies below differ in how they spend that loss.

1The exploration-exploitation trade-off

Exploitation uses current knowledge to take the action believed best, maximising reward now. Exploration takes an action that looks worse, to gather information that may pay off later. Silver's everyday examples (Silver, Lecture 9): go to your favourite restaurant or try a new one; show the most successful advert or a different one; drill for oil at the best known site or at a new one; play the move you believe is best or an experimental one. Both extremes fail. Pure exploitation gets stuck on the first decent option it finds, because it never collects the evidence that something else is better. Pure exploration never cashes in what it has learned.

Why this is special to reinforcement learning: in supervised learning the training labels arrive whatever the model predicts. Here the agent's own choices decide which data it sees, so a wrong belief about an action can never be corrected if the agent stops taking it. The value of trying an action is therefore partly the reward it pays now and partly the information it buys. Bandits isolate this: one situation repeated, no state, no delay between action and reward, so nothing is left except exploration (Sutton and Barto, chapter 2).

There is a sense in which the trade-off is "solved": if you have a prior over the arms and a known horizon, the best possible policy is the Bayes-optimal one, computed by planning over all future beliefs. It is almost never computed, because the tree of possible futures explodes; Sutton and Barto note that for 1,000 steps of a two-armed bandit with two possible rewards the tree would have 22000 leaves (§2.10). For the discounted, infinite-horizon case, Gittins (1979) showed that the Bayes-optimal policy reduces to an index computed per arm. Everything else on this page is a cheaper rule that gets close.

2The k-armed bandit and action values

At each step t the agent picks an action At from k options and receives a reward Rt drawn from that action's distribution. The value of an action is its expected reward, q*(a) = E[Rt | At = a]; if the agent knew it, the problem would be trivial. It keeps an estimate Qt(a) instead.

Qn+1 = Qn + (1/n) [Rn − Qn]
Qn
the average of the first n − 1 rewards from this action
Rn − Qn
the error: how surprising the new reward is
1/n
the step size; with 1/n the estimate is exactly the sample average

The sample average can be updated without storing past rewards, with the incremental rule above; its general form, NewEstimate ← OldEstimate + StepSize [Target − OldEstimate], recurs through all of RL, from TD learning to the deep methods (Sutton and Barto §2.4, eq. 2.3).

When the arms' values drift over time (a nonstationary problem), the sample average is a poor tracker: it weights a reward from step 1 as much as one from yesterday. A constant step size α ∈ (0, 1] fixes this. Unrolling the update gives an exponential recency-weighted average (§2.5, eq. 2.6):

Qn+1 = (1 − α)n Q1 + Σi=1..n α (1 − α)n−i Ri
(1 − α)n−i
a reward's weight decays geometrically with the number of later rewards; the weights sum to 1
(1 − α)n Q1
the initial estimate never fully disappears: a constant step size keeps a (shrinking) bias

Stochastic approximation says when a schedule of step sizes αn converges to the true value: Σ αn = ∞ (steps large enough to overcome any start) and Σ αn2 < ∞ (small enough to settle). The sample average's 1/n meets both; a constant α fails the second, which is exactly what lets it keep tracking a moving target (§2.5, eq. 2.7). The testbed tab's Nonstationary preset shows the difference: 10-armed testbed.

The initial estimate Q1 is a bias: with sample averages it disappears the first time an arm is pulled; with a constant α it is permanent, though decreasing (§2.6). That bias is the lever behind optimistic initial values, below.

3The strategies

Greedy and ε-greedy

Greedy always takes argmaxa Qt(a) (ties broken at random). It locks onto whichever arm looked good first. ε-greedy takes the greedy action with probability 1 − ε and otherwise picks uniformly among all m actions, so the greedy action has probability 1 − ε + ε/m and each other action ε/m; with ε = 0.1 and m = 4 that is 0.9 + 0.025 = 0.925 and 0.025 (Silver, Lecture 5). Every action is tried infinitely often, so every estimate converges; the price is that ε-greedy keeps picking bad arms at the same rate forever, so its regret grows linearly. Decaying ε towards 0 while still trying every action infinitely often is called GLIE (greedy in the limit with infinite exploration); it is the condition under which Monte Carlo control and SARSA converge to the optimal policy (Silver, Lecture 5; Model-free prediction and control).

Optimistic initial values

Set every initial estimate far above any plausible reward (Sutton and Barto use +5 when the true values are drawn from N(0, 1)). Whatever the greedy agent tries disappoints it, so it moves on to the next arm, and every arm is tried several times before the estimates settle (§2.6). It needs a constant step size: with sample averages the optimism is erased by the first pull of each arm. The book calls it "a simple trick that can be quite effective on stationary problems, but it is far from being a generally useful approach": its drive to explore is spent at the start, so it cannot respond when the task changes. The same idea, made principled, is R-MAX in full RL (section 6).

Upper confidence bound (UCB)

ε-greedy explores indiscriminately. Better: prefer arms that could plausibly be the best, given how uncertain their estimates are (§2.7, eq. 2.10):

At = argmaxa [ Qt(a) + c √( ln t / Nt(a) ) ]
Nt(a)
how often a has been pulled before t; an unpulled arm counts as maximising
√(ln t / N)
shrinks each time a is pulled, grows slowly (logarithmically) while it is not, so every arm is eventually retried, ever more rarely
c
the confidence level; UCB1 (Auer et al. 2002) uses x̄j + √(2 ln n / nj), that is c = √2, for rewards in [0, 1]

This is optimism in the face of uncertainty: act as if each arm is as good as it plausibly could be. Either the optimism was right and you collect, or it was wrong and the pull shrinks the bonus. The book adds a caution: UCB "is more difficult than ε-greedy to extend beyond bandits", to nonstationary problems and to large state spaces with function approximation, where counting visits is not practical; section 6 is about how deep RL works around that.

Gradient bandits

Instead of estimating values, learn a preference Ht(a) per action and act by softmax, πt(a) = eHt(a) / Σb eHt(b). After each reward (§2.8, eq. 2.12):

H(At) ← H(At) + α (Rt − R̄t)(1 − πt(At)),   H(a) ← H(a) − α (Rt − R̄t) πt(a) for a ≠ At
R̄t
the baseline: the average reward so far (the book's experiments include Rt itself, per its footnote on p. 37)
Rt − R̄t
better than usual raises the chosen action's probability; worse lowers it; the others move the opposite way

The box in §2.8 proves that the expected update equals the gradient of expected reward, so this is stochastic gradient ascent: the one-state case of the policy gradient (REINFORCE with a baseline). The baseline does not change the expected update, only its variance; Figure 2.5 shows what happens without it when all rewards are shifted by +4 (10-armed testbed, Fig. 2.5). The full derivation and the general case live on Policy gradients and actor-critic.

Thompson sampling

Keep a posterior distribution over each arm's mean. At every step draw one sample from each posterior and pull the arm whose sample is highest; update that arm's posterior with the reward. An arm is pulled with exactly the probability that it is the best arm under the current belief ("probability matching"). For 0/1 rewards with a uniform prior the posterior is Beta(1 + successes, 1 + failures), so the whole algorithm is two counters per arm and one Beta draw. It is the oldest method on the page: Thompson (1933) proposed it "for allocating experimental effort in two-armed bandit problems arising in clinical trials" (Russo et al., tutorial). Uncertain arms have wide posteriors, so they sometimes produce a high draw and get tried; as evidence accumulates a bad arm's posterior narrows below the best arm's and it stops being chosen. Sutton and Barto note that it "often performs similarly to the best of the distribution-free methods" (§2.10).

4Regret, and the floor under it

Testbed curves average over a family of tasks. The theory asks a sharper question: on any task, how much reward does a strategy lose against an oracle that always pulls the best arm? That loss is the regret:

Regret(T) = T μ* − E[ Σt=1..T μAt ] = Σa Δa E[Na(T)]
μ*
the best arm's mean; Δa = μ* − μa is arm a's gap
Na(T)
how often arm a is pulled in T steps; the second form (the regret decomposition, Lattimore and Szepesvári, Lemma 4.5) says regret is just the pulls wasted on each bad arm times its gap

A strategy that pulls a bad arm a fixed fraction of the time (greedy stuck on it, ε-greedy with fixed ε) has regret growing linearly in T. The question is how slowly it can grow.

The Lai and Robbins lower bound (Lai and Robbins 1985): for any strategy that is good on every instance (its regret is o(Ta) for every a > 0), each suboptimal arm must be pulled at least about ln T / KL(pa, p*) times, so

lim infT→∞ Regret(T) / ln T ≥ Σa: Δa > 0 Δa / KL(pa, p*)
KL(pa, p*)
the Kullback-Leibler divergence between arm a's reward distribution and the best arm's: how many bits each pull gives towards telling them apart. For 0/1 rewards KL(p, q) = p ln(p/q) + (1 − p) ln((1 − p)/(1 − q))

The intuition: to be sure an arm is worse, you must pull it until the evidence rules out that it is the best one, and the number of pulls that takes grows with how sure you need to be (ln T) and shrinks with how distinguishable the arms are (KL). Arms that are close (small Δ, small KL) are both cheap to confuse and expensive to separate. The bound is asymptotic: it fixes the slope against ln T as T grows without limit, not the regret at any finite T. (The original paper is behind a paywall; the statement here follows the restatement in Auer et al. 2002, p. 236: "IE[Tj(n)] ≥ (ln n)/D(pj||p*) asymptotically".)

Strategies that reach logarithmic regret:

  • UCB1, for any rewards in [0, 1], has regret at most 8 ΣΔi>0 (ln n)/Δi + (1 + π²/3) Σ Δj after any number of plays n (Auer et al. 2002, Theorem 1). Logarithmic and finite-time, but the authors note its leading constant "8/Δi2 is worse than the corresponding constant 1/D(pj||p*) in Lai and Robbins' result".
  • εn-greedy: ε-greedy with εn = min(1, cK/(d²n)), where d is a lower bound on the gap, is also logarithmic (Auer et al., Theorem 3); it needs d, which you do not know in practice.
  • Thompson sampling had no regret analysis for almost eighty years. Agrawal and Goyal (2012) proved logarithmic regret "for the first time", and Kaufmann, Korda and Munos (2012) gave "the first finite-time analysis that matches the asymptotic rate given in the Lai and Robbins lower bound" for 0/1 rewards. Thompson sampling is asymptotically optimal there; UCB1 is not (KL-UCB, which builds its confidence bound from the KL divergence itself, is the UCB-family method that closes the gap; Lattimore and Szepesvári, §10.2).

Try: switch to far arms: the bound's constant drops from 12.3 to 4.0 because well-separated arms are cheap to tell apart, and every method's regret falls. Change the number of runs to see which differences are noise.

Two other ways to score a bandit. The bound above is instance-dependent: it depends on the gaps, and blows up as a gap shrinks towards 0, even though tiny gaps cost little per pull. The worst case over all instances is a different floor: for any policy there is an instance with means in [0, 1] whose regret is at least (1/27) √((k − 1)n) (Lattimore and Szepesvári, Theorem 15.2), a square root of time rather than a logarithm, because the adversary can choose gaps of about √(k/n) that are too small to detect yet still cost something every pull. The Bayesian view (expected regret under a prior, where the Gittins index is optimal for discounted problems) is a third. Which one a paper optimises changes which algorithm "wins".

Where UCB went next. The same index applied at every node of a search tree is UCT (Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006), the selection rule behind Monte Carlo tree search, and through it AlphaGo's search (with a learned prior added): see Model-based RL and planning. And Agent57, the first deep RL agent above the human benchmark on all 57 Atari games, chooses among its exploration policies with a bandit: "a simplified sliding-window UCB with εUCB-greedy exploration" over the last 160 episodes (Badia et al. 2020), because a plain UCB "will not be able to adapt to the changes of the reward through time".

5Contextual bandits, and where bandits run in production

A plain bandit has one situation. A contextual bandit sees a context first (a user's features, a patient's history, the page being viewed), then picks an action, then sees the reward of that action only. The goal is a policy from contexts to actions. Sutton and Barto call it associative search and place it exactly between the bandit and full RL: the action affects the reward, as in a bandit, but there is a situation to recognise; if actions also changed the next situation, it would be full RL (§2.9). It is the most deployed form of RL by far, because many products face exactly this loop: show something, see a click or not, never see what the other options would have earned.

LinUCB

Li, Chu, Langford and Schapire (2010) model each article's click probability as linear in a feature vector xt,a of the user and article, fit by ridge regression, and add an upper-confidence width (their eq. 5):

at = argmaxa ( xt,a⊤ θ̂a + α √( xt,a⊤ Aa−1 xt,a ) )
Aa
= I + Σ x x⊤ over the contexts where a was shown: how much evidence there is in each feature direction
θ̂a
= Aa−1 ba, with ba = Σ (reward) x: the ridge-regression estimate
√(x⊤A−1x)
the standard deviation of the prediction in this context's direction; it plays the part of √(ln t / N) in UCB

On the Yahoo! Front Page Today Module, with "over 33 million events", LinUCB gave "a 12.5% click lift compared to a standard context-free bandit algorithm, and the advantage becomes even greater when data gets more scarce". They evaluated offline, which is the second contribution people still use: replay logged traffic in which articles were chosen uniformly at random, keep only the events where the logged article matches the one the new policy would have chosen, and average their clicks. Because the logging was random, this "can provide provably unbiased evaluations" (Li, Chu, Langford and Wang 2011).

Where bandits are used

  • Recommendation and content: Yahoo! news (above). Microsoft's Decision Service, a general contextual-bandit system, reported "two live production deployments for content recommendation that achieved click-through improvements of 25-30%, another with 18% revenue lift in the landing page" (Agarwal et al. 2016).
  • Advertising: Bing's adPredictor paper proposes that instead of always feeding the expected click rate to the auction, "the system can sample from the posterior weight distribution", an idea it traces to Thompson, which has "the effect of bubbling up ads about whose CTR the system has a high degree of uncertainty left" (Graepel et al. 2010, §5; the paper says "can", not that this was the deployed default). Yahoo!'s Chapelle and Li (2011) found on display-ad selection and news recommendation that "Thompson sampling achieves state-of-the-art results, and in some cases significantly outperforms other alternatives like UCB", which is what revived the method.
  • Clinical trials: the field's origin (Thompson 1933) and its sharpest tension. Assigning more patients to the arm that looks better is the bandit's point and an ethical gain, but it leaves fewer patients on the other arm and weakens the trial's statistical power: Villar, Bowden and Wason (2015) find bandit designs "offer significant advantages, in terms of assigning more patients to better treatments, and severe limitations, in terms of their resulting statistical power", and propose a rule that keeps power. The cautionary example is the 1985 ECMO trial, which used randomised play-the-winner: one infant was assigned to conventional treatment and died, eleven to ECMO and all survived (Bartlett et al. 1985), a result too lopsided for many to accept as evidence.
  • Inside other learners: Agent57's policy chooser and UCT's tree search (section 4).
What a bandit does not do
A bandit's action does not change the next context. If showing an article changes what the user does next week, or a treatment changes the patient's later state, the problem is full RL with delayed consequences, and a bandit optimising the immediate click can be confidently wrong about the long run.

6Exploration in full RL

With states, an exploratory action's payoff may only show up many steps later, after a specific sequence of further actions. That changes the problem in three ways.

  • Dithering is exponentially slow. ε-greedy and softmax noise explore one step at a time. If a reward sits at the end of a chain of N decisions where only one of two actions keeps you on track, a uniformly random policy reaches it with probability 2−N per attempt, so it needs about 2N attempts derived. What is needed is deep (temporally extended) exploration: commit to a plan for finding out, across many steps. Osband et al. put it this way: "Unlike dithering strategies such as epsilon-greedy exploration, bootstrapped DQN carries out temporally-extended (or deep) exploration; this can lead to exponentially faster learning" (Osband et al. 2016).
  • Sparse rewards give no gradient. If almost every episode returns 0, every action looks the same and value estimates have nothing to learn from until something succeeds by chance.
  • Distractors trap exploiters. A small nearby reward is found first, and a greedy-ish learner stops looking for the larger one far away: greedy on the testbed, scaled up.

The families of answers, roughly in the order they appeared:

Optimism with counts (tabular, with guarantees)

E3 (Kearns and Singh 2002) and R-MAX (Brafman and Tennenholtz 2002) gave the first polynomial-time guarantees for exploring an MDP. R-MAX's model "is initialized in an optimistic fashion: all actions in all states return the maximal possible reward (hence the name)"; once a state-action pair has been tried enough times its true model replaces the optimistic one. MBIE-EB (Strehl and Littman 2008) turns this into a bonus β/√N(s, a) added to the reward in the Bellman equation (as restated by Bellemare et al. 2016), the direct MDP analogue of UCB. UCRL2 (Jaksch, Ortner and Auer 2010) plans optimistically within confidence sets on the transition model and proves regret Õ(DS√(AT)) against a lower bound Ω(√(DSAT)) (D the MDP's diameter, S states, A actions). All of them need to count visits to each state, which is impossible when states are images.

Pseudo-counts and hashing (counts for large state spaces)

Bellemare et al. (2016) derive a pseudo-count N̂(x) from a density model of observations: how much the model's probability of x rises after one more look at x says how familiar x is. The bonus is β (N̂ + 0.01)−1/2 with β = 0.05. On Montezuma's Revenge their agent "consistently navigates through 15 rooms" of the first level's 24 within 50 million frames, where the agent without the bonus saw two, and scored 3,439 on average by 100 million frames. Tang et al. (2017) count hashed states instead (SimHash of pixels or of learned features), bonus β/√n(φ(s)).

Curiosity and prediction error

Reward the agent where its model of the world is wrong. The Intrinsic Curiosity Module uses "the error in an agent's ability to predict the consequence of its own actions in a visual feature space learned by a self-supervised inverse dynamics model" (Pathak et al. 2017); learning features through an inverse model is meant to ignore what the agent cannot affect. A large-scale study trained agents with curiosity alone, no extrinsic reward, on 54 environments, and documented the main failure, the noisy TV: add a TV with a channel-changing action to a maze and "the presence of the TV drastically slows down learning", because unpredictable noise stays surprising forever. Prediction error mixes up "new" with "random".

Random Network Distillation (RND)

Burda et al. (2018) fix the target: a randomly initialised network that is never trained maps each observation to a feature vector, and a second network is trained to predict those features. Its error is high on observations unlike those seen before and falls with familiarity, but because the target is a deterministic function of the observation, noise does not keep it high. RND was the "first method that achieves better than average human performance on this game without using demonstrations or having access to the underlying state": a mean of 8,152 on Montezuma's Revenge against an average human 4,753 (Table 1), with the best agent often finding "22 out of the 24 rooms on the first level".

Remember and return: NGU, Agent57, Go-Explore

Never Give Up combines a per-episode novelty bonus with RND's lifelong one and was the "first algorithm to achieve non-zero rewards (with a mean score of 8,400) in the game of Pitfall! without using demonstrations" (Badia et al. 2020a); Agent57 adds the bandit meta-controller of section 4 to choose how much to explore, and is "the first deep RL agent that outperforms the standard human benchmark on all 57 Atari games" (Badia et al. 2020b; see the Milestones and benchmarks tab of Topic: rl for its scores, including two mistyped MuZero entries found there). Go-Explore (Ecoffet et al. 2019) splits the problem: keep an archive of distinct states ("cells") reached so far, pick a promising one, return to it (by restoring the emulator's state, which needs a resettable simulator), explore from there, and add new cells; then train a robust policy from the best trajectories. Without domain knowledge its policies averaged 43,763 on Montezuma's Revenge, "higher than the human expert mean of 34,900", and solved level 1 in 99.8% of attempts. Its journal version, "First return, then explore", adds a policy that learns to return without resets and reports that it "solves all heretofore unsolved Atari games"; with easy-to-provide domain knowledge it reaches 102,571 on Pitfall and 1,731,645 on Montezuma's Revenge (figures from the preprint).

Uncertainty about values: posterior sampling in deep RL

Thompson sampling's deep-RL form keeps several plausible value functions and acts greedily with respect to one of them for a whole episode, which gives the temporally extended exploration that per-step noise lacks: Bootstrapped DQN trains several Q heads on bootstrapped data (Osband et al. 2016); the idea is developed in Osband, Van Roy, Russo and Wen (2019). NoisyNet instead learns noise in the network's weights, replacing ε-greedy and entropy bonuses, with initial noise scale σ0 = 0.5 (Fortunato et al. 2017); it is one of the six extensions Rainbow combines (Method atlas tab of Topic: rl; details on Value-based deep RL).

Entropy bonuses (policy methods)

Policy-gradient methods explore by sampling from their own stochastic policy, and the danger is that it collapses onto one action too early. A3C adds the policy's entropy to the objective, "with a weight β = 0.01 for all Atari and TORCS experiments" (Mnih et al. 2016); SAC makes it the objective itself, J(π) = Σt E[r(st, at) + α H(π(·|st))], "to succeed at the task while acting as randomly as possible", with the temperature α weighing entropy against reward (Haarnoja et al. 2018). Both are local: they keep the policy from collapsing, but do not by themselves send the agent anywhere new. The mechanics are on Policy gradients and actor-critic.

One idea under all of these
Every method on this page either adds a bonus where knowledge is thin (UCB, counts, pseudo-counts, RND), starts optimistic and lets evidence correct it (optimistic values, R-MAX), or acts on a plausible guess drawn from its uncertainty (Thompson sampling, bootstrapped and noisy networks). Randomness alone (ε-greedy, entropy) is the cheap fallback that works when rewards are dense.

7Exploration when training language models

RL on a pretrained language model starts from a policy that already produces sensible answers, so exploration is mostly inherited from pretraining and controlled through sampling: several responses per prompt at a temperature around 1, so that some are right and some wrong and the advantage has something to compare (GRPO's group). Three findings frame what this exploration can and cannot do:

  • RL sharpens more than it discovers. Yue et al. (2025) find that "while RLVR-trained models outperform their base models at small k (e.g., k = 1), the base models achieve a higher pass@k score when k is large", and conclude that the reasoning abilities they measured "originate from and are bounded by the base model". In bandit terms: RL concentrates probability on arms the base model already pulls sometimes; it rarely finds arms the base model never pulls.
  • Entropy collapses, and that caps performance. Cui et al. (2025) fit "a transformation equation R = −a eH + b between entropy H and downstream performance R", so that when entropy runs out "the ceiling is fully predictable H = 0, R = −a + b"; they trace the entropy change to "the covariance between action probability and the change in logits" and propose Clip-Cov and KL-Cov to slow it.
  • Clip asymmetrically to keep exploring. DAPO's "Clip-Higher ... promotes the diversity of the system and avoids entropy collapse" by letting low-probability tokens grow more than PPO's symmetric clip allows: εlow = 0.2, εhigh = 0.28 (Yu et al. 2025).

The algorithms (PPO for RLHF, GRPO, RLVR, DAPO) are on RL for LLMs; the pipeline around them on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR; the paper pages DeepSeekMath (GRPO) and DeepSeek-R1 (RL from a base model without supervised warm-up) show what this exploration produced at scale.

Common mistakes

Breaking ties by taking the first arm
With all estimates equal at the start, "argmax" in most libraries returns the first index, so a greedy agent always starts on arm 1 and the results depend on arm order. Sutton and Barto break ties at random; so does every simulation on this page except the Reading animation, where the fixed tie rule is visible and stated.
Reading one run, or a few, as a result
A single bandit run is dominated by luck (try the five seeds in section 3). The book averages 2,000 tasks; on the testbed tab, compare 200 with 2,000 to see how much of a curve's wiggle is noise.
Using UCB's constant outside its scale
UCB1's √(2 ln n / nj) assumes rewards in [0, 1]. With rewards in the hundreds the bonus is negligible and UCB is greedy; with rewards in [0, 0.01] it explores almost uniformly. Rescale the rewards or tune c (Figure 2.6 shows how much c matters).
Treating optimistic initial values as a general method
Optimism explores only at the start. With sample averages it vanishes after one pull of each arm, and in a nonstationary task it never comes back (§2.6). R-MAX makes it principled by tying optimism to counts.
Using sample averages on a problem that drifts
The 1/n step gives yesterday's reward the same weight as last year's, so the estimate follows a moving arm ever more slowly. Use a constant step size (the testbed tab's Nonstationary preset shows the gap).
Reading the Lai and Robbins bound as a finite-time floor
It is a statement about the slope against ln T in the limit. A good algorithm can sit below the line at any T you can afford to run (the regret chart in section 4 shows Thompson sampling doing exactly that).
Crediting Thompson sampling for a matched prior
On the 10-armed testbed Thompson sampling with a N(0, 1) prior uses the exact distribution the tasks are drawn from. With a wrong or overconfident prior it can under-explore; report the prior with the result.
Evaluating a new bandit policy on logs from the old one
Logged clicks only show the arm the old policy chose. Li et al.'s replay estimator is unbiased because the logging policy chose uniformly at random; with a non-random logging policy you need its propensities (inverse propensity weighting) or a randomised slice of traffic.
Expecting a novelty bonus to ignore noise
Prediction-error curiosity is drawn to anything unpredictable (the noisy TV). RND's fixed random target and counts of states avoid that particular trap, but any bonus can distract from the task if it is not scaled down as the task reward appears.
Comparing hard-exploration scores across papers
Human baselines, frame budgets, sticky actions, emulator resets and domain knowledge all differ (see the Montezuma's Revenge table). Compare within one paper's table, or re-run.
Thinking ε-greedy is always the wrong choice
In Auer et al.'s experiments "an optimally tuned εn-GREEDY performs almost always best" (Auer et al. 2002); its weakness is that the tuning depends on the unknown gap, and in full RL that its exploration is shallow. With dense rewards (most of Atari) it is still the default.

The 10-armed testbed, run live

Sutton and Barto's testbed (§2.3): each task is a 10-armed bandit whose true values q*(a) are drawn from a standard normal; pulling arm a pays q*(a) plus standard normal noise. One run is 1,000 pulls on one task; a curve is the average over 2,000 tasks. Everything below is computed in your browser when you open a figure, in slices so the page stays responsive, with seeded random numbers: task i is the same task for every method, and the reward noise is drawn from its own stream, so methods differ only in the arms they choose. The engine is checked against an independent Python implementation (every value equal on 40 tasks of every setting).

Try: switch to 200 tasks and back to see how much of a curve's wiggle is noise; on Fig. 2.4, find the spike at step 11; on Fig. 2.6, compare each method's best setting and how wide its plateau is (the book: all are "fairly insensitive, performing well over a range of parameter values varying by about an order of magnitude"); on Nonstationary, watch sample averages fall behind.

What the book's figures show, and what they do not

  • Greedy is not a strawman. It is ahead for the first few steps on Fig. 2.2, and its failure is specific: in about two thirds of tasks the first sample of the best arm happened to be disappointing and it never looked again.
  • The ranking depends on the task. With reward variance 10 instead of 1 the book expects ε-greedy to gain on greedy; with zero variance greedy would find each arm's value after one pull and could do best (§2.3). Optimistic starts and sample averages both treat "the beginning of time" specially and cannot help when the task changes (§2.6), which the nonstationary run shows.
  • A testbed is not a guarantee. These are averages over one distribution of tasks; the regret bounds in the Reading tab are what hold for every task. On this testbed Thompson sampling also knows the exact prior the tasks come from.

Reproduction notes: ties between equal estimates are broken at random (as in the book); UCB treats an unpulled arm as maximising (eq. 2.10); the gradient baseline is the average of all rewards including the current one (the book's footnote on p. 37); "best possible" is the mean, over the tasks run, of each task's largest true value. Figure 2.6's parameter ranges (ε from 1/128 to 1/4, α from 1/32 to 2, c from 1/16 to 4, Q₀ from 1/4 to 4) follow Shangtong Zhang's reproduction, since the book's figure gives only the shared axis (1/128 to 4).

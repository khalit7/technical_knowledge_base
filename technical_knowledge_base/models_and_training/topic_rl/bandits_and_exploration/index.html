Bandits and exploration: the idea

An agent only sees the reward of the action it takes, so it must spend some reward finding out; random exploration pays the same price every step forever (linear regret), while exploring where its estimates are uncertain makes the price shrink, growing only like the logarithm of time, which is the proven floor.

1The exploration-exploitation trade-off

Exploitation takes the action believed best, maximising reward now; exploration takes one that looks worse, to buy information that may pay later (Silver's examples: your favourite restaurant or a new one, the best-known advert or a different one; Silver, Lecture 9). Pure exploitation gets stuck on the first decent option, because it never collects the evidence that something else is better; pure exploration never cashes in.

What makes this special to reinforcement learning: in supervised learning the labels arrive whatever the model predicts, but here the agent's own choices decide which data it sees, so a wrong belief about an action is never corrected if the agent stops taking it. A multi-armed bandit isolates exactly this: one situation repeated, k actions with unknown mean payoffs, no state and no delay, so nothing is left but exploration (Sutton and Barto, chapter 2).

The trade-off is "solved" in theory: Bayes-optimal play and the Gittins index (1 min)

With a prior over the arms and a known horizon, the best possible policy is the Bayes-optimal one, computed by planning over all future beliefs. It is almost never computed, because the tree of possible futures explodes: Sutton and Barto note that for 1,000 steps of a two-armed bandit with two possible rewards the tree would have 22000 leaves (§2.10). For the discounted, infinite-horizon case, Gittins (1979) showed that the Bayes-optimal policy reduces to an index computed per arm. Everything else on this page is a cheaper rule that gets close.

2The k-armed bandit and action values

At each step t the agent picks an action At from k options and receives a reward Rt from that action's distribution. An action's value is its expected reward, q*(a) = E[Rt | At = a]; the agent keeps an estimate Qt(a), usually the sample average, updated without storing past rewards:

Qn+1 = Qn + αn [Rn − Qn],   αn = 1/n (sample average) or α (constant)
Rn − Qn
the error: how surprising the new reward is. The form NewEstimate ← OldEstimate + StepSize [Target − OldEstimate] recurs through all of RL, from TD learning to the deep methods (Sutton and Barto §2.4, eq. 2.3)
constant α
unrolls to an exponential recency-weighted average, Qn+1 = (1 − α)n Q1 + Σi α (1 − α)n−i Ri (§2.5, eq. 2.6): weights decay geometrically with age and sum to 1

A constant step forgets old rewards; the sample average never does

When a step-size schedule converges: the stochastic approximation conditions (under 1 min)

A schedule αn converges to the true value when Σ αn = ∞ (steps large enough to overcome any start) and Σ αn2 < ∞ (small enough to settle). The sample average's 1/n meets both; a constant α fails the second, which is exactly what lets it keep tracking a moving target (§2.5, eq. 2.7). The initial estimate Q1 is a bias: with sample averages it disappears the first time an arm is pulled; with a constant α it is permanent, though decreasing.

Greedy and ε-greedy

Greedy always takes argmaxa Qt(a) and locks onto whichever arm looked good first. ε-greedy takes the greedy action with probability 1 − ε and otherwise picks uniformly among all m actions, so the greedy action has probability 1 − ε + ε/m: with ε = 0.1 and m = 4 that is 0.9 + 0.025 = 0.925, and each other action 0.025 (Silver, Lecture 5). Every estimate converges, but ε-greedy keeps picking bad arms at the same rate forever, so its regret grows linearly. Decaying ε towards 0 while still trying every action infinitely often is GLIE (greedy in the limit with infinite exploration), the condition under which SARSA and Monte Carlo control converge; it is used on Model-free prediction and control.

Optimistic initial values

Start every estimate far above any plausible reward (Sutton and Barto use +5 when true values are drawn from N(0, 1)). Whatever greedy tries disappoints it, so it moves on, and every arm is tried several times before the estimates settle (§2.6). It needs a constant step size (a sample average erases the optimism on the first pull), and the book calls it "a simple trick that can be quite effective on stationary problems, but it is far from being a generally useful approach": its drive is spent at the start. Made principled, it is R-MAX in full RL (section 6).

Upper confidence bound (UCB)

ε-greedy explores indiscriminately; UCB prefers arms that could plausibly be the best, given how uncertain their estimates are (§2.7, eq. 2.10):

At = argmaxa [ Qt(a) + c √( ln t / Nt(a) ) ]
Nt(a)
how often a has been pulled before t; an unpulled arm counts as maximising
√(ln t / N)
shrinks each time a is pulled and grows slowly while it is not, so every arm is eventually retried, ever more rarely
c
the confidence level; UCB1 (Auer et al. 2002) uses x̄j + √(2 ln n / nj), that is c = √2, for rewards in [0, 1] (the hero's left panel)

This is optimism in the face of uncertainty: act as if each arm is as good as it plausibly could be; either the optimism was right and you collect, or the pull shrinks the bonus. The book's caution: UCB "is more difficult than ε-greedy to extend beyond bandits", to nonstationary problems and to large state spaces where counting visits is not practical; section 6 is about how deep RL works around that.

Thompson sampling

Keep a posterior distribution over each arm's mean; at every step draw one sample from each and pull the arm whose sample is highest, then update that arm's posterior. An arm is pulled with exactly the probability that it is the best under the current belief ("probability matching"). For 0/1 rewards with a uniform prior the posterior is Beta(1 + successes, 1 + failures), so the algorithm is two counters per arm and one Beta draw. Uncertain arms have wide posteriors and sometimes draw high; a bad arm's posterior narrows below the best arm's and it stops being chosen. It is the oldest method here: Thompson (1933) proposed it "for allocating experimental effort in two-armed bandit problems arising in clinical trials" (Russo et al., tutorial), and Sutton and Barto note that it "often performs similarly to the best of the distribution-free methods" (§2.10).

Gradient bandits: the one-state policy gradient (1 min)

Instead of estimating values, learn a preference Ht(a) per action and act by softmax, πt(a) = eHt(a) / Σb eHt(b). After each reward (§2.8, eq. 2.12):

H(At) ← H(At) + α (Rt − R̄t)(1 − πt(At)),   H(a) ← H(a) − α (Rt − R̄t) πt(a) for a ≠ At
R̄t
the baseline: the average reward so far (the book's experiments include Rt itself, per its footnote on p. 37)
Rt − R̄t
better than usual raises the chosen action's probability; worse lowers it; the others move the opposite way

The box in §2.8 proves that the expected update equals the gradient of expected reward, so this is stochastic gradient ascent: the one-state case of the policy gradient (REINFORCE with a baseline). The baseline does not change the expected update, only its variance; Figure 2.5 shows what happens without it when all rewards are shifted by +4 (10-armed testbed, Fig. 2.5). The general case lives on Policy gradients and actor-critic (section 2 for the theorem, section 4 for baselines).

4Regret, and the floor under it

Testbed curves average over a family of tasks; theory asks how much a strategy loses on any task against an oracle that always pulls the best arm. That loss is the regret, and it splits into pulls wasted on each bad arm times its gap:

Regret(T) = T μ* − E[ Σt=1..T μAt ] = Σa Δa E[Na(T)]
μ*
the best arm's mean; Δa = μ* − μa is arm a's gap
Na(T)
how often arm a is pulled in T steps (the regret decomposition, Lattimore and Szepesvári, Lemma 4.5)

A strategy that pulls a bad arm a fixed fraction of the time (greedy stuck on it, ε-greedy with fixed ε) has regret growing linearly in T. How slowly can it grow? The Lai and Robbins lower bound (Lai and Robbins 1985): any strategy that is good on every instance (regret o(Ta) for every a > 0) must pull each suboptimal arm at least about ln T / KL(pa, p*) times, so

lim infT→∞ Regret(T) / ln T ≥ Σa: Δa > 0 Δa / KL(pa, p*)
KL(pa, p*)
the Kullback-Leibler divergence between arm a's reward distribution and the best arm's: how much each pull tells them apart. For 0/1 rewards KL(p, q) = p ln(p/q) + (1 − p) ln((1 − p)/(1 − q))

To be sure an arm is worse you must pull it until the evidence rules it out, which takes more pulls the surer you need to be (ln T) and fewer the more distinguishable the arms (KL). The bound is asymptotic: it fixes the slope against ln T, not the regret at any finite T. (The original is paywalled; the statement follows Auer et al. 2002, p. 236: "IE[Tj(n)] ≥ (ln n)/D(pj||p*) asymptotically".)

The Lai and Robbins constant is 12.28 per unit of ln T on close arms and 4.00 on far ones, from the formula above. On close arms UCB1 trails ε-greedy at this horizon: its constant is large when gaps are small.

Who reaches the floor. UCB1 is logarithmic after any number of plays for rewards in [0, 1], but the authors note its leading constant "8/Δi2 is worse than the corresponding constant 1/D(pj||p*) in Lai and Robbins' result" (Auer et al. 2002, Theorem 1). Thompson sampling had no regret analysis for almost eighty years; Kaufmann, Korda and Munos (2012) gave "the first finite-time analysis that matches the asymptotic rate given in the Lai and Robbins lower bound" for 0/1 rewards, so it is asymptotically optimal there. KL-UCB builds its confidence bound from the KL divergence itself and is the UCB-family method that closes the gap (Lattimore and Szepesvári, §10.2).

The finite-time bounds, and two other ways to score a bandit (2 min)
  • UCB1's bound: regret at most 8 ΣΔi>0 (ln n)/Δi + (1 + π²/3) Σ Δj after any number of plays n (Auer et al. 2002, Theorem 1).
  • εn-greedy: ε-greedy with εn = min(1, cK/(d²n)), where d is a lower bound on the gap, is also logarithmic (Auer et al., Theorem 3); it needs d, which you do not know in practice.
  • Thompson sampling's first proof: Agrawal and Goyal (2012) proved logarithmic regret "for the first time".
  • Thompson below the line is not a contradiction. The bound constrains the slope as T grows without limit; on two arms 0.6 and 0.5 the same engine's Thompson slope rises from 3.2 (103 to 104 pulls) to 4.2 (105 to 106), 20 runs, towards the bound's 4.9 (src/README.md).
  • Worst case. The bound above is instance-dependent: it blows up as a gap shrinks, even though tiny gaps cost little per pull. Over all instances, for any policy there is one with means in [0, 1] whose regret is at least (1/27) √((k − 1)n) (Lattimore and Szepesvári, Theorem 15.2): a square root of time, because an adversary can choose gaps of about √(k/n), too small to detect yet still costly every pull.
  • Bayesian regret (expected regret under a prior, where the Gittins index is optimal for discounted problems) is a third yardstick. Which one a paper optimises changes which algorithm "wins".

Where UCB went next. The same index applied at every node of a search tree is UCT (Kocsis and Szepesvári 2006), the selection rule of Monte Carlo tree search and, with a learned prior, of AlphaGo's search: see Model-based RL and planning. Agent57 chooses among its exploration policies with "a simplified sliding-window UCB with εUCB-greedy exploration" over the last 160 episodes (Badia et al. 2020), because a plain UCB "will not be able to adapt to the changes of the reward through time".

5Contextual bandits, A/B tests, and where bandits run in production

A contextual bandit sees a context first (a user's features, the page being viewed), picks an action, and sees the reward of that action only; the goal is a policy from contexts to actions. Sutton and Barto call it associative search and place it exactly between the bandit and full RL (§2.9). It is the most deployed form of RL, because many products face exactly this loop: show something, see a click or not, never see what the other options would have earned.

Actions have delayed consequences: credit assignment and planning (Dynamic programming).

What a bandit does not do
A bandit's action does not change the next context. If showing an article changes what the user does next week, or a treatment changes the patient's later state, the problem is full RL with delayed consequences, and a bandit optimising the immediate click can be confidently wrong about the long run.

A/B tests are a bandit strategy, and usually not the cheapest

The classic product experiment splits users into two groups (Lattimore and Szepesvári's example: two groups of 10,000), shows each a version, and ships the winner. In bandit terms that is explore-then-commit (ETC): pull each arm m times, then commit to the one that looked best. Its regret has exactly two parts, the test's cost and the risk of shipping the wrong version (Lattimore and Szepesvári, Theorem 6.1 and eq. 6.4):

Rn ≤ m Δ + (n − 2m) Δ · P(wrong winner),   P(wrong winner) ≤ exp(−m Δ2 / 4)
m Δ
the visitors sent to the worse version during the test, times the gap: paid in full however obvious the difference becomes
(n − 2m) Δ P(wrong)
everyone after the test, if the test picked the worse version; a longer test lowers this and raises the first term
tuning m
needs the gap Δ, "seldom reasonable to assume" known; with m tuned the regret is at most Δ + C√n, and with m chosen from n alone it is O(n2/3) (the book's eqs. 6.5 to 6.7 and Exercise 6.5)

Lattimore and Szepesvári put the difference in purpose plainly: "In traditional A/B testing, the objective of the statistician is to decide which website is better. When using a bandit algorithm, there is no need to end the trial" (§1.2). The same tension is sharpest in clinical trials, below.

Where bandits are used

  • Recommendation and content: Yahoo! news (above). Microsoft's Decision Service, a general contextual-bandit system, reported "two live production deployments for content recommendation that achieved click-through improvements of 25-30%, another with 18% revenue lift in the landing page" (Agarwal et al. 2016).
  • Advertising: Bing's adPredictor paper proposes that instead of always feeding the expected click rate to the auction, "the system can sample from the posterior weight distribution", an idea it traces to Thompson, which has "the effect of bubbling up ads about whose CTR the system has a high degree of uncertainty left" (Graepel et al. 2010, §5; the paper says "can", not that this was the deployed default). Yahoo!'s Chapelle and Li (2011) found on display ads and news that "Thompson sampling achieves state-of-the-art results, and in some cases significantly outperforms other alternatives like UCB", which revived the method.
  • Clinical trials: the field's origin (Thompson 1933) and its sharpest tension. Assigning more patients to the arm that looks better is an ethical gain, but it weakens the trial's statistical power (the A/B card's interval, in medicine): Villar, Bowden and Wason (2015) find bandit designs "offer significant advantages, in terms of assigning more patients to better treatments, and severe limitations, in terms of their resulting statistical power", and propose a rule that keeps power. The cautionary example is the 1985 ECMO trial, which used randomised play-the-winner: one infant was assigned to conventional treatment and died, eleven to ECMO and all survived (Bartlett et al. 1985), a result too lopsided for many to accept as evidence.
  • Inside other learners: Agent57's policy chooser and UCT's tree search (section 4).

6Exploration in full RL, in brief

With states, an exploratory action's payoff may only show up many steps later, after a specific sequence of further actions. Three things get harder:

  • Dithering is exponentially slow. If a reward sits at the end of a chain of N decisions where only one of two actions keeps you on track, a uniformly random policy reaches it with probability 2−N per attempt, so it needs about 2N attempts derived. What is needed is deep (temporally extended) exploration: "Unlike dithering strategies such as epsilon-greedy exploration, bootstrapped DQN carries out temporally-extended (or deep) exploration; this can lead to exponentially faster learning" (Osband et al. 2016).
  • Sparse rewards give no gradient. If almost every episode returns 0, every action looks the same until something succeeds by chance.
  • Distractors trap exploiters. A small nearby reward is found first, and a greedy-ish learner stops looking for the larger one far away: greedy on the testbed, scaled up.

The families in detail: sources, quotes and numbers (5 min)

Optimism with counts. E3 (Kearns and Singh 2002) and R-MAX (Brafman and Tennenholtz 2002) gave the first polynomial-time guarantees for exploring an MDP. R-MAX's model "is initialized in an optimistic fashion: all actions in all states return the maximal possible reward (hence the name)"; once a state-action pair has been tried enough times its true model replaces the optimistic one. MBIE-EB (Strehl and Littman 2008) turns this into a bonus β/√N(s, a) added to the reward in the Bellman equation (as restated by Bellemare et al. 2016). UCRL2 (Jaksch, Ortner and Auer 2010) plans optimistically within confidence sets on the transition model and proves regret Õ(DS√(AT)) against a lower bound Ω(√(DSAT)) (D the MDP's diameter, S states, A actions).

Pseudo-counts and hashing. Bellemare et al. (2016) derive a pseudo-count N̂(x) from a density model of observations: how much the model's probability of x rises after one more look at x says how familiar x is. The bonus is β (N̂ + 0.01)−1/2 with β = 0.05. On Montezuma's Revenge their agent "consistently navigates through 15 rooms" of the first level's 24 within 50 million frames, where the agent without the bonus saw two, and scored 3,439 on average by 100 million frames. Tang et al. (2017) count hashed states instead (SimHash of pixels or of learned features), bonus β/√n(φ(s)).

Curiosity. The Intrinsic Curiosity Module uses "the error in an agent's ability to predict the consequence of its own actions in a visual feature space learned by a self-supervised inverse dynamics model" (Pathak et al. 2017); learning features through an inverse model is meant to ignore what the agent cannot affect. A large-scale study trained agents with curiosity alone, no extrinsic reward, on 54 environments, and documented the noisy TV: add a TV with a channel-changing action to a maze and "the presence of the TV drastically slows down learning". Prediction error mixes up "new" with "random".

RND. Burda et al. (2018) fix the target: a randomly initialised network that is never trained maps each observation to a feature vector, and a second network is trained to predict those features; because the target is a deterministic function of the observation, noise does not keep the error high. RND was the "first method that achieves better than average human performance on this game without using demonstrations or having access to the underlying state": a mean of 8,152 on Montezuma's Revenge against an average human 4,753 (Table 1), the best agent often finding "22 out of the 24 rooms on the first level".

Remember and return. Never Give Up combines a per-episode novelty bonus with RND's lifelong one and was the "first algorithm to achieve non-zero rewards (with a mean score of 8,400) in the game of Pitfall! without using demonstrations" (Badia et al. 2020a); Agent57 adds the bandit meta-controller of section 4 and is "the first deep RL agent that outperforms the standard human benchmark on all 57 Atari games" (Badia et al. 2020b; the Milestones and benchmarks tab of Topic: rl has its scores, including two mistyped MuZero entries found there). Go-Explore (Ecoffet et al. 2019) keeps an archive of distinct states ("cells") reached so far, picks a promising one, returns to it (by restoring the emulator's state), explores from there, then trains a robust policy from the best trajectories. Without domain knowledge its policies averaged 43,763 on Montezuma's Revenge, "higher than the human expert mean of 34,900", and solved level 1 in 99.8% of attempts. The journal version, "First return, then explore", adds a policy that learns to return without resets and reports that it "solves all heretofore unsolved Atari games"; with easy-to-provide domain knowledge it reaches 102,571 on Pitfall and 1,731,645 on Montezuma's Revenge (figures from the preprint).

Posterior sampling. Thompson sampling's deep-RL form keeps several plausible value functions and acts greedily on one of them for a whole episode, the temporally extended exploration per-step noise lacks: Bootstrapped DQN trains several Q heads on bootstrapped data (Osband et al. 2016), developed in Osband, Van Roy, Russo and Wen (2019). NoisyNet learns noise in the network's weights, replacing ε-greedy and entropy bonuses, with initial noise scale σ0 = 0.5 (Fortunato et al. 2017); it is one of the six extensions Rainbow combines (Method atlas tab of Topic: rl).

Entropy. A3C adds the policy's entropy to the objective, "with a weight β = 0.01 for all Atari and TORCS experiments" (Mnih et al. 2016); SAC makes it the objective itself, J(π) = Σt E[r(st, at) + α H(π(·|st))], "to succeed at the task while acting as randomly as possible", with the temperature α weighing entropy against reward (Haarnoja et al. 2018).

7Exploration when training language models

RL on a pretrained language model starts from a policy that already produces sensible answers, so exploration is inherited from pretraining and controlled through sampling: several responses per prompt at a temperature around 1, so that some are right and some wrong and the advantage has something to compare (GRPO's group). In bandit terms, RL concentrates probability on arms the base model already pulls sometimes; it rarely finds arms the base model never pulls. Three results frame this, each taught on RL for LLMs: base models overtake their RL-trained versions on pass@k at large k (Yue et al. 2025); performance follows R = −a eH + b in the policy's entropy H, capped at −a + b when entropy runs out (Cui et al. 2025, with Clip-Cov and KL-Cov to slow the collapse); and DAPO's Clip-Higher (εlow = 0.2, εhigh = 0.28) lets rare tokens grow to keep exploring. The pipeline around them is on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR; DeepSeekMath (GRPO) and DeepSeek-R1 (RL from a base model without supervised warm-up) show what this exploration produced at scale.

Common mistakes

Breaking ties by taking the first arm
With all estimates equal at the start, "argmax" in most libraries returns the first index, so a greedy agent always starts on arm 1 and the results depend on arm order. Sutton and Barto break ties at random; so does every simulation on this page except the strategies animation, where the fixed tie rule is visible and stated.
Reading one run, or a few, as a result
A single bandit run is dominated by luck (try the five seeds in section 3). The book averages 2,000 tasks; on the testbed tab, compare 200 with 2,000 to see how much of a curve's wiggle is noise.
Using UCB's constant outside its scale
UCB1's √(2 ln n / nj) assumes rewards in [0, 1]. With rewards in the hundreds the bonus is negligible and UCB is greedy; with rewards in [0, 0.01] it explores almost uniformly. Rescale the rewards or tune c (Figure 2.6 shows how much c matters).
Treating optimistic initial values as a general method
Optimism explores only at the start. With sample averages it vanishes after one pull of each arm, and in a nonstationary task it never comes back (§2.6). R-MAX makes it principled by tying optimism to counts.
Reading the Lai and Robbins bound as a finite-time floor
It is a statement about the slope against ln T in the limit. A good algorithm can sit below the line at any T you can afford to run (the regret chart in section 4 shows Thompson sampling doing exactly that).
Crediting Thompson sampling for a matched prior
On the 10-armed testbed Thompson sampling with a N(0, 1) prior uses the exact distribution the tasks are drawn from. With a wrong or overconfident prior it can under-explore; report the prior with the result.
Stopping an A/B test the first time it looks significant
The test's p-values and intervals assume its length was fixed in advance; they "are wholly unreliable if users endogenously choose samples sizes by continuously monitoring their tests" (Johari, Pekelis and Walsh, Always valid inference). To stop early, use a sequential test built for it (their always-valid p-values) or a bandit, which needs no stopping rule.
Expecting a novelty bonus to ignore noise
Prediction-error curiosity is drawn to anything unpredictable (the noisy TV). RND's fixed random target and counts of states avoid that particular trap, but any bonus can distract from the task if it is not scaled down as the task reward appears.
Comparing hard-exploration scores across papers
Human baselines, frame budgets, sticky actions, emulator resets and domain knowledge all differ (see the Montezuma's Revenge table). Compare within one paper's table, or re-run.
Thinking ε-greedy is always the wrong choice
In Auer et al.'s experiments "an optimally tuned εn-GREEDY performs almost always best" (Auer et al. 2002); its weakness is that the tuning depends on the unknown gap, and in full RL that its exploration is shallow. With dense rewards (most of Atari) it is still the default.

The 10-armed testbed, run live

Sutton and Barto's testbed (§2.3): each task is a 10-armed bandit whose true values q*(a) are drawn from a standard normal; pulling arm a pays q*(a) plus standard normal noise. One run is 1,000 pulls on one task; a curve is the average over 2,000 tasks. Everything below is computed in your browser when you open a figure, in slices so the page stays responsive, with seeded random numbers: task i is the same task for every method, and the reward noise is drawn from its own stream, so methods differ only in the arms they choose. The engine is checked against an independent Python implementation (every value equal on 40 tasks of every setting).

Try: switch to 200 tasks and back to see how much of a curve's wiggle is noise; on Fig. 2.4, find the spike at step 11; on Fig. 2.6, compare each method's best setting and how wide its plateau is (the book: all are "fairly insensitive, performing well over a range of parameter values varying by about an order of magnitude"); on Nonstationary, watch sample averages fall behind.

What the book's figures show, and what they do not

  • Greedy is not a strawman. It is ahead for the first few steps on Fig. 2.2, and its failure is specific: in about two thirds of tasks the first sample of the best arm happened to be disappointing and it never looked again.
  • The ranking depends on the task. With reward variance 10 instead of 1 the book expects ε-greedy to gain on greedy; with zero variance greedy would find each arm's value after one pull and could do best (§2.3). Optimistic starts and sample averages both treat "the beginning of time" specially and cannot help when the task changes (§2.6), which the nonstationary run shows.
  • A testbed is not a guarantee. These are averages over one distribution of tasks; the regret bounds in the Reading tab are what hold for every task. On this testbed Thompson sampling also knows the exact prior the tasks come from.

Reproduction notes: ties between equal estimates are broken at random (as in the book); UCB treats an unpulled arm as maximising (eq. 2.10); the gradient baseline is the average of all rewards including the current one (the book's footnote on p. 37); "best possible" is the mean, over the tasks run, of each task's largest true value. Figure 2.6's parameter ranges (ε from 1/128 to 1/4, α from 1/32 to 2, c from 1/16 to 4, Q₀ from 1/4 to 4) follow Shangtong Zhang's reproduction, since the book's figure gives only the shared axis (1/128 to 4).

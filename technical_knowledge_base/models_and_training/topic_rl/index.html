Reinforcement learning in one screen

Reinforcement learning (RL) is learning to act by trial and error. An agent takes actions in an environment, receives a scalar reward after each one, and adjusts its behaviour to maximise the total reward it collects over time. Nobody tells it the correct action: it only learns how good the outcome was, often many steps later. That makes RL the tool for problems where behaviour can be scored but not demonstrated: games, control and, since InstructGPT in 2022, the post-training of language models, where a reward model or a programmatic verifier scores whole responses.

The field arrived in three layers, and this page follows them in order:

  • The classical core defines the problem (Markov decision processes and the Bellman equations) and solves it with tables, either by planning with a known model (dynamic programming) or by learning from samples (Monte Carlo, temporal-difference learning, SARSA, Q-learning). Sections 1 to 10.
  • Deep RL replaces the tables with neural networks and adds the tricks that keep that stable (DQN, policy gradients, PPO, SAC, AlphaZero, MuZero). Sections 11 to 17.
  • RL for language models applies the same machinery to token generation (RLHF, GRPO, RLVR), where the environment is trivial and the reward is the expensive part. Section 18.
The one idea that runs through the page: one target, many estimators
Every method estimates the same quantity, the expected return that the Bellman equation describes, and improves behaviour against that estimate. The methods differ mainly in what they use in place of that expectation: the exact model, a whole sampled episode, one sampled step plus the current estimate, a learned critic, a tree search, or the mean reward of a group of sampled answers. 1What RL is, and why it is its own subject

Three things set RL apart from supervised learning (Sutton and Barto, §1.1; Silver, Lecture 1):

  • The feedback is a score, not a label. It says how good an action was, not which action was right. A supervised model is told "the answer was 7"; an RL agent is told "that scored 0.3", and must find out by trying what would have scored higher.
  • The feedback can be delayed. A move in chess is rewarded only when the game ends, dozens of moves later, so the agent has to work out which earlier action earned a later reward. This is the credit assignment problem, and it runs through every section of this page up to the last token of a language model's answer.
  • The agent's own actions decide what data it sees next. The data are neither fixed nor independent, so the agent faces a trade-off no supervised learner has: exploit what it already knows works, or explore to find something better (section 7).

Silver's first lecture gives the classic examples: flying stunt manoeuvres in a helicopter (positive reward for following the desired trajectory, negative for crashing), playing backgammon (reward for winning or losing a game), and playing Atari games (reward for increasing the score). Since 2022 the most consequential example is a language model: the "environment" is a prompt, the "actions" are tokens, and the reward is a learned judge of human preference or a program that checks the answer. The Milestones and benchmarks tab follows these from TD-Gammon to DeepSeek-R1.

The reward hypothesis. RL rests on one assumption: all goals can be described as the maximisation of expected cumulative scalar reward. It is an assumption, not a theorem, and it breaks in instructive ways for language models. Goals such as "helpful" or "honest" have no mathematical definition, so training maximises a proxy: a learned reward model, a test suite, an LLM judge. Optimise a proxy hard enough and the policy finds shortcuts that raise the proxy without meeting the intent, such as longer answers because the reward model correlates length with quality, agreeing with the user's wrong claims, or special-casing the tests in coding tasks. That failure, reward hacking, returns in section 18 and is the subject of Reward Hacking. The agent maximises cumulative reward, not immediate reward, so it can be better to give up reward now to gain more later: refuelling a helicopter costs time now but may prevent a crash hours later, and a financial investment may take months to mature.

Two problems share the vocabulary. In learning, the environment is initially unknown and the agent improves its policy by interacting with it. In planning, a model of the environment is known and the agent improves its policy by computing with the model, without interacting, for example by tree search over a game whose rules are known. Sections 8 and 16 are planning; sections 9 to 15 are learning.

2The agent-environment loop, state and the Markov property

At each time step t (Silver, Lecture 1): the agent receives an observation Ot and a scalar reward Rt; it chooses and executes an action At; the environment receives At and emits the next observation Ot+1 and reward Rt+1, and t increments. So the reward that follows action At is Rt+1. This indexing, used by Silver and by Sutton and Barto, is why every return below starts at Rt+1; some texts write rt for the reward of the action taken at t, and only the subscript shifts.

Ht = O1, R1, A1, O2, R2, A2, ..., At−1, Ot, Rt
Ht
the history: everything observed up to time t. It ends with the latest observation and reward and does not yet contain At, because the agent chooses At from it. It grows without bound, which is why agents summarise it.

A state is the information used to decide what happens next: formally, any function of the history, St = f(Ht). Two kinds matter. The environment state Set is the environment's private representation, whatever it uses to pick the next observation and reward; it is usually invisible to the agent, and even when visible may hold irrelevant detail, so it is useful for reasoning about the environment, not as an input. The agent state Sat = f(Ht) is the agent's own summary of the history, the information it uses to pick its next action; this is the state RL algorithms work with.

ℙ[St+1 | St] = ℙ[St+1 | S1, ..., St]
Markov
a state is Markov if the next state depends only on the current one, not on the rest of the history: "the future is independent of the past given the present". Once a Markov state is known the history can be thrown away, because the state is a sufficient statistic of the future. S1, ..., St are all the states so far; ℙ[· | ·] is a conditional probability.

The environment state is Markov, and so, trivially, is the full history Ht. The design problem is a compact agent state that is still Markov, or close to it. A single Atari frame is not Markov: it shows where the ball is but not where it is going. Stacking the last four frames, as DQN does, recovers the velocity (Mnih et al. 2015, Methods).

Observability. When the agent directly observes the environment state, Ot = Sat = Set, the problem is fully observable and formally a Markov decision process (MDP, section 4); most of RL theory lives here. When it observes the environment only indirectly (a robot camera that is not told its absolute location, a trading agent that sees only prices, a poker player who sees only the public cards), the problem is a partially observable MDP (POMDP), and the agent must build its own state. Three standard choices:

  • the complete history, Sat = Ht;
  • a belief: the vector of probabilities (ℙ[Set = s1], ..., ℙ[Set = sn]) over the n possible environment states;
  • a learned recurrent summary, an RNN state Sat = σ(Ws Sat−1 + Wo Ot), where σ is an elementwise nonlinearity such as the logistic sigmoid or tanh, Ws a learned matrix applied to the previous agent state and Wo a learned matrix applied to the new observation.

The language-model case. A decoder conditioning on its full token prefix is the Sat = Ht choice. In plain text generation nothing is hidden: the state is the prompt plus the tokens so far, and appending a token is deterministic, so generation is a fully observable MDP with a trivial, known transition. Section 18 builds on exactly this.

3Rewards, returns and discounting

The quantity the agent maximises is the return, the total discounted reward from step t onwards:

Gt = Rt+1 + γRt+2 + γ²Rt+3 + ... = Σk=0∞ γk Rt+k+1    and recursively    Gt = Rt+1 + γGt+1
Rt+k+1
the reward k + 1 steps ahead
γ ∈ [0, 1]
the discount factor: a reward received k + 1 steps from now counts γk times as much as the same reward now. Near 0 gives myopic evaluation, near 1 far-sighted evaluation. In an episodic task the sum stops at the terminal step.

The weights 1, γ, γ², ... sum to 1/(1 − γ), so γ = 0.9 behaves roughly like a horizon of 10 steps and γ = 0.99 like 100 derived. The recursion Gt = Rt+1 + γGt+1 is the seed of everything in section 5: it is what lets a long-horizon problem be cut into one-step pieces.

How far ahead a discount factor looks

Why discount at all? Four reasons, from Silver's second lecture:

  1. Cycles. It keeps returns finite in cyclic or never-ending processes (the student chain below can loop on Facebook indefinitely).
  2. Uncertainty. The future is less certain than the present, and the model of it may be imperfect, so it is weighted less.
  3. Convenience. With γ < 1 the Bellman operators of section 5 are contractions, so iterating them converges to a unique answer from any start (section 8).
  4. Preference. Human and animal behaviour prefers reward sooner, and immediate money can earn interest.

Undiscounted returns (γ = 1) are fine when every sequence is guaranteed to terminate. LLM RL typically uses γ = 1, because every response ends and the reward arrives at the end. A larger γ looks further ahead, but returns have more variance and iterative solvers converge more slowly: the number of sweeps needed grows like 1/(1 − γ) (section 8).

Episodic and continuing tasks. A game, a maze or an LLM response ends: the task is episodic, and the return is a finite sum. A thermostat or a trading system never stops: the task is continuing, and γ < 1 (or an average-reward formulation, Sutton and Barto §10.3) is what keeps the return finite.

4The Markov decision process, built in three steps

The formal object all of RL optimises over is built in three steps, each adding one ingredient. The running example is David Silver's student chain (Silver, Lecture 2): the states Class 1, Class 2, Class 3, Pass, Pub, Facebook and Sleep, where Sleep is terminal.

1. Markov process (Markov chain) ⟨𝒮, P⟩: a memoryless random sequence of states with the Markov property, where 𝒮 is a finite set of states and P the state transition matrix, Pss′ = ℙ[St+1 = s′ | St = s], one row per current state and one column per successor, each row summing to 1. In the student chain, Class 1 goes to Class 2 or Facebook with probability 0.5 each; Class 2 goes to Class 3 with 0.8 or Sleep with 0.2; Class 3 goes to Pass with 0.6 or Pub with 0.4; Pass goes to Sleep with 1.0; Pub goes to Class 1, Class 2 or Class 3 with 0.2, 0.4 and 0.4; Facebook stays on Facebook with 0.9 and returns to Class 1 with 0.1.

2. Markov reward process (MRP) ⟨𝒮, P, R, γ⟩: a Markov chain with rewards. Rs = 𝔼[Rt+1 | St = s] is the expected reward for leaving state s. In the student MRP each class gives −2, Facebook −1, Pub +1, Pass +10 and Sleep 0. An MRP defines a return and a value, but nobody decides anything.

3. Markov decision process (MDP) ⟨𝒮, 𝒜, P, R, γ⟩: an MRP with decisions. 𝒜 is a finite set of actions, and transitions and rewards now depend on the action:

Pass′ = ℙ[St+1 = s′ | St = s, At = a],    Ras = 𝔼[Rt+1 | St = s, At = a]

You no longer drift between states at random: your action moves you, possibly stochastically. The only new ingredient is agency. In the student MDP, from Class 1 you choose Study (−2, to Class 2) or Facebook (−1, to Facebook); from Facebook, Facebook again (−1) or Quit (0, back to Class 1); from Class 2, Study (−2, to Class 3) or Sleep (0, the end); from Class 3, Study (+10, pass, the end) or Pub (+1, after which chance sends you to Class 1, Class 2 or Class 3 with 0.2, 0.4 and 0.4).

A policy is a distribution over actions given states, π(a | s) = ℙ[At = a | St = s]. Fixing a policy turns an MDP back into an MRP, with Pπss′ = Σa π(a | s) Pass′ and Rπs = Σa π(a | s) Ras. Averaging over the policy's choices removes the agency, which is why evaluating a fixed policy is an MRP problem. The return is a random variable. Start in Class 1 with γ = ½ and sample the episode Class 1, Class 2, Class 3, Pass, Sleep: the rewards for leaving each state are −2, −2, −2 and +10, so G1 = −2 + ½(−2) + ¼(−2) + ⅛(10) = −2 − 1 − 0.5 + 1.25 = −2.25. Another episode from the same start, Class 1, Facebook, Facebook, Class 1, Class 2, Sleep, gives −2 − ½ − ¼ − 2/8 − 2/16 = −3.125. 5Policies, V and Q, and the Bellman equations

The value function is the expected return, which removes the randomness of individual episodes. In an MRP, V(s) = 𝔼[Gt | St = s]. In an MDP the return depends on how you act, so values are defined for a policy π:

Vπ(s) = 𝔼π[Gt | St = s],    Qπ(s, a) = 𝔼π[Gt | St = s, At = a]
Vπ(s)
the state value: expected return from state s when following π
Qπ(s, a)
the action value: expected return from taking action a in s, then following π
𝔼π
an expectation over the randomness of both the policy and the environment

The two are one step apart: Vπ(s) = Σa π(a | s) Qπ(s, a), which averages the action values under the policy, and Qπ(s, a) = Ras + γ Σs′ Pass′ Vπ(s′), a one-step lookahead that needs the model. That asymmetry is why Q is what you want for model-free control: acting greedily on Q is argmaxa Q(s, a), a lookup over actions, while acting greedily on V needs the lookahead and so the transition dynamics.

Optimal values. V*(s) = maxπ Vπ(s) and Q*(s, a) = maxπ Qπ(s, a). An MDP is solved once Q* is known, because the policy that picks argmaxa Q*(s, a) in every state is optimal. For any finite MDP there is an optimal policy at least as good as every other policy in every state, and there is always a deterministic one (Silver, Lecture 2).

The Bellman equations

The Bellman equation cuts a long-horizon problem into one-step pieces: the value of a state is the immediate reward plus the discounted value of the successor. It follows from the return's recursion:

V(s) = 𝔼[Gt | St = s] = 𝔼[Rt+1 + γGt+1 | St = s] = 𝔼[Rt+1 + γV(St+1) | St = s]
last step
replaces the random tail Gt+1 by its expectation V(St+1), which the Markov property allows. For an MRP with known P and R it reads V(s) = Rs + γ Σs′ Pss′ V(s′).

Bellman expectation equations, for a fixed policy π:

Vπ(s) = Σa π(a | s) [ Ras + γ Σs′ Pass′ Vπ(s′) ]
Qπ(s, a) = Ras + γ Σs′ Pass′ Σa′ π(a′ | s′) Qπ(s′, a′)
a′
the action taken in the successor s′; every other symbol as above

These are linear in the values. In matrix form Vπ = Rπ + γPπVπ, with the direct solution Vπ = (I − γPπ)−1 Rπ, where Vπ and Rπ are column vectors with one entry per state, Pπ is the state-to-state transition matrix under π and I the identity. Inversion costs O(n³) for n states, so the direct solution is only for small problems; large ones use the iterative methods of sections 8 and 9.

Bellman optimality equations, for the optimal values:

V*(s) = maxa [ Ras + γ Σs′ Pass′ V*(s′) ],    Q*(s, a) = Ras + γ Σs′ Pass′ maxa′ Q*(s′, a′)

The max replaces the average under a policy. It makes the equations nonlinear, so in general there is no closed form: they are solved iteratively, by value iteration and policy iteration when the model is known (section 8) and by Q-learning, and SARSA with exploration decaying to greedy, from samples when it is not (section 9). Everything downstream, from value iteration to DQN's TD target to the value model in PPO-based RLHF, is a way of approximately solving one of these equations.

6What an agent contains, and three axes that place any method

An agent may include any subset of three components (Silver, Lecture 1):

  • Policy π: the behaviour function, a map from state to action. Deterministic, a = π(s), or stochastic, π(a | s). An LLM is a stochastic policy over tokens.
  • Value function: a prediction of expected future reward, Vπ or Qπ, used to judge states or state-action pairs and so to choose between actions.
  • Model: the agent's own representation of the environment. A transition model predicts the next state, P̂ass′ ≈ ℙ[St+1 = s′ | St = s, At = a]; a reward model predicts the next immediate reward, R̂as ≈ 𝔼[Rt+1 | St = s, At = a]. The hats mark estimates, which may be wrong. In LLM work "reward model" usually means something else: a network trained on human preferences that scores whole responses. It plays the environment's reward role; the agent does not plan with it.

Which components an agent keeps, and how it gets its data, place it on three independent axes; every agent sits somewhere on each:

  • What the agent stores. A value-based agent keeps V or Q and acts greedily on it; the policy is implicit. A policy-based agent keeps the policy π(a | s) itself and moves it along the gradient of expected return. An actor-critic keeps both: the value function (the critic) judges the policy's actions (the actor) and lowers the variance of its updates.
  • Whether it models the environment. Model-based methods plan with a model, either given (dynamic programming, the rules of the game in AlphaZero) or learned (MuZero); model-free methods go from experience straight to values or a policy. The reward model in RLHF does not make it model-based: it stands in for the reward, and nothing plans with it.
  • Whose experience it learns from. On-policy methods learn about the policy that generated the data. Off-policy methods learn about a target policy, usually the greedy one, from data generated by a different behaviour policy, which is what lets them reuse old experience from a replay buffer (section 9). Dynamic programming sits outside this axis: it computes from the model and uses no experience at all.

Prediction and control. Prediction evaluates the future: given a fixed policy π, how much reward will it collect? Compute Vπ or Qπ. Control optimises the future: find an optimal policy π*, equivalently V* or Q*. You usually solve prediction to solve control: evaluate the current policy, improve it by acting greedily on its values, repeat. With a model that loop is policy iteration (section 8); without one, the same loop runs on samples: Monte Carlo and TD for prediction, SARSA and Q-learning for control (section 9).

7Exploration against exploitation: bandits

Exploitation uses current knowledge to take the action believed best, maximising reward now. Exploration deliberately tries actions that look worse, to gather information that may pay off later. Silver's everyday examples: go to your favourite restaurant or try a new one; drill for oil at the best known site or at a new location; play the move you believe is best or an experimental one. Every RL agent must balance the two. Pure exploitation gets stuck on the first decent behaviour it finds, because it never collects the evidence that something else is better; pure exploration never cashes in what it has learned.

The cleanest setting for the trade-off is the multi-armed bandit: an MDP with a single state. Choose one of k levers; each pays a reward from its own unknown distribution; maximise the total (Sutton and Barto, chapter 2). There is no credit-assignment problem and no state to track, so only exploration is left. Performance is measured by regret: the reward lost, summed over time, against always pulling the best arm. Lai and Robbins showed that any good strategy must suffer regret growing at least logarithmically with time (Lai and Robbins 1985), so the question is how close to log T a strategy gets. The standard strategies:

  • Greedy: always pull the arm with the highest estimated mean. It locks onto whichever arm looked good first.
  • ε-greedy: with probability 1 − ε take the greedy action, otherwise an action chosen uniformly at random. With m actions, π(a | s) = 1 − ε + ε/m for the greedy action and ε/m for each other; the greedy action also gets its share of the random picks, hence the ε/m added to it. With ε = 0.1 and m = 4 the greedy action is taken with probability 0.9 + 0.025 = 0.925 and each other with 0.025 (Silver, Lecture 5). Decaying ε so that the policy becomes greedy in the limit while every action is still tried infinitely often is called GLIE (greedy in the limit with infinite exploration).
  • Optimistic initialisation: start every estimate high, so a greedy agent is disappointed by whatever it tries and moves on to the untried actions. It needs a constant step size; with sample averages the optimism is erased by the first pull.
  • Upper confidence bound (UCB): pull argmaxa [ Q(a) + c √(ln t / N(a)) ], where N(a) is how often arm a has been pulled: the bonus is large for arms pulled rarely, so the agent explores where it is uncertain rather than at random. Its regret grows logarithmically (Auer et al. 2002; Sutton and Barto §2.7).
  • Thompson sampling: keep a posterior over each arm's mean, draw one sample from each, pull the best sample. It explores in proportion to the probability that an arm is the best (Russo et al., tutorial).

Beyond bandits. In a full MDP, exploration has to be deep: the payoff of an exploratory action may only show up many steps later, and dithering strategies like ε-greedy explore exponentially slowly in sparse-reward problems. Atari's Montezuma's Revenge, where a random agent essentially never scores, became the test case; count-based bonuses and curiosity, such as Random Network Distillation's bonus for states whose features a network cannot yet predict (Burda et al. 2018), and Go-Explore's "remember promising states, return to them, then explore" (Ecoffet et al. 2019) were the responses; Lilian Weng's survey maps them. Two mechanisms recur in the rest of this page: entropy bonuses in deep RL (add the policy's entropy to the objective so it does not collapse onto one action too early; SAC builds its whole objective on this, section 15) and sampling temperature in LLM rollouts (sampling several different responses per prompt at a temperature around 1 gives GRPO a spread of rewards to learn from, section 18). For language models, exploration is largely inherited: RL can only reinforce answers the pretrained model already sometimes produces.

8Planning with a known model: dynamic programming

When the transition probabilities and rewards are known, the Bellman equations can be solved by turning them into update rules and sweeping them over every state (Sutton and Barto, chapter 4; Silver, Lecture 3). This is dynamic programming (DP), and it is the exact answer every other method on this page approximates. Its target is the full expectation over the model: no sample, no noise.

  • Policy evaluation (prediction): apply the expectation equation as an update to every state, Vk+1(s) = Σa π(a | s) [Ras + γ Σs′ Pass′ Vk(s′)], until the values stop changing.
  • Policy improvement: make the policy greedy on those values. The policy improvement theorem guarantees the greedy policy is at least as good everywhere; if nothing changes, the Bellman optimality equation already holds.
  • Policy iteration (control): alternate evaluation and improvement until the policy stops changing. It terminates, because a finite MDP has finitely many deterministic policies.
  • Value iteration (control): skip the explicit policy and apply the optimality equation directly, Vk+1(s) = maxa [Ras + γ Σs′ Pass′ Vk(s′)]; read the policy off at the end. Its intermediate values need not be the value of any policy.
  • Generalised policy iteration (GPI): any interleaving of some evaluation with some improvement converges, as long as both keep happening. Policy iteration and value iteration are its two extremes, and most of RL, up to PPO for language models, has this shape: a critic doing approximate evaluation, a policy step doing approximate improvement.

Why it converges, and how fast. With γ < 1 the Bellman operators are contractions in the max norm: one sweep shrinks the distance between any two value functions by at least γ, so the error after k sweeps is at most γk times the initial error. Cutting the error a thousandfold needs about 66 sweeps at γ = 0.9 and 688 at γ = 0.99 derived: k ≥ ln 1000 / ln(1/γ). One sweep costs O(mn²) for n states and m actions, which is why DP stops at small state spaces: ten variables with ten values each are already ten billion states (the curse of dimensionality). Asynchronous variants (in-place updates, prioritised sweeping, real-time DP) update only the states that matter, and everything from section 9 on replaces the full sweep by samples.

One world, two ways to get its values: sweep the model, or learn from samples

Russell and Norvig's 4×3 world (AIMA, Figure 17.1; the book's code): each move goes where intended with probability 0.8 and sideways with 0.1 each; bumping a wall stays put; every step costs 0.04; the two exits pay +1 and −1; γ = 1. Value iteration converges on exactly the utilities printed in AIMA's Figure 17.3 (0.812, 0.868, 0.918 on the top row; 0.705, 0.655, 0.611, 0.388 on the bottom), reproduced independently. The Q-learning agent never sees the 0.8 and 0.1: it starts each episode in a random cell, behaves ε-greedily (ε = 0.3), updates Q(s, a) toward −0.04 + maxa′ Q(s′, a′) with step 1/N(s, a)0.8, and shows maxa Q. That is the trade: the model buys exactness and speed; samples buy independence from the model. 9Learning from samples: Monte Carlo, TD, SARSA and Q-learning

Without a model the agent cannot compute the expectation in the Bellman equation, so it substitutes samples. All the methods in this section use one incremental update, moving the estimate a fraction of the way toward a target:

V(St) ← V(St) + α ( target − V(St) )
V(St)
the current estimate
α ∈ (0, 1]
the step size
target
a stand-in for the expectation in the Bellman equation; the methods differ only here
  • Monte Carlo (MC) waits for the end of the episode and uses the actual return Gt. It is unbiased, but a whole episode's randomness lands in every estimate, so variance is high, and it must wait for termination. It does not rely on the Markov property. First-visit MC averages the return after the first visit to a state in each episode; every-visit MC averages after every visit; both converge.
  • Temporal-difference learning, TD(0) (Sutton 1988), uses one real reward plus its own estimate of the next state: target Rt+1 + γV(St+1). The gap δt = Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St) is the TD error. It learns online, after every step, works on tasks that never end, and has low variance; it is biased by whatever error the estimate has, and in practice usually learns faster than MC.

Two independent properties describe how a value update is built (Silver's "unified view", Lecture 4). Bootstrapping: the target involves an existing estimate; solve one step, then trust your current answer for the rest. Sampling: the target uses one sampled successor instead of the full expectation over all of them.

Control from samples: SARSA and Q-learning

For control, learn Q rather than V (greedy on Q needs no model) and keep exploring with an ε-greedy policy:

A′

SARSA bootstraps off the action actually taken next, so it learns the value of the exploratory policy it is running: it is on-policy. Q-learning (Watkins and Dayan 1992) bootstraps off the best next action, so it learns the greedy policy's values while behaving exploratorily: it is off-policy, which is exactly what makes replaying old experience legitimate (section 12). Q-learning is sampled value iteration; SARSA is sampled policy iteration. Expected SARSA replaces Q(S′, A′) with its expectation under the policy, removing the variance of the next action (Sutton and Barto §6.6).

Cliff walking shows the difference in one picture (Sutton and Barto, Example 6.6): on a grid whose shortest path runs along a cliff edge, Q-learning learns the edge path (optimal for a greedy agent) but, exploring with ε = 0.1, keeps falling off while it learns; SARSA learns a safer path one row up and collects more reward online. When they converge. Tabular TD(0) converges to Vπ with step sizes satisfying Σα = ∞ and Σα² < ∞ (the Robbins-Monro conditions). SARSA converges to Q* under GLIE exploration with such step sizes; Q-learning converges to Q* as long as every state-action pair keeps being updated, whatever the behaviour policy (Sutton and Barto §6.4 and §6.5). These guarantees are for tables; section 11 is what happens without them.

Off-policy learning and importance sampling

On-policy learning ("learn on the job") learns about π from experience sampled from π. Off-policy learning ("look over someone's shoulder") learns about a target policy π from experience sampled from a different behaviour policy μ. It is useful for learning from human demonstrations or other agents, reusing data from old policies (replay buffers), learning a greedy target while behaving exploratorily, and learning about several policies from one stream of behaviour (Silver, Lecture 5). The general correction is importance sampling: weight each sample by how much more or less likely the target policy was to take the action:

ρt = π(At | St) / μ(At | St),    because   𝔼X~μ[ (π(X)/μ(X)) f(X) ] = 𝔼X~π[ f(X) ]
ρt
the importance ratio; f is any function

Example: the behaviour policy took an action with probability 0.25 that the target policy would take with probability 0.5, so the sample counts double (ρ = 2); an action the target policy never takes gets weight 0. Ratios multiply along a trajectory, so off-policy Monte Carlo can have very high variance. One-step Q-learning needs no ratio at all, because its target takes the max over next actions instead of using the action μ chose. The same ratio, clipped, is the heart of PPO (section 14) and GRPO (section 18), whose rollouts come from a slightly stale copy of the policy, computed by an inference engine whose probabilities differ slightly from the trainer's: LLM RL is mildly off-policy.

10The bias-variance dial: n-step returns, TD(λ) and GAE

MC and TD are the two ends of a dial. Between them are targets that use n real rewards before trusting the estimate:

Gt(n)
the n-step return: n real rewards, then the estimate of the state reached. An n-step return that reaches past the end of the episode is just the full return Gt.
λ ∈ [0, 1]
how fast the weights decay with n. At λ = 0 only the one-step TD target is left; at λ = 1 only the Monte Carlo return. In an episode that ends at time T, the weights are (1 − λ)λn−1 for n = 1, ..., T − t − 1, and the remaining λT−t−1 falls on Gt.

The λ-return is the forward view; eligibility traces compute the same thing online, as a backward view: each state keeps a trace Et(s) = γλEt−1(s) + 1(St = s) of how recently and how often it was visited, and every TD error is credited to all states in proportion to their traces. This is TD(λ) (Sutton and Barto, chapter 12). In deep RL the same dial reappears as generalised advantage estimation (GAE; Schulman et al. 2015), a λ-weighted sum of TD errors used as the advantage in actor-critic methods (section 13): Ât = Σl≥0 (γλ)l δt+l. PPO's reference settings use λ = 0.95 and γ = 0.99 (Schulman et al. 2017, Tables 3 to 5).

Why there is a dial
A target built from real rewards is unbiased but carries every step's randomness; a target that stops after one step and trusts the estimate carries only one step's randomness but inherits whatever error the estimate has. Short targets win when the estimate is good, long ones when it is poor. The same trade appears on every layer: TD(λ) with tables, GAE's λ in PPO, and GRPO, which takes the pure Monte Carlo end with a sampled baseline because a per-token critic for a language model is as large as the policy and hard to fit from a single end-of-sequence reward.

11Function approximation, and the deadly triad

Tables need one entry per state, and real problems have too many states to visit even once: Go has about 2 × 10170 legal positions (Tromp), far more than the roughly 1080 atoms in the observable universe, and an Atari screen is 210 × 160 pixels of colour. The fix is to approximate the value function with a parameterised function, v̂(s, w) ≈ Vπ(s): a linear function of hand-made features, or a neural network. Approximation is what lets an agent generalise: an update at one state moves the estimates of similar states, including ones never visited. It is also what breaks the table guarantees of section 9, because an update aimed at one state now changes others.

w ← w + α [ Rt+1 + γ v̂(St+1, w) − v̂(St, w) ] ∇w v̂(St, w)
semi-gradient TD
the TD update for a parameterised estimate: it follows the gradient of the estimate at St but treats the target as a constant, ignoring that the target also depends on w. That is why it is only a "semi" gradient, and why it can fail.

With on-policy data and linear features, semi-gradient TD converges, to a point near the best linear fit (Tsitsiklis and Van Roy 1997). Sutton and Barto name the combination that can make it diverge: function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy training, the deadly triad. Any two are safe; all three together can send the estimates to infinity (Sutton and Barto §11.3). The smallest example is two states whose estimates share one weight, w and 2w (§11.2). The transition from the first to the second pays 0, so its TD error is γ·2w − w = (2γ − 1)w, and each update multiplies w by 1 + α(2γ − 1): greater than 1 whenever γ > 0.5, whatever the step size. Off-policy, nothing ever corrects it; on-policy, the agent would also visit the 2w state and update it toward what follows, which pulls w back.

Starting from w = 10, as in Sutton and Barto's text. On-policy here means each visit to the first state is followed by the 2w state's own update, a transition to the end with reward 0 (its gradient is 2, its feature value). The chart's vertical axis is logarithmic in |w|.

In practice, deep Q-learning lives with the triad. When van Hasselt and colleagues ran hundreds of DQN variants on Atari, value estimates often grew past the largest value achievable in the game (100 with clipped rewards and γ = 0.99), which they call soft divergence, but never became unbounded (van Hasselt et al. 2018). The tricks of the next section, the replay buffer and the frozen target network, are there to keep the triad tame. Two more practical problems that tables never had: consecutive transitions are strongly correlated, which a network trained by stochastic gradient descent dislikes, and the regression target moves every time the network is updated.

12Deep value methods: DQN and its fixes

DQN (deep Q-network) is Q-learning with a convolutional network as Q(s, a; θ), two stabilisers, and one set of hyperparameters for every game (Mnih et al. 2015, Nature; the 2013 workshop version used replay only, on seven games, arXiv 1312.5602):

L(θ) = 𝔼(s, a, r, s′) ~ buffer [ ( r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ−) − Q(s, a; θ) )² ]
buffer
the experience replay memory: the last 1 million transitions, sampled uniformly in minibatches of 32. Random sampling breaks the correlation of consecutive transitions and reuses each one in many updates; it is legitimate because the Q-learning target is off-policy.
θ−
the target network: a frozen copy of the weights, refreshed every C updates (10,000 in the paper), so the regression target stops moving while the network chases it.

The rest of the recipe: the input is the last four 84 × 84 greyscale frames (section 2's Markov fix); rewards clipped to [−1, 1] so one learning rate fits all games; γ = 0.99; ε-greedy with ε annealed from 1.0 to 0.1 over the first million frames; 50 million frames of training per game (about 38 days of play). Across 49 Atari games it outperformed the best earlier RL methods on 43 and reached more than 75% of a professional human tester's score on 29 (Nature letter). The scores and what came after, through Agent57 above the human baseline on all 57 games (Badia et al. 2020), are in Milestones and benchmarks.

Overestimation, and Double DQN. The max in the Q-learning target is biased upward whenever the estimates are noisy: the maximum of several noisy estimates is, on average, above the maximum of their true values, even if each estimate is unbiased (maximisation bias, Sutton and Barto §6.7). Double Q-learning decouples choosing the action from valuing it; Double DQN does it with the two networks DQN already has: y = r + γ Q(s′, argmaxa′ Q(s′, a′; θ); θ−), the online network picks, the target network values (van Hasselt et al. 2015, which found substantial overestimation in DQN on several games).

Maximisation bias: every action is worth 0, every estimate is unbiased, and still the max is positive

The Rainbow of fixes. Hessel and colleagues combined six extensions: Double DQN; dueling heads that split Q into a state value and action advantages (Wang et al.); prioritised replay that samples transitions with large TD error more often (Schaul et al.); multi-step targets (section 10's dial); distributional RL that learns the whole return distribution over 51 atoms (Bellemare et al.); and noisy nets for exploration. In their ablation, prioritised replay and multi-step returns mattered most, distributional RL next, and Double DQN and dueling made no significant aggregate difference once the rest were in (Hessel et al. 2017).

13Policy gradients, baselines and actor-critic

Value methods learn values and act greedily; policy-gradient methods parameterise the policy πθ(a | s) directly and climb the gradient of its expected return J(θ). That handles continuous and huge action spaces (no max over actions needed), learns stochastic policies naturally, and changes the policy smoothly. The key identity is the log-derivative trick, ∇θ π = π ∇θ log π, which turns the gradient of an expectation into an expectation you can sample, without differentiating through the environment. The result is the policy gradient theorem (Sutton et al. 1999):

∇θ J(θ) = 𝔼πθ [ Σt ∇θ log πθ(At | St) · Ât ]

Raising the log-probability of actions in proportion to Ât is the whole of policy-gradient RL. What differs between methods is, again, what stands in for Ât:

  • REINFORCE (Williams 1992) uses the sampled return Gt: unbiased, very high variance, needs episodes that end.
  • A baseline b(s) subtracted from the return leaves the gradient unbiased, because 𝔼a~π[∇ log π(a | s) b(s)] = b(s) ∇ Σa π(a | s) = b(s) ∇ 1 = 0, while it can cut the variance a lot. The natural baseline is V(s), which turns the return into the advantage A(s, a) = Q(s, a) − V(s): how much better the action did than expected (Sutton and Barto §13.4).
  • Actor-critic: learn that baseline with a critic, and use the critic's TD error δt = Rt+1 + γV(St+1) − V(St), or GAE's λ-weighted sum of them (section 10), as the advantage. Lower variance; biased by the critic. A3C (asynchronous advantage actor-critic) ran parallel workers asynchronously on CPUs and beat the Atari state of the art in half the training time (Mnih et al. 2016); A2C is its synchronous, batched version, which matched or beat A3C (the asynchrony was not what helped; see the Method atlas) and is structurally what modern LLM RL loops are. 14Trust regions: TRPO and PPO

A policy-gradient step is only trustworthy near the policy that collected the data: step too far and the new policy visits states the data never covered, the estimate is wrong, and one bad update can collapse performance. To reuse a batch for several updates you also need the importance ratio of section 9, rt(θ) = πθ(At | St) / πθold(At | St), between the policy being trained and the one that sampled. TRPO (trust region policy optimisation) made "take the largest step that is still safe" precise: maximise 𝔼[rt Ât] subject to the average KL divergence between old and new policies staying below δ, solved with conjugate gradients and a line search (Schulman et al. 2015). It works, at the cost of second-order machinery. PPO (proximal policy optimisation) gets the same effect first-order by clipping the ratio (Schulman et al. 2017):

LCLIP(θ) = 𝔼t [ min( rt(θ) Ât,  clip(rt(θ), 1 − ε, 1 + ε) Ât ) ]

Once the ratio has moved past 1 + ε in the direction a positive advantage wants (or below 1 − ε for a negative one), the objective goes flat and its gradient is zero: there is no reward for moving further. That is what makes several epochs of minibatch SGD on one rollout batch safe (10 epochs in the paper's MuJoCo runs, 3 on Atari). In the paper's own ablation over seven MuJoCo tasks, no clipping or penalty scored −0.39 (normalised) against 0.82 for clipping at ε = 0.2, the best of every variant tried, including fixed and adaptive KL penalties (0.62 to 0.74; PPO, Table 1). 15Continuous control: DDPG, TD3 and SAC

For a robot arm the action is a vector of torques, so Q-learning's max over actions is itself an optimisation problem. The off-policy actor-critics solve it by training an actor to output the action that maximises the critic:

  • DDPG (deep deterministic policy gradient) learns a deterministic actor μ(s) by following ∇a Q(s, a) through it, with DQN's replay and target networks (Lillicrap et al. 2015). Sample-efficient, but brittle.
  • TD3 (twin delayed DDPG) found that overestimation (section 12) persists in actor-critics and fixed it with three changes: two critics and the minimum of their targets, delayed actor updates, and noise added to the target action (Fujimoto et al. 2018).
  • SAC (soft actor-critic) maximises reward plus the policy's entropy, J(π) = Σt 𝔼[ r(st, at) + α H(π(· | st)) ], with a stochastic actor (Haarnoja et al. 2018). The entropy term keeps exploration from collapsing and smooths learning; α sets the trade (a later version tunes it automatically to a target entropy, Haarnoja et al. 2018b). Off-policy with replay, it became a common default for continuous control and robotics.

The trade-off among the families shows here most clearly. PPO is robust and simple and handles any action space, but it is on-policy, so every batch is used for a few epochs and discarded; SAC and TD3 reuse every transition many times from replay and need far fewer environment steps, which matters when the environment is a real robot.

16Models and search: Dyna, AlphaZero, MuZero, world models

Everything since section 9 has been model-free. Model-based RL learns or is given a model of the environment and uses it to plan, which can save enormous amounts of real experience: every imagined transition is free. Dyna (Sutton 1991; Sutton and Barto §8.2) is the simplest form: learn a model from real transitions, then run extra Q-learning updates on transitions the model imagines. The catch is that errors in a learned model compound over a plan: a small error per step becomes a large one fifty steps out, and a planner happily exploits the model's mistakes. So model-based methods tend to plan short, re-plan often, or plan in a learned space that only needs to predict what matters.

Search as policy improvement. When the model is exact (the rules of a board game), the best planner is a tree search guided by learned networks. Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) runs many simulated games from the current position, choosing moves in the tree by a mix of the network's prior, the values found so far and an exploration bonus (PUCT, a UCB descendant from section 7), and returns visit counts that are a better policy than the network's own prior. That turns search into the improvement step of generalised policy iteration (section 8): the search's move distribution is the policy target, the game's outcome the value target.

It does not mean "learned model": dynamic programming and AlphaZero are model-based with a given model, and of the methods on this page only MuZero and the world-model agents learn one. 17Learning without interacting: imitation and offline RL

Sometimes the agent cannot explore: a hospital cannot try random treatments, and a self-driving car cannot crash to learn. Two families learn from a fixed dataset instead.

  • Imitation learning. Behaviour cloning is supervised learning on expert (state, action) pairs. Its flaw is compounding error: a small mistake takes the agent to a state the expert never visited, where it makes bigger mistakes, so errors grow with the square of the horizon rather than linearly. DAgger fixes it by letting the learner act and asking the expert to label the states the learner actually reaches (Ross, Gordon and Bagnell 2011). Inverse RL infers the reward the expert seems to optimise and then runs RL on it; GAIL does this adversarially (Ho and Ermon 2016). Supervised fine-tuning of a language model on demonstrations is behaviour cloning.
  • Offline (batch) RL learns a policy better than the one that collected a fixed dataset, with no further interaction (Levine et al. 2020, tutorial). Its central problem is distribution shift: the max in a Q-learning target queries actions the data never tried, whose values are pure extrapolation, and the policy is drawn to exactly those overestimated actions. Offline methods therefore stay close to the data: Conservative Q-learning pushes down the values of unseen actions (Kumar et al. 2020); Implicit Q-learning never evaluates actions outside the dataset (Kostrikov et al. 2021); Decision Transformer treats RL as sequence modelling, conditioning on the return it wants (Chen et al. 2021).

"On-policy means online, off-policy means offline" is a common confusion. On- and off-policy say which policy the values describe; online and offline say whether the agent still interacts. Q-learning and DQN are off-policy while interacting online; PPO reuses each batch for several epochs and is still approximately on-policy. DPO, which trains a language model directly on a fixed set of preference pairs (section 18), is the best-known offline method in LLM post-training. The Taxonomy tab sets on-policy, off-policy and offline side by side.

18RL for language models: RLHF, GRPO and RLVR

Token generation is an MDP with unusual proportions. The state is the prompt plus the tokens generated so far (fully observable, section 2); the action is the next token, from a vocabulary of about 105; the transition is deterministic and known (append the token); the episode ends at an end-of-sequence token; and there is usually one reward, at the end, for the whole response, with γ = 1. So the environment model is trivial, and the hard parts are two: credit assignment (which of thousands of tokens earned the final score?) and the reward itself (where does a trustworthy score for "a good answer" come from?). Rollouts, sampling whole responses from a large model, dominate the cost.

RLHF: a learned reward and PPO

Reinforcement learning from human feedback learns the reward from people's comparisons, then optimises the policy against it. It began with simulated robots and Atari learning from human preferences between clips, optimised there with A2C and TRPO (Christiano et al. 2017); PPO arrived with fine-tuning language models from preferences (Ziegler et al. 2019); then it moved to summarisation (Stiennon et al. 2020), and became the standard recipe with InstructGPT (Ouyang et al. 2022; verified on the InstructGPT page):

  1. Supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations (behaviour cloning, section 17).
  2. A reward model (6B parameters) trained on rankings of 4 to 9 answers per prompt with a Bradley-Terry loss, −log σ(r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)) for every preferred-rejected pair.
  3. PPO (section 14) against that reward, with a per-token KL penalty to the frozen SFT model: the per-token reward is rt = 1[t = T] rφ(x, y) − β log(πθ(yt) / πref(yt)), β = 0.02, plus a pretraining-gradient mix ("PPO-ptx") that limits regressions on public benchmarks.

The headline: outputs of the 1.3B InstructGPT model were preferred to those of 175B GPT-3, a comparison made through a common baseline (win rates of 52.7% and 24.0% against 175B SFT), and 175B InstructGPT was preferred to 175B GPT-3 85 ± 3% of the time. The RL stage cost 60 petaflop/s-days against 3,640 for pretraining GPT-3, about 1.6%. PPO for RLHF keeps four models in memory: the policy, the frozen reference, the reward model and the value model (the critic), which is usually as large as the policy. The KL penalty does three jobs at once: it keeps the policy on the distribution where the reward model is accurate, it preserves what pretraining taught, and it acts as a trust region.

Two biases in GRPO's normalisation were pointed out in 2025 (Dr. GRPO, Liu et al.). Dividing by |oi| gives each token of a long wrong answer a smaller penalty than each token of a short one, so wrong answers drift longer (length bias); dividing by the group's standard deviation gives the lone correct answer in a hard group (1 of 16) nearly four times the weight of a correct answer in a medium one (8 of 16) (difficulty bias). GSPO (Zheng et al. 2025) moved the ratio and the clip from tokens to whole sequences. And because rollouts come from an inference engine whose probabilities differ slightly from the trainer's, LLM RL is mildly off-policy (section 9); current frameworks correct it with truncated importance weights.

RLVR: delete the learned reward

Where answers can be checked by a program (a maths answer matches, code compiles and passes tests), the reward model can be replaced by a verifier: reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards. The name comes from Tülu 3 (November 2024, optimised there with PPO and a value model; Lambert et al.). It removes reward-model error and the hacking it invites, and it is limited to checkable tasks. Read as a lineage, the three are two deletions: GRPO deletes RLHF's critic, and RLVR deletes its learned reward model.

DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al. 2025; verified on the DeepSeek-R1 page) made GRPO plus RLVR the default open recipe. R1-Zero ran it directly on the DeepSeek-V3 base with no supervised step: rule-based accuracy and format rewards only, no neural reward model "because of reward hacking". Its AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% in the paper's Nature version (71.0% in the January 2025 preprint), while answers grew from about 500 to about 14,200 tokens. R1 added a cold-start SFT stage, reasoning RL, rejection sampling into about 800K new SFT samples, and a final RL stage that used preference reward models only in its last 400 steps. Its RL and data generation cost 147K H800 GPU-hours (about $294K at $2 per hour) on top of the base model's 2.788M hours: a post-training bill, not "the cost of R1" (the Milestones and benchmarks tab keeps the dated figures side by side). Distilling R1's outputs into Qwen2.5-32B by plain SFT reached 72.6 on AIME, against 47.0 for R1-Zero-style RL on the same base.

Reward hacking and the limits of a proxy

Every RL method optimises the reward it is given, not the one intended (section 1's reward hypothesis). Classic RL has a long list of examples (DeepMind's specification-gaming list); with language models the proxy is a reward model or a test suite, and the policy finds length, flattery, format tricks and special-cased tests. Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured it: as the policy moves away from its start (measured as √KL), the proxy reward keeps rising while the true ("gold") reward rises, peaks and falls; bigger reward models over-optimise later (Gao et al. 2022). The KL penalty is the standard leash; the training topic animates it (Topic: llm-training-and-post-training, axis 4), and Reward Hacking catalogues measured cases and defences. Verifiers help but can be hacked too (a test that can be bypassed is a reward model with a bug).

Does RL teach or elicit? RLVR reliably turns pass@k into pass@1: it makes the model's best answers its usual answers. Whether it adds abilities the base model lacked is contested: Yue et al. found that at large k base models solve more problems than their RLVR-trained versions (2025), and DeepSeekMath already saw RL raise majority-vote accuracy but not pass@64. Other open threads are entropy collapse during RL, process rewards that score steps rather than outcomes, and agentic RL over many tool-using turns, where environment design and exact token accounting matter as much as the algorithm (Mercor's 397B-parameter run with SkyRL reports this reported; see RL for LLMs).

DPO, and where RL stops. Direct preference optimisation skips both the reward model and the RL loop: it rewrites the RLHF objective so that the policy itself is trained on preference pairs with a classification-style loss, −log σ(β log[πθ(yw)/πref(yw)] − β log[πθ(yl)/πref(yl)]) (Rafailov et al. 2023; verified on the DPO page). It is offline (section 17) and much cheaper; well-tuned online RL has generally matched or beaten it since. Constitutional AI replaces human harmlessness labels with AI feedback (RLAIF; Constitutional AI page). Four more trade-offs that cut across the table. Value-based, policy-based or actor-critic: value-based methods are sample-efficient with discrete actions and can learn off-policy from replay, but acting needs a max over actions; policy-based methods handle any action space and stochastic policies, with high-variance gradients; actor-critic buys lower variance with a learned critic and costs a second model (in PPO-based RLHF as large as the policy, which is exactly what GRPO deletes). Model-free or model-based: model-free methods need no model but learn only about the states they visit and usually need many samples; model-based methods can plan and reuse the model, but a learned model's errors compound over a plan. Monte Carlo or TD: unbiased, high-variance and episode-bound against biased, low-variance and online. On- or off-policy: simpler and more stable but data-hungry, against data reuse at the price of corrections and instability.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Reward and value are the same thing."
The reward is the one-step signal; the return is the discounted sum of rewards along one trajectory; the value is the expected return. A state with a negative reward can have a high value (Class 2 in the student MRP at γ = 1: reward −2, value 1.5).
"The observation is the state."
A single Atari frame, or the last message in a chat, can leave out what the future depends on. Markov is a property of the representation you choose (section 2).
"The history includes the action being chosen."
Ht ends at Ot, Rt, and the reward for At is Rt+1.
"γ = 1 is always wrong."
It is standard when every episode terminates, as in LLM RL. It is only a problem for continuing tasks, where returns can be infinite.
"A greedy policy can be read off V."
Greedy on V needs a one-step lookahead, so a model; greedy on Q does not. A model-free agent that stores only V cannot pick actions from it.
"The optimality equation can be solved by matrix inversion."
Matrix inversion solves the linear expectation equation for a fixed policy; the optimality equation's max makes it nonlinear, so it needs iteration.
"Model-based means a learned model."
Dynamic programming and AlphaZero are model-based with a given model; of the methods here, only MuZero and the world-model agents learn one.
"The reward model makes RLHF model-based."
It replaces the reward signal and nothing plans with it, so PPO-based RLHF is model-free. Model-based means predicting transitions and planning with them.
"On-policy means online, off-policy means offline."
On- and off-policy say which policy the values describe; online and offline say whether the agent interacts. Q-learning and DQN are off-policy while interacting online; PPO reuses each batch for several epochs and is still approximately on-policy.
"Q-learning is off-policy, so it needs importance sampling."
One-step Q-learning needs no ratio: its target takes the max over next actions instead of using the action the behaviour policy chose. Multi-step off-policy targets do need corrections.
"A baseline biases the policy gradient."
Any baseline that depends only on the state leaves the gradient unbiased, because the expected score function is zero; it only changes the variance (section 13).
"PPO's clip bounds the ratio."
It removes the incentive to move further; a large step can still overshoot 1 + ε (the animation in section 14 does it). Watch the KL and the fraction of clipped samples.
"GRPO has no value estimate, so it is something new."
It is a policy-gradient method whose baseline is sampled rather than learned: the group mean plays the role V(s) plays in an actor-critic. With one update per batch it is REINFORCE with a group baseline.
"Deep RL is a different theory."
Its targets are the classical ones: the Q-learning target in DQN, TD errors in PPO's critic, Monte Carlo returns in REINFORCE and GRPO. What is new is function approximation and the tricks that keep it stable.
"Token generation is a hard environment."
The transition is trivial (append the token). What is hard is assigning the credit for one end-of-sequence reward across thousands of tokens, and making the reward itself trustworthy.
"A higher reward means a better model."
When the reward is a learned proxy, the policy can keep climbing it while the true objective stalls or degrades (reward hacking), which is why RLHF keeps a KL penalty to a reference model and why RLVR prefers verifiers.
"Any goal can be written as a reward."
That is the reward hypothesis, an assumption rather than a theorem. When the scalar reward is a proxy for an intent with no mathematical definition ("helpful", "honest"), maximising it can defeat the intent (section 1).
"A pass@1 gain means a new capability."
It can be the model's best answers becoming its usual ones; compare pass@k at large k before claiming new abilities (section 18).
"One seed shows it works."
Deep RL results vary widely across seeds and implementation details; report many runs with intervals (section 14).

Open problems

  • Sample efficiency. Model-free deep RL needs millions of steps for tasks a person learns in minutes; model-based methods and better reuse of data (replay, offline RL) are the main answers, and none is general yet.
  • Exploration with sparse rewards. Section 7's strategies explore locally; deep exploration over long horizons, and exploration beyond what a pretrained model already does, remain open.
  • Reward specification. The reward hypothesis holds only as well as the reward is written: reward hacking scales with optimisation pressure (Gao et al.), and verifiers exist only for checkable tasks. Scoring open-ended work (rubrics, generative verifiers, AI feedback) is a main research front.
  • Credit assignment over long horizons. One reward at the end of a thousands-token answer, or of a hundred-turn agent episode, says little about which step mattered; process rewards and learned critics for LLMs are unresolved.
  • Generalisation. Agents overfit their training environments; procedurally generated benchmarks showed how far (Cobbe et al. 2019), and sim-to-real transfer in robotics is the practical version.
  • Stability and evaluation. The deadly triad has no general cure, and results vary across seeds; honest evaluation with many runs is still not the norm (Agarwal et al. 2021).
  • What RL adds to language models. Whether RLVR teaches new reasoning or sharpens what pretraining put there, and how far it scales, is the field's most debated question in 2026 (Yue et al.; RL for LLMs).

TD(0) moves V(S) a step α toward R + V(S′) after every step. Constant-α Monte Carlo waits until the episode ends, then moves every visited state toward the return that actually followed (here 0 or 1). Same walks for both.

    Batch updating: after each new episode, all episodes so far are replayed with a tiny α until the values stop moving.       3. The dial between them: n steps, or a decaying mix of all n

      Example 7.1 and Figure 7.2 (p. 145), Figure 12.3 (p. 291), Figure 12.6 (p. 295)

      The same kind of walk with 19 states, −1 for falling off the left and +1 off the right, values starting at 0 (true values −0.9 to 0.9). n-step TD waits n steps and uses the n rewards plus the estimate where it landed; n = 1 is TD(0), n longer than the episode is Monte Carlo. The λ-return averages all the n-step returns with weights (1 − λ)λn−1; the off-line version applies it at the end of each episode. TD(λ) gets nearly the same effect on-line: every visited state keeps an eligibility trace that decays by λ per step, and each step's TD error is shared out in proportion to the traces.

        4. Whose next action the target assumes: Sarsa against Q-learning

        Example 6.6 (p. 132)

        A 4 × 12 grid; start bottom left, goal bottom right, and between them a cliff: stepping in costs −100 and sends you back to the start; every other move costs −1. Both agents act ε-greedily (a random move 10% of the time) and update the value of the move they just made. Sarsa (on-policy) targets the value of the next move it will actually make, random moves included, so the cliff edge looks costly and it learns a path further up. Q-learning (off-policy) targets the best next move, as if it would never slip, so it learns the shortest path along the edge, and then keeps falling off it while exploring. Optionally, Expected Sarsa targets the average over the ε-greedy policy.

          How the lab works

          • Seeded and deterministic. Every random draw comes from a seeded generator (mulberry32), one stream per purpose and run, so a run replays identically, the two sides of each comparison see the same walks where that makes sense, and a Python port can match the page exactly. Long averages run in small slices between frames, so the page stays responsive on a phone.
          • Checked against Python. src/lab/check.py ports the engine line by line and reproduces the page's numbers bit for bit on every experiment (random walks, every parameter and step size of the three 19-state families, the three cliff agents, all five gridworld methods, the step-by-step traces). It also checks independently: Figure 4.1 against its printed values, true values by linear solve, the batch closed forms against replayed batches, the λ-return recursion against equation 12.3, the cases where methods coincide (n = 1 and λ = 0 are TD(0); n ≥ episode length and λ = 1 are Monte Carlo), and the cliff's exact ε-greedy returns by linear solve.
          • Exact definitions. Undiscounted everywhere (γ = 1). Monte Carlo on the walks is every-visit constant-α (equation 6.1); on the gridworld it is first-visit sample averages (the book's box in Section 5.1). Dynamic programming sweeps are synchronous (two arrays), which is what Figure 4.1's numbers require; an in-place sweep gives different intermediate values. Value iteration and policy iteration stop when no value moves by more than 0.0001; policy iteration starts from the random policy and breaks ties toward the first move in the order up, down, right, left. n-step TD follows the box in Section 7.1. The off-line λ-return takes its targets from the values held at the start of the episode and applies them in time order at its end (equation 12.4). TD(λ) uses accumulating traces (Section 12.2). On the cliff, ties between equal Q-values are broken at random; the greedy path reported afterwards takes the first best move and counts as "none" if it loops or falls.
          • What is assumed, and what is not compared. The book does not state α for the cliff (0.5 here, labelled) or how many runs its cliff graph averages; it states 100 runs for the random walks and 10 episodes for the 19-state sweeps, used here. Apart from Figure 4.1, whose values are printed, the book's results exist only as plotted curves, and this page never reads numbers off a plot: it tests the shapes and orderings the text and captions state.
          • Not built. The 10-armed testbed (Figures 2.2 and 2.4) and Expected Sarsa's step-size sweep (Figure 6.3): see src/lab/viz_ideas.md for why.

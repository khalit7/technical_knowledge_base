The MoE layer as DeepSeek configured it

DeepSeekMoE, from V2, makes two changes to the Mixtral-style mixture of experts (a handful of large experts, two active per token): fine-grained experts, each expert split into narrower ones with more active per token, so V3 routes 8 of 256 rather than 2 of 8 at a similar active budget; and shared experts that every token passes through, absorbing the common computation that would otherwise be duplicated inside every routed expert. Mixtral's 2 of 8 gives 28 possible expert sets per token per layer; V3's 8 of 256 gives about 4.1 × 1014, which is what lets experts specialise narrowly. Why both moves work, measured, is on {{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}} (section 5).

Balancing without a loss (V3)

Left alone, routers collapse onto a few experts, and under expert parallelism the busiest GPU sets everyone's step time. The standard fix, an auxiliary loss, is a second objective pushing tokens away from the expert that would have modelled them best. V3 instead adds a per-expert bias bi to the affinity for the top-k choice only; the gate weight that scales the expert's output still comes from the unbiased affinity, so the bias never enters the gradient. After each step every overloaded expert's bias drops by γ and every underloaded one's rises by γ: 0.001 for V3's first 14.3T tokens and 0 for the last 500B. V3 kept only a tiny sequence-wise auxiliary loss (weight α = 0.0001) and dropped no tokens in training or inference; V4 uses the same values ({{V3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}).

Worked example (illustrative numbers). A token scores expert A at 0.62 and expert B at 0.60, with one slot left. A has been overloaded for 30 consecutive steps, so bA = −30 × 0.001 = −0.03; B's bias is 0. The selection scores are 0.59 for A and 0.60 for B, so the token goes to B, and B's gate weight is computed from its true 0.60: the bias moved the decision boundary and nothing else. Real load curves with and without the bias, from trained models, are animated on {{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}} (section 3).

Anticipatory routing (V4) answers loss spikes, which DeepSeek traced to outliers in the MoE layers that routing amplifies: at step t the features use the current parameters but the routing indices are computed with the parameters of step t − Δt, decoupling the router's updates from the backbone's. It is not always on: an automatic detector triggers a short rollback and switches it on only when a spike occurs, at about 20% extra wall-clock time while active ({{V4 report|https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19348}}).

Multi-token prediction (V3 and V4)

V3 trains one extra MTP module that predicts the token after next. Unlike parallel independent heads, it is a sequential transformer block fed the trunk's representation and the next token's embedding, so the causal chain is kept. V3 and V4 use depth 1, weighted 0.3 in the loss for most of training and 0.1 at the end. DeepSeek use it twice: denser supervision in training, and at inference a built-in draft for speculative decoding, the second token accepted 85% to 90% of the time for 1.8 times the tokens per second ({{V3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}). The objective and its measured worth are on {{Families and objectives|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8105b616c17c1439f766}}; drafting on {{Speculative decoding|n:3f35c17b0d0d81e086fde85940ebac1c}}.

Training systems: FP8, DualPipe, DeepEP

DeepSeek trained V3 on H800s, whose interconnect bandwidth is cut relative to the H100, so communication rather than FLOPs was the binding constraint: 20 of each H800's 132 streaming multiprocessors were given over to communication, and V3 trained with 16-way pipeline parallelism, 64-way expert parallelism across 8 nodes and ZeRO-1 data parallelism ({{V3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}). Each choice below trades complexity for bandwidth.

FP8 training (V3), FP4 experts (V4)

V3 was the first validation of FP8 training at this scale, roughly halving memory traffic against BF16. Naive FP8 fails because the format's dynamic range is small: one outlier forces a tensor's scale down and small gradients flush to zero. The recipe: one scale per 1 × 128 tile of activations and per 128 × 128 block of weights, so one outlier cannot poison a whole tensor; master weights, optimiser state, normalisation, the embedding and the output layers kept in higher precision; and accumulation promoted to FP32 every 128 elements, because the H800 tensor cores keep only about 14 bits when accumulating FP8 products. The loss stays within 0.25% relative error of BF16 ({{V3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}). V4 goes further at the serving end: routed expert weights stored in FP4 through quantisation-aware training, and the lightning indexer computing in FP4 ({{V4 report|https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19348}}). The formats and scaling granularity are taught on {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}.

DualPipe: feed the pipeline from both ends

Pipeline parallelism leaves bubbles, stretches where a stage waits on its neighbour. DualPipe feeds micro-batches in from both ends and pairs one micro-batch's forward chunk with another's backward chunk (F&B), so the MoE all-to-all of one hides behind the compute of the other. The chunks have durations F (forward), B (full backward) and W (the backward-for-weights part, which zero-bubble schedules split off to fill gaps). DeepSeek publish each schedule's bubble and memory ({{DualPipe repository|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DualPipe}}; Table 2 of the {{V3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}):

DeepEP: kernels shaped like MoE traffic

DeepEP, DeepSeek's open expert-parallel communication library, is hand-written dispatch and combine kernels with separate NVLink and RDMA paths and a low-latency variant for decode. It exists because MoE traffic is a skewed, dynamically routed, token-level all-to-all, not the uniform exchange generic collectives are built for. V4 folds computation, communication and memory access for the MoE layer into a single fused kernel ({{V4 report|https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19348}}).

V4's residual stream and optimiser: mHC and Muon

mHC widens the residual stream to nhc = 4 streams mixed around each block, and projects the stream-mixing matrix onto the doubly stochastic matrices (rows and columns sum to one) with 20 Sinkhorn-Knopp iterations, so a deep stack can average streams but never amplify them; unconstrained hyper-connections had been unstable. Why, with DeepSeek's measured gains, is on {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}}. Muon replaces AdamW for most weight matrices (each momentum update orthogonalised by Newton-Schulz iterations; {{Optimisers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}} teaches it), with AdamW kept for the embedding, the output head and the RMSNorm weights. V4-Flash was pretrained on 32T tokens and V4-Pro on 33T ({{V4 report|https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19348}}).

mHC's equation and the Sinkhorn-Knopp projection, on a 4 × 4 matrix (interactive)
Xl+1=BlXl+Clℱl(AlXl)
Xl
the residual state before layer l, of shape nhc × d
ℱl
the layer (attention or MoE), with its usual d-dimensional input and output
Al
(1 × nhc) mixes the streams into the layer's input
Cl
(nhc × 1) spreads the layer's output back over them
Bl
(nhc × nhc) mixes the streams with each other, projected to be doubly stochastic

A doubly stochastic matrix has spectral norm at most 1, so the residual mapping cannot amplify the signal, and the set is closed under multiplication, so a deep stack stays bounded too. The three maps are generated from the input, which is why this costs little: nhc is tiny next to d ({{V4 report|https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19348}}).

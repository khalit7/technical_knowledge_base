The idea in one screen

Every named DeepSeek idea answers one question at a different layer of the transformer: what does a frontier-quality model cost to serve, and to train on bandwidth-limited GPUs? Each release keeps the earlier answers and attacks the next cost.

DeepSeek is a spinoff of High-Flyer, a Chinese quantitative hedge fund. It publishes weights and tech reports, though not data, under permissive licences (R1 and V4.1-Flash are MIT), so its layout became the template other open MoE models reuse: {{Kimi K2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f5abf5dbdbac821a12}} keeps MLA and the fine-grained-plus-shared expert layout, at 384 experts (compare them on the {{Config diff|#t-cfg}} tab). Its price cuts are where the serving arithmetic reaches the market: V2 started the Chinese API price war, and V3.2-Exp cut API prices by more than half ({{DeepSeek|https://api-docs.deepseek.com/news/news250929/}}).

Attention: three cuts to what a decoded token reads

Decode reads every cached key and value once per new token, so the cache decides how many sequences fit on a GPU and what each token costs. What one token reads in one layer is entries read × numbers per entry, and DeepSeek cut each factor in turn, then cut what is stored at all. Fig. 2 counts it for the whole model at a 131,072-token context: the solid column is what one decoded token reads (blue cache entries, gold indexer keys), the teal glass behind it what the cache stores.

  1. The baseline: MHA at V3's dimensions. Every cached key and value is read: per layer 131,072 entries × 32,768 numbers (2 × 128 heads × 128) = 4.29 billion, and over V3's 61 layers 262 billion numbers for one decoded token. Read and stored are the same thing here.
  2. Cut 1, MLA (V2): a smaller entry. One 576-number entry per token (a 512-number latent plus a 64-number RoPE key), 57× fewer: 75.5 million numbers a layer, 4.61 billion for the model. The scale changes, so the MHA column, 57 times taller, is cut at the top of the card. Every entry is still read.
  3. Cut 2, DSA (V3.2-Exp): read fewer entries. Attention reads only the top 2,048 entries, 64 times fewer (1.18 million numbers a layer), but the lightning indexer scores all 131,072 with its own 128-number keys (16.8 million, gold): 18.0 million a layer, 1.10 billion for the model, 24% of MLA's. Most of what a DSA token reads is the indexer. The cache grows: 5.63 billion numbers stored, 22% more than MLA, because every indexer key stays resident.
  4. Cut 3, V4-Pro: store fewer entries. 30 CSA layers keep one 512-number entry per 4 tokens (32,768, plus 128 in the exact window), read the top 1,024 and the window, and index the 32,768; 31 HCA layers keep one entry per 128 tokens (1,024 plus the window) and read them all. One token reads 162 million numbers, 6.8× fewer than DSA and about 1,600× fewer than MHA; 649 million are stored. The scale changes again: MLA's column is cut now.
  5. V4-Pro close up: two kinds of layer. A CSA layer reads 4,784,128 numbers, most of them indexer keys; an HCA layer reads 589,824 (drawn alternating). The CSA indexer key is taken as 128 numbers, as DSA's (an assumption: Attention variants gives the V4 indexer as 64 heads of 128, not the key size). These are counts of numbers, not bytes: the indexers run in FP8 (DSA) or FP4 (V4), so in bytes the gold share is smaller.

Fig. 2: heights to scale within each step, derived from the configs (V3, V3.2-Exp and V4-Pro config.json; the formulas are in parts/26_js_ds_0data.js). MLA (V2): a 576-number entry where MHA caches 32,768

At V3's 61 layers in BF16 that entry is 70,272 bytes per token (68.6 KiB), against 3.81 MiB for MHA and 244 KiB for 8-group GQA, yet every head still rebuilds its own keys and values from the latent instead of sharing them as GQA does. Against DeepSeek's own dense 67B, V2 reported a 93.3% smaller cache and 5.76 times the maximum generation throughput ({{V2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04434}}). The price is extra projection arithmetic and kernels of its own (FlashMLA), which is why DSA and V4's CSA are built on MLA's decode shape rather than on GQA. {{See MLA step by step on Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}}: the before/after animation first built for this page.

DSA (V3.2-Exp): keep the whole cache, read the top 2,048

V3.2-Exp (29 September 2025) left MLA's cache in place: the lightning indexer, a few small heads in FP8 with ReLU instead of a softmax for throughput, scores every cached entry, and attention reads only the top 2,048. It is an approximation, so it was trained in, continued from V3.1-Terminus, and DeepSeek reported parity with V3.1-Terminus on standard and long-context benchmarks. Every entry stays resident and the indexer still scores them all. DeepSeek cut API prices by more than 50% when it shipped ({{DeepSeek announcement|https://api-docs.deepseek.com/news/news250929/}}).

CSA and HCA (V4): store less

CSA layers merge every 4 tokens into one entry and run DSA's selection over the merged entries (top 1,024 in V4-Pro, 512 in V4-Flash); HCA layers merge every 128 tokens and read all of them; both keep a 128-token exact window. V4-Pro interleaves 30 CSA and 31 HCA layers; at a 1,048,576-token context a CSA layer stores 262,144 entries and an HCA layer 8,192, each plus the window. DeepSeek report the cache at about 2% of BF16 GQA-8 at 1M tokens (a baseline already 16 times smaller than MHA), and, against V3.2 at 1M tokens, 10% of the cache and 27% of the single-token FLOPs for V4-Pro, 7% and 10% for V4-Flash ({{V4 report|https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19348}}). {{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}} reproduces the 2% from the config.

V4.1-Flash: the cache argument reaches the shape of the network

V4.1-Flash (September 2026) is a 552B encoder-decoder, the first shipped at frontier scale since the field settled on decoder-only stacks, and DeepSeek's reason is the KV cache, not quality ({{model card|https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash}}). With cross-layer sparse attention with index reuse, hierarchical retrieval, an Engram memory and FP4 cache storage, the reported result is 890 bytes per token, roughly a quarter of V4-Flash's HBM footprint and an eighth of its SSD footprint ({{zartbot|https://zartbot.github.io/blog/model_arch/dsv41flash_arch/en.html}} gives the HBM and SSD ratios as runtime and persistent cache storage). {{T5|n:3d45c17b0d0d81ec8ebfec02e953a7fd}} had argued the encoder-decoder case on quality grounds without moving anyone; {{End-to-End Context Compression at Scale|n:3d45c17b0d0d81eb90a8d70a5ce84d19}} argued it on this systems ground.

Predict: how many active parameters does a prompt position use, against a generated token? Step 2 and step 4 of Fig. 3 answer it.

  1. 40 layers, cut in two. A 40-layer transformer split into a 20-layer causal encoder (layers 0 to 19, numbered from 0) and a 20-layer decoder (layers 20 to 39). The experts: 384 routed experts with 6 active per token.
  2. Prefill: a prompt position stops at the encoder. Prompt positions pass through the 20 encoder layers only, so prefill activates 8B parameters. The prompt is encoded once into a shared representation rather than carried as a per-layer cache through one causal stack.
  3. One shared cache, projected from the encoder. The halves connect not through T5-style cross-attention: the decoder's global keys and values are projected from the encoder's 20th-layer hidden state by layer-dependent projection weights, so decoder layers reuse one shared cache.
  4. Decode: a generated token runs all 40 layers. Each generated token passes through all 40 layers, so decode activates 16B. One active-parameter count does not describe this model.
  5. Four layers of forty store a cache. Each layer runs in one of three modes: full layers compute their own cache entries and top-k indices; reindex layers reuse a full layer's entries but pick new top-k indices with their own indexer query; reuse layers take both unchanged. Layers 0 and 1 are sliding-window only (the last 128 positions); the rest of the encoder is three blocks of one full layer and five reuse layers, its entries merging two adjacent positions into one (compress_ratios = 2); the decoder keeps every position: layer 20 is full and also builds the indexer's candidate pool, layers 24, 28, 32 and 36 reindex, all the others reuse. So the stack keeps 4 cache copies for 40 layers (3 encoder, 1 decoder), and 8 layers run an indexer. The Engram memory (gold) is injected at layers 1 and 14.

Fig. 3: layer modes from the released {{config.json|https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash/blob/main/config.json}}, read with {{zartbot|https://zartbot.github.io/blog/model_arch/dsv41flash_arch/en.html}}; active parameters from the {{model card|https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash}}. Slabs are layers, not to scale in parameters.

The indexer's cost stops growing with the context. At decode, the full layer 20 ranks the context in blocks of 8 positions and keeps up to 2,048 blocks, a candidate pool of up to 16,384 positions; each later indexing layer ranks only that pool and keeps its own top 512 ({{zartbot|https://zartbot.github.io/blog/model_arch/dsv41flash_arch/en.html}}; candidate_topk_blocks, candidate_block_size and index_topk in the config). The model card's phrase is "bounding deeper indexer cost independently of context length". Slide the context: the first stage grows with it, the pool stops at 16,384.

What one V4.1 cache entry costs in bytes (an estimate against the reported 890)

DeepSeek's attention kernels store a V4.1 cache entry as 528 bytes in FP8 (512 values plus 16 scale bytes) or 288 bytes in FP4 (512 values at half a byte plus 32 scale bytes) ({{FlashMLA|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/FlashMLA}}), and the model card says the main cache is FP4. As an estimate, not DeepSeek's breakdown: if the three encoder copies hold one entry per two positions and the decoder copy one per position, the main cache is 3 × 288 ÷ 2 + 288 = bytes per token; the reported 890 also counts the indexer keys.

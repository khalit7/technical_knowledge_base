Serving: split the phases, replicate the hot experts

DeepSeek serve prefill and decode on separate GPU pools (disaggregation, now standard; taught on {{Distributed and disaggregated serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d8170bd7cdf47af0ec07b}}): V3 and R1 prefill on units of 4 nodes (32 GPUs, EP32) and decode on 18 nodes (144 GPUs, EP144), each with 32 redundant experts, so 256 + 32 = 288 expert copies: 9 routed experts per GPU in prefill and 2 in decode, each GPU also holding the shared expert ({{day 6|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/open-infra-index/blob/main/202502OpenSourceWeek/day_6_one_more_thing_deepseekV3R1_inference_system_overview.md}}).

The bias loop balances the router in training, but a deployed model still has hot experts, and the GPU holding them sets everyone's step time. The Expert Parallelism Load Balancer (EPLB) duplicates the heaviest experts and packs the copies so every GPU carries a similar load, each copy taking an equal share of its expert's traffic ({{EPLB repository|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/EPLB}}). Its hierarchical policy, used when the number of nodes divides the number of expert groups, packs whole expert groups onto nodes, then replicates and packs within each node: V3's routing is group-limited (a token's experts come from at most 4 of its 8 expert groups, topk_group and n_group in its {{config.json|https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3/blob/main/config.json}}), so this keeps that traffic inside a node. The global policy replicates anywhere. The README suggests hierarchical for prefill (smaller expert parallelism) and global for decode (larger).

The engine that runs this is partly public. DeepInfer, open-sourced on 20 October 2026 under Apache 2.0, is the offline-inference version of "the high-performance inference framework that powers DeepSeek's large-scale production services": it serves V4.1-Flash on NVIDIA SM100 (Blackwell) GPUs and Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs with attention data parallelism, MoE expert parallelism plus EPLB and DeepSeek's own kernels, and descends from vLLM. DeepSeek kept back the KV cache system, prefill/decode disaggregation and load balancing across data-parallel replicas, the parts that make the deployment above a deployment ({{DeepInfer|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepInfer}}; where it sits among serving engines: {{Topic: inference-and-serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}}).

Try: the default is the README's worked example (12 experts in 4 groups, 4 redundant copies, 2 nodes of 4 GPUs, the published loads of its first layer); the EPLB view reproduces its published placement exactly. Switch to the naive view (expert i on GPU i mod 8, no copies) and read the max-to-mean ratio: how much longer the busiest GPU takes than an even split. Post-training: GRPO, R1 and one checkpoint

GRPO, from DeepSeek's {{DeepSeekMath|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} work, is PPO with the value network deleted: sample a group of completions for one prompt, score them, and use the group's own statistics as each completion's baseline, Âi = (ri − mean(r)) / std(r). Rewards 1, 0, 0, 1 give mean 0.5 and standard deviation 0.5, so the correct answers get +1 and the wrong ones −1; a group with identical rewards teaches nothing. That buys roughly half the memory and one fewer model to babysit, and fits verifiable-reward RL, where many rollouts per prompt are cheap to score; it moves the cost into sampling, and later work showed the standard-deviation normalisation biases across prompt difficulty and response length. The full objective, its group visual and the successors are on {{RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}.

R1-Zero and R1. R1-Zero applied GRPO directly to the V3 base with rewards only from automatically checkable outcomes (mathematics with a known answer, code that passes tests) plus format, with no supervised reasoning traces: RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Long chains of thought, self-checking and backtracking emerged from that alone, and the emergence, not the benchmark number, is the result that mattered: reasoning was reachable without proprietary reasoning data. R1 proper alternates supervised fine-tuning and RL four times over, and most of its data was generated by its own predecessors: the cold-start set was partly R1-Zero output made readable, and 600K of the 800K supervised samples were sampled from the RL checkpoint itself, keeping only correct answers ({{R1 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948}}, section 2.3).

Try: click a stage for its data, its reward and what it fixed. Band widths follow the published sample counts (the cold start is only "thousands", so it is drawn thin).

R1 is V3-Base fine-tuned on data its own RL checkpoints wrote

The stages in numbers: R1-Zero's AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 71.0% (86.7% with majority voting), with reflection appearing unprompted but poor readability and language mixing; R1's reasoning RL added a language-consistency reward; rejection sampling kept about 600K reasoning samples, joined by about 200K non-reasoning samples from V3's pipeline; V3-Base itself was fine-tuned for two epochs on the 800K, then a final RL stage used rule-based rewards for mathematics, code and logic and learned reward models for general prompts; R1 scores 79.8% ({{R1 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948}}).

Distilled students. DeepSeek fine-tuned Qwen and Llama bases from 1.5B to 70B on the same 800K samples, with SFT only, showing the behaviour transfers by ordinary supervised fine-tuning on traces; that is why half the open reasoning models of 2025 were R1 distillations. Distillation in general is on {{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}.

At 14B and 32B the students keep most of the teacher's AIME score

AIME 2024 pass@1 of the students: Qwen-1.5B 28.9, Qwen-7B 55.5, Llama-8B 50.4, Qwen-14B 69.7, Qwen-32B 72.6, Llama-70B 70.0, against the teacher's 79.8 and o1-mini's 63.6; with cons@64 the 7B reaches 83.3 against o1-mini's 80.0.

Hybrid thinking (V3.1 onward) folds chat and reasoning into one checkpoint, long-chain mode selected by the prompt template rather than by loading different weights ({{DeepSeek|https://api-docs.deepseek.com/news/news250821/}}): one set of weights, one cache, one deployment, and the caller pays for deliberation only where it earns its latency. Specialists, then one student (V4): V4's post-training first trains a separate expert model per domain (mathematics, coding, agents, instruction following), each by SFT and then GRPO against domain reward models, then consolidates them into one model by on-policy distillation: the unified model samples its own outputs and is trained to minimise the reverse KL divergence to the teachers ({{V4 report|https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.19348}}).

What it cost: the $5.6M is one run

V3's official training run took 2,788K H800 GPU hours, priced by DeepSeek at an assumed $2 per GPU hour on a 2,048-GPU cluster: $5.576M. DeepSeek say it excludes prior research and ablations ({{V3 report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437}}, Table 1). R1's own training added a reported 147K hours, $294K at the same rate ({{R1 paper, v2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948v2}}, appendix B.4.4).

The headline figure is the rented-hour price of the final runs, not the cost of building the model

GPU hours to scale: pre-training 2,664K, context extension 119K, post-training 5K (V3 report, Table 1), then R1's 147K. The dashed box is what DeepSeek exclude and do not disclose. Outside estimates use different units and are not drawn: SemiAnalysis put the hardware available to DeepSeek at about 50,000 Hopper GPUs and $1.6B of server capex ({{SemiAnalysis|https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/deepseek-debates}}, an outside estimate).

Serving margin. DeepSeek's "545% cost-profit margin" is theoretical: one day (27 to 28 February 2025) cost $87,072, an average of 226.75 H800 nodes at $2 per GPU-hour, against $562,027 of revenue had every token been billed at R1's prices; actual revenue was substantially lower, because V3 is cheaper, web and app use is free and night-time discounts applied ({{day 6|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/open-infra-index/blob/main/202502OpenSourceWeek/day_6_one_more_thing_deepseekV3R1_inference_system_overview.md}}).

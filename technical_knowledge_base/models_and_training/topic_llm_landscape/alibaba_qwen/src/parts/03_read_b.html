The MoE layer: from no shared expert to 512 tiny experts

Qwen3: 128 experts, top-8, no shared expert. Qwen3-235B-A22B has 94 layers, each with 128 experts, of which the router picks 8 per token ({{Qwen3 report|@q3r}}, Table 2; {{config|@c235}}). The report states both departures from its predecessor: "Unlike Qwen2.5-MoE, the Qwen3-MoE design excludes shared experts", and it adopts the global-batch load-balancing loss "to encourage expert specialization". DeepSeek, by contrast, routes 8 of 256 finer experts plus a shared expert that absorbs the common computation. Leaving it out is simpler and adds no fixed cost per token, at the risk that every routed expert relearns general behaviour.

Global-batch balancing: balance over the mix, not over each document

A load-balancing loss ({{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}} owns it) is usually computed per micro-batch: a few sequences, often packed from one domain, so a micro-batch of pure code is pushed to spread over experts that should not see code. Qwen computes the same statistics over the whole global batch, synchronised across data-parallel ranks, so a code sequence may route to code experts as long as the diverse mix is balanced.

The same routing costs 4 times the minimum per micro-batch and nothing over the global batch

Predict, then slide: at 100% each domain sends its tokens to its own two experts; towards 0% routing is uniform and the two measures agree. Illustrative toy: 8 experts, four single-domain micro-batches, Pi = fi; the formula is the paper's (eq. 2 and 3), in the depth block.

The evidence ({{Qiu et al., 2025|@gbl}}, up to 42.8B total parameters on 400B tokens): about 0.1 lower pre-training perplexity, about 2 points on benchmarks, much stronger domain specialisation of experts, and under 3% extra latency, because the synchronised statistic is one number per expert. The catch: a router balanced over the corpus can be unbalanced within one sequence, and so on individual devices at serving time.

From Qwen3-Next on: 512 experts, 10 routed plus 1 shared

Qwen3-Next reversed the no-shared-expert choice and pushed granularity much further: 512 experts, 10 routed per token plus 1 shared, each expert's hidden width only 512, and the router's parameters normalised at initialisation so no expert is favoured before training ({{model card|@nxc}}, {{blog|@nxb}}, {{config|@nxcfg}}). Every later large Qwen keeps the layout: Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next all route 10 of 512 plus 1 shared ({{Qwen3.5 card|@c35}}, {{2.4T card|@c24}}, {{Flash-Next card|@fn}}). "Qwen has no shared expert" is true of the Qwen3 generation only.

A token now lights 2.1% of a layer's experts instead of 6.25%, and each expert is bigger

One square is one expert in one layer, area to scale between the two layouts; blue squares are the routed experts this token uses, green the shared expert. 10 of 512 routed is 1.95% (2.1% counting the shared expert) against 8 of 128, 6.25%: that is what lets total parameters grow far faster than per-token compute. The routing pattern is random, as an illustration; counts and parameters come from each config.json.

Where the parameters live. Each expert is a SwiGLU block with three matrices (gate, up, down), so Pexpert = 3dmodeldff, stored L × E × Pexpert and active L × k × Pexpert (E and k count the shared expert).

For Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B: 3 × 8,192 × 2,048 = 50,331,648 per expert, × 513 experts (512 routed plus 1 shared of the same width) × 92 layers = 2.375T, reproducing the 2.4T headline ({{config|@c24cfg}}). Experts are 97 to 99% of every Qwen MoE; the worked examples are in the depth block.

What 6B active buys is the absolute figure, since 6B active costs roughly what a 6B dense model costs to decode. High sparsity still costs memory for every expert, all-to-all traffic, harder balancing and harder fine-tuning, because each expert sees fewer tokens; the shared expert's return answers the last: at 512 experts each routed expert sees about one fiftieth of the token stream (10/512), and an always-on expert keeps the common computation in one well-trained place.

Attention: the 3:1 hybrid of Gated DeltaNet and gated attention

A linear-attention layer keeps a fixed-size state per head instead of a growing cache, so its memory per request is constant and its cost linear in sequence length; the price is lossy recall of early tokens, which a few full-attention layers buy back. {{Beyond attention|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8142bc9ecac1fc366966}} owns the mechanism (the gated delta rule St = αtSt−1(I − βtktkt⊤) + βtvtkt⊤ of {{Yang et al., 2024|@gdn}}, with a worked demo of why a write that replaces beats one that piles up). What Qwen chose:

  • The ratio. Qwen3-Next has 48 layers as 12 × (3 × (Gated DeltaNet → MoE) → 1 × (Gated Attention → MoE)): 36 linear and 12 full. Qwen's blog says that at 3:1 "the model consistently outperforms any monolithic architecture", all-attention or all-linear ({{Qwen3-Next blog|@nxb}}).
  • The output gate. In Flash-Next the DeltaNet output gate is a sigmoid where the original Gated DeltaNet uses SiLU; the paper reports "consistent improvements" ({{Flash-Next architecture paper|@fnp}}, eq. 6 to 11). The Qwen3.8-27B and 2.4T configs still list output_gate_type: swish ({{27B config|@c27cfg}}, {{2.4T config|@c24cfg}}); Flash-Next's lists sigmoid ({{config|@fncfg}}).
  • The interval, everywhere since. Qwen3.5-397B has 60 layers, 15 full; Qwen3.8-27B 64, 16 full (its feed-forward layers are dense, so it is "dense" in the MoE sense only); Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B 92, 23 full; Flash-Next 48, 12 of them sparse attention.
With one full-attention layer in four, the Gated DeltaNet hybrid beats all-full attention by 3.9 points

Average of 9 base-model benchmarks (MMLU, MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, MATH, GSM8K, BBH, MMMLU, EvalPlus, MultiPL-E), unweighted. Each model is a 28-layer 25B-A3B MoE on the Qwen3.5 architecture, trained on 400B tokens at 4K and then 80B at 32K; the sliding-window (SWA) layers use a 128-token window. The GDN hybrid beats full attention on 8 of the 9 benchmarks and the SWA hybrid on 7 ({{Flash-Next paper|@fnp}}, Table 1: 49.87, 51.15, 53.81).

Why not more linear layers. Recall through the state is lossy; the retrieval and copying behaviours agents depend on travel through the full-attention layers, which the paper calls "particularly important for long-context performance". Those layers keep their rotary embedding: a variant without positional encoding (NoPE) looked the same in pre-training, but after post-training showed "a substantially higher rate of endless generation" ({{Flash-Next paper|@fnp}}).

Qwen's full-attention layer: gated attention

  • An output gate: each head's output is multiplied by σ(Wgxt) before the output projection. Qwen's own NeurIPS 2025 paper found it removes the attention sink (attention on the first token fell from 46.7% to 4.8%) and the massive activations that go with it, and improves stability and long-context extrapolation ({{Qiu et al., 2025|@gat}}); {{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}} owns sinks and gates, with the full numbers.
  • Head dimension 256 instead of 128, with few key-value heads: 2 in Qwen3-Next, Qwen3.5 and Flash-Next, 4 in Qwen3.8-27B and the 2.4T model.
  • Rotary embedding on the first 25% of dimensions only (64 of 256, partial_rotary_factor: 0.25 in every hybrid's config); the rest carry no position signal, which the blog credits with better length extrapolation. Partial RoPE is taught on {{Positional encodings|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}.

What the cache costs. Only the full-attention layers grow a cache. Qwen3-235B (94 full layers, 4 KV heads of 128) stores 188 KiB per token, 47 GiB for one request at 262,144 tokens; Qwen3-Next stores 24 KiB per token, 6 GiB, plus 36 MiB of fixed DeltaNet states, which are cheaper than the cache they replace beyond 512 tokens. Across the 3.8 generation: Qwen3.5-397B 30 KiB, Qwen3.8-27B 64 KiB, Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B 92 KiB, half of what Qwen3-235B needs for ten times the parameters. The arithmetic is in the depth block; every Qwen checkpoint is a preset in the {{KV-cache calculator|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5#t-kv}}.

What it bought, as Qwen reported it ({{Qwen3-Next blog|@nxb}}, vendor figures on Qwen's own serving stack):

  • Training. Qwen3-Next was pretrained on 15T tokens, a uniformly sampled subset of Qwen3's 36T, with less than 80% of the GPU hours of Qwen3-30B-A3B and 9.3% of the compute cost of the dense Qwen3-32B, and outperformed Qwen3-32B-Base on most benchmarks.

QSA and the Qwen4 preview: Qwen3.8-Flash-Next

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next was released on 26 August 2026 as an explicit preview of the Qwen4 architecture, to give developers "an opportunity to prepare for the full Qwen4 model family" ({{TechNode|@tnfn}}). It keeps the 3:1 layout and changes what the quarter of global layers do ({{Flash-Next card|@fn}}, {{Flash-Next architecture paper|@fnp}}). The model is pretrained with ordinary full attention there; at continued pretraining (256K-token sequences) those layers, and the one in the MTP module, are replaced by Qwen Sparse Attention, two passes per query:

  1. A compressed indexer scores micro-blocks. Keys are average-pooled over blocks of r = 4 tokens, and a small multi-query attention (4 query heads, 1 shared key head of 128) scores every block.
  2. Attention reads only what was selected. Attention (24 query heads, 2 KV heads of 256) reads the top ⌈K / r⌉ = 512 blocks, a token budget of K = 2,048, plus the tokens of the last, unfinished block, which are always kept.

Why blocks. The indexer's own cost. DeepSeek's DSA scores individual tokens, and "as sequence length increases, the overhead of its O(n²) indexer remains non-negligible"; compressing keys fourfold cuts indexer work and top-k selection by about the compression ratio, from O(n2) to O(n2/r). Switch the animation above to "Hybrid + QSA" to watch both passes. With QSA, Qwen joins DeepSeek (DSA, then CSA), Moonshot (KDA) and MiniMax (MSA) in shipping a trainable sparse or linear attention in an open-weight line; Zhipu's GLM-5.3-Flash, released the same week, pools every four indexer keys into one in the same way ({{Zhipu: GLM|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a88a84c2ca84b4daf4}}; {{MarkTechPost comparison|@mtpcmp}}).

Trained in two phases (indexer alone on about 2B tokens, then indexer and backbone together on about 200B), the language-modelling loss stays within about 10−4 of the full-attention run.

Results against full attention (same model, {{Flash-Next paper|@fnp}}, Tables 2 to 4): QSA matches or beats full attention on 7 of 8 short-context benchmarks (average 75.9 to 76.8), and does better as context grows: RULER beyond 512K from 90.08 to 93.00; 8-needle MRCR from 30.66 to 40.53 at 512K and from 20.71 to 26.44 at 1M. The MTP draft's mean accepted length is unchanged (4.06 against 4.07).

Worked example at T = 1,000,000 (Flash-Next's extended context): the indexer scores 1,000,000 ÷ 4 = 250,000 blocks; attention reads 2,048 tokens instead of 1,000,000, about 488 times fewer. The measured speedup of the attention module at 1M is 7.6 times for prefill and 4.9 times for decode, not 488, because the indexer still scans every block and selection and gathering cost time (kernel-level measurements; speedups start at 64K).

The cache does not shrink

Every key must stay available for the indexer to choose from, so Flash-Next still stores 12 × 2 × 2 × 256 × 2 bytes, 24 KiB per token, plus the indexer's keys. QSA cuts reads and arithmetic, not storage. And the selection is hard: a block the indexer scores low is never read, however much it mattered.

The other Qwen4-preview changes: capacity off the accelerator

  • The n-gram embedding is looked up at layer 2 with a contextual gate. Its address is known from the input tokens alone, so the table can live in host memory and be prefetched while layer 1 runs: capacity at negligible per-token compute and no accelerator memory. A larger n-gram vocabulary lowered loss monotonically while downstream accuracy saturated, one of several places where the paper found loss and benchmarks disagreeing.
  • A gated residual stream widened to 4 branches, read through a sigmoid gate and written back with one scalar per branch, instead of hyper-connections' 4 × 4 mixing matrix.
  • Muon for the two-dimensional weight matrices, AdamW for the rest; the full run had no loss spike, without qk-clip or other activation clipping.
  • Also: a vision encoder, and 262,144 tokens of native context, extensible to 1,000,000.

The headline. The base model was compared with the previous generation's 397B-A17B flagship, which the paper's Table 11 names as Qwen3.7-Plus, a closed, hosted model (not the open Qwen3.5-397B-A17B of the same size). Flash-Next leads on 8 of 14 benchmarks and trails on the other 6 by at most 2.6 points, with a third of the activated parameters, a third of the training tokens and about a ninth of the training FLOPs. It also beats Qwen3.8-27B-Base on all 14.

Test-time compute: computation spent at inference, deliberating before answering; a reasoning model is trained to use it well.
Chain of thought (CoT): the tokens a model generates before its final answer, holding intermediate steps. In reasoning models it usually sits in a separate thinking block that the product may hide, summarise or show.
Verifier: a program that checks an answer: exact or numeric match against a reference, a unit-test suite, a proof checker. Correct by construction on the property it checks.
Reward model: a learned network that scores an answer. It generalises to tasks with no checker, and it has errors that optimisation can find. An outcome reward model scores the finished answer, a process reward model (PRM) every step.
pass@k: the probability that at least one of k independent samples is correct: what best-of-k with a perfect verifier achieves.
Self-consistency (majority voting): sample several answers and return the most common final answer. Written cons@k or maj@k for k samples. Best-of-n: sample n answers and return the one a scorer (verifier or reward model) rates highest.
In-context learning: picking up a task from demonstrations in the prompt, weights frozen; zero-, one-, few-shot name how many demonstrations (section 10).
RLVR: reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: the model's own attempts are scored by a program, not a learned model.

One question, three places to spend the budget

Serial: one chain written longer, which can re-read and revise its own working (section 1, 2). Parallel: several independent chains and a selector, a checker, a vote or a reward model, that picks one answer (sections 3 to 5). Latent: the same layers looped inside the network, depth that never becomes text (section 9). Underneath: how long to think (section 6), what the tokens cost (section 7), and how a model learns to use any of it (section 8).

1. Why a chain of thought adds computation

The intuition is scratch paper. A model that must answer in its first token gets one forward pass of computation, however hard the question. A model allowed to write its working first gets one forward pass per token written, can look back at what it wrote, and can cross it out. A transformer forward pass has fixed depth, so the serial computation available to produce one next-token distribution is bounded by the layer count; a chain of thought removes that bound, and the context window acts as an external working memory persisting across the steps.

Three capabilities follow. Decomposition: a problem needing more serial steps than the model has layers becomes reachable. Externalised state: intermediate results are written down instead of held in activations discarded at every token. Revision: the model can read what it wrote, notice a contradiction and start again, which no single forward pass can do. A reasoning model is trained, typically by large-scale reinforcement learning on tasks whose answers can be checked mechanically (mathematics with a reference answer, code with unit tests), to plan, verify, backtrack and self-correct in that working rather than to emit a plausible-looking derivation (section 8).

The working memory is not free: every generated token leaves keys and values in the KV cache for every later token to attend to. The formula (2 × layers × key-value heads × head dimension × bytes per number, per token) and a calculator for 45 real models are on Long context (its KV-cache calculator), with T counting the prompt as well as the thinking; the serving techniques that make long traces affordable (paged attention, cache quantisation, multi-query and latent attention) are on Batching, KV cache and scheduling in depth.

What test-time compute does not buy. It adds no knowledge that is not in the weights or retrievable by a tool. It does not make a problem solvable if the model cannot make any correct partial progress on it, since there is nothing for later steps to build on. And the returns are shaped badly for economics (sections 3 and 7).

The theory: a change in kind, not only in amount

Transformer decoders allowed a number of decoding steps logarithmic in the input length gain "only slightly" over a single pass, while a linear number of steps lets them recognise all regular languages and a polynomial number lets them recognise exactly the problems solvable in polynomial time, under standard complexity conjectures and a mild generalisation of pre-norm in the normalisation layers (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023). That is the whole mechanism: each emitted token is another full forward pass conditioned on everything written so far, so serial steps become unbounded.

Every token written is another full pass through the layers

Dserial=L×T
Dserial

L

T

Answering directly in one token allows 60 × 1 = 60 serial layer applications; thinking for 10,000 tokens first allows 60 × 10,000 = 600,000, four orders of magnitude more from the same weights. 2. One long chain or many: the same budget, to scale

Sequential and parallel share one budget in different shapes. Sequential costs irreducible latency (10,000 thinking tokens at an illustrative 50 a second is 200 seconds before the answer starts), a KV cache growing linearly with the trajectory, and attention over an ever-longer scratchpad getting more expensive per token. Parallel need not add wall-clock time given the hardware, but total tokens scale with the sample count and it lives or dies on the selector. Predict before it plays: which shape spends less wall-clock time, and which one can fix its own mistake?

  1. The budget. 10,000 thinking tokens drawn as 40 blocks of 250. Every block is 250 forward passes through all 60 layers, and every token in it leaves 240 KiB of keys and values in the cache.
  2. One long chain: attempt 1. Laid end to end along wall-clock time, one block every 5 seconds at 50 tokens a second. The first 3,500 tokens work the problem and reach a candidate answer, 412. Each new token attends to everything before it (the dots run back along the chain).
  3. Re-check ("Wait"), then attempt 2. The model rereads its own working, finds a step that contradicts an earlier one, backs up (1,000 tokens), and attacks again from the corrected step (3,500 more). Only a sequential chain can do this: the later tokens see the earlier ones. Nothing here runs in parallel: token 6,000 cannot start before token 5,999 exists.
  4. Verify, then answer at 200 s. The model substitutes its result back into the problem (1,500 tokens), then commits to 384 (500). 10,000 tokens at 50 a second is 200 seconds before the answer starts. How likely the answer is to be right has no formula: it depends on how well the model revises.
  5. The same 40 blocks as five chains. The same prompt sampled five times, independently and at a temperature above zero, 5 × 2,000 tokens. All five generate at once, so wall-clock time follows the longest chain, not the total: the same 10,000 tokens take 200 seconds as one chain and 40 as five, and hold the same total KV memory.
  6. Five answers at 40 s. Each chain commits: 384, 412, 384, 97, 384. No chain saw another, so chain 2's mistake was never corrected; it can only be outvoted or rejected.
  7. Majority vote. The most common final answer wins: 384, three votes of five. If each chain is right with p = 0.6 and the wrong ones all agree on one answer, a five-vote is right 68.3% of the time; if wrong answers scatter over four values, 83.5%. Voting needs answers in a comparable form (a number, a choice), which is why it works for mathematics and not for essays.
  8. A verifier instead. Where a checker exists (unit tests, a proof checker, a reference answer), only accepted answers survive (chains 1, 3 and 5) and the selector needs just one: pass@5 = 1 − (1 − 0.6)5 = 0.990. A learned reward model in the checker's place is weaker: a larger sample is also a larger search for its errors (section 3).

Illustrative throughout: the problem, the traces and the answers are invented to show the mechanism, on section 1's illustrative model (240 KiB of KV cache a token, 50 tokens a second). The vote and verifier probabilities are computed from section 3's formulas at p = 0.6 per chain (0.683, 0.835 and 0.990).

What it shows. The same 10,000 tokens and the same total KV memory, five times faster in parallel. The single chain can notice its own error and repair it; the five chains can only be outvoted or rejected, so the result is only as good as the selector. They compose: "heavy" tiers sample several long sequential traces in parallel and aggregate them, which the root compares lab by lab (Topic: llm-landscape section 2).

3. What parallel sampling is worth: the selector decides

Parallel sampling is the one way of spending test-time compute whose returns can be written down exactly, and the formulas explain most of the folklore about it. Three selectors, three formulas, one chart.

A perfect verifier reaches pass@k

If one sample solves a problem with probability p, and samples are independent:

pass@k=1−(1−p)k
p
the single-sample success probability on this problem
k
the number of samples drawn
(1 − p)k
the probability that all k fail

With p = 0.2: one sample succeeds 20% of the time; five give 1 − 0.85 = 1 − 0.32768 = 0.672; ten give 1 − 0.810 = 1 − 0.107 = 0.893. A verifier that recognises a correct answer turns this directly into accuracy, which is why best-of-n against unit tests or a proof checker is the strongest form of parallel scaling. It also shows the ceiling: a problem with p = 0 stays unsolved at any k.

Majority voting amplifies whatever is most likely

Self-consistency needs no verifier, only answers that can be compared. In the simplest case, one correct answer against one competing wrong answer, with n odd:

Pmaj=∑j>n/2(nj)pj(1−p)n−j
Pmaj
the probability that the majority answer is correct
j
runs over the numbers of correct samples that form a majority
term
the binomial probability of exactly j correct samples out of n

With p = 0.6 and n = 5: 10 × 0.63 × 0.42 + 5 × 0.64 × 0.4 + 0.65 = 0.3456 + 0.2592 + 0.0778 = 0.683, up from 0.6. With p = 0.4 the same vote gives 0.317, down from 0.4, and more samples make it worse: voting amplifies whichever answer is most likely, right or wrong. Real wrong answers usually scatter, which is why voting works as well as it does: with p = 0.4 and the remaining 0.6 spread evenly over four different wrong answers (0.15 each), the correct answer is the most likely single answer, and a plurality vote is right 54.9% of the time over 5 samples and 78.0% over 15.

The stage runs the scattered case at p = 0.4 on fifteen samples, then lets the wrong answers agree.

  1. One problem, five possible answers. The right answer comes up with p = 0.4 per sample; the other 0.6 is spread evenly over four wrong answers, 0.15 each. The floor plates are the five answers; nothing has been sampled yet.
  2. Fifteen samples land in piles. Fifteen independent samples, each sorted onto the plate of its final answer. This draw is illustrative, its counts at their expectations rounded (6 right; 3, 2, 2 and 2 wrong).
  3. A perfect verifier needs one right sample. It checks every pile and lights the right one; it succeeds whenever that pile is not empty: pass@5 = 1 − 0.65 = 0.922 and pass@15 = 1 − 0.615 = 0.9995.
  4. A vote picks the tallest pile. With the wrong answers scattered, the right pile (6) is the tallest, because the right answer is the most likely single answer. The exact plurality vote is right 54.9% of the time over 5 samples and 78.0% over 15.
  5. Let the wrong answers agree. Merge the four wrong answers into one at 0.6: its pile (9) now outgrows the right one, and the vote returns the wrong answer. Over 5 samples the vote is right 31.7% of the time, below one sample's 40%; over 15, 21.3%. The verifier is unaffected: it never compares piles.
  6. What each selector is worth as n grows. The three rules at n = 5, 15 and 63, computed exactly (all three are 40% at n = 1): the verifier climbs to 1, the scattered vote to 99.1% at 63, and the agreeing vote falls to 5.4%.

The original self-consistency paper sampled 40 reasoning paths and reported gains over single-sample chain-of-thought prompting of 17.9 points on GSM8K (60.1% to 78.0% with code-davinci-002), 11.0 on SVAMP, 12.2 on AQuA, 6.4 on StrategyQA and 3.9 on ARC-challenge (Wang et al., 2022, Tables 2 and 3). The gap between voting and a verifier is the part people forget: on MATH with Llama-3-8B-Instruct, the chance that some sample is right rose from 82.9% at 100 samples to 98.44% at 10,000, while majority voting or a reward model rose only from 40.50% to 41.41% over the same range (Brown et al., 2024).

Over a whole benchmark the curves change shape: problems have different p, so coverage follows a power law in k and a vote saturates at the share of problems whose right answer is the most likely one. The Benchmark sampling lab tab computes both exactly and recomputes Brown et al.'s coverage fits.

Best-of-n against a learned reward model is a search for its errors

With a learned reward model as the selector, a larger n is a stronger optimisation of the proxy, and the true quality first rises and then falls. Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) measured this against a much larger "gold" reward model standing in for the true objective, and fitted:

Rbon(d)=d(αbon−βbond),d=KLbon,KLbon=lnn−n−1n
Rbon
the gold reward gained over the unoptimised policy (zero at n = 1 by definition)
KLbon
the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the best-of-n distribution from the original policy, which has this closed form (computed analytically, not measured)
d
its square root, the paper's measure of how far optimisation has moved the policy
αbon, βbon
fitted coefficients that change smoothly with the proxy reward model's size; the paper shows them only as plots, so the values used here are illustrative
n
the number of samples

The KL grows only logarithmically in n, so the peak arrives late but it arrives: past it the proxy score keeps rising while true quality falls, and nothing in the proxy signals it. The general over-optimisation picture is on Reward Hacking.

The gold reward peaks and falls while the proxy keeps climbing

Defaults reproduce the worked numbers by construction: with n = 16, KL = ln 16 − 15/16 = 2.773 − 0.938 = 1.835 and d = 1.355; with illustrative coefficients α = 1 and β = 0.25 (not the paper's fitted values), R = 1.355 × (1 − 0.25 × 1.355) = 0.896; the peak is at d* = α/(2β) = 2, reached near n = 147, and by n = 4,096 the gold reward has fallen back to 0.876. A larger reward model behaves like a smaller β: the peak moves out, which is why a larger reward model beats a larger n.

The unbiased pass@k estimator, and Gao et al.'s RL form

In evaluation, pass@k is estimated from n ≥ k samples of which c are correct, with the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021), who drew n = 200 samples per problem for k ≤ 100 and showed that the naive 1 − (1 − c/n)k is biased:

pass@k^=1−(n−ck)(nk)
n
samples drawn per problem
c
how many of them are correct
ratio
the probability that a random subset of k of the n samples contains no correct one

Defaults: C(8, 5) = 56 and C(10, 5) = 252, so the estimate is 1 − 56/252 = 0.778.

How far the naive estimate falls below the true value, and how the gap closes as n grows, is drawn with both estimators side by side on Coding benchmarks, which owns pass@k as benchmarks report it.

For RL against the same proxy Gao et al. fit a different form, RRL(d) = d(αRL − βRL ln d), which declines more slowly than best-of-n's.

4. Verifiers and process reward models

Where no program can check an answer, the selector is learned, and where it looks matters. An outcome reward model (ORM) reads a finished solution and predicts whether it is right. A process reward model (PRM) predicts, for each intermediate step, whether the solution so far is on track. That buys two things: an error is caught at the step where it happens rather than diluted over a long answer, and search can prune a bad partial solution before paying to finish it.

Lightman et al. (2023), "Let's Verify Step by Step", compared the two on MATH: "process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision", and their process-supervised model "solves 78% of problems from a representative subset of the MATH test set"; they released PRM800K, "800,000 step-level human feedback labels". Human step labels are expensive, so later work labels steps automatically: Snell et al. (2024) found a PRM trained on PRM800K "easy to exploit" with their PaLM 2 models (a distribution shift from its GPT-4 samples) and instead estimated each step's correctness from Monte Carlo rollouts continued from it, so the PRM's per-step prediction is a value estimate of reward-to-go; their PRM "consistently outperforms the ORM".

  1. One solution, step by step. A three-step solution to one problem, each step a plate along the way from the problem to its final answer, 17. It is one of the solutions the beam search below will finish.
  2. An outcome reward model reads only the end. The ORM sees the finished solution and returns one score for all of it: nothing says which step went wrong, and nothing can be pruned until the solution is complete.
  3. A process reward model scores every step. The PRM predicts after each step how likely the solution so far is to end correct: 0.82, 0.88, then 0.62 at the step where it goes wrong. The error is caught where it happens.
  4. Beam search: N = 4 first steps, scored. For the same problem the base model proposes four first steps; the PRM scores each partial solution: 0.82, 0.35, 0.67, 0.21.
  5. Keep the top N / M = 2. Only the two best prefixes survive (0.82 and 0.67). Compute is no longer spent on the two that already look wrong.
  6. Expand each M = 2 times, score again. Each kept prefix gets two continuations, back to four candidates (0.88, 0.54, 0.71, 0.30), and the top two (0.88 and 0.71) are kept again.
  7. Finish four solutions. The kept prefixes are expanded to complete answers; a solution's score is the PRM's prediction at its last step: 0.91 and 0.62 from the first, 0.74 and 0.40 from the second.
  8. Best-of-N weighted. Scores are summed per final answer: 42 gets 0.91 + 0.74 + 0.40 = 2.05, 17 gets 0.62. The answer 42 is returned, a vote weighted by the verifier.

Snell et al.'s procedure (Section 5.2) with N = 4 beams and beam width M = 2: sample N first steps, score them with the PRM, keep the top N/M, sample M continuations of each, repeat to the end (at most 40 rounds in the paper), then pick the answer by best-of-N weighted, summing the PRM's scores over solutions with the same final answer. A solution's score is the PRM's prediction at its last step, which they found worked better than the product or minimum of the step scores. The scores and answers here are illustrative.

Search against a verifier is still optimisation against a learned model, with section 3's failure. Snell et al. found beam search best at low generation budgets and on harder questions, while "on the easier problems (bins 1 and 2), beam search shows signs of over-optimization with higher budgets, whereas best-of-N does not". Where answers can be checked by a program, prefer the program: it cannot be over-optimised on the property it checks (only gamed around it, section 8).

5. Compute-optimal test-time scaling: the best spend depends on the question

Which way to spend a fixed budget depends on how hard the question is for this model. Snell et al. (2024) binned questions by the base model's own success rate and compared, per bin and per budget, sequential revisions (the model rewrites its previous answer) against parallel samples, and beam search against a PRM against best-of-N.

Choosing the setting per question, "compute-optimal" allocation, improved "the efficiency of test-time compute scaling by more than 4× compared to a best-of-N baseline", and "on problems where a smaller base model attains somewhat non-trivial success rates, test-time compute can be used to outperform a 14× larger model" in a FLOPs-matched evaluation. The qualifier is the point: on problems the small model cannot make progress on, pretraining scale still wins. In practice the difficulty is not known in advance, so it is estimated (by the verifier, by a router, or by the model itself deciding how long to think), which is where section 6 begins.

The best way to spend the budget changes with how hard the question is

Easier for this modelPurely sequential revisions win: "easier questions benefit from purely sequential test-time compute". Against a PRM, best-of-N beats beam search, which over-optimises.

6. How long to think: budgets, effort, routers, and overthinking

Once a model reasons, the control problem is how much. There are three levers, from the bluntest to the most adaptive: a token budget the caller sets, an effort level within which the model decides, and a router that picks a model or mode per request. Which lab ships which, and the five production mechanisms side by side, is the root's comparison (Topic: llm-landscape section 2, "Who decides how much it thinks"); here is how each works.

Budgets. Anthropic's budget_tokens (introduced with Claude 3.7 Sonnet, "think for no more than N tokens, for any value of N up to its output limit of 128K tokens", Anthropic, February 2025, and the only mode on the Claude 4 and 4.5 models) and Gemini 2.5's original thinking budget are the token-count form. A budget is the tightest bound on cost, but a fixed allowance whatever the difficulty, and truncation can leave a derivation with no conclusion. The better version conditions the model on the budget during training, so it learns to compress and land a conclusion inside the allowance; that is where the research is.

Effort levels and routers move the decision to the model or to a classifier: an effort level is soft guidance within which the model may skip thinking on easy queries, and a router picks a fast or a deep model per request; research systems now train the decision per step and report cutting reasoning tokens by roughly half at little cost in success (details closed below).

Overthinking is real and measurable. On some inputs, longer chains reduce accuracy: the model revisits a correct answer and argues itself out of it, or drifts towards distractors. Gema et al. (2025) built tasks where "extending the reasoning length of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) deteriorates performance", with five failure modes across model families: Claude models increasingly distracted by irrelevant information, o-series models overfitting to problem framings, a shift from reasonable priors to spurious correlations, loss of focus on long deductions, and amplified concerning behaviour. This is the failure mode adaptive-depth work exists to fix, and it means "always think harder" is not a safe default.

Vendor details: budget minimums and targets, effort levels, routers

Effort levels. Anthropic replaced the budget with adaptive thinking and an effort setting: manual budgets are deprecated on the 4.6 models, and Claude 4.7 and later "reject requests that use it, returning a 400 error" (Anthropic docs). Effort is "soft guidance for how willing Claude should be to think and how deeply", and at low effort the model may skip thinking on simple queries (Anthropic: steering thinking). Gemini models "engage in dynamic thinking by default", controlled by thinking_level; the accepted levels (minimal, low, medium, high) and the default vary by model, and the Gemini API now uses it for the 2.5 models too (Gemini docs).

Routers. GPT-5 shipped as "a smart, efficient model", "a deeper reasoning model" and "a real-time router that quickly decides which to use based on conversation type, complexity, tool needs, and your explicit intent" (OpenAI, August 2025). Research systems now train the decision per step: CogRouter reports an 82.3% success rate with "62% fewer tokens" than its baselines (Yang et al., February 2026), and Ares cuts reasoning tokens "by up to 52.7% compared to fixed high-effort reasoning, while introducing minimal degradation in task success rates" (Yang et al., March 2026).

Anthropic's budget must be at least 1,024 tokens and below max_tokens, and it "sets a target" rather than a strict cap; with interleaved thinking it can exceed max_tokens because "the budget spans all thinking blocks within one assistant turn" (Anthropic docs). So max_tokens, not the budget, is the hard ceiling on a single response.

Budget forcing: stop the thinking at the budget, or suppress the stop and append "Wait"

7. What reasoning tokens cost

Thinking is paid for three times. Money: thinking tokens are billed as output tokens, the expensive side of the price sheet; Claude 3.7 Sonnet cost "$3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens", which "includes thinking tokens" (Anthropic, February 2025). Time: the answer cannot start until the thinking ends (section 2). Memory: every thinking token holds cache for the rest of the request (section 1). And the bill is for the thinking the model did, not the thinking you see: "You are billed for the full thinking process, not the thinking content visible in the response" when the API returns a summary; usage.output_tokens_details.thinking_tokens reports how many billed output tokens were reasoning, 312 of 348 in Anthropic's own example (Anthropic: steering thinking).

This is why the economics of test-time compute are unfavourable by construction: cost and latency grow linearly with thinking tokens while accuracy grows roughly with their logarithm over a useful range and then flattens, so each point costs more than the last; and why launches now lead with "same score, fewer tokens". Cost per task across the labs, with each one's prices and token counts, is the root's section 6.

8. How a model learns to spend it, and what that training produces

How a model learns to use a scratchpad well has two answers, and current practice uses both. The algorithms (PPO, GRPO and its fixes) are on RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR; this section is about what each recipe produces.

Supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces. Collect problems, generate or obtain long chains of thought, keep the ones whose final answers are correct (rejection sampling, the STaR idea), and train on them as ordinary next-token prediction. Cheap, stable, no rollout infrastructure, and how a small model inherits a large one's reasoning style: DeepSeek's distilled R1 variants from 1.5B to 70B were produced exactly this way, from 800,000 R1-curated samples, "only SFT" and no RL stage (DeepSeek-R1; the R1 paper page). Small curated sets go further than expected: s1 fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on s1K, "1,000 questions paired with reasoning traces" (Muennighoff et al., 2025). Its limits are structural. The student is bounded by the teacher. It imitates the surface form of deliberation, including steps it could not have derived itself, so it learns what reasoning looks like rather than a policy it can execute reliably. And because the training set is filtered for success, it contains almost no genuine errors and so teaches almost nothing about recovering from one.

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Let the model generate its own trajectories and score the final answer with a program: string or numeric match against a reference for mathematics, a test suite for code, a checker for formal proofs. Two properties follow. First, the reward is not a learned model, so the usual reward-hacking channel (exploiting a reward model's misgeneralisation) is closed; what remains is gaming the checker itself, for example code that special-cases the visible tests. Second, and more important, the trajectories are the model's own, so credit assignment is on-policy: what gets reinforced is behaviour this model can actually produce. That is why long traces, self-checking and backtracking emerge under RLVR rather than having to be demonstrated.

R1-Zero was the clean demonstration: pure RL on a strong base model (DeepSeek-V3-Base), no supervised reasoning data at all, produced spontaneous re-derivation and reflection, and its responses grew over training "from hundreds to thousands" of reasoning tokens purely because longer thinking earned more reward, while AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (86.7% with self-consistency) in the expanded version (Nature, 2025; the January 2025 arXiv v1 reported 71.0%, and 86.7% with majority voting over 64 samples). The model learned to spend test-time compute because spending it was rewarded. The production recipe combines the two in a specific order, and R1's is the openly documented version:

RLVR's binding constraint is that it needs a verifier, so it covers mathematics, code and formal domains cleanly and writing, analysis and judgement poorly. Extending it means reintroducing a learned reward model or model-graded rubrics, which reopens exactly the reward-hacking channel that made RLVR attractive; length penalties and process reward models (section 4) only partially fix the verbosity and hacking that follow. The optimiser behind most reasoning RL is GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, from DeepSeekMath): per prompt it samples a group of G completions, scores each with the verifier, and gives every token of a completion the same advantage, its reward minus the group mean over the group standard deviation, with no critic network. For test-time compute this means an RL run's bottleneck becomes generation throughput, since every prompt needs G long rollouts, which is why RL stacks run vLLM or SGLang inside the training loop and treat rollout and training weight synchronisation as a first-order engineering concern.

GRPO's zero-signal groups: why easy and impossible prompts waste the batch

A group where all G completions are correct, or all wrong, has every advantage zero and contributes nothing but its sampling cost; a prompt the model solves with probability p gives such a group with probability pG + (1 − p)G, so on easy or impossible problems that is most of the batch. Every token of a failed trajectory is penalised equally, including correct early steps, because no critic remains to tell them apart. The worked example (rewards 1, 0, 0, 0 give +1.732 and −0.577), the PPO comparison, the KL term and the fixes most 2026 recipes use in place of textbook GRPO (Dr. GRPO, DAPO's higher upper clip, dynamic sampling of degenerate groups) are on RL for LLMs.

Each square is one completion with its advantage (green: verifier accepted). The zero-signal share is derived: with G = 4 a prompt solved 5% of the time wastes 81% of its groups; with G = 16, 44%.

R1's four stages: format first, then capability, then distil, then polish

1. Cold start a small SFT set of curated long chains of thought fixes format and readability (R1-Zero's traces were correct but mixed languages and were painful to read).
2. Reasoning RL large-scale RLVR for capability.
3. Rejection sampling the RL model's own best trajectories become a new SFT set, used both to re-tune the flagship and to distil smaller models.
4. Second RL a final RL stage over all scenarios.

RLVR sharpens; it does not extend

RLVR-trained models beat their base models at small k such as pass@1, but the base models reach a higher pass@k when k is large: the RL model concentrates probability on solutions the base model could already sample rather than acquiring new ones. In pass@k terms, RL raises p on problems where it was already above zero and can drive it to zero where it was small, and 1 − (1 − p)k rewards breadth at large k. Pretraining quality still bounds the ceiling, which is the most important thing to hold before assuming an RL stage will fix a capability gap.

Over 450 GRPO steps, pass@1 climbs and pass@256 does not

Yue et al. (2025) trained Qwen2.5-7B with GRPO and measured both ends of the pass@k curve at each checkpoint (their Tables 3 and 4), plotted as published. On Omni-MATH-Train pass@1 rises from 9.9% to 42.5% while pass@256 falls from the base model's 67.2% to 64.3%. published Whether newer, longer RL runs escape the pattern is debated on RL for LLMs, and agentic post-training shows the same trade on Sharpening Tax in Post-Training.

The mechanism on ten illustrative problems: sharpening alone costs no coverage, dropping problems does

Ten illustrative problems with base-model p from 0.6 down to 0.005. After RL, p becomes 1 − (1 − p)s on the problems it kept and 0 on the ones it dropped. pass@k is the mean of 1 − (1 − p)k over the ten. With nothing dropped there is no crossover: sharpening alone costs no coverage, which is why the crossover Yue et al. measured is evidence that RL narrows what the model samples.

9. Recurrent depth: spending the budget inside the network

The latent way spends the budget in activations rather than in tokens. A looped transformer reuses the same layers several times per token, so serial depth grows without adding parameters and without writing anything down. The idea goes back to the Universal Transformer (Dehghani et al., 2018), which applied one block repeatedly with a learned halting rule. The clearest modern form is Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025): a prelude that embeds the token, a recurrent core iterated r times with the input re-injected each time, and a coda that decodes. Its 3.5-billion-parameter proof of concept, trained on 800 billion tokens with layers (2, 4, 2) and a mean of 32 iterations, improved on reasoning benchmarks "up to a computation load equivalent to 50 billion parameters".

Dtoken=Lprelude+Lcore×r+Lcoda
Dtoken
serial layer applications spent on one token
Lprelude, Lcore, Lcoda
layers in each part (2, 4 and 2 in Huginn)
r
how many times the core runs, chosen at inference

Huginn at its training mean: 2 + 4 × 32 + 2 = 132 layer applications per token from 8 distinct layers. The stage runs the same 8 layers of weights three ways, to scale: an ordinary model buying 32 layers of depth by writing four tokens, Huginn's shape buying the same 32 in one token by looping its core, and RLT carrying a state from token to token.

  1. A token arrives. An ordinary transformer with 8 distinct layers, drawn as a stack of plates. One forward pass is 8 layer applications in a row, and that is all the serial computation one token can get.
  2. Token 1. The activations climb the 8 layers once and the model emits a token. The only way to compute more is to write something down and start again from it.
  3. Token 2. The next forward pass reads token 1 back in. Depth now accumulates across tokens, but only through the text: the top-layer state of token 1 is discarded, and what crosses to token 2 is the sampled token.
  4. Token 3. 8 more applications, one more token of chain of thought.
  5. Token 4: depth by writing. 32 layer applications of serial depth (D = L × T = 8 × 4), bought with 4 tokens a monitor can read, a 4-step wait, and 4 tokens of KV cache in every layer. This is chain-of-thought reasoning.
  1. A token arrives. The same 8 distinct layers, arranged as in Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025): a 2-layer prelude, a 4-layer recurrent core and a 2-layer coda. The core's output is fed back into its own input, with the token's embedding re-injected each time.
  2. Prelude. The prelude embeds the token into the latent space the core works in: 2 layer applications.
  3. Core, loop 1. The 4 core layers run once. Instead of passing on to the coda, the result loops back to the core's first layer.
  4. Loops 2 to 7. The same 4 layers, the same weights, six more times. Serial depth climbs with no token emitted and nothing written: the deliberation lives in activations.
  5. Coda: one token. After r = 7 loops the coda decodes one token. Depth 2 + 4 × 7 + 2 = 32, the same as four tokens of chain of thought, from the same 8 layers of weights, and nothing a monitor can read. Huginn trained with a mean of 32 loops, 2 + 4 × 32 + 2 = 132 layers of depth from 8 real ones.
  1. A token arrives. RLT (Zhang, Feng and Qin, 2026) in its 4+4 split: 4 causal encoder blocks supply token representations and a global key-value memory, and 4 decoder blocks do the stepping. Every token still evaluates all 8 blocks.
  2. Token 1. The encoder and then the decoder run once, and the model emits a token, as an ordinary model would.
  3. Token 2: the state crosses. The previous token's final decoder state is fed into this token's decoder input through a gated merge. In an ordinary decoder nothing computed at the last layer of token 1 reaches the first layer of token 2; here it does, continuously, without passing through the text.
  4. Token 3. The carried state has now passed through 12 decoder blocks: the path grows by 4 every token, at fixed per-token compute.
  5. Token 4: unbounded temporal depth. After t tokens the state path has traversed 4t decoder blocks (48t in the report's tied 48+48 illustration). The tokens are still written, but part of what links them is an activation no transcript shows.

Shapes are scaled down so they fit: 8 layers instead of a frontier model's dozens, 7 loops instead of Huginn's 32 (2 + 4 × 7 + 2 = 32), RLT's 4+4 split from its experiments rather than the report's 48+48 illustration. Layer counts, loop structure and the carried state follow Geiping et al. and the RLT project page; the tokens are placeholders.

What it buys

Compute without the token bill and without the latency of emitting the reasoning; serial depth without parameters.

What it costs

Everything downstream of the transcript: a monitor cannot read deliberation that was never written, an evaluation harness cannot reconstruct the working state, and a serving stack has to persist the recurrent state between requests or recompute it.

What it does not save

A looped block halves the block parameters to hold in memory, but costs about the same compute and the same KV cache as the unlooped depth it replaces: 22 blocks run twice need the cache of 44 distinct blocks (Raschka).

Other members of the family: Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025) pre-trains looped models on 7.7 trillion tokens with a learned depth allocation and reports 1.4B and 2.6B models matching up to 12B ones; Nanbeige4.2-3B runs its 22-layer stack twice, which its report found kept about 75% of a standard model's token efficiency, and Mixture-of-Recursions adds a router deciding per token how many passes it gets (both as summarised by Raschka). The chain of thought was never a faithful window (models can reach an answer for reasons their stated reasoning does not reflect); recurrent depth is the sharper version, no window at all.

RLT: a state carried from token to token, and what its experiments show

The Recurrent Looped Transformer (Yifan Zhang, Jichen Feng and Shihan Qin, September 2026) closes a different loop. In a standard decoder-only model nothing computed at the last layer of token t reaches the first layer of token t + 1; RLT pairs a causal encoder, which supplies token representations and a global key-value memory, with a decoder that feeds the previous token's final decoder state into the current token's input through a gated merge and keeps local sliding-window attention caches, in chunks that continue across prompt, response and message boundaries with no reset. That gives unbounded temporal depth at fixed per-token compute. The report illustrates a tied 48+48 layout, weights shared between encoder and decoder, so 96 logical blocks per token, and after t tokens the state path has traversed 48t decoder blocks. Its experiments use untied eight- and sixteen-layer models on six algorithmic tasks against a decoder-only transformer, three seeds each: the 5+3 and 7+1 splits keep 100% accuracy on 256-bit parity, and on permutation tracking at length 512 the 4+4 split reaches 55.70% ± 25.78 final-state accuracy against the transformer's 0.85% ± 0.30. The gains depend on the task and the split (addition accuracy falls beyond the trained operand widths), and the report "explicitly reports no measured efficiency, reasoning quality, or scaling results" and trains no language model (MarkTechPost), so for frontier models it is vocabulary rather than evidence.

GPT-6 Astra and the monitorability disagreement (September 2026)

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra in September 2026, and reports that it uses this technique ("opaque recurrence", looping activations back through its layers so part of the deliberation never becomes text) came from press coverage rather than from OpenAI's announcement, which does not describe the architecture (OpenAI; TechCrunch). TechCrunch called Astra's use of it "reportedly limited", and OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki wrote that "OpenAI has worked to preserve and utilize chain-of-thought monitoring since our very first reasoning models". The system card finds Astra's chain-of-thought controllability "substantially higher than that of GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.5 Thinking", and that told a monitor is present it can shorten its chain of thought enough to "occasionally evade CoT-only monitors", while saying OpenAI is "quite confident that changes in CoT controllability are not differentially due to any architectural changes" (system card). Astra's product side is on OpenAI: GPT family.

Buck Shlegeris (Redwood Research) warned that if OpenAI pushes it further, "they'll have the option to massively increase the recurrence and totally destroys CoT monitorability" (TechCrunch). Ryan Greenblatt wrote that Astra "appears to be a massive jump in opaque reasoning ability", able to solve hard competition problems "entirely in its head", guessed the jump is downstream of architectural changes with more serial depth (with a normal pretraining scale-up a plausible alternative), and said that if later generations move to "full-on opaque reasoning architectures with extreme depth", chain of thought "would no longer be a meaningful oversight tool" (Greenblatt); a later post he co-authored argues the same at length (Finnveden et al.). Sebastian Raschka is the sceptical counterweight: the gains are "likely primarily due to other reasons, namely improved training recipes and training data" (Raschka's survey), reusing layers "does not by itself suppress visible chain of thought", and fewer reasoning tokens from more passes is the same effect as scaling up the model (Raschka on Astra).

10. In-context learning: the prompt as the first test-time adaptation, and the few-shot protocol

Before any model was trained to think, the cheapest way to spend inference compute on a task was to put the task in the prompt. In-context learning (ICL): the model picks up a task from text in its context window, inside the forward passes that read it, with frozen weights. No gradient step happens; what changes is the input, and every demonstration adds prompt tokens that each layer must process (prefill compute and KV cache, section 1), so it is test-time compute spent on reading rather than on writing.

It grows with scale. GPT-3's own measurement: averaged over 41 accuracy tasks, few-shot minus zero-shot widens from 1.8 points at 125M parameters to 14.8 at 175B, so larger models use the examples better (the paper page owns the numbers task by task). Many-shot: with long context windows the same protocol scales to hundreds or thousands of demonstrations; Agarwal et al. (Many-Shot In-Context Learning, 2024) report consistent gains over few-shot across many tasks, that many demonstrations can override pretraining biases, and that inference cost grows linearly with the number of examples.

Learning or recognising? GPT-3 left open whether the model learns the task "from scratch" in context or recognises one learned in pretraining. Min et al. (Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations, 2022) found that "ground truth demonstrations are in fact not required": replacing the labels with random ones barely hurts on classification and multiple-choice tasks across 12 models including GPT-3, because the demonstrations mostly convey the label space, the distribution of the input text and the format. A candidate mechanism is the induction head, an attention circuit that completes [A][B] ... [A] with [B]; Olsson et al. (In-context Learning and Induction Heads, 2022) find that induction heads form at the same point in training as a sharp jump in in-context learning, with causal evidence in small attention-only models and correlational evidence in larger ones (The transformer from zero builds the circuit).

The protocol is fragile, so it is part of every score. Zhao et al. (Calibrate Before Use, 2021) show that the prompt format, the choice of demonstrations and even their order can move accuracy from near chance to near state of the art (on GPT-3 2.7B, the order of the same four examples moves SST-2 from 54.3% to 93.4%), because the model favours certain answers; estimating that bias from a content-free input and correcting for it raised GPT-3 and GPT-2 accuracy by up to 30 points. That is why a benchmark score names its shot count ("5-shot MMLU") and why scores with different K, formats or example draws are not comparable (Topic: benchmarks).

From demonstrations to thinking. Chain-of-thought prompting began as a few-shot protocol: Wei et al. (2022) put worked reasoning in the demonstrations, and "just eight chain of thought exemplars" took a 540B-parameter model to the state of the art on GSM8K. Kojima et al. (2022) then removed the demonstrations: appending "Let's think step by step" to a zero-shot prompt raised text-davinci-002 from 17.7% to 78.7% on MultiArith and from 10.4% to 40.7% on GSM8K. Reasoning models internalise that move: they are trained to write the working themselves (section 8), so they are usually prompted zero-shot, and the test-time compute moves from reading demonstrations to writing a chain (section 1).

Four mistakes the maths above guards against

The comparison-level mistakes (comparing scores without budget and harness, reading the chain of thought as an explanation, expecting thinking to add knowledge, treating a budget as a hard cap) are on the root; these are the ones this page's formulas catch.

Reading a pass@1 gain from RL as new capability. RLVR reliably converts pass@k into pass@1. Measure pass@k at large k against the base model before claiming the model can now solve problems it could not (section 8).

Assuming majority voting always helps. It amplifies the most likely answer. Where a systematic wrong answer is more likely than the right one, more votes lower accuracy (set p below the wrong answer's share in section 3's selector chart).

Scaling best-of-n against a reward model without limit. Past the peak the proxy score keeps rising while true quality falls, and nothing in the proxy signals it (section 3).

Forgetting that sequential tokens are serial. Doubling the thinking budget doubles the time to the first answer token; no amount of hardware parallelism removes it (section 2).

Why aggregate coverage follows a power law

On one problem the failure rate (1 − p)k falls exponentially, a curve that bends down on log-log axes. Averaged over a benchmark it falls as a straight line, a power law, because the few problems with tiny p dominate the average at large k. Schaeffer et al. (2025) showed this explains the power laws of Brown et al.: per-problem exponential scaling "can be made consistent with aggregate polynomial scaling if the distribution of single-attempt success probabilities is heavy tailed".

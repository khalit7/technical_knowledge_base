6. How long to think: budgets, effort, routers, and overthinking

Once a model reasons, the control problem is how much. There are three levers, from the bluntest to the most adaptive: a token budget the caller sets, an effort level within which the model decides, and a router that picks a model or mode per request. Which lab ships which, and the five production mechanisms side by side, is the root's comparison ({{Topic: llm-landscape|n:3c65c17b0d0d812d9e00f6ec89965286}} section 2, "Who decides how much it thinks"); here is how each works.

Budget forcing: stop the thinking at the budget, or suppress the stop and append "Wait"

s1's control, the simplest that works (Muennighoff et al., 2025): end the thinking forcibly when the budget is spent, or append "Wait" when the model tries to stop early, which makes it re-examine its answer; this took s1-32B "from 50% to 57% on AIME24". The traces are illustrative.

Budgets. Anthropic's budget_tokens (introduced with Claude 3.7 Sonnet, "think for no more than N tokens, for any value of N up to its output limit of 128K tokens", Anthropic, February 2025, and the only mode on the Claude 4 and 4.5 models) and Gemini 2.5's original thinking budget are the token-count form. A budget is the tightest bound on cost, but a fixed allowance whatever the difficulty, and truncation can leave a derivation with no conclusion. The better version conditions the model on the budget during training, so it learns to compress and land a conclusion inside the allowance; that is where the research is.

Effort levels and routers move the decision to the model or to a classifier: an effort level is soft guidance within which the model may skip thinking on easy queries, and a router picks a fast or a deep model per request; research systems now train the decision per step and report cutting reasoning tokens by roughly half at little cost in success (details closed below).

Overthinking is real and measurable. On some inputs, longer chains reduce accuracy: the model revisits a correct answer and argues itself out of it, or drifts towards distractors. Gema et al. (2025) built tasks where "extending the reasoning length of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) deteriorates performance", with five failure modes across model families: Claude models increasingly distracted by irrelevant information, o-series models overfitting to problem framings, a shift from reasonable priors to spurious correlations, loss of focus on long deductions, and amplified concerning behaviour. This is the failure mode adaptive-depth work exists to fix, and it means "always think harder" is not a safe default.

Vendor details: budget minimums and targets, effort levels, routers

Effort levels. Anthropic replaced the budget with adaptive thinking and an effort setting: manual budgets are deprecated on the 4.6 models, and Claude 4.7 and later "reject requests that use it, returning a 400 error" (Anthropic docs). Effort is "soft guidance for how willing Claude should be to think and how deeply", and at low effort the model may skip thinking on simple queries (Anthropic: steering thinking). Gemini models "engage in dynamic thinking by default", controlled by thinking_level; the accepted levels (minimal, low, medium, high) and the default vary by model, and the Gemini API now uses it for the 2.5 models too (Gemini docs).

Routers. GPT-5 shipped as "a smart, efficient model", "a deeper reasoning model" and "a real-time router that quickly decides which to use based on conversation type, complexity, tool needs, and your explicit intent" (OpenAI, August 2025). Research systems now train the decision per step: CogRouter reports an 82.3% success rate with "62% fewer tokens" than its baselines (Yang et al., February 2026), and Ares cuts reasoning tokens "by up to 52.7% compared to fixed high-effort reasoning, while introducing minimal degradation in task success rates" (Yang et al., March 2026).

Anthropic's budget must be at least 1,024 tokens and below max_tokens, and it "sets a target" rather than a strict cap; with interleaved thinking it can exceed max_tokens because "the budget spans all thinking blocks within one assistant turn" (Anthropic docs). So max_tokens, not the budget, is the hard ceiling on a single response.

7. What reasoning tokens cost

Thinking is paid for three times. Money: thinking tokens are billed as output tokens, the expensive side of the price sheet; Claude 3.7 Sonnet cost "$3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens", which "includes thinking tokens" (Anthropic, February 2025). Time: the answer cannot start until the thinking ends (section 2). Memory: every thinking token holds cache for the rest of the request (section 1). And the bill is for the thinking the model did, not the thinking you see: "You are billed for the full thinking process, not the thinking content visible in the response" when the API returns a summary; usage.output_tokens_details.thinking_tokens reports how many billed output tokens were reasoning, 312 of 348 in Anthropic's own example (Anthropic: steering thinking).

Past a few thousand thinking tokens, the thinking is almost the whole bill

cost=NinPin+(Nthink+Nanswer)Pout

One request with a 1,000-token prompt and a 500-token visible answer at $3 and $15 per million. At the default 10,000 thinking tokens: $0.003 for input plus 10,500 × $15 per million = $0.1575 for output, $0.1605 in all, 93.5% of it thinking; the same request answered without thinking costs $0.0105. Five parallel chains of 2,000 thinking tokens spend the same thinking but pay the prompt and the answer five times: $0.2025. derived from the list prices above.

This is why the economics of test-time compute are unfavourable by construction: cost and latency grow linearly with thinking tokens while accuracy grows roughly with their logarithm over a useful range and then flattens, so each point costs more than the last; and why launches now lead with "same score, fewer tokens".

8. How a model learns to spend it, and what that training produces

How a model learns to use a scratchpad well has two answers, and current practice uses both. The algorithms (PPO, GRPO and its fixes) are on {{RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}; this section is about what each recipe produces.

Supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces. Collect problems, generate or obtain long chains of thought, keep the ones whose final answers are correct (rejection sampling, the STaR idea), and train on them as ordinary next-token prediction. Cheap, stable, no rollout infrastructure, and how a small model inherits a large one's reasoning style: DeepSeek's distilled R1 variants from 1.5B to 70B were produced exactly this way, from 800,000 R1-curated samples, "only SFT" and no RL stage (DeepSeek-R1; {{the R1 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}). Small curated sets go further than expected: s1 fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on s1K, "1,000 questions paired with reasoning traces" (Muennighoff et al., 2025). Its limits are structural. The student is bounded by the teacher. It imitates the surface form of deliberation, including steps it could not have derived itself, so it learns what reasoning looks like rather than a policy it can execute reliably. And because the training set is filtered for success, it contains almost no genuine errors and so teaches almost nothing about recovering from one.

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Let the model generate its own trajectories and score the final answer with a program: string or numeric match against a reference for mathematics, a test suite for code, a checker for formal proofs. Two properties follow. First, the reward is not a learned model, so the usual reward-hacking channel (exploiting a reward model's misgeneralisation) is closed; what remains is gaming the checker itself, for example code that special-cases the visible tests. Second, and more important, the trajectories are the model's own, so credit assignment is on-policy: what gets reinforced is behaviour this model can actually produce. That is why long traces, self-checking and backtracking emerge under RLVR rather than having to be demonstrated.

R1-Zero was the clean demonstration: pure RL on a strong base model (DeepSeek-V3-Base), no supervised reasoning data at all, produced spontaneous re-derivation and reflection, and its responses grew over training "from hundreds to thousands" of reasoning tokens purely because longer thinking earned more reward, while AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (86.7% with self-consistency) in the expanded version (Nature, 2025; the January 2025 arXiv v1 reported 71.0%, and 86.7% with majority voting over 64 samples). The model learned to spend test-time compute because spending it was rewarded. The production recipe combines the two in a specific order, and R1's is the openly documented version:

R1's four stages: format first, then capability, then distil, then polish

1. Cold start a small SFT set of curated long chains of thought fixes format and readability (R1-Zero's traces were correct but mixed languages and were painful to read).
2. Reasoning RL large-scale RLVR for capability.
3. Rejection sampling the RL model's own best trajectories become a new SFT set, used both to re-tune the flagship and to distil smaller models.
4. Second RL a final RL stage over all scenarios.

RLVR's binding constraint is that it needs a verifier, so it covers mathematics, code and formal domains cleanly and writing, analysis and judgement poorly. Extending it means reintroducing a learned reward model or model-graded rubrics, which reopens exactly the reward-hacking channel that made RLVR attractive; length penalties and process reward models (section 4) only partially fix the verbosity and hacking that follow. The optimiser behind most reasoning RL is GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization, from {{DeepSeekMath|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}}): per prompt it samples a group of G completions, scores each with the verifier, and gives every token of a completion the same advantage, its reward minus the group mean over the group standard deviation, with no critic network. For test-time compute this means an RL run's bottleneck becomes generation throughput, since every prompt needs G long rollouts, which is why RL stacks run vLLM or SGLang inside the training loop and treat rollout and training weight synchronisation as a first-order engineering concern.

GRPO's zero-signal groups: why easy and impossible prompts waste the batch

A group where all G completions are correct, or all wrong, has every advantage zero and contributes nothing but its sampling cost; a prompt the model solves with probability p gives such a group with probability pG + (1 − p)G, so on easy or impossible problems that is most of the batch. Every token of a failed trajectory is penalised equally, including correct early steps, because no critic remains to tell them apart. The worked example (rewards 1, 0, 0, 0 give +1.732 and −0.577), the PPO comparison, the KL term and the fixes most 2026 recipes use in place of textbook GRPO (Dr. GRPO, DAPO's higher upper clip, dynamic sampling of degenerate groups) are on RL for LLMs.

RLVR sharpens; it does not extend

RLVR-trained models beat their base models at small k such as pass@1, but the base models reach a higher pass@k when k is large: the RL model concentrates probability on solutions the base model could already sample rather than acquiring new ones. In pass@k terms, RL raises p on problems where it was already above zero and can drive it to zero where it was small, and 1 − (1 − p)k rewards breadth at large k. Pretraining quality still bounds the ceiling, which is the most important thing to hold before assuming an RL stage will fix a capability gap.

Over 450 GRPO steps, pass@1 climbs and pass@256 does not

Yue et al. (2025) trained Qwen2.5-7B with GRPO and measured both ends of the pass@k curve at each checkpoint (their Tables 3 and 4), plotted as published. On Omni-MATH-Train pass@1 rises from 9.9% to 42.5% while pass@256 falls from the base model's 67.2% to 64.3%. published Whether newer, longer RL runs escape the pattern is debated on RL for LLMs, and agentic post-training shows the same trade on {{Sharpening Tax in Post-Training|n:3fe5c17b0d0d81efa2a9f532c14c87a3}}.

The mechanism on ten illustrative problems: sharpening alone costs no coverage, dropping problems does

Ten illustrative problems with base-model p from 0.6 down to 0.005. After RL, p becomes 1 − (1 − p)s on the problems it kept and 0 on the ones it dropped. pass@k is the mean of 1 − (1 − p)k over the ten. With nothing dropped there is no crossover: sharpening alone costs no coverage, which is why the crossover Yue et al. measured is evidence that RL narrows what the model samples.

9. Recurrent depth: spending the budget inside the network

The latent way spends the budget in activations rather than in tokens. A looped transformer reuses the same layers several times per token, so serial depth grows without adding parameters and without writing anything down. The idea goes back to the Universal Transformer (Dehghani et al., 2018), which applied one block repeatedly with a learned halting rule. The clearest modern form is Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025): a prelude that embeds the token, a recurrent core iterated r times with the input re-injected each time, and a coda that decodes. Its 3.5-billion-parameter proof of concept, trained on 800 billion tokens with layers (2, 4, 2) and a mean of 32 iterations, improved on reasoning benchmarks "up to a computation load equivalent to 50 billion parameters".

What it buys

Compute without the token bill and without the latency of emitting the reasoning; serial depth without parameters.

What it costs

Everything downstream of the transcript: a monitor cannot read deliberation that was never written, an evaluation harness cannot reconstruct the working state, and a serving stack has to persist the recurrent state between requests or recompute it.

What it does not save

A looped block halves the block parameters to hold in memory, but costs about the same compute and the same KV cache as the unlooped depth it replaces: 22 blocks run twice need the cache of 44 distinct blocks (Raschka).

Other members of the family: Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025) pre-trains looped models on 7.7 trillion tokens with a learned depth allocation and reports 1.4B and 2.6B models matching up to 12B ones; Nanbeige4.2-3B runs its 22-layer stack twice, which its report found kept about 75% of a standard model's token efficiency, and Mixture-of-Recursions adds a router deciding per token how many passes it gets (both as summarised by Raschka). The chain of thought was never a faithful window (models can reach an answer for reasons their stated reasoning does not reflect); recurrent depth is the sharper version, no window at all.

RLT: a state carried from token to token, and what its experiments show

The Recurrent Looped Transformer (Yifan Zhang, Jichen Feng and Shihan Qin, September 2026) closes a different loop. In a standard decoder-only model nothing computed at the last layer of token t reaches the first layer of token t + 1; RLT pairs a causal encoder, which supplies token representations and a global key-value memory, with a decoder that feeds the previous token's final decoder state into the current token's input through a gated merge and keeps local sliding-window attention caches, in chunks that continue across prompt, response and message boundaries with no reset. That gives unbounded temporal depth at fixed per-token compute. The report illustrates a tied 48+48 layout, weights shared between encoder and decoder, so 96 logical blocks per token, and after t tokens the state path has traversed 48t decoder blocks. Its experiments use untied eight- and sixteen-layer models on six algorithmic tasks against a decoder-only transformer, three seeds each: the 5+3 and 7+1 splits keep 100% accuracy on 256-bit parity, and on permutation tracking at length 512 the 4+4 split reaches 55.70% ± 25.78 final-state accuracy against the transformer's 0.85% ± 0.30. The gains depend on the task and the split (addition accuracy falls beyond the trained operand widths), and the report "explicitly reports no measured efficiency, reasoning quality, or scaling results" and trains no language model (MarkTechPost), so for frontier models it is vocabulary rather than evidence.

GPT-6 Astra and the monitorability disagreement (September 2026)

OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra in September 2026, and reports that it uses this technique ("opaque recurrence", looping activations back through its layers so part of the deliberation never becomes text) came from press coverage rather than from OpenAI's announcement, which does not describe the architecture (OpenAI; TechCrunch). TechCrunch called Astra's use of it "reportedly limited", and OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki wrote that "OpenAI has worked to preserve and utilize chain-of-thought monitoring since our very first reasoning models". The system card finds Astra's chain-of-thought controllability "substantially higher than that of GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.5 Thinking", and that told a monitor is present it can shorten its chain of thought enough to "occasionally evade CoT-only monitors", while saying OpenAI is "quite confident that changes in CoT controllability are not differentially due to any architectural changes" (system card). Astra's product side is on {{OpenAI: GPT family|n:3c65c17b0d0d814abf90d5b9e567d72f}}.

Buck Shlegeris (Redwood Research) warned that if OpenAI pushes it further, "they'll have the option to massively increase the recurrence and totally destroys CoT monitorability" (TechCrunch). Ryan Greenblatt wrote that Astra "appears to be a massive jump in opaque reasoning ability", able to solve hard competition problems "entirely in its head", guessed the jump is downstream of architectural changes with more serial depth (with a normal pretraining scale-up a plausible alternative), and said that if later generations move to "full-on opaque reasoning architectures with extreme depth", chain of thought "would no longer be a meaningful oversight tool" (Greenblatt); a later post he co-authored argues the same at length (Finnveden et al.). Sebastian Raschka is the sceptical counterweight: the gains are "likely primarily due to other reasons, namely improved training recipes and training data" (Raschka's survey), reusing layers "does not by itself suppress visible chain of thought", and fewer reasoning tokens from more passes is the same effect as scaling up the model (Raschka on Astra).

10. In-context learning: the prompt as the first test-time adaptation, and the few-shot protocol

Before any model was trained to think, the cheapest way to spend inference compute on a task was to put the task in the prompt. In-context learning (ICL): the model picks up a task from text in its context window, inside the forward passes that read it, with frozen weights. No gradient step happens; what changes is the input, and every demonstration adds prompt tokens that each layer must process (prefill compute and KV cache, section 1), so it is test-time compute spent on reading rather than on writing.

The protocol GPT-3 named (its Figure 2.1 example; {{Language Models are Few-Shot Learners (GPT-3)|n:3c65c17b0d0d8193ac92c7648cfaca12}} animates the same example against fine-tuning, token by token). A demonstration is one solved example placed in the prompt; the highlighted line is the query the model completes.

It grows with scale. GPT-3's own measurement: averaged over 41 accuracy tasks, few-shot minus zero-shot widens from 1.8 points at 125M parameters to 14.8 at 175B, so larger models use the examples better (the paper page owns the numbers task by task). Many-shot: with long context windows the same protocol scales to hundreds or thousands of demonstrations; Agarwal et al. ({{Many-Shot In-Context Learning, 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.11018}}) report consistent gains over few-shot across many tasks, that many demonstrations can override pretraining biases, and that inference cost grows linearly with the number of examples.

Learning or recognising? GPT-3 left open whether the model learns the task "from scratch" in context or recognises one learned in pretraining. Min et al. ({{Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations, 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837}}) found that "ground truth demonstrations are in fact not required": replacing the labels with random ones barely hurts on classification and multiple-choice tasks across 12 models including GPT-3, because the demonstrations mostly convey the label space, the distribution of the input text and the format. A candidate mechanism is the induction head, an attention circuit that completes [A][B] ... [A] with [B]; Olsson et al. ({{In-context Learning and Induction Heads, 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11895}}) find that induction heads form at the same point in training as a sharp jump in in-context learning, with causal evidence in small attention-only models and correlational evidence in larger ones ({{The transformer from zero|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8174980ddde4a20c7b3c}} builds the circuit).

The protocol is fragile, so it is part of every score. Zhao et al. ({{Calibrate Before Use, 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09690}}) show that the prompt format, the choice of demonstrations and even their order can move accuracy from near chance to near state of the art (on GPT-3 2.7B, the order of the same four examples moves SST-2 from 54.3% to 93.4%), because the model favours certain answers; estimating that bias from a content-free input and correcting for it raised GPT-3 and GPT-2 accuracy by up to 30 points. That is why a benchmark score names its shot count ("5-shot MMLU") and why scores with different K, formats or example draws are not comparable ({{Topic: benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}}).

From demonstrations to thinking. Chain-of-thought prompting began as a few-shot protocol: Wei et al. ({{2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903}}) put worked reasoning in the demonstrations, and "just eight chain of thought exemplars" took a 540B-parameter model to the state of the art on GSM8K. Kojima et al. ({{2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916}}) then removed the demonstrations: appending "Let's think step by step" to a zero-shot prompt raised text-davinci-002 from 17.7% to 78.7% on MultiArith and from 10.4% to 40.7% on GSM8K. Reasoning models internalise that move: they are trained to write the working themselves (section 8), so they are usually prompted zero-shot, and the test-time compute moves from reading demonstrations to writing a chain (section 1).

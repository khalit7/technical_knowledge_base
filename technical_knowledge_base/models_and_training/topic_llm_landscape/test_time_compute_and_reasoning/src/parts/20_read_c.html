3. What parallel sampling is worth: the selector decides

Parallel sampling is the one way of spending test-time compute whose returns can be written down exactly, and the formulas explain most of the folklore about it. Three selectors, three formulas, one chart.

A perfect verifier reaches pass@k

If one sample solves a problem with probability p, and samples are independent:

pass@k=1−(1−p)k
p
the single-sample success probability on this problem
k
the number of samples drawn
(1 − p)k
the probability that all k fail

With p = 0.2: one sample succeeds 20% of the time; five give 1 − 0.85 = 1 − 0.32768 = 0.672; ten give 1 − 0.810 = 1 − 0.107 = 0.893. A verifier that recognises a correct answer turns this directly into accuracy, which is why best-of-n against unit tests or a proof checker is the strongest form of parallel scaling. It also shows the ceiling: a problem with p = 0 stays unsolved at any k.

Majority voting amplifies whatever is most likely

Self-consistency needs no verifier, only answers that can be compared. In the simplest case, one correct answer against one competing wrong answer, with n odd:

Pmaj=∑j>n/2(nj)pj(1−p)n−j
Pmaj
the probability that the majority answer is correct
j
runs over the numbers of correct samples that form a majority
term
the binomial probability of exactly j correct samples out of n

With p = 0.6 and n = 5: 10 × 0.63 × 0.42 + 5 × 0.64 × 0.4 + 0.65 = 0.3456 + 0.2592 + 0.0778 = 0.683, up from 0.6. With p = 0.4 the same vote gives 0.317, down from 0.4, and more samples make it worse: voting amplifies whichever answer is most likely, right or wrong. Real wrong answers usually scatter, which is why voting works as well as it does: with p = 0.4 and the remaining 0.6 spread evenly over four different wrong answers (0.15 each), the correct answer is the most likely single answer, and a plurality vote is right 54.9% of the time over 5 samples and 78.0% over 15.

A verifier climbs towards 1; a vote climbs only if the right answer is the most likely one

Predict: set p = 0.4 with one wrong answer. Does a vote over 63 samples beat one sample?

How the wrong answers spread (each gets an equal share of 1 − p)

The plurality curve is computed exactly by enumerating how the wrong samples split among the wrong answers, ties broken at random (checked by brute force in src/recompute.py). At p = 0.4 with four wrong answers it gives 54.9% at n = 5 and 78.0% at n = 15; with one wrong answer at p = 0.6, 68.3% at n = 5 and at p = 0.4, 31.7%.

The original self-consistency paper sampled 40 reasoning paths and reported gains over single-sample chain-of-thought prompting of 17.9 points on GSM8K (60.1% to 78.0% with code-davinci-002), 11.0 on SVAMP, 12.2 on AQuA, 6.4 on StrategyQA and 3.9 on ARC-challenge (Wang et al., 2022, Tables 2 and 3). The gap between voting and a verifier is the part people forget: on MATH with Llama-3-8B-Instruct, the chance that some sample is right rose from 82.9% at 100 samples to 98.44% at 10,000, while majority voting or a reward model rose only from 40.50% to 41.41% over the same range (Brown et al., 2024).

What the selector keeps, measured: the same samples, scored four ways

A perfect verifier (the coverage ceiling) is only available where answers can be checked; voting and learned scorers recover part of the gap, and how much depends on the task. Best-of-n against a learned reward model is a search for its errors

With a learned reward model as the selector, a larger n is a stronger optimisation of the proxy, and the true quality first rises and then falls. Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) measured this against a much larger "gold" reward model standing in for the true objective, and fitted:

Rbon(d)=d(αbon−βbond),d=KLbon,KLbon=lnn−n−1n
Rbon
the gold reward gained over the unoptimised policy (zero at n = 1 by definition)
KLbon
the Kullback-Leibler divergence of the best-of-n distribution from the original policy, which has this closed form (computed analytically, not measured)
d
its square root, the paper's measure of how far optimisation has moved the policy
αbon, βbon
fitted coefficients that change smoothly with the proxy reward model's size; the paper shows them only as plots, so the values used here are illustrative
n
the number of samples

The gold reward peaks and falls while the proxy keeps climbing

Defaults reproduce the worked numbers by construction: with n = 16, KL = ln 16 − 15/16 = 2.773 − 0.938 = 1.835 and d = 1.355; with illustrative coefficients α = 1 and β = 0.25 (not the paper's fitted values), R = 1.355 × (1 − 0.25 × 1.355) = 0.896; the peak is at d* = α/(2β) = 2, reached near n = 147, and by n = 4,096 the gold reward has fallen back to 0.876. A larger reward model behaves like a smaller β: the peak moves out, which is why a larger reward model beats a larger n.

The KL grows only logarithmically in n, so the peak arrives late but it arrives: past it the proxy score keeps rising while true quality falls, and nothing in the proxy signals it. The general over-optimisation picture is on {{Reward Hacking|n:3c65c17b0d0d8196a6f1f29a817aa974}}.

The unbiased pass@k estimator, and Gao et al.'s RL form

In evaluation, pass@k is estimated from n ≥ k samples of which c are correct, with the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021), who drew n = 200 samples per problem for k ≤ 100 and showed that the naive 1 − (1 − c/n)k is biased:

pass@k^=1−(n−ck)(nk)
n
samples drawn per problem
c
how many of them are correct
ratio
the probability that a random subset of k of the n samples contains no correct one

Defaults: C(8, 5) = 56 and C(10, 5) = 252, so the estimate is 1 − 56/252 = 0.778.

How far the naive estimate falls below the true value, and how the gap closes as n grows, is drawn with both estimators side by side on {{Coding benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81bfa09ad4ba5eef0c4e#rd-fn}}, which owns pass@k as benchmarks report it.

For RL against the same proxy Gao et al. fit a different form, RRL(d) = d(αRL − βRL ln d), which declines more slowly than best-of-n's.

4. Verifiers and process reward models

Where no program can check an answer, the selector is learned, and where it looks matters. An outcome reward model (ORM) reads a finished solution and predicts whether it is right. A process reward model (PRM) predicts, for each intermediate step, whether the solution so far is on track. That buys two things: an error is caught at the step where it happens rather than diluted over a long answer, and search can prune a bad partial solution before paying to finish it.

Lightman et al. (2023), "Let's Verify Step by Step", compared the two on MATH: "process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision", and their process-supervised model "solves 78% of problems from a representative subset of the MATH test set"; they released PRM800K, "800,000 step-level human feedback labels". Human step labels are expensive, so later work labels steps automatically: Snell et al. (2024) found a PRM trained on PRM800K "easy to exploit" with their PaLM 2 models (a distribution shift from its GPT-4 samples) and instead estimated each step's correctness from Monte Carlo rollouts continued from it, so the PRM's per-step prediction is a value estimate of reward-to-go; their PRM "consistently outperforms the ORM".

Beam search against a PRM spends the budget on prefixes that still look right

Snell et al.'s procedure (Section 5.2) with N = 4 beams and beam width M = 2: sample N first steps, score them with the PRM, keep the top N/M, sample M continuations of each, repeat to the end (at most 40 rounds in the paper), then pick the answer by best-of-N weighted, summing the PRM's scores over solutions with the same final answer. A solution's score is the PRM's prediction at its last step, which they found worked better than the product or minimum of the step scores. The scores and answers here are illustrative.

Search against a verifier is still optimisation against a learned model, with section 3's failure. Snell et al. found beam search best at low generation budgets and on harder questions, while "on the easier problems (bins 1 and 2), beam search shows signs of over-optimization with higher budgets, whereas best-of-N does not". Where answers can be checked by a program, prefer the program: it cannot be over-optimised on the property it checks (only gamed around it, section 8).

5. Compute-optimal test-time scaling: the best spend depends on the question

Which way to spend a fixed budget depends on how hard the question is for this model. Snell et al. (2024) binned questions by the base model's own success rate and compared, per bin and per budget, sequential revisions (the model rewrites its previous answer) against parallel samples, and beam search against a PRM against best-of-N.

Easier for this modelPurely sequential revisions win: "easier questions benefit from purely sequential test-time compute". Against a PRM, best-of-N beats beam search, which over-optimises.
Harder, but some progressA mix of sequential and parallel works best, "some ideal ratio of sequential to parallel compute"; beam search against the PRM beats best-of-N, especially at low budgets.
Out of reachTest-time compute stops substituting for scale: on the hardest questions, a larger pretrained model wins in a FLOPs-matched comparison.

Read from the paper's takeaways (Sections 5 and 6) and its FLOPs-matched comparison (Section 7), PaLM 2-S* on MATH. published

Choosing the setting per question, "compute-optimal" allocation, improved "the efficiency of test-time compute scaling by more than 4× compared to a best-of-N baseline", and "on problems where a smaller base model attains somewhat non-trivial success rates, test-time compute can be used to outperform a 14× larger model" in a FLOPs-matched evaluation. The qualifier is the point: on problems the small model cannot make progress on, pretraining scale still wins. In practice the difficulty is not known in advance, so it is estimated (by the verifier, by a router, or by the model itself deciding how long to think), which is where section 6 begins.

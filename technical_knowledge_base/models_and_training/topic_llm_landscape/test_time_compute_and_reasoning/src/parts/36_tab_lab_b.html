A whole benchmark, not one problem

The formulas in the {{Reading|#t-read}} tab's section 3 are for one problem with one success probability p. A benchmark is many problems with different p, and that changes the shape of every curve. Here p varies across problems as a Beta(α, β) distribution, an illustrative choice you can move; wrong answers on each problem split evenly over m values. Everything is computed exactly except the optional simulated benchmark.

Why aggregate coverage follows a power law

On one problem the failure rate (1 − p)k falls exponentially, a curve that bends down on log-log axes. Averaged over a benchmark it falls as a straight line, a power law, because the few problems with tiny p dominate the average at large k. Schaeffer et al. (2025) showed this explains the power laws of Brown et al.: per-problem exponential scaling "can be made consistent with aggregate polynomial scaling if the distribution of single-attempt success probabilities is heavy tailed". For a Beta distribution it is exact:

Check by simulation

Draw 200 problems from the distribution, sample 64 answers for each, and score them the way an evaluation would: pass@k with the unbiased estimator from all 64, the vote from the first k. The dots should sit on the exact curves, within sampling noise.

The "fitted to Brown et al." preset reproduces their stated MATH coverage by construction (α and β were solved from those two numbers, Llama-3-8B-Instruct: 82.9% at 100 samples, 98.44% at 10,000; Brown et al.): α = 0.5239 and β = 3.786 give 82.9% and 98.44%. It does not reproduce what they measured for voting: the paper reports 40.50% at 100 samples and 41.41% at 10,000 for majority voting or a reward model, a plateau the Beta model can match only through m, which the paper does not report. The preset's implied one-sample accuracy, α/(α + β) = 12.2%, is derived, not measured. The other presets are illustrative. On the default heavy-tail preset (α = 0.3, β = 1.5) the exact failure curve's slope between 1,000 and 10,000 samples is −0.300, equal to −α as the power law predicts.

Published coverage curves, recomputed

The fit is the authors'; the numbers they state in the text are an independent check on it. Compare shapes within one model and task, never numbers across them.

Brown et al. (2024) sampled up to 10,000 times per problem and found that coverage (the fraction of problems some sample solves, pass@k with a perfect verifier) follows an exponentiated power law in the number of samples k:

The fits reproduce Brown et al.'s stated coverage within 2 points, independently (the fits come from the curves, the stated numbers are separate measurements): 17.6% and 57.9% against 15.9% and 56% on SWE-bench Lite; 83.2% and 97.5% against 82.9% and 98.44% on MATH. Gemma-2B on CodeContests does not reproduce at the far end: the fit gives 9.5% at 10,000 samples against the stated 7.1%.

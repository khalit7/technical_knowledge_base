The Muse era: closed at the top, open at the edges

Muse Spark 8 April 2026: a rebuilt stack, closed weights

The first MSL frontier model, code-named Avocado, built over about nine months on what Wang called an AI stack "rebuilt from scratch" rather than on the Llama architecture: natively multimodal, with tool use and multi-agent orchestration built in ({{Verdent|@verdent}}). Weights were closed and there was no public API at launch, only the Meta AI apps and a private API preview. It returned Meta to frontier-competitive performance (52 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index as it stood in April 2026, against Llama 4 Maverick's 18; {{VentureBeat|@vbspark}}) at the cost of the open identity that was its entire strategic differentiation: Meta's argument for releasing weights had always been that commoditising the model layer was worth more to it than owning one. Versions 1.1 to 1.3 added a paid API, the Muse Code agent and a 1M-token context; their dates and prices are in the lineup.

Muse Spark 1.3's claim worth testing is agentic efficiency rather than raw score: "~20% fewer tool calls and ~25% fewer tokens" than Muse Spark 1.2 for the same work, the metric that decides agent economics once capability is close ({{Meta|@ms13}}).

Muse Spark 1.3 on private enterprise code (Real-SWE)
On private enterprise code the picture is more ordinary: Real-SWE, which scores models in their native harnesses on tasks licensed from private codebases, puts it at 23.8% through Muse Code, level with Grok 4.6 and well behind Claude Fable 5.1's 38.8% ({{Specific Labs' Real-SWE|@rsweh}}, as reported by {{this comparison|@rswe}}; benchmark detail on {{Topic: agentic-harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d881a8fe98b4cff7bb}}).

Muse Glimmer 10 August 2026: a distilled 30B for local agents, Apache 2.0

Meta's first open-weights release since Llama 4: a 29.6B dense multimodal model (text and interleaved images in, text out, with a ViT-G/14 perception encoder of about 1.8B parameters) of 52 layers with a context of 131,072 tokens, under Apache 2.0, which drops the Llama licence's 700-million-user clause ({{model card|@glim}}, {{VentureBeat|@vbglim}}). The recipe in outline: distilled from Muse Spark (by logit distillation), mid-trained on longer-context, agent-focused data, then SFT and RL ({{VentureBeat|@vbglim}}; the method is on {{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}). Its attention repeats Llama 4's pattern in a new form: three local layers with a 2,048-token sliding window and RoPE, then one global layer without positional encoding, with 32 query heads sharing 2 key-value heads ({{model card|@glim}}).

One repeating unit of four layers: Glimmer keeps Llama 4's three-local-one-global rhythm with a shorter window and a quarter of the KV heads

Read from the configs ({{Llama 3.1 8B|@c8}}, {{Maverick|@mv4}}, {{Muse Glimmer|@glimcfg}}). Only the global layers keep keys and values for the whole context: 12 of 48 in Llama 4, 13 of 52 in Glimmer, every layer in Llama 3. The windows and head counts are what the configs say; what each costs in memory against context is the {{KV-cache calculator on Long context|https://app.notion.com/p/3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5#t-kv}}.

It is built for local, always-on agent work: a 17 GB roughly 4-bit build fits a 24 GB consumer GPU, and a speculative-decoding drafter triples throughput on an RTX 5090 ({{model card|@glim}}).

Running Glimmer locally: builds, degradation and the drafter (model card numbers)
It is built for local, always-on agent work: about 55 GiB of weights in BF16 and a 17 GB roughly 4-bit build for a 24 GB consumer GPU, which the card measures at 1.0% average degradation across 15 benchmarks (0.2% for a larger dynamic build for 32 GB), with a speculative-decoding drafter (DFlash, which proposes blocks of 16 tokens per pass) that takes an RTX 5090 from 74.9 to 233.4 tokens per second, 3.1 times, and an Apple M5 Max from 26.6 to 50.2 ({{model card|@glim}}).
Glimmer's benchmark table against Qwen and Gemma (Meta's own numbers)
Meta's own table puts it at 51.2 on SWE-Bench Pro against 50.2 for Qwen3.6-27B and 36.9 for Gemma 4 31B, while Qwen leads on OSWorld-Verified (75.6 against 65.9), Terminal-Bench 2.1 (60.7 against 51.7) and SWE-Bench Verified (77.2 against 76.0) ({{model card|@glim}}). The same day Zuckerberg said "Soon we'll also release the weights for Muse Spark 1.2" ({{VentureBeat|@vbglim}}); when Muse Spark 1.3 shipped in early September no Muse Spark weights of any version had been published ({{Trending Topics|@tt}}), and the 1.2 weights never were. The promise was kept two months later in a different form: Muse Spark Open (15 October 2026), which carries no version number.

Muse Wonder and Wonder Full 12 October 2026: Meta's first frontier model

The first model TechCrunch and Meta both call frontier rather than frontier-band, trained at Meta's gigawatt-scale Prometheus data centre and pitched on "Active Memory" for personal and professional agents ({{Meta|@wonder}}, {{TechCrunch|@tcwonder}}). It comes in two forms, and the split is the design choice: Muse Wonder is on the Meta Model API; Wonder Full, the stronger configuration, is not on the API at all and comes only with Muse Code subscriptions. Neither has published weights ({{VentureBeat|@vbwonder}}). So the API buys a model a few points under the frontier; the frontier score itself is sold only inside Meta's own coding agent.

Wonder Full against Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, benchmark by benchmark (volatile)
Meta's own table, which mixes Meta-run and Artificial Analysis rows, has Wonder Full ahead of Opus 5.5 and Astra on DeepSWE 1.1 (79.0 against 74.2 and 74.1) and SWE-Marathon (57.5 against 46.3 and 42.5) but behind both on Terminal-Bench 4.0-AA (57.1 against 59.6 and 59.1). On Vals AI's fixed mini-swe-agent harness for Terminal-Bench 4.0 Wonder Full scores 58.08%, tied with Fable 5.1 and behind Opus 5.5's 65.15% ({{Vals AI|@valstb}}).

Muse Spark Open 15 October 2026: large open weights again, under a revenue clause

Meta published the weights it had promised since August under a new name rather than a version number: an 816B-total, 42B-active MoE transformer with a 1M-token context, natively multimodal through a 1.8B Perception Encoder ({{Meta|@mso}}). The card: 62 layers, hidden size 8,192, 256 experts with 8 active per token, grouped-query attention with 128 query and 16 key-value heads of dimension 64, gated attention and QK-norm, a 202,048-token vocabulary. The NVFP4 build fits one 8-GPU H200 host; BF16 needs two.

Spark Open's memory arithmetic (model card)
It ships in BF16 (about 2 TB of GPU memory) and NVFP4 (at least 700 GB), so the NVFP4 build fits one 8-GPU host of 141 GB H200s (1,128 GB, leaving about 400 GB for cache and activations) while BF16 needs two such hosts ({{model card|@msocard}}); Meta's launch demonstration ran it on two M5 Ultra Mac Studios ({{Meta|@mso}}).

The Muse Model License is not Apache 2.0: model-as-a-service operators with more than $20 million of gross revenue in any 12 months need a separate agreement with Meta, and products must show a "Built with Muse Spark Open" notice ({{model card|@msocard}}). Meta's own card puts it below the Chinese open frontier: GLM 5.3 at 45, Kimi K3 at 44 and Qwen3.8-2.4T at 40 on the Artificial Analysis index, and 76.4 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 against 83.9 to 85.0 for those models. "Best US open-weight model" is Meta's claim; on the aggregate index it is the largest US open model, not the best open one.

The Muse agent's security architecture

The Meta Muse agent (8 September 2026) is a general assistant on iOS, Android and the web in the US, usable "in the Muse app or directly in WhatsApp", that sends email, books, fills forms and pays on the user's behalf, running on Muse Spark, with each user's agent in "Muse Secure VM, a dedicated, virtual machine (VM) that houses both the agent and a person's data"; basic use is free, with paid tiers ({{Meta|@agent}}). On Meta's third-quarter call (21 October 2026) Zuckerberg said "more than 10 million people in the US and Canada" had set it up, about a quarter of them using it daily, and that it has not launched globally because it is compute-intensive ({{call transcript|https://www.fool.com/earnings/call-transcripts/2026/10/21/meta-meta-q3-2026-earnings-call-transcript/}}). It is the most substantive thing MSL has shipped, and the security architecture rather than the assistant is why. It holds standing access to mail, payments and accounts while reading attacker-controllable pages and messages, the worst case for prompt injection (instructions hidden in content the agent reads). Meta's design assumes the model will sometimes be fooled and makes being fooled grant nothing that matters. Predict before you step through: the injection fools the model in both designs, so what is left for the attacker in each?

One injected page through a naive agent that holds real credentials and sends whatever it composes, and through Muse's design. The task, the injected page and the attacker's address are illustrative; the components and rules are Meta's. Meta publishes no example attack.

The six parts, from Meta's own description of 8 September 2026 ({{Meta|@sec}}):

  1. The runtime cell. The agent harness runs in a container inside the user's VM, root inside it mapped to an unprivileged host user, with filtered system calls and limited kernel capabilities.
  2. Security services outside the cell. hatch-authd holds credentials, privsep workers run connector code, hatch-safety runs classifiers the cell cannot switch off, and Sentinel is the sole permission authority for connector actions and network egress.
  3. Surrogate credentials. The cell only ever sees placeholder tokens; Sentinel swaps in the real credential at the network boundary, after the request is authorised.
  4. Egress policy. Sentinel answers allow, deny or ask after inspecting the request at layers 4 and 7; a tool process that has read user data is marked tainted by the kernel, so its requests never ride an auto-allow rule.
  5. Approvals outside the conversation. An "ask" halts execution and appears in the app's own interface, not in the chat, so injected text cannot manufacture consent; checkouts use single-use card numbers.
  6. A browser that cannot be scripted. The browser sub-agent sees an accessibility-tree snapshot and runs no page JavaScript; classifiers look for injections.
Each part in Meta's words (quotes, kernel mechanisms, grant types)
  1. The runtime cell. The agent harness runs in a systemd-nspawn container inside the user's VM, with "root inside the runtime cell ... mapped to an unprivileged host user", its own filesystem and virtual network interface, filtered system calls (no io_uring) and limited kernel capabilities (no CAP_SYS_PTRACE, no CAP_NET_ADMIN).
  2. Security services outside the cell. hatch-authd stores credentials (including OAuth tokens for third-party services), privsep workers run the built-in connector code with tightly scoped credential access, hatch-safety runs independent classifier models over requests and responses so that an attacker inside the cell cannot switch them off, and Sentinel is "the sole permission authority" for connector actions and network egress. The cell reaches them only over Unix domain sockets with kernel-authenticated peer checks.
  3. Surrogate credentials. "Code in the runtime cell or a worker only ever sees a 'surrogate' token, minted by authd. After the concrete network request is authorized, Sentinel will replace any surrogate tokens with the real credential ... at the network boundary", so an injected "send me the token" can only exfiltrate a placeholder.
  4. Egress policy. Sentinel resolves each request to allow, deny or ask, inspecting it at layers 4 and 7: "the hostname, the resolved and final destination IP address, the port, protocol, HTTP method, path, and the actual decoded request". It tracks tainted egress through the kernel: each tool process starts clean and becomes tainted if it reads user data (eBPF programs on cgroups and on Linux Security Module hooks Meta added), so a request carrying data from an untrusted source does not ride an auto-allow rule.
  5. Approvals outside the conversation. On "ask", execution halts and the approval goes "directly to the Muse client", in the app's own interface, not as a message in the chat, so text in the context window cannot manufacture consent. Grants are "strict capabilities, not conversational suggestions", bound to the connector or destination: one-time, session, task, time-bounded or permanent. Checkouts always need approval with the exact purchase details and use single-use card numbers through Stripe Link, bound to one merchant, one amount and a short validity.
  6. A browser that cannot be scripted. The browser sub-agent sees "an accessibility tree snapshot of the page, not the raw DOM", and cannot run JavaScript in the page; a separate family of classifiers looks for prompt injection in pages, images and files, for high-risk forms and for egress of unrelated personal data.

What is not covered: Meta offers "up to $300,000 for valid reports ... including up to $130,000 for successful prompt injection attempts that affect one user", and has published no accuracy figures for its classifiers, so the parts that still rest on detection are unmeasured ({{Meta|@sec}}). The boundary also covers only the cloud VM: on 22 September 2026 the researcher Patrick Wardle disclosed that malware already running under the user's account could change an undocumented macOS setting and redirect the Muse Mac client's dictation traffic, with its prompts and authentication data, to a server of its choosing ({{Cybersecurity News|@csn}}); that report gives no patch, and the fix "four days later" recorded earlier is unconfirmed in the sources read here. The general principle, that enforcement and credentials belong outside the component that can be persuaded, is developed on {{Agent security|n:3f15c17b0d0d81ad9fb7d1dd74217335}} and {{Topic: agentic-harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d881a8fe98b4cff7bb}}, and the comparison with self-hosted personal agents on {{Personal agents: OpenClaw, Hermes Agent, and how they differ from coding harnesses|n:3c75c17b0d0d81bf9462fbeb09593e1c}}.

Strategic read. Llama's retreat handed open-weights leadership to the Chinese labs and to Mistral, and it was a retreat from the open frontier only; October 2026 settled both halves. Muse Wonder Full put Meta at the closed frontier (second on the Artificial Analysis index), though only inside its own coding agent. Muse Spark Open put Meta back into large open weights, but not at the open frontier: at 39 it sits six or seven points below the Chinese leaders (Xiaomi's MiMo, DeepSeek, Zhipu's GLM, Moonshot's Kimi), under a licence with a revenue clause. So the open frontier stayed where it was; Meta's open contribution is size and US provenance, not rank. Llama 3.1 and 3.3 remain default bases in many fine-tuning stacks purely through incumbency: tooling, LoRA adapters, quantised builds and tribal knowledge accumulated around them, and that inertia outlives the quality argument.

Products around the models, and scores in context (volatile: Muse Code, Muse Image, Voice Transcribe, index versions, other labs)

Muse Code is Meta's terminal and CI (continuous integration) coding agent, with sandbox-and-approval defaults, that can run several sub-agents at once; Wikipedia adds that 1.2 was served through the Meta Model API and OpenRouter, with a cheaper contributor tier under which Meta keeps the submitted data ({{Wikipedia|@wspark}}). The image model code-named Mango shipped quietly in early July 2026 as Muse Image ({{The Agent Report|@agentrep}}), and on 1 September Muse Voice Transcribe, streaming speech recognition with speaker identification, covering more than 70 languages with 25 extensively validated at launch ({{9to5Mac|@vt}}).

Artificial Analysis changed its Intelligence Index twice after Muse Spark 1.3 shipped: v4.2 on 4 September 2026 (GPQA Diamond removed) and v4.3 on 7 September (Terminal-Bench moved from 2.1 to 4.0, τ³-Banking replaced by AutomationBench-AA, private tests weighted 45% instead of 40%), with a methodology update to v4.3.2 on 19 September ({{Artificial Analysis|@aalb}}, read 1 October 2026). A number from one version never compares with another.

At release Artificial Analysis scored Muse Spark 1.3 61 at xhigh reasoning and 62 at a max tier available only in limited preview; only Anthropic models scored higher (Claude Fable 5.1 at 66, Opus 5 at 63; Fable 5 tied at 62), and at 61 it tied GPT-5.6 Sol (max) and Grok 4.6 (high); at $0.55 per index task it was the cheapest model scoring 59 or above on that scale ({{Artificial Analysis, 2 September 2026|@aaart}}). Those are on the scale before v4.2. The "cheapest at its level" point does not survive the rescaling: on v4.3 GPT-6.1 Sol (max) scores 51.8 at $0.72 a task, above Muse Spark 1.3 (xhigh) and cheaper per task. Weights are closed, it is served only through Meta's first-party API and Muse Code, and the top number comes from a max tier developers cannot broadly use.

Muse Spark 1.3's best run (48.1) left Meta fourth among labs by each lab's best model, behind Anthropic (57.6), OpenAI (52.7) and Google DeepMind (52.6) on the 1 October reading, derived from the same leaderboard. Muse Wonder Full moved Meta to second: on the 19 October reading it scores 56, behind only Anthropic's Opus 5.5 (58) and level with Sonnet 5.5, ahead of Moonshot's Kimi K3.1 Pro (55, read 26 October) and OpenAI's and Google's best (53 each); Wonder costs $4.52 per index task against Opus 5.5's $5.98 and GPT-6 Astra's $3.26 ({{VentureBeat|@vbwonder}}, read 19 October 2026). In open weights, Muse Glimmer (17.5 for $0.06 a task) sits in the small-model cluster and Muse Spark Open (39 at $0.84) below the open frontier, where Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Pro leads at 46 and DeepSeek V4.1-Flash-1017 and GLM 5.3 follow at 45 (read 19 October 2026). Glimmer's price is Artificial Analysis's median across third-party hosts; Muse Spark's is Meta's API.

Which Meta model when

  • Frontier capability from Meta through a hosted API: Muse Wonder. It buys near-frontier scores, and costs closed weights, a single vendor's API and no technical report; the stronger Wonder Full is reachable only through Muse Code subscriptions. Muse Spark 1.3 remains the cheaper per token for work that does not need Wonder.
  • Weights you can run locally or modify, with no licence conditions: Muse Glimmer. Apache 2.0 removes the 700-million-user clause, and a 24 GB GPU is enough for the 17 GB build; it is a 30B model, so do not expect frontier reasoning.
  • An existing Llama fine-tuning pipeline: stay on Llama 3.1 or 3.3 while its adapters and quantised builds earn their keep, and plan a move, since nothing newer will ship under the Llama name.
  • A large open MoE from Meta: Muse Spark Open, if its licence's revenue clause does not bind you; it scores well above Llama 4 but below the leading Chinese open models. Llama 4 Maverick fits one 8-GPU H100 host in FP8 (400 GB of weights against 8 × 80 GB = 640 GB), but test it on your own tasks first; its public numbers did not hold up in practice, and the open models on the provider pages ({{DeepSeek|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}, {{Alibaba: Qwen|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fd8c36ce06672bb235}}, {{Mistral AI|n:3c65c17b0d0d814a9882e6ebc326fa56}}) have overtaken it.
  • A personal agent with account access: study the Muse agent's design (surrogate credentials, egress control outside the model, approvals outside the conversation) whatever model you use.

Whether a model fits your GPU, with its context, is on Parameters and fit.

Common mistakes and misconceptions (nine, each with the evidence)

"Llama 4 has a usable 10M-token context." That is Scout's claimed limit, resting on iRoPE and attention temperature scaling; a claimed window is not demonstrated quality at that length, and Maverick's limit is 1M. It is also not a single-GPU figure: even with only the 12 global layers growing, Scout's cache at 10M tokens is about 481 GiB in BF16 (derived from the configuration on Parameters and fit), six H100s' worth before any weights.

"17B active means 17B of memory." Active parameters set the compute per token; all 109B or 400B parameters must still be stored. Scout fits one H100 only with Int4 weights (109B at half a byte is about 54.5 GB).

"Llama is open source." The Llama licences restrict very large users and are Meta's own terms. Muse Glimmer's Apache 2.0 is the first Meta licence that is open source in the usual sense.

"Meta overtrained its flagship." The 405B sat close to compute-optimal at about 38.5 tokens per parameter; the heavy overtraining was in the 8B and 70B (about 1,875 and 214 tokens per parameter), which are the ones served at volume.

"The 1417 LMArena score was Maverick's." It belonged to an unreleased experimental checkpoint tuned for chat preference.

"Muse Spark 1.3's 61 compares with today's index numbers." The 61 and 62 are on the scale the index used at release, before v4.2. On the current v4.3 the same model is 45.1 at xhigh and 48.1 at max, the figures to set beside other v4.3 scores.

"Muse Spark Open is Muse Spark 1.3 with its weights published." Meta labels the release "Muse Spark Open", not any numbered version, and publishes no mapping to 1.2 or 1.3; its index score (39) is below 1.3's 45.1 to 48.1, so do not assume the hosted and open models are the same checkpoint.

"The Muse agent cannot be prompt-injected." It can; the design limits what a successful injection achieves. The classifiers that still catch some attacks are unmeasured in public, and the Mac client sat outside the VM boundary.

"Chinchilla says train 20 tokens per parameter, full stop." The 20 is the training-only optimum, and even that depends on whose fit you use: Hoffmann et al.'s own fitted law implies about 70 tokens per parameter, Epoch's replication about 20 ({{Besiroglu et al.|@epoch}}). Once serving is counted the optimum moves far higher; see the explorer.

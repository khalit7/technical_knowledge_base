The idea: change the layers everyone else treats as settled

Moonshot starts from DeepSeek-V3's design (MLA, fine-grained experts with a shared expert, 61 layers, hidden size 7,168) and replaces, one ablation at a time, the parts most labs inherit without question: AdamW, softmax attention in every layer, and the plain residual sum. Moonshot AI is a Beijing lab whose Kimi models are, by most aggregate measures, among the strongest open-weight lines of 2025 and 2026; its edge is not data scale or distribution but this willingness to change what is settled, each change justified by an ablation. Kimi K2 (July 2025) is a DeepSeek-V3 derivative trained with Muon instead of AdamW (K2 report); Kimi K3 (July 2026) puts Kimi Delta Attention in three of every four layers and replaces the residual sum with Attention Residuals (K3 report). The other half of the bet is cycle time: Kimi Linear went from paper (October 2025, arXiv 2510.26692) to flagship backbone in well under a year, fast for a change that touches every kernel in the stack.

You need: multi-head latent attention (MLA) from Attention variants, linear attention and the delta rule from Beyond attention, routing and shared experts from Mixture of experts, and Muon's orthogonalised update from Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers. This page says what Kimi chose and why, and links those owners for the mechanisms.

QK-Clip Moonshot's rule: after an optimiser step, shrink a head's query and key weights whenever that head's largest attention logit (the scaled query-key product qi⊤kj / √d that enters the softmax) passed a threshold. MuonClip: Muon, plus a fix for what Muon breaks

Muon treats each 2D weight matrix's momentum as a matrix and replaces it with an approximately orthogonal one (every singular value near 1), so the update pushes in all directions at comparable scale instead of mostly along the few largest; embeddings, the output head and 1D parameters stay on AdamW. The mechanism and its Newton-Schulz stepper on a real gradient are on Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers. Moonshot's contribution was making it work at scale: its study reports about 2× the computational efficiency of AdamW under compute-optimal training, matching AdamW-trained models with about 52% of the training FLOPs (Muon is Scalable for LLM Training, arXiv 2502.16982), with one momentum buffer against AdamW's two, real memory in a sharded trillion-parameter run. The update for a weight matrix W ∈ ℝA×B at step t, with Moonshot's two additions:

Mt=μMt−1+Gt,Ot=NS(Mt)Wt=Wt−1−η(0.2max(A,B)Ot+λWt−1)
Gt, Mt
the gradient, and the momentum buffer (M0 = 0, momentum coefficient μ = 0.95)
NS
five Newton-Schulz iterations, matrix products that approximate M = UΣV⊤ ↦ UV⊤ without an SVD
η, λ
the learning rate and the decoupled weight decay
0.2√max(A, B)
Moonshot's addition: a full-rank orthogonal A × B matrix has root-mean-square entry 1/√max(A, B), so without it the step size would depend on the matrix's shape

The rescaled update has root-mean-square 0.2, inside the 0.2 to 0.4 range AdamW updates typically show, which lets Muon reuse AdamW's tuned learning rate and weight decay. Weight decay was the second fix: without it, Muon converged faster at first but some weights grew too large over long, over-trained runs; with it, Muon beat both vanilla Muon and AdamW.

Newton-Schulz by hand, Jordan's coefficients, the cost and the distributed version (depth)

X0 = M / ‖M‖F puts every singular value in (0, 1]; each step Xk = aX + b(XX⊤)X + c(XX⊤)2X keeps the singular vectors and maps each singular value σ to f(σ) = aσ + bσ3 + cσ5, with a, b, c = 3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315, Keller Jordan's coefficients (Muon). They are tuned to pull small singular values up fast rather than converge exactly: the design target is a band of 0.7 to 1.3, which Jordan reports does not hurt the loss.

Worked example, singular values 3 and 0.3 (a 10 to 1 spread): ‖M‖F = √(9 + 0.09) = 3.015, so X0 has 0.9950 and 0.0995; f(0.9950) = 3.4445(0.9950) − 4.7750(0.9852) + 2.0315(0.9754) = 0.7047 and f(0.0995) = 0.3381, a spread of 2.08 to 1. Steps 2 to 5 give (1.1093, 0.9889), (0.7153, 0.7097), (1.0967, 1.1034), (0.7020, 0.7087): after 5 steps the spread is 0.991 to 1, and the pair keeps oscillating inside the band rather than settling at 1 (recompute.py). Both directions get the same step, where the raw momentum would have spent ten times more on the first.

Cost. A handful of matrix multiplies per weight per step; Jordan estimates the FLOP overhead below 1% for typical language-model training (0.5% for a Llama-405B-scale run). The awkward part is distributed: orthogonalisation needs the whole matrix while the training job holds it sharded, which is why Moonshot published a distributed implementation built on ZeRO-1 sharding (arXiv 2502.16982). K3's report replaces the all-gather of the whole parameter buffer with peer-to-peer fetches of only the shards each rank owns, cutting memory and communication (K3 report, §5.2.2).

QK-Clip: what Muon broke at scale

In a 53B-total, 9B-active test model trained with plain Muon, the largest attention logits passed 1,000 within a few thousand steps; logits that large saturate the softmax and are followed by loss spikes and occasional divergence, and Moonshot saw this more often with Muon than with AdamW (K2 report, §2.1). The usual fixes (owned by Normalisation, residuals and stability) do not fit K2: a logit soft-cap clips the logits, but the query-key products can still grow without bound before the cap applies; QK-Norm cannot be used with MLA, because MLA never materialises full per-head keys at inference, they are absorbed into the latent projection (Attention variants derives the absorption). What it removed. K2's 15.5T-token pretraining run of a model of 1.04T parameters showed no loss spikes at all (K2 report, Fig. 3), which at that scale means no weeks lost restarting from checkpoints. QK-Clip was only transiently active: in the first 70,000 steps 12.7% of attention heads triggered it at least once and were clamped to 100; after that every head's max logit stayed below 100 on its own and the clip never fired again, so the finished model is not shaped by it (Appendix D). The same appendix's small-scale ablations, including an aggressive τ = 30, showed no statistically significant loss penalty.

K3 keeps it, per head. K3 trains with Per-Head Muon "together with the weight-clipping mechanism introduced in Kimi K2", weight decay 0.1 and a cosine schedule (K3 report, §3.3). Per-Head Muon splits the Q, K and V projections' momentum matrices along the head dimension and orthogonalises each head's block separately, because full-matrix orthogonalisation lets heads with larger gradient scales dominate the shared update direction while small-scale heads are under-normalised; per head, every head gets an equal-scale update, and Newton-Schulz on tall per-head blocks is slightly cheaper (§2.5).

Adoption since: DeepSeek V4 trains most weight matrices with Muon (DeepSeek), Zhipu's GLM line also uses Muon-family optimisers (Zhipu: GLM), and K2 itself is fine-tuned with Muon, which Moonshot recommends for a Muon-pretrained checkpoint.

Kimi Delta Attention and the linear/full hybrid

Full softmax attention keeps every past key and value, so its cache grows with context and every decoded token reads all of it. A linear-attention layer instead keeps a fixed-size state matrix per head, at the price of capacity: writes superimpose and precise recall far back degrades. The recurrence, the delta rule and Gated DeltaNet (GDN), the design KDA extends, are owned by Beyond attention, whose "one state, five write rules" animation shows each write rule on the same words.

KDA, from the Kimi Linear paper (arXiv 2510.26692), is a gated delta-rule linear attention: each write first subtracts what the state retrieves for that key (so a new association overwrites the old one instead of piling on top), and learned decay makes old content fade. Kimi's contribution is the gate's granularity. GDN, like Mamba-2, has one forget rate per head per token, a scalar; KDA gives every key channel its own forget rate, a diagonal matrix, so a layer can keep one feature for thousands of tokens while flushing another at once.

Predict, then try: a fact sits under key channel 1 and scratch under channel 2; ten tokens then write elsewhere. With one scalar gate, can you keep the fact and lose the scratch? Per-channel gates of 0.99 and 0.5 keep 0.904 of the fact and 0.001 of the scratch; switch to GDN's scalar gate and it must pick one rate for both. The other preset, Overwrite, writes v1 = (1, 2) then v2 = (3, −1) under k = (1, 0): plain linear attention reads back the blend (4, 1), the delta rule the new value (3, −1).

The kernel, and what Kimi Linear's ablations kept (depth)

The transition is a diagonal-plus-low-rank (DPLR) matrix of a constrained form, and that constraint is what lets Moonshot's chunkwise-parallel kernel run on tensor cores with substantially less computation than a general DPLR kernel. Kimi Linear also adds a low-rank sigmoid output gate and a short convolution (kernel 4) on the queries, keys and values, both of which its ablations keep: without the gate validation perplexity rose from 5.65 to 5.67, with a swish gate instead to 5.81, without the convolution to 5.70.

  1. A token arrives. Token t enters one of K3's 69 KDA layers as its hidden state ht, 7,168 numbers (front left). Each of the 96 heads projects it to a query, a key and a value of 128 numbers (after a short convolution, Swish and, for q and k, L2 normalisation), a write strength β and a forget rate for each of the 128 key channels. The field is one head's state, 128 × 128 = 16,384 numbers drawn as one bar per 8 × 8 block, holding everything earlier tokens wrote; whatever the context length, it is this size.
  2. Decay, channel by channel. Diag(αt) multiplies each key-channel row of the state by its own rate (green, on the right; the rates are illustrative, and K3 bounds every one above e−5 ≈ 0.0067): rows fade at different rates. Rows near 1 keep their content for thousands of tokens; rows near 0.5 are flushed within a few. Gated DeltaNet would fade every row by the same scalar.
  3. Erase what the key retrieves. The delta rule subtracts βk(k⊤S): whatever the state currently returns for this key (k, on the left) is removed along the rows where k is large. This is what lets a new association overwrite an old one instead of piling on top.
  4. Write the new value. The outer product βkv⊤ writes the token's value (v, at the back) into the same rows. The state has changed in place; its size has not: still 16,384 numbers per head, 1,572,864 for the layer's 96 heads.
  5. Read with the query. The output is o = S⊤q (q on the left, o at the front), then a full-rank sigmoid gate and the output projection. A decoded token reads the whole state once per head: 1,572,864 numbers for the layer, the same at token 10 as at token 1,000,000.
  6. The same token through a full-attention layer. In one of K3's 24 Gated MLA layers, WDKV maps ht to the 512-number latent plus a 64-number key part shared by all 96 heads (not rotated: NoPE), one 576-number entry, and appends it: the cache gains an entry per token. Drawn to the KDA block's scale (equal cross-sections, so length is numbers stored), at 4,096 tokens it holds 2,359,296. In decode the up-projections are absorbed into the query side, so all 96 query heads read every entry, 576 × t numbers, every token.
  7. Let the context grow. The sweep runs the context to 16,384 tokens. The KDA layer stays at 1,572,864 numbers (plus 110,592 of short-convolution state); the MLA cache grows by 576 per token, passes it at about 2,731 tokens and keeps going: at K3's 1,048,576 it would hold 603,979,776 numbers, 384 times as much, far off the card.
  8. The K3 stack. K3 interleaves 3 KDA layers with 1 Gated MLA layer, 23 times, then closes with one more MLA layer: 69 + 24 = 93. Only the 24 MLA layers grow with context, so per token the stack stores 24 × 576 = 13,824 numbers against 53,568 for an all-MLA 93-layer stack; with the 69 fixed states added, that is a 74.0% cut at 1M tokens counted in numbers (73.8% in bytes if the state is kept in FP32), approaching 1 − 24/93 = 74.2% as the context grows.

The hybrid: three KDA layers, then one full MLA layer

Kimi Linear ablated the ratio on the same 1.4T-token recipe: 3:1 had the lowest validation perplexity (5.65); 7:1 matched its training loss but generalised worse (5.70); 1:1 was about as good (5.66) at a higher inference cost; pure full attention was worst (5.77), and 15:1 worse still (5.82). The full layers keep an exact, lossless recall path for retrieval and copying; the KDA layers carry most of the depth at constant memory.

No positions in the full layers. The MLA layers use no positional encoding at all (NoPE): KDA's decay is itself position-aware, so position and recency are handed entirely to the KDA layers. With nothing rotated, MLA needs no decoupled RoPE key, so these layers run as plain multi-query attention at inference (why: Attention variants), and the RoPE adjustments that long-context training otherwise needs (frequency-base tuning, YaRN: Positional encodings) disappear; K3 extrapolates to 1M tokens "without any positional-encoding modification" (K3 report, §3.4).

The payoff, against a full-MLA baseline trained the same way on 1.4T tokens: up to 75% less KV cache, up to 6 times the decoding throughput at 1M-token context (time per output token 1.84 ms against 11.48 ms, 6.3×), and equal or better quality on short-context, long-context and RL-style evaluations. It costs custom chunked-parallel kernels (needed to use tensor cores at all), and any capability that depends on exact long-range recall now leans on the one-in-four full layers.

Where "up to 75%" comes from

Only full-attention layers grow a cache, so with r KDA layers per full layer the cache is 1/(r + 1) of an all-full stack of the same shape: 1/4 at 3:1, a 75% cut. In K3, 24/93 = 25.8% of the layers cache, about a 74% cut against an all-MLA 93-layer stack, plus a fixed KDA state per request that does not grow with context: 96 heads × 128 × 128 = 1,572,864 numbers per KDA layer, 108.5 million over 69 layers. That state is why the cut is "up to": at 1M tokens it is 74.0% counted in numbers, 73.8% in bytes if the state is kept in FP32, but below a few thousand tokens the hybrid holds more than an all-MLA stack would. What K3 changed in the hybrid

From the K3 report, §2.1:

  • A bounded decay (config gate_lower_bound −5), so every chunk of the kernel runs as a dense tensor-core matrix multiply instead of a slow position-pair path.
  • Full-rank gates: KDA's output gate becomes a full-rank projection, and the MLA layers get the same kind of channel-wise sigmoid gate on their output, hence "Gated MLA" (output gates across models: Attention variants, sinks and gates).
  • One extra global layer: the 3:1 pattern repeats 23 times (92 layers) and one more Gated MLA layer closes the stack so the final layer always attends globally: 69 KDA plus 24 MLA, 93 layers (config full_attn_layers ends 92, 93).
Why the decay had to be bounded, and the full-rank gate's formula (depth)

Kimi Linear maps the gate logit to a log-decay with g = −eA Softplus(z), which is unbounded below; the chunkwise kernel rescales keys by the reciprocal of the cumulative decay, which can overflow, so the diagonal 16-token tiles needed a slow position-pair path. K3 uses g = gmin Sigmoid(eAz) with gmin = −5: every retention factor exceeds e−5 ≈ 0.0067, a 16-token tile's cumulative log-decay stays in (−80, 0), the reciprocal stays below e80 inside BF16's range, and every tile runs as a dense tensor-core matrix multiply. The full-rank output gate: y = Wo[Sigmoid(Wgx) ⊙ RMSNorm(õ)].

Moonshot fitted a sparsity scaling law with Muon: holding active experts at 8 (plus 1 shared) and FLOPs constant, raising the total kept lowering loss; to reach a validation loss of 1.5, sparsity 48 needed 1.69×, 1.39× and 1.15× fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 16 and 32 (K2 report, §2.3).   3. K2: half the heads. DeepSeek-V3 used 128 heads, about twice its layer count; K2 uses 64.   4. K3: 896 narrower experts behind a latent. 896 routed experts, 16 active (sparsity 56), 2 shared experts at full width, 96 heads. Stable LatentMoE: experts in a half-width latent

In a conventional MoE each selected expert receives the full d-wide token, so communication and expert-weight traffic grow with the number of experts each token visits. LatentMoE separates the two widths: shared experts keep a full-width path, while routed experts work in a compact latent of width ℓ (3,584, half of 7,168) (K3 report, §2.3):

"Stable" names three fixes for failure modes that get worse at 896 experts. The routed path chains W↓, a gated expert network and W↑ into nearly four consecutive matrix multiplies, which at 2.8T scale produced exploding internal activations; an RMSNorm before the up-projection (which also improved validation loss and benchmarks) and SiTU-GLU, a SwiGLU whose gate and up branches are each soft-capped with β tanh(x/β) (β1 = 4, β2 = 25, so the output is bounded by β1β2 = 100 while it tracks SwiGLU near the origin), suppress it. And balancing nearly 103 experts exceeds the regime where DeepSeek's fixed-step bias update behaves, so K3 uses Quantile Balancing: each expert's routing bias is set directly from the quantile of router-score margins that would give it exactly its target load, q = mk/n tokens.

Predict, then try: 16 tokens choose among 4 experts with skewed router scores (illustrative). Which update reaches equal loads first? Press the Quantile Balancing step a few times: it jumps most of the way to the target from one pass and settles within a token of it after two or three, with no step size to tune. Reset and press the fixed-step update instead: it creeps, then keeps oscillating, faster or slower with γ.

Attention Residuals: attention across depth

Attention Residuals (arXiv 2603.15031, March 2026) change the residual stream itself. In a standard PreNorm transformer each layer adds its output to a running sum, hl = hl−1 + fl−1(hl−1), so layer l sees the embedding plus every earlier output with weight exactly 1. Two problems follow: the sum's magnitude grows roughly linearly with depth, diluting each new layer's contribution (later layers learn ever larger outputs to be heard, which can destabilise training), and no layer can pick out one earlier layer's output once it has been summed in. Moonshot's observation is that this is the depth-wise analogue of an RNN compressing a sequence into one state, and the fix is the one attention brought to sequences. (Other attempts at the stream, such as DeepSeek's mHC, are on Normalisation, residuals and stability.)

Predict, then watch: at layer 40 of K3, how many vectors does each scheme keep per token? The plain residual keeps one running sum, Full AttnRes every earlier output, Block AttnRes at most 9. The "trained" weights in step 4 are illustrative: only their shape (a softmax over sources) is real.

  1. The plain residual: one running sum. Each sublayer output is a plate along depth (attention and MLP sublayers count as separate layers), the gold plate the token embedding. The input to layer 40's attention sublayer is the sum of all 79 before it, each with weight exactly 1: the sum grows roughly linearly with depth (grey), so each new output is diluted, and once summed no layer can pick out one earlier output.
  2. Full AttnRes: a softmax over depth. Layer 40 attends back over all 79 earlier outputs instead, with weights from its learned pseudo-query against each RMS-normalised output. The pseudo-queries start at zero, so at initialisation every source gets 1/79 and the model starts out behaving like a standard transformer. The price: all 79 vectors kept per token, 79 × 7,168 × 2 = 1,132,544 bytes in BF16, and shipped between pipeline stages.
  3. Block AttnRes: sum inside a block, attend across blocks. Production groups the layers into blocks of 12 layers: inside a block the outputs are summed as usual into one summary plate, and the softmax runs over the block summaries and the embedding. At layer 40 that is 5 sources (the embedding, blocks 1 to 3 and block 4 so far), 0.2 each at initialisation, 5 × 7,168 × 2 = 71,680 bytes per token.
  4. Trained, the weights differ by token. Training moves the pseudo-query off zero, so the weights stop being uniform, and because they depend on the token's own outputs, different tokens can retrieve different depths (here 71% on the current block; the weights are illustrative). No token attends to another token through them.
  5. K3: 8 blocks and the embedding. K3 partitions its 93 layers into 8 blocks of 12 (the last block partial, 9 layers), "9 total blocks when counting the embedding" (K3 report, §2.2), so its last layers mix 9 sources. Its EAGLE-3 draft model reads features from the 1st, 4th and final blocks.

The RMSNorm on the keys stops a large-magnitude layer from dominating the weights by size alone. The weights depend on the token, because the vi do, so different tokens can retrieve different depths; no token attends to another through them. The cost per layer is one d-dimensional vector of parameters. The pseudo-queries start at zero, so training begins with uniform weights: at layer 4, the four sources each get 0.25, the standard residual sum divided by 4, a scale the next RMSNorm removes, so the model starts out behaving like a standard transformer.

Block AttnRes in production. At scale every layer's output would have to be kept and, under pipeline parallelism, shipped between stages, so production groups layers into about 8 blocks, sums outputs inside a block as usual, and runs the softmax only over the block summaries and the embedding, cutting memory and communication from one vector per layer to one per block. Moonshot reports Block AttnRes matching a baseline trained with 1.25× more compute, under 4% end-to-end training overhead with pipeline parallelism, under 2% inference latency overhead, and bounded hidden-state magnitudes with gradients spread more evenly across depth. It was validated on the Kimi Linear 48B architecture over 1.4T tokens before K3 adopted it.

Low precision as a release format, not a post-processing step

Moonshot ships its models natively quantised: the published checkpoint is the deployable one, trained to tolerate its own precision (formats, PTQ against QAT and the hardware: Quantization and Precision). Weight memory is bytes = Σparts Npart × bpart / 8, with N each part's parameter count and b the bits stored per weight, including its share of any per-block scale.

Worked example: K3's weights. At BF16, 2.78 × 1012 × 2 = 5.56 TB. MXFP4 stores 4-bit elements plus one 8-bit scale per block of 32, so b = 4 + 8/32 = 4.25. But only the routed experts are MXFP4: 2,722,740,830,208 of them, 98% of the parameters; attention, the latent projections, shared experts, routers, embeddings and the vision encoder stay in higher precision (K3 report, §4.1.4; config ignore list). So the weights take 2.7227 × 1012 × 4.25/8 + 57.18 × 109 × 2 = 1.561 TB, the checkpoint's published size to the byte (safetensors index): about 28% of BF16, the difference between a model a few labs can run and one a well-equipped team can serve. An earlier version of this page put every weight at 4.25 bits and got 1.49 TB.

INT4 QAT, K2 Thinking. Moonshot applied quantisation-aware training in the post-training stage, with weight-only INT4 on the MoE components, which hold nearly all the parameters (model card). The forward pass sees the 4-bit weights while training continues, so the model adapts to the coarse grid instead of being rounded onto it afterwards. It matters most for reasoning models: post-training quantisation introduces small per-step errors that compound over long chains of dependent reasoning steps, so the loss shows up far more in long chains than in short answers. Moonshot reports a lossless 2× speed-up in low-latency mode, since decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and the expert weights are a quarter of their BF16 size; all its benchmark results are reported at INT4.

MXFP4, K3. K3 ships MXFP4 expert weights with MXFP8 activations, trained in with QAT from the SFT stage onward and through RL, with rollout and training sharing the same quantisation so there is no train-inference mismatch (model card; report §4.1.4). Block-wise scaling is what makes 4 bits survivable: an outlier distorts only its own 32-value block instead of the whole tensor's resolution, and the format has native tensor-core support on Blackwell-class hardware, so it is fast rather than merely small. Shipping the flagship as an MXFP4 checkpoint is how a 2.8T model gets to be practically open-weight.

Only the experts are 4-bit, and that is enough to cut K3 to 28% of BF16

Post-training: manufacturing agentic data, and rewards where no checker exists

There is very little naturally occurring data for multi-step tool use, so Moonshot manufactures it (K2 report, §3.1.1), as large-scale rejection sampling in three stages (the diagram).

The kept trajectories feed supervised fine-tuning. Reinforcement learning then runs in a Gym-like framework over many tasks with verifiable rewards (tests, checkers), the RLVR recipe owned by RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR, with K1.5's policy objective optimised with Muon. Three additions keep it stable across domains: a per-task token budget, with responses that exceed it truncated and penalised (RL otherwise inflates lengths everywhere, including where thinking does not pay); an auxiliary loss on hand-picked high-quality data (PTX) so RL does not erase it; and a sampling temperature that starts high for exploration and decays.

K2's policy objective, from K1.5 (depth)
LRL(θ)=𝔼x∼D[1K∑i=1K(r(x,yi)−r‾(x)−τlogπθ(yi∣x)πold(yi∣x))2]

Minimising the squared term pushes each response's log-probability ratio towards its advantage divided by τ.

The self-critique rubric reward. Where no automatic checker exists (writing quality, judgement, following a complex instruction), the model, bootstrapped as a judge during supervised fine-tuning, ranks its own candidate responses by pairwise comparison against rubrics (core values, prescriptive rubrics written to block known reward hacks, and human-written rubrics for specific instructions), and the ranking becomes the reward. It buys reward coverage over the large fraction of real work that is not verifiable, and costs the honesty of the grader: a policy trained against a judge derived from itself has an obvious incentive to learn what the judge likes. Moonshot's mitigation is to keep retraining the critic on on-policy rollouts from verifiable tasks, so its judgement stays tied to outcomes that can be checked. What defeats that is any quality the verifiable tasks never exercise, such as style in creative writing, where nothing anchors the critic (Reward Hacking owns the general failure).

The thinking variants are additionally trained for interleaved reasoning, alternating reasoning and tool calls rather than thinking once and then acting, which is what agentic browsing benchmarks reward. K2 Thinking's card claims coherent behaviour across 200 to 300 consecutive tool calls, where prior models degraded after 30 to 50 (model card); test-time compute in general is on Test-time compute and reasoning.

1. ToolsMore than 3,000 real MCP (Model Context Protocol) tool specs taken from GitHub, plus over 20,000 synthetic tools evolved from a hierarchy of categories and domains (financial trading, robot control, software applications and so on).
2. Agents and tasksThousands of agents, each a generated system prompt with a sampled tool set, and for each agent tasks from simple to complex, every task paired with an explicit rubric: success criteria, expected tool-use pattern and checkpoints.
3. What K3 added

How effort, unverifiable rewards and long rollouts are trained in K3 (depth)

Effort is trained with a per-problem budget: a trajectory longer than τ · b0(x), with b0 estimated from the cold-start model, gets reward −1, and annealing τ down yields the high and low experts. Misreadings worth avoiding (depth)
  • "Muon replaces AdamW everywhere." It handles 2D hidden-layer matrices only; embeddings, the output head and norm gains stay on AdamW.
  • "Muon makes the update exactly orthogonal." Five tuned Newton-Schulz steps leave singular values spread around 1 (roughly 0.7 to 1.2 in practice, inside Jordan's 0.7 to 1.3 target), by design.
  • "QK-Clip clips logits." It rescales weights after the step; a logit soft-cap is the method that clips logits.
  • "A linear-attention layer always uses less memory than a cache." Its state is fixed, not small: a K3 KDA layer holds 1,572,864 numbers per request, as much as an MLA layer's cache at about 2,700 tokens.
  • "The 4-bit checkpoint is a community quantisation." The 4-bit weights are the trained release format, and only the routed experts are 4-bit: the other 57 billion parameters stay in higher precision, which is why the checkpoint is 1.56 TB rather than 1.49 TB.

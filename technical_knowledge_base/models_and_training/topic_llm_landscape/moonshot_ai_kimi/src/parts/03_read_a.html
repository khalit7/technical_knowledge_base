The idea: change the layers everyone else treats as settled

Moonshot starts from DeepSeek-V3's design (MLA, fine-grained experts with a shared expert, 61 layers, hidden size 7,168) and replaces, one ablation at a time, the parts most labs inherit without question: AdamW, softmax attention in every layer, and the plain residual sum. Moonshot AI is a Beijing lab whose Kimi models are, by most aggregate measures, among the strongest open-weight lines of 2025 and 2026; its edge is not data scale or distribution but this willingness to change what is settled, each change justified by an ablation. Kimi K2 (July 2025) is a DeepSeek-V3 derivative trained with Muon instead of AdamW ({{K2 report|@k2}}); Kimi K3 (July 2026) puts Kimi Delta Attention in three of every four layers and replaces the residual sum with Attention Residuals ({{K3 report|@k3rep}}). The other half of the bet is cycle time: Kimi Linear went from paper (October 2025, {{arXiv 2510.26692|@kl}}) to flagship backbone in well under a year, fast for a change that touches every kernel in the stack.

This page says what Kimi chose and why, and links those owners for the mechanisms.

QK-Clip Moonshot's rule: after an optimiser step, shrink a head's query and key weights whenever that head's largest attention logit (the scaled query-key product qi⊤kj / √d that enters the softmax) passed a threshold. MuonClip is Muon plus weight decay, Adam-matched update scaling and QK-Clip, the optimiser K2 was pretrained with.
KDA (Kimi Delta Attention): a gated delta-rule linear attention with a per-channel forget gate. Kimi Linear: the 48B-total, 3B-active research model that introduced it in a 3:1 hybrid with full MLA layers.
AttnRes (Attention Residuals): each layer takes a learned softmax-weighted mix of earlier layers' outputs instead of their sum.
Sparsity (Moonshot's usage): total experts divided by activated experts, 384 / 8 = 48 for K2. A different number from total over active parameters.
QAT and MXFP4: quantisation-aware training, training with simulated low-precision weights; and microscaling FP4, 4-bit elements with one shared 8-bit scale per block of 32 values (the formats in general: {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}).

The line: each change proven small, then shipped big

The order explains the design. Muon was proven on a 16B model (Moonlight) before it trained the 1T K2; KDA and Attention Residuals were proven on the 48B Kimi Linear before K3 combined them; 4-bit QAT went into K2 Thinking before K3 shipped its whole expert set in MXFP4. MuonClip: Muon, plus a fix for what Muon breaks

Muon treats each 2D weight matrix's momentum as a matrix and replaces it with an approximately orthogonal one (every singular value near 1), so the update pushes in all directions at comparable scale instead of mostly along the few largest; embeddings, the output head and 1D parameters stay on AdamW. The mechanism and its Newton-Schulz stepper on a real gradient are on {{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}}. Moonshot's contribution was making it work at scale: its study reports about 2× the computational efficiency of AdamW under compute-optimal training, matching AdamW-trained models with about 52% of the training FLOPs ({{Muon is Scalable for LLM Training, arXiv 2502.16982|@muon}}), with one momentum buffer against AdamW's two, real memory in a sharded trillion-parameter run. The update for a weight matrix W ∈ ℝA×B at step t, with Moonshot's two additions:

Mt=μMt−1+Gt,Ot=NS(Mt)Wt=Wt−1−η(0.2max(A,B)Ot+λWt−1)
Gt, Mt
the gradient, and the momentum buffer (M0 = 0, momentum coefficient μ = 0.95)
NS
five Newton-Schulz iterations, matrix products that approximate M = UΣV⊤ ↦ UV⊤ without an SVD
η, λ
the learning rate and the decoupled weight decay
0.2√max(A, B)
Moonshot's addition: a full-rank orthogonal A × B matrix has root-mean-square entry 1/√max(A, B), so without it the step size would depend on the matrix's shape

The rescaled update has root-mean-square 0.2, inside the 0.2 to 0.4 range AdamW updates typically show, which lets Muon reuse AdamW's tuned learning rate and weight decay. Weight decay was the second fix: without it, Muon converged faster at first but some weights grew too large over long, over-trained runs; with it, Muon beat both vanilla Muon and AdamW.

Newton-Schulz by hand, Jordan's coefficients, the cost and the distributed version (depth)

X0 = M / ‖M‖F puts every singular value in (0, 1]; each step Xk = aX + b(XX⊤)X + c(XX⊤)2X keeps the singular vectors and maps each singular value σ to f(σ) = aσ + bσ3 + cσ5, with a, b, c = 3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315, Keller Jordan's coefficients ({{Muon|@kj}}). They are tuned to pull small singular values up fast rather than converge exactly: the design target is a band of 0.7 to 1.3, which Jordan reports does not hurt the loss.

Worked example, singular values 3 and 0.3 (a 10 to 1 spread): ‖M‖F = √(9 + 0.09) = 3.015, so X0 has 0.9950 and 0.0995; f(0.9950) = 3.4445(0.9950) − 4.7750(0.9852) + 2.0315(0.9754) = 0.7047 and f(0.0995) = 0.3381, a spread of 2.08 to 1. Steps 2 to 5 give (1.1093, 0.9889), (0.7153, 0.7097), (1.0967, 1.1034), (0.7020, 0.7087): after 5 steps the spread is 0.991 to 1, and the pair keeps oscillating inside the band rather than settling at 1 (recompute.py). Both directions get the same step, where the raw momentum would have spent ten times more on the first.

Cost. A handful of matrix multiplies per weight per step; Jordan estimates the FLOP overhead below 1% for typical language-model training (0.5% for a Llama-405B-scale run). The awkward part is distributed: orthogonalisation needs the whole matrix while the training job holds it sharded, which is why Moonshot published a distributed implementation built on ZeRO-1 sharding ({{arXiv 2502.16982|@muon}}). K3's report replaces the all-gather of the whole parameter buffer with peer-to-peer fetches of only the shards each rank owns, cutting memory and communication ({{K3 report|@k3rep}}, §5.2.2).

QK-Clip: what Muon broke at scale

In a 53B-total, 9B-active test model trained with plain Muon, the largest attention logits passed 1,000 within a few thousand steps; logits that large saturate the softmax and are followed by loss spikes and occasional divergence, and Moonshot saw this more often with Muon than with AdamW ({{K2 report|@k2}}, §2.1). The usual fixes (owned by {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}}) do not fit K2: a logit soft-cap clips the logits, but the query-key products can still grow without bound before the cap applies; QK-Norm cannot be used with MLA, because MLA never materialises full per-head keys at inference, they are absorbed into the latent projection ({{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}} derives the absorption). QK-Clip acts on the weights instead, after the optimiser step, using the max logit the forward pass already computed. For each head h:

Smaxh=1dmaxX∈Bmaxi,j𝐪ih⊤𝐤jh,γh=min(1,τSmaxh)Wqh←γhαWqh,Wkh←γh1−αWkh
B, X
the current batch, and a training sequence in it
i, j
token positions
d
the head dimension
τ
the threshold, 100 for K2
γh
the head's shrink factor
α
the split of the shrink between queries and keys, 0.5 (an equal split)

A logit is linear in both Wq and Wk, so the scaled weights multiply every logit of that head by γhαγh1−α = γh, bringing the max logit back to exactly τ. That step's forward and backward passes are unchanged.

Clipping is per head, not per layer, because only a few heads blow up; a naive version that clips every head by the layer's worst would shrink healthy heads too. On MLA each head's logit is the sum of a content part and a rotary part, and the rotary key is shared across heads (MLA's decoupled RoPE key), so the rule becomes: scale the head-specific content query and key by √γh each, scale the head-specific rotary query by γh, and leave the shared rotary key alone so that clipping one head does not touch the others. Both parts of that head's logit then scale by γh.

Predict, then try: the third head reports 150 against τ = 100. What happens to the head at 80, and to the head at 230, under each rule? Per head: γ = 0.667 for the third head, its content query and key each multiplied by 0.816, its max logit back to 100; the head at 80 untouched. Switch to the naive rule and every head shrinks by the worst head's factor. The logits are illustrative.

What it removed. K2's 15.5T-token pretraining run of a model of 1.04T parameters showed no loss spikes at all ({{K2 report|@k2}}, Fig. 3), which at that scale means no weeks lost restarting from checkpoints. QK-Clip was only transiently active: in the first 70,000 steps 12.7% of attention heads triggered it at least once and were clamped to 100; after that every head's max logit stayed below 100 on its own and the clip never fired again, so the finished model is not shaped by it (Appendix D). The same appendix's small-scale ablations, including an aggressive τ = 30, showed no statistically significant loss penalty.

K3 keeps it, per head. K3 trains with Per-Head Muon "together with the weight-clipping mechanism introduced in Kimi K2", weight decay 0.1 and a cosine schedule ({{K3 report|@k3rep}}, §3.3). Per-Head Muon splits the Q, K and V projections' momentum matrices along the head dimension and orthogonalises each head's block separately, because full-matrix orthogonalisation lets heads with larger gradient scales dominate the shared update direction while small-scale heads are under-normalised; per head, every head gets an equal-scale update, and Newton-Schulz on tall per-head blocks is slightly cheaper (§2.5).

Adoption since: DeepSeek V4 trains most weight matrices with Muon ({{DeepSeek|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}), Zhipu's GLM line also uses Muon-family optimisers ({{Zhipu: GLM|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a88a84c2ca84b4daf4}}), and K2 itself is fine-tuned with Muon, which Moonshot recommends for a Muon-pretrained checkpoint.

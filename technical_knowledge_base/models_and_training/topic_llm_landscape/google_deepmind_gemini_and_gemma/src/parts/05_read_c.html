Gemma: the open end of the pipeline, built to fit one machine

Every Gemma generation is distilled from Gemini research and spends its architectural effort on one question: what fits in the memory of one card or one phone. Gemma 1 (21 February 2024, 2B and 7B) started the line; Gemma 2 (27 June 2024; 2B, 9B, 27B) trained its 2B and 9B by knowledge distillation from a larger teacher rather than plain next-token prediction, and interleaved local and global attention layers one to one, which is why its small models competed with models two to three times their size ({{Wikipedia|@wgemma}}, {{Gemma 2 report|@g2}}). Gemma 3 (12 March 2025; 1B, 4B, 12B and 27B; 32K context on the 1B and 128K on the rest; image input through a 400M-parameter SigLIP encoder that turns an 896 by 896 image into 256 tokens) moved to the 5:1 layout below; Gemma 3n (full release 26 June 2025) added the phone tricks; Gemma 4 (2 April 2026) added a mixture of experts and Apache 2.0 ({{Gemma 3 report|@g3}}).

Gemma 4 came in four sizes, E2B, E4B, 26B A4B (a mixture of experts) and a dense 31B, joined on 3 June 2026 by a dense 12B Unified ({{Wikipedia|@wgemma}}). It is the first Gemma under Apache 2.0; earlier generations used Google's own Gemma terms, so this is the release that made Gemma unrestricted for commercial use. Google reports over 400 million Gemma downloads since the first generation; at launch the 31B ranked third among open models on the Arena text leaderboard and the 26B sixth ({{Google|@g4b}}). From the model card and report ({{model card|@g4c}}, {{Gemma 4 report|@g4r}}):

  • 12B Unified is encoder-free. Instead of a separate vision encoder (550M parameters on the 26B and 31B, 150M on E2B and E4B), it projects raw 48 by 48 pixel patches straight into the embedding space with one 35M-parameter matrix, and audio the same way: native multimodality carried to the open family by dropping the encoder altogether.
  • A built-in draft model. Each model ships a separate 4-layer multi-token prediction (MTP) drafter for speculative decoding (76M parameters on E2B to 500M on the 31B). What it buys depends on an acceptance rate Google does not publish: Gemma on one machine works it through; the mechanism is on {{Speculative decoding|n:3f35c17b0d0d81e086fde85940ebac1c}}.

Gemma attention: why memory stops growing

A sliding-window ("local") layer only ever looks back W tokens, so its share of the KV cache stops growing at the window; a global layer keeps every token. Gemma's choice is the ratio: Gemma 2 alternated one to one with a 4,096-token window; Gemma 3 uses five local layers per global layer with a 1,024-token window, and Gemma 4 keeps 5:1 on every size except E2B (4:1), the final layer always global. The global layers use RoPE base 1,000,000 against 10,000 on the local ones, since only they see long distances ({{Gemma 3 report|@g3}}; RoPE bases on {{Positional encodings|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}).

Past the window only the dark (global) layers keep growing

Predict, then try: at 128K tokens, how much of Gemma 3 27B's cache do the 52 local layers hold? Each cell is one layer of the chosen model; global layers are dark. The default reproduces about 10.4 GiB of cache against 62 GiB if every layer were global. Move the context below 1,024 tokens: the two layouts cost the same. Switch to Gemma 4 31B to see wider global heads paid for by reusing keys as values.

Gemma 3 27B: 62 layers (52 local, 10 global), 16 KV heads of dimension 128, so 8 KiB per layer per token in bf16, the gallery's 496 KiB per token if no window were credited ({{gallery|@gallery}}; {{LLM Architecture Gallery page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81be8a07f2562fa2030a}}). The middle option is illustrative: Gemma 3 27B's dimensions under Gemma 2's one-to-one layout with a 4,096 window. Gemma 4 31B from its {{config|@cfg31}}: 50 local layers with 16 KV heads of 256, 10 global layers with 4 KV heads of 512 and keys reused as values. GiB is 230 bytes.

Gemma 3's report puts the cache's share of inference memory at about 60% for a global-only model against under 15% with sliding windows of 1,024, measured with a 32K-token prefill ({{Gemma 3 report|@g3}}). The cost is that most layers cannot see distant tokens directly and depend on the global layers to carry them. Sizing a cache for any model, Gemma presets included, is the {{Long context|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5}} calculator's job.

The cache formula behind the strip

For one sequence of T tokens:

KV=(LgTeg+Llmin(T,W)el)×bel=2Hkvdh
Lg, Ll
number of global and local layers
eg, el
elements stored per token in one global or local layer: keys plus values, 2 × KV heads × head dimension (one copy instead of two where keys are reused as values)
W
the sliding window, 1,024 tokens on Gemma 3 and the larger Gemma 4 models
b
bytes per element: 2 for bf16, 1 for int8

Past T = W only the global term grows, so long-context memory is set almost entirely by the global layers.

Gemma 4's two global-layer savings

Keys reused as values (on all but E2B and E4B) stores one vector per global head instead of two. p-RoPE applies rotary position to only a fraction p = 0.25 of each global head's dimensions ({{config|@cfg31}}: partial_rotary_factor 0.25, base 1,000,000 against 10,000 on local layers). The report says these, "combined with KV cache sharing and the reuse of keys as values in global layers ... reduce the global KV cache footprint by up to 37.5%" ({{Gemma 4 report|@g4r}}), and does not show the arithmetic.

One accounting lands exactly on the report's 37.5%

Our reconstruction (not Google's): if the rotated part of each key must be stored separately from the unrotated value, a global head stores the value (d) plus the rotated fraction of the key (p d) instead of key and value (2d), a saving of (1 − p) / 2. Move p: at 0.25 it lands on the report's figure; at 1 (ordinary RoPE) sharing saves nothing; at 0 it saves half.

The report's own memory table, and the gallery's 840 KiB per token for the 31B, count one stored vector per global head, a 50% cut; recomputed from the config that reproduces the table's 1.10 GB cache at 32K (see Gemma on one machine). So the two figures in the report imply slightly different accounting.

The edge models: an 8B checkpoint that runs like a 4B one

Gemma 3n introduced two ideas that make a large checkpoint behave like a small one on a phone ({{Gemma 3n docs|@g3n}}, {{developer guide|@g3nb}}), and Gemma 4's E2B and E4B keep the naming:

  • Per-Layer Embeddings (PLE). Each decoder layer gets its own small embedding table per token; those tables can be computed outside accelerator memory, cached to fast storage and added as each layer runs. So E2B, which loads over 5B parameters in standard execution, can run with just under 2B (1.91B) resident.
  • MatFormer (a Matryoshka transformer) nests a smaller model inside the larger: E2B is a sub-model of E4B trained with it, so layers can be mixed to fit a memory budget between the two sizes.
  • KV caches shared across layers. In Gemma 4 E2B the last 20 of 35 layers, and in E4B the last 18 of 42, reuse earlier layers' caches ({{Gemma 4 report|@g4r}}; num_kv_shared_layers in the configs).

Gemma 4 on one machine: sparse against dense

Gemma 4 offers one quality band two ways, the 26B A4B mixture of experts (each token routed to 8 of 128 experts plus 1 shared) and the 31B dense model, and the choice is memory against compute: compute per token scales with the active parameters, F ≈ 2Nactive, while memory must hold every parameter. Figures from the Gemma 4 report's memory table (text only, KV cache in int8 at a 32K context, {{Gemma 4 report|@g4r}}):

  1. Compute: 26B A4B has 3.8B active, so F ≈ 2 × 3.8 × 109 = 7.6 GFLOP per token; 31B has F ≈ 2 × 30.7 × 109 = 61.4 GFLOP. The MoE does about 8 times less arithmetic per token.
  2. Weights in bf16: 52.0 GB against 64.0 GB, only about 20% less (52 / 64 = 0.81), because every expert has to be resident. Both fit one 80 GB H100 unquantized ({{Google|@g4b}}). At 4 bits (Q4_0): 16.2 GB against 19.2 GB, so both fit a 24 GB card, the MoE with more room to spare.
  3. Decode reads the weights it uses for every token: the MoE's active weights are 7.6 GB in bf16 and 2.8 GB at 4 bits, against all 19.2 GB for the dense 31B at 4 bits, about 7 times less to read per token. KV cache at 32K: 0.28 GB against 1.10 GB; the MoE has 30 layers against the dense model's 60, and fewer, narrower KV heads.

So with memory to spare, the MoE generates far faster at a similar footprint: a bet that the memory-versus-compute trade favours sparsity even on one workstation. When memory is the constraint (a 16 GB card), neither fits at 4 bits, and the 12B Unified (24.0 GB in bf16, 7.65 GB at 4 bits) is the model that does. Batching erodes the MoE's lead, because a batch touches more experts: Gemma on one machine sets memory, context, precision and batch. The routing mechanism and the general trade are on {{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}}.

The encoder-decoder branch: T5Gemma and EmbeddingGemma

T5Gemma (April 2025) and T5Gemma 2 (December 2025) are a research line rather than a product tier, and the only open encoder-decoder LLMs a frontier lab currently ships. The method is adaptation: copy a pretrained decoder-only Gemma checkpoint into an encoder-decoder shell (the encoder's self-attention switched from causal to bidirectional, the decoder gaining cross-attention over the encoder output) and continue pretraining, which is cheaper than pretraining from scratch and, above roughly 100M parameters, better. Two results carry beyond Gemma: asymmetric sizing (a 9B encoder with a 2B decoder runs at Gemma 2 2B's latency and scores clearly above it, the right shape for long-input, short-output work and impossible in a decoder-only model) and long context (T5Gemma 2 4B-4B scores 81.7 on RULER 32K against Gemma 3 4B's 66.8, despite pretraining at only 16K). The method, ablations and every number are on {{Encoder-Decoder Gemma and T5Gemma 2|n:3d45c17b0d0d81a9a22ffaada508db5c}}.

EmbeddingGemma and EmbeddingGemma 2

The first EmbeddingGemma is built on T5Gemma 2 checkpoints. EmbeddingGemma 2 (6 October 2026, 740M parameters, Apache 2.0) is described instead as built on the Gemma 4 architecture, and it embeds text, image, audio, video and code into one space: a 270M text path, with optional 170M vision and 300M audio encoders, 768 dimensions with Matryoshka truncation to 512, 256 or 128, and 8K tokens of context, four times the first version's. Google reports MTEB Code rising from 68.76 to 78.68 ({{Google|@eg2}}).

How Google trains

Everything on TPUs

Google's model cards name only its own hardware and software. Gemini 3 Pro was trained on TPUs with JAX (Python traced into XLA programs, sharding written as annotations on arrays) and ML Pathways (the orchestration layer that lets one program drive many TPU pods asynchronously) ({{model card|@mc3}}). Gemini 2.5 trained with synchronous data parallelism across multiple 8,960-chip TPU v5p pods in several datacenters, and its slice-granularity elasticity kept about 97% of throughput through local failures by continuing on fewer slices ({{Gemini 2.5 report|@g25}}). Gemma 4 trained on TPU v6e (4,096 chips for E2B up to 10,240 for the 31B), the 12B on 12,288 TPU v4 chips ({{Gemma 4 report|@g4r}}).

It buys freedom from GPU supply, pricing and allocation politics, and a pod interconnect built for exactly this collective pattern. It costs transferability: essentially none of the stack carries to an outside practitioner the way PyTorch, FSDP and NCCL do, and the work is correspondingly hard to reproduce. Since 2026 the dependency also runs outward: Ironwood, the seventh TPU generation, is the first Google sells for other companies' workloads, and Anthropic's deal for up to a million TPUs runs on it ({{TPUs: systolic arrays and pod-scale machines|n:3c65c17b0d0d8151bbc2e0ceea196187}}); Alphabet's Q2 2026 results say Google Cloud "generates product revenues primarily from the sale of TPU systems" ({{Alphabet Q2 2026|@alpha}}). The software is on {{Topic: jax-and-tpu|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ecbc04eea6aa45ea1a}}.

Native multimodality from pretraining

Trained in from the start rather than added as an adapter, since 1.0: text, images, audio and video become tokens in one sequence, trained jointly, rather than a separately trained vision encoder projected into a finished text model; images come out through integrated generation (the Nano Banana image models), all through one context window. Gemma 4's 12B Unified carries the idea to the open family. Where this sits among shallow, modular and unified designs is on {{Multimodal LLM Architectures|n:3c65c17b0d0d8139bd6cd4137dd2a4d8}}.

One distillation pipeline, many products

The student matches the teacher's whole next-token distribution, not just the correct token (the method is on {{Distillation and small language models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}). Storing a full distribution over a vocabulary of about 262,000 tokens for every training token is too expensive, so Google keeps part of it: Gemini 2.5 approximates the teacher "using a k-sparse distribution over the vocabulary" (only the most likely tokens) ({{Gemini 2.5 report|@g25}}), and Gemma 3 samples 256 logits per token weighted by the teacher's probabilities, pretraining from 2T tokens for the 1B to 14T for the 27B ({{Gemma 3 report|@g3}}). Flash and below have been distilled this way since Gemini 1.5, and Gemma is the open end of the same pipeline, which is why the open models track the closed ones so closely in behaviour.

Why only k entries per token: the full distribution would not fit any store

Try: how much teacher output one student run would have to store. The default is Gemma 3 27B's 14T tokens with 256 entries kept per token.

Vocabulary 262,144 entries. Bytes per entry are illustrative: 4 bytes per probability for the full distribution, 4 for the probability plus 4 for the token id when only k entries are kept. Google does not publish how it stores them.

Distribution is the strategic asset

Search (AI Overviews and AI Mode), Workspace, Android and Vertex all ship Gemini by default, and since September 2026 so does a rival's flagship surface: Apple's rebuilt Siri. Google supplies the assistant model on the phones it competes with: distribution bought rather than owned, and evidence that the training stack rather than the app is the durable asset.

Common mistakes

Ten mistakes, each with the fact that prevents it
  • Comparing Flash generations on the per-token price. 3.6, 3.7 and 3.8 Flash have the same price list; 3.8 Flash costs about a third more per task ($1.24 against $0.93 on the Intelligence Index v4.3) because it spends more tokens.
  • Sending one thinking configuration to every model. Minimal is not supported on 3.8 Flash, 3.7 Flash or 3.1 Pro, defaults differ (medium, high or minimal), and thinking_budget belongs to an API that has moved.
  • Assuming you pay only for the summary. Thinking tokens are billed in full as output even when only a summary returns.
  • Dropping thought signatures. Rebuilding history without the thought blocks breaks multi-turn tool use.
  • Taking "1M tokens" as "an hour of anything". An hour of video is 342,000 tokens at the default resolution and over the limit at high.
  • Planning around a preview model as if it were stable. 3 Pro Preview (18 November 2025) was shut down and redirected on 9 March 2026, within four months of launch.
  • Taking the Pro tier for the strongest open-API model. On the current index 3.1 Pro (29.7) sits below 3.8 Flash at high (40.9); tier names track size and price, not this year's scores.
  • Reading E4B as a 4B checkpoint. It is an 8B checkpoint with 4.5B effective parameters; the saving depends on running PLE outside accelerator memory.
  • Choosing the MoE when memory is the constraint. 26B A4B is about 8 times cheaper in compute per token than 31B but only about 20% smaller in memory.
  • Assuming Gemma carries its old licence. Gemma 4 is Apache 2.0; Gemma 3 and earlier are under Google's Gemma terms.

Axis 1: where the thinking goes

Test-time compute is computation spent at inference, deliberating before answering. A reasoning (or "thinking") model is trained to use it well, and every flagship is now one. The labs differ in where the computation goes, who decides how much, and what the caller can see.

Try: step through the four designs and watch what reaches the caller. Recurrent depth is the only one that leaves no text at all.

Thinking is paid for as output tokens, so these designs are also price decisions: more effort buys more score for more tokens (Cost per task). The open labs mostly expose thinking as an on, off or effort switch.

The question every lab answers differently is how much thinking to buy and where to spend it: longer, wider, or deeper inside the network. Cost, latency and quality are all functions of that choice.

Three ways to spend it

Sequential: one longer chain

Self-correction inside one trajectory (the o1 and R1 pattern): later steps see earlier ones, so the model can backtrack, and this is the axis RL training scales. Costs latency no hardware removes and a KV cache that grows with every token.

Parallel: many chains, then a selector

Sample independent solutions and pick one. Wall-clock time need not grow, but tokens scale with the sample count, and the result is only as good as the selector: a real verifier (unit tests, a proof checker) is strongest, voting needs comparable answers, and a learned reward model is overoptimised as n grows.

Latent: depth inside the network

Loop activations back through the same layers, so part of the deliberation never becomes text: compute without the token bill or emission latency, at the price of what a transcript gave (monitoring, evaluation, simple serving). A vendor's architecture decision, never a caller's.

They compose. "Heavy" tiers such as Grok 4 Heavy ("parallel test-time compute, which allows Grok to consider multiple hypotheses at once", xAI) and Gemini Deep Think are parallel sampling of already-long sequential traces, with aggregation between the branches rather than one final vote; the branches are still token traces a monitor can read. That is why they cost an order of magnitude more than the standard tier for a few points of benchmark score. The same 10,000-token budget spent both ways, to scale, and recurrent depth animated layer by layer: Deeper: test-time compute.

Where it stands

  • Token efficiency is the competition. At equal accuracy the thinking is the price, so launches lead with "same score, fewer tokens": GPT-5.6 Sol outperforms "with fewer tokens and at lower estimated cost" (OpenAI); a customer at the Opus 5 launch reported "similar performance while generating 26% fewer tokens on average compared to Opus 4.8 at max reasoning" (Anthropic).
  • The harness carries part of the score once deliberation lives outside the transcript (Reading the numbers). On the 167 game-reasoning pairs both of ARC Prize's harnesses solved, Astra's Provider Adapter was also about 3.66 times faster and used 49% fewer tokens (ARC Prize).
  • Verification-heavy pipelines hold the top of the hardest benchmarks (HLE, olympiad sets, ARC-AGI): parallel sampling plus a verifier (Deep Think, Grok Heavy), and deep-research products chaining reasoning with search and tools.
  • Open replication is complete. R1's lineage (Qwen3, GLM-5.x, Kimi K3, OLMo 3 Think, MiniMax M3) made RLVR recipes and reasoning datasets public, with full training logs for OLMo; the remaining frontier gaps are scale, data and infrastructure.

Where the scaling stops paying

  • Overthinking: on some tasks longer reasoning lowers accuracy, across model families (Gema et al.), so "always think harder" is not a safe default.
  • RL sharpens, it does not extend: RLVR models beat their base models at pass@1, but the base models reach more problems at large k (Yue et al.); pretraining still sets the ceiling.
  • The selector caps parallel scaling: best-of-n against a learned reward model saturates and then reverses; a vote stops improving once settled, and gets worse where a wrong answer is the most likely one.
  • The economics are unfavourable by construction: cost and latency grow linearly with thinking tokens, accuracy roughly with their logarithm over a useful range (o1's curves use a log axis, OpenAI) and then flattens, so each point costs more than the last.

Axis 2: what it costs to serve

Weights: total against active parameters

In a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model each layer's feed-forward block is split into many experts and a router sends each token to a few. Total parameters must all sit in memory; active parameters, the ones a token passes through, set the compute per token. "600B/27B" means 600 billion total, 27 billion active; total divided by active is the sparsity ratio, 1 for a dense model. Why the labs went sparse

At the same active budget, more total parameters buy quality: Mixtral 8x7B (47B total, 13B active) "outperforms or matches Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 across all evaluated benchmarks" (Mixtral paper). Why, and why experts also shard across GPUs so total parameters can exceed one machine: Mixture of experts.

The ratio then climbed and settled into a band: Mixtral at 3.6x in 2023, DeepSeek-V3 at 18x at the end of 2024, Kimi K2 at about 32x in 2025 (1.04T over 32B, K2 report), and 2026 frontier-size models between about 16x and 33x, the shaded band above: 2026 moved the band's centre rather than its ceiling. Moonshot's sparsity scaling law shows the pull: at constant active parameters, more experts kept lowering loss, and reaching a validation loss of 1.5 at sparsity 48 (8 of 384 experts) took 1.69x, 1.39x and 1.15x fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 16 and 32; K2 stopped at 48 because "this gain comes with increased infrastructure complexity" (K2 report). Moonshot's "sparsity" counts experts (384 / 8), not parameters (about 32x).

Three things set the ceiling. Memory: every total parameter is stored and paid for at serving time, which is why native low-precision checkpoints matter: Kimi K2 Thinking ships INT4 (card), Kimi K3 MXFP4 weights with MXFP8 activations from quantisation-aware training (card), gpt-oss MXFP4 experts (model card). Communication: each token is sent to k experts, so traffic between GPUs grows with k. Under-trained experts: each expert sees about k/N of the tokens, so at very large N experts may see too few to specialise; Kimi K3's Stable LatentMoE (896 experts, 16 active, 2 shared) targets this, and Moonshot claims "an approximate 2.5× improvement in overall scaling efficiency over Kimi K2" (K3 card).

Dense or MoE

Choosing dense or MoE, top-1, top-2 or fine-grained top-k, and a shared expert for a serving budget is an architecture decision, worked through with a dense-against-MoE calculator on Mixture of experts. What the labs chose: MoE everywhere above about 130B; dense survives below it, where Mistral Medium 3.5 (128B, card) and Command A (111B, card) are the largest current dense models on the scatter.

Context: the KV cache

Attention stores a key and a value for every earlier token, the KV cache, and it grows with the context, so a million-token window is first a memory problem: at Mixtral 8x7B's layout (32 layers, 8 key-value heads of dimension 128, 16-bit; Mixtral paper) it is 128 KiB per token, 128 GiB for one sequence stretched to a million tokens, past one 80GB accelerator. The formula, cache growth and compression, and how much of a window a model really uses: Long context; grouped-query, latent (MLA), sliding-window and sparse attention: Attention variants; linear attention, state-space layers and hybrids: Beyond attention.

Linear attention replaces the growing cache with a fixed-size state; sparse attention attends to a learned selection of earlier positions. A fixed-size state cannot hold every token exactly, so real designs mix them with some full-attention layers. Every open-frontier lab that publishes its architecture now ships one of them:

How we got here

Every lab on this page converged on the same skeleton in under three years, and the order is visible in the release history: 140 sourced releases from 24 labs, February 2023 to September 2026.

  • Mixture-of-experts came first. xAI (Grok-1, November 2023) and Mistral (Mixtral, December 2023) shipped the first MoE rows; DeepSeek and Google followed within two months, and 17 labs had one by September 2026.
  • Reasoning spread fastest. From OpenAI's o1-preview (September 2024) to eleven labs within eight months and 18 by April 2026.
  • Hybrid attention is the newest. From MiniMax-Text-01 (January 2025) to ten labs. Nine labs now have all three in the table, all of them labs that publish weights; the closed US labs disclose too little about architecture to count, which is "not disclosed", not "not used".
  • The pace and the field grew together. Rows per year went 12, 22, 46 and 60 (2026 to September only); the number of labs releasing in a quarter went from 3 in early 2023 to 20 in 2026 Q3, the busiest quarter (38 rows).
  • Total size grew, compute per token did not. The largest open model went from 65.2B (LLaMA) to 2.8T (Kimi K3), about 43-fold, while the median active size of open releases stayed between 13B and 32B a year, because MoE went from 2 of 9 open sized releases in 2023 to 26 of 33 in 2026.
  • Open releases got more permissive, and a smaller share. Open weights were 75% of rows in 2023 and 70% in 2025 but 55% in 2026, as the closed labs shipped more often; within open releases, Apache 2.0 or MIT went from 4 of 9 (2023) to 24 of 33 (2026).
How reasoning models formed, 2022 to 2026
  • 2022 to 2023: chain-of-thought prompting, self-consistency (Wang et al.) and STaR (fine-tuning on the model's own correct chains) showed reasoning could be elicited, and trained on.
  • September 2024: o1, the first production model trained with large-scale RL to reason in a hidden chain of thought, with large jumps on AIME, GPQA and Codeforces, made accuracy against inference compute a public curve (OpenAI).
  • January 2025: DeepSeek-R1 replicated it openly, MIT-licensed, with the recipe and distilled 1.5B to 70B variants that seeded an open ecosystem (DeepSeek-R1).
  • 2025: hybridisation: budgets (Claude 3.7 Sonnet, Gemini 2.5), two modes in one checkpoint (Qwen3), a router (GPT-5); o3, Grok 4, Kimi K2 Thinking, GLM-4.x and MiniMax M1 and M2 shipped reasoning-first. In July Gemini Deep Think's "parallel thinking" solved five of six IMO problems for 35 points (Google DeepMind), and an experimental OpenAI model also reached gold-medal level (Alexander Wei).
  • 2026: absorption: no flagship ships without reasoning, budgets gave way to effort levels, and in September GPT-6 Astra was reported to move part of the budget into recurrent depth.

Common mistakes

Reading total parameters as cost or quality
Memory follows total parameters, compute per token follows active ones, and neither is a score.
"Sparsest" without saying of what
Three quantities get the label. The ratio sets memory per unit of decode speed: DeepSeek V4 Pro leads at about 33x. The active count sets what a token costs: Step 5 Preview's 27B is the smallest in the frontier band although its 22x ratio is unremarkable, and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next's 6B of 125B the smallest among flash models. A phase-dependent model breaks both: DeepSeek V4.1-Flash activates 8B of 552B in prefill (69x) and 16B in decode (35x) (DeepSeek changelog). And Moonshot's "sparsity 48" counts experts, not parameters.
Taking a benchmark speed as a property of an MoE model
Routing depends on the input, so throughput does too: a speed describes the model under that prompt mix, batch size and expert placement (Mixture of experts).
"Mixtral 8x7B is eight 7B models"
Only the feed-forward blocks are replicated; attention and embeddings are shared, so it is about 47B total and 13B active, not 56B and 14B (Mixtral paper).
Treating a tier name as an architecture
OpenAI's Sol, Terra and Luna are price bands; OpenAI has published nothing about how they differ inside (VentureBeat).
Taking an unchanged token price for an unchanged cost
Gemini 3.8 Flash kept 3.7 Flash's token price but takes more steps, so it costs $1.24 per index task against $0.93, about a third more (Artificial Analysis); Google itself put the rise at about 40% (Google).
Treating open weights as open source
Downloadable weights under a custom licence are not a fully open release; only OLMo and K2 Horizon publish the data and recipe.
Believing a vendor table because it is detailed
Weight the independent reruns, such as ARC Prize's harness and Artificial Analysis' runs.
Comparing test-time-compute results without the budget and the harness
A score is a point on a cost curve; a comparison that omits thinking tokens, sample count or harness compares nothing (the 62.7% and 99.9% in Reading the numbers are the same weights).
Expecting thinking to add knowledge
More tokens reorganise what the weights and tools already hold. A factual gap needs retrieval or a better base model.

    Why a chain of thought adds computation

    The intuition is scratch paper. A model that must answer in its first token gets one forward pass of computation, however hard the question. A model allowed to write its working first gets one forward pass per token written, can look back at what it wrote, and can cross it out. A reasoning model is trained, typically by large-scale reinforcement learning on tasks whose answers can be checked mechanically (mathematics with a reference answer, code with unit tests), to plan, verify, backtrack and self-correct in that working rather than to emit a plausible-looking derivation.

    Chain of thought (CoT)

    The tokens a model generates before its final answer, holding intermediate steps. In reasoning models it usually sits in a separate thinking block that the product may hide, summarise or show.

    Verifier

    A program that checks an answer: exact or numeric match against a reference, a unit-test suite, a proof checker. Correct by construction on the property it checks.

    Reward model

    A learned network that scores an answer. It generalises to tasks with no checker, and it has errors that optimisation can find.

    pass@k

    The probability that at least one of k independent samples is correct: what best-of-k with a perfect verifier achieves.

    Self-consistency (majority voting)

    Sample several answers and return the most common final answer. Written cons@k or maj@k for k samples.

    Best-of-n

    Sample n answers and return the one a scorer (verifier or reward model) rates highest.

    A transformer forward pass has fixed depth, so the serial computation available to produce one next-token distribution is bounded by the layer count and cannot be raised at inference. A chain of thought removes that bound: each emitted token is another full forward pass conditioned on everything written so far, so serial steps become unbounded and the context window acts as an external working memory persisting across them. That is the whole mechanism.

    Dserial=L×T
    Dserial
    the number of layer applications that can happen one after another before the answer is committed
    L
    the number of layers in the model
    T
    the number of tokens generated before the answer (at least 1)

    Worked example. Take an illustrative 60-layer model. Answering directly in one token allows 60 × 1 = 60 serial layer applications. Thinking for 10,000 tokens first allows 60 × 10,000 = 600,000, four orders of magnitude more serial depth from the same weights. Theory agrees that this is a change in kind and not only in amount: transformer decoders allowed a number of decoding steps logarithmic in the input length gain "only slightly" over a single pass, while a linear number of steps lets them recognise all regular languages and a polynomial number lets them recognise exactly the problems solvable in polynomial time, under standard complexity conjectures and a mild generalisation of pre-norm in the normalisation layers (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023).

    Three capabilities follow. Decomposition: a problem needing more serial steps than the model has layers becomes reachable. Externalised state: intermediate results are written down instead of held in activations discarded at every token. Revision: the model can read what it wrote, notice a contradiction and start again, which no single forward pass can do.

    The working memory is not free. Every generated token leaves keys and values in the KV cache for every later token to attend to, at the rate of the KV-cache formula in the Reading tab's Cost to serve section (2 × layers × key-value heads × head dimension × bytes per number, per token), with T here counting the prompt as well as the thinking.

    With the illustrative 60 layers, 8 KV heads of dimension 128 in BF16, one token costs 2 × 60 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 245,760 bytes (240 KiB), and a 32,768-token trace costs 7.5 GiB for a single request. Inference techniques: what actually makes serving fast covers the serving techniques (paged attention, cache quantisation, multi-query and latent attention) that make this affordable.

    Defaults reproduce the worked numbers above by construction (60 layers, 8 KV heads of 128, BF16, 32,768 tokens: 240 KiB a token, 7.5 GiB). Drag T to 1 to see what answering directly buys.

    What test-time compute does not buy matters equally. Beyond adding no knowledge that is not in the weights or retrievable by a tool, it does not make a problem solvable if the model cannot make any correct partial progress on it, since there is nothing for later steps to build on. And the returns are shaped badly for economics (Where it stops paying).

    One long chain or many: the same budget, to scale

    The Reading tab names three ways to spend the budget: sequentially in one longer chain, in parallel across many chains with a selector, or latently inside the network. Sequential costs irreducible latency (10,000 thinking tokens at an illustrative 50 a second is 200 seconds before the answer starts), a KV cache growing linearly with the trajectory, and attention over an ever-longer scratchpad getting more expensive per token. Parallel need not add wall-clock time given the hardware, but total tokens scale with the sample count and it lives or dies on the selector.

    Recurrent depth: spending the budget inside the network

    The latent way spends the budget in activations rather than in tokens. A looped transformer reuses the same layers several times per token, so serial depth grows without adding parameters and without writing anything down. The idea goes back to the Universal Transformer (Dehghani et al., 2018), which applied one block repeatedly with a learned halting rule. The clearest modern form is Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025): a prelude that embeds the token, a recurrent core iterated r times with the input re-injected each time, and a coda that decodes. Its 3.5-billion-parameter proof of concept, trained on 800 billion tokens with layers (2, 4, 2) and a mean of 32 iterations, improved on reasoning benchmarks "up to a computation load equivalent to 50 billion parameters".

    Dtoken=Lprelude+Lcore×r+Lcoda
    Dtoken
    serial layer applications spent on one token
    Lprelude, Lcore, Lcoda
    layers in each part (2, 4 and 2 in Huginn)
    r
    how many times the core runs, chosen at inference

    Huginn at its training mean: 2 + 4 × 32 + 2 = 132 layer applications per token from 8 distinct layers. Other members of the family: Ouro (Zhu et al., 2025) pre-trains looped models on 7.7 trillion tokens with a learned depth allocation and reports 1.4B and 2.6B models matching up to 12B ones; Nanbeige4.2-3B runs its 22-layer stack twice, which its report found kept about 75% of a standard model's token efficiency, and Mixture-of-Recursions adds a router deciding per token how many passes it gets (both as summarised by Raschka).

    The animation runs the same 8 layers of weights three ways, to scale: an ordinary model buying 32 layers of depth by writing four tokens, Huginn's shape buying the same 32 in one token by looping its core, and RLT carrying a state from token to token.

    Shapes are scaled down so they fit: 8 layers instead of a frontier model's dozens, 7 loops instead of Huginn's 32, RLT's 4+4 split from its experiments rather than the report's 48+48 illustration. Layer counts, loop structure and the carried state follow Geiping et al. and the RLT project page; the tokens are placeholders.

    RLT. The Recurrent Looped Transformer (Yifan Zhang, Jichen Feng and Shihan Qin, September 2026) closes a different loop. In a standard decoder-only model nothing computed at the last layer of token t reaches the first layer of token t + 1; RLT pairs a causal encoder, which supplies token representations and a global key-value memory, with a decoder that feeds the previous token's final decoder state into the current token's input through a gated merge and keeps local sliding-window attention caches, in chunks that continue across prompt, response and message boundaries with no reset. That gives unbounded temporal depth at fixed per-token compute. The report illustrates a tied 48+48 layout, weights shared between encoder and decoder, so 96 logical blocks per token, and after t tokens the state path has traversed 48t decoder blocks. Its experiments use untied eight- and sixteen-layer models on six algorithmic tasks against a decoder-only transformer, three seeds each: the 5+3 and 7+1 splits keep 100% accuracy on 256-bit parity, and on permutation tracking at length 512 the 4+4 split reaches 55.70% ± 25.78 final-state accuracy against the transformer's 0.85% ± 0.30. The gains depend on the task and the split (addition accuracy falls beyond the trained operand widths), and the report "explicitly reports no measured efficiency, reasoning quality, or scaling results" and trains no language model (MarkTechPost), so for frontier models it is vocabulary rather than evidence.

    GPT-6 Astra. OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra in September 2026, and reports that it uses this technique ("opaque recurrence", looping activations back through its layers so part of the deliberation never becomes text) came from press coverage rather than from OpenAI's announcement, which does not describe the architecture (OpenAI; TechCrunch). TechCrunch called Astra's use of it "reportedly limited", and OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki wrote that "OpenAI has worked to preserve and utilize chain-of-thought monitoring since our very first reasoning models". The system card finds Astra's chain-of-thought controllability "substantially higher than that of GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.5 Thinking", and that told a monitor is present it can shorten its chain of thought enough to "occasionally evade CoT-only monitors", while saying OpenAI is "quite confident that changes in CoT controllability are not differentially due to any architectural changes" (system card).

    What it buys

    Compute without the token bill and without the latency of emitting the reasoning; serial depth without parameters.

    What it costs

    Everything downstream of the transcript: a monitor cannot read deliberation that was never written, an evaluation harness cannot reconstruct the working state, and a serving stack has to persist the recurrent state between requests or recompute it.

    What it does not save

    A looped block halves the block parameters to hold in memory, but costs about the same compute and the same KV cache as the unlooped depth it replaces: 22 blocks run twice need the cache of 44 distinct blocks (Raschka).

    The disagreement. Buck Shlegeris (Redwood Research) warned that if OpenAI pushes it further, "they'll have the option to massively increase the recurrence and totally destroys CoT monitorability" (TechCrunch). Ryan Greenblatt wrote that Astra "appears to be a massive jump in opaque reasoning ability", able to solve hard competition problems "entirely in its head", guessed the jump is downstream of architectural changes with more serial depth (with a normal pretraining scale-up a plausible alternative), and said that if later generations move to "full-on opaque reasoning architectures with extreme depth", chain of thought "would no longer be a meaningful oversight tool" (Greenblatt); a later post he co-authored argues the same at length (Finnveden et al.). Sebastian Raschka is the sceptical counterweight: the gains are "likely primarily due to other reasons, namely improved training recipes and training data" (Raschka's survey), reusing layers "does not by itself suppress visible chain of thought", and fewer reasoning tokens from more passes is the same effect as scaling up the model (Raschka on Astra).

    The maths of parallel sampling

    Parallel sampling is the one way of spending test-time compute whose returns can be written down exactly, and the formulas explain most of the folklore about it. Three selectors, three formulas.

    pass@k: the ceiling a perfect verifier reaches

    If one sample solves a problem with probability p, and samples are independent:

    pass@k=1−(1−p)k
    p
    the single-sample success probability on this problem
    k
    the number of samples drawn
    (1 − p)k
    the probability that all k fail

    With p = 0.2: one sample succeeds 20% of the time; five give 1 − 0.85 = 1 − 0.32768 = 0.672; ten give 1 − 0.810 = 1 − 0.107 = 0.893. A verifier that recognises a correct answer turns this directly into accuracy, which is why best-of-n against unit tests or a proof checker is the strongest form of parallel scaling. It also shows the ceiling: a problem with p = 0 stays unsolved at any k.

    In evaluation, pass@k is estimated from n ≥ k samples of which c are correct, with the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021), who drew n = 200 samples per problem for k ≤ 100 and showed that the naive 1 − (1 − c/n)k is biased:

    pass@k^=1−(n−ck)(nk)
    n
    samples drawn per problem
    c
    how many of them are correct
    ratio
    the probability that a random subset of k of the n samples contains no correct one

    Defaults are the worked example above: C(8, 5) = 56 and C(10, 5) = 252, so the estimate is 1 − 56/252 = 0.778.

    Majority voting: it amplifies whatever is most likely

    Self-consistency needs no verifier, only answers that can be compared. In the simplest case, one correct answer against one competing wrong answer, with n odd:

    Pmaj=∑j>n/2(nj)pj(1−p)n−j
    Pmaj
    the probability that the majority answer is correct
    j

    Compute-optimal allocation

    Which way to spend a fixed budget depends on the problem. Snell et al. (2024) allocated test-time compute adaptively per prompt between searching against a process verifier and letting the model revise its own answer sequentially, and found that this compute-optimal strategy improved "the efficiency of test-time compute scaling by more than 4× compared to a best-of-N baseline", and that "on problems where a smaller base model attains somewhat non-trivial success rates, test-time compute can be used to outperform a 14× larger model" in a FLOPs-matched evaluation. The qualifier is the point: on problems the small model cannot make progress on, pretraining scale still wins.

    Training a model to use its scratchpad

    How a model learns to use a scratchpad well has two answers, and current practice uses both.

    Supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces. Collect problems, generate or obtain long chains of thought, keep the ones whose final answers are correct (rejection sampling, the STaR idea), and train on them as ordinary next-token prediction. Cheap, stable, no rollout infrastructure, and how a small model inherits a large one's reasoning style: DeepSeek's distilled R1 variants from 1.5B to 70B were produced exactly this way, from 800,000 R1-curated samples, "only SFT" and no RL stage (DeepSeek-R1; the R1 paper page). Small curated sets go further than expected: s1 fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on s1K, "1,000 questions paired with reasoning traces" (Muennighoff et al., 2025). Its limits are structural. The student is bounded by the teacher. It imitates the surface form of deliberation, including steps it could not have derived itself, so it learns what reasoning looks like rather than a policy it can execute reliably. And because the training set is filtered for success, it contains almost no genuine errors and so teaches almost nothing about recovering from one.

    RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Let the model generate its own trajectories and score the final answer with a program: string or numeric match against a reference for mathematics, a test suite for code, a checker for formal proofs. Two properties follow. First, the reward is not a learned model, so the usual reward-hacking channel (exploiting a reward model's misgeneralisation) is closed; what remains is gaming the checker itself, for example code that special-cases the visible tests. Second, and more important, the trajectories are the model's own, so credit assignment is on-policy: what gets reinforced is behaviour this model can actually produce. That is why long traces, self-checking and backtracking emerge under RLVR rather than having to be demonstrated.

    R1-Zero was the clean demonstration: pure RL on a strong base model (DeepSeek-V3-Base), no supervised reasoning data at all, produced spontaneous re-derivation and reflection, and its responses grew over training "from hundreds to thousands" of reasoning tokens purely because longer thinking earned more reward, while AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (86.7% with self-consistency) in the expanded version (Nature, 2025; the January 2025 arXiv v1 reported 71.0%, and 86.7% with majority voting over 64 samples). The model learned to spend test-time compute because spending it was rewarded.

    The production recipe combines them in a specific order, and R1's is the openly documented version:

    1. Cold start a small SFT set of curated long chains of thought fixes format and readability (R1-Zero's traces were correct but mixed languages and were painful to read).
    2. Reasoning RL large-scale RLVR for capability.
    3. Rejection sampling the RL model's own best trajectories become a new SFT set, used both to re-tune the flagship and to distil smaller models.
    4. Second RL a final RL stage over all scenarios.

    RLVR's binding constraint is that it needs a verifier, so it covers mathematics, code and formal domains cleanly and writing, analysis and judgement poorly. Extending it means reintroducing a learned reward model or model-graded rubrics, which reopens exactly the reward-hacking channel that made RLVR attractive.

    GRPO: the optimiser behind most reasoning RL

    The RL step itself is a policy-gradient update with a clipped surrogate objective: the ratio of new-policy to old-policy probability per token, times an advantage, clipped so that one update cannot move the policy too far. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), the RLHF default, gets the advantage from a learned value network (the critic), which in LLM RL means holding up to four large networks at once (policy, frozen reference, critic, reward model) and asking the critic to predict, from a half-finished derivation, whether a reward arriving thousands of tokens later will be 1. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization), introduced in DeepSeekMath and used to train R1, deletes the critic. Per prompt it samples a group of G completions, scores each with the verifier, and gives every token of completion i the same advantage, the reward "normalized by subtracting the group average and dividing by the group standard deviation" (DeepSeekMath, Section 4.1.2):

    A^i=ri−mean(r1,…,rG)std(r1,…,rG)
    Âi
    the advantage of completion i, shared by all its tokens
    ri
    its reward (1 if the verifier accepts the answer, 0 if not)
    G
    the group size, the number of completions sampled for the same prompt

    With G = 4 and one correct answer, rewards 1, 0, 0, 0 have mean 0.25 and standard deviation 0.433, so the correct completion gets +1.732 and each wrong one −0.577. If all four are correct, or all wrong, every advantage is zero and the group contributes nothing but its sampling cost; a prompt the model solves with probability p gives such a group with probability pG + (1 − p)G, so on easy or impossible problems that is most of the batch.

    Each square is one completion with its advantage (green: verifier accepted). Defaults reproduce the worked example (+1.732, −0.577). The zero-signal share is derived: with G = 4 a prompt solved 5% of the time wastes 81% of its groups; with G = 16, 44%.

    What this means for test-time compute: an RL run's bottleneck becomes generation throughput, since every prompt needs G long rollouts, which is why RL stacks run vLLM or SGLang inside the training loop and treat rollout and training weight synchronisation as a first-order engineering concern. Every token of a failed trajectory is penalised equally, including correct early steps, because no critic remains to tell them apart. RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR owns the algorithm: the PPO comparison, the KL term, and the fixes most 2026 recipes use in place of textbook GRPO (Dr. GRPO removing the standard-deviation and length normalisations as biases, DAPO's higher upper clip, dynamic sampling of degenerate groups, token-level loss and overlong penalties).

    Where the scaling stops paying: the depth

    • Overthinking is real and measurable. On some inputs, longer chains reduce accuracy: the model revisits a correct answer and argues itself out of it, or drifts towards distractors. Gema et al. (2025) built tasks where "extending the reasoning length of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) deteriorates performance", with five failure modes across model families: Claude models increasingly distracted by irrelevant information, o-series models overfitting to problem framings, a shift from reasonable priors to spurious correlations, loss of focus on long deductions, and amplified concerning behaviour. This is the failure mode adaptive-depth work exists to fix, and it means "always think harder" is not a safe default.
    • RLVR sharpens, it does not extend. RLVR-trained models beat their base models at small k such as pass@1, but the base models reach a higher pass@k when k is large (Yue et al., 2025): the RL model concentrates probability on solutions the base model could already sample rather than acquiring new ones. In pass@k terms, RL raises p on problems where it was already above zero and can drive it to zero where it was small, and 1 − (1 − p)k rewards breadth at large k. Pretraining quality still bounds the ceiling, which is the most important thing to hold before assuming an RL stage will fix a capability gap. Whether newer, longer RL runs escape the pattern is debated on RL for LLMs; Published curves plots Yue's own checkpoints.

    Ten illustrative problems with base-model p from 0.6 down to 0.005. After RL, p becomes 1 − (1 − p)s on the problems it kept and 0 on the ones it dropped. pass@k is the mean of 1 − (1 − p)k over the ten. With nothing dropped there is no crossover: sharpening alone costs no coverage, which is why the crossover Yue et al. measured is evidence that RL narrows what the model samples.

    The other two limits, that the selector caps parallel scaling and that accuracy grows roughly with the logarithm of compute while cost grows linearly, are comparisons and sit in the Reading tab's Where the thinking goes.

    Controls in detail

    The Reading tab compares the five control mechanisms in production (two modes in one checkpoint, a token budget, an effort level with adaptive thinking, interleaved thinking, routed reasoning) and which lab uses which. The vendor details and the research behind them:

    Budgets. Anthropic's budget_tokens (introduced with Claude 3.7 Sonnet, "think for no more than N tokens, for any value of N up to its output limit of 128K tokens", Anthropic, February 2025, and the only mode on the Claude 4 and 4.5 models) and Gemini 2.5's original thinking budget are the token-count form. Anthropic's must be at least 1,024 tokens and below max_tokens, and it "sets a target" rather than a strict cap; with interleaved thinking it can exceed max_tokens because "the budget spans all thinking blocks within one assistant turn" (Anthropic docs). s1's budget forcing is the simplest working control: end the thinking forcibly when the budget is spent, or append "Wait" when the model tries to stop early, which makes it re-examine its answer; this took s1-32B "from 50% to 57% on AIME24" (Muennighoff et al., 2025). The better version conditions the model on the budget during training, so it learns to compress and land a conclusion inside the allowance; that is where the research is.

    Effort levels. Anthropic replaced the budget with adaptive thinking and an effort setting: manual budgets are deprecated on the 4.6 models, and Claude 4.7 and later "reject requests that use it, returning a 400 error" (Anthropic docs). Effort is "soft guidance for how willing Claude should be to think and how deeply", and at low effort the model may skip thinking on simple queries (Anthropic: steering thinking). Gemini models "engage in dynamic thinking by default", controlled by thinking_level; the accepted levels (minimal, low, medium, high) and the default vary by model, and the Gemini API now uses it for the 2.5 models too (Gemini docs).

    Routers. GPT-5 shipped as "a smart, efficient model", "a deeper reasoning model" and "a real-time router that quickly decides which to use based on conversation type, complexity, tool needs, and your explicit intent" (OpenAI, August 2025). Research systems now train the decision per step: CogRouter reports an 82.3% success rate with "62% fewer tokens" than its baselines (Yang et al., February 2026), and Ares cuts reasoning tokens "by up to 52.7% compared to fixed high-effort reasoning, while introducing minimal degradation in task success rates" (Yang et al., March 2026).

    A caveat and common mistakes

    Reward hacking and verbosity bias are endemic. Verifiable rewards remove the reward-model channel but not the checker channel, and length penalties and process reward models (scoring intermediate steps rather than only the final answer) only partially fix the resulting behaviours. The comparison-level mistakes (comparing scores without budget and harness, reading the chain of thought as an explanation, expecting thinking to add knowledge, treating a budget as a hard cap) are on the Reading tab; these are the ones the maths above guards against.

    Reading a pass@1 gain from RL as new capability
    RLVR reliably converts pass@k into pass@1. Measure pass@k at large k against the base model before claiming the model can now solve problems it could not.
    Assuming majority voting always helps
    It amplifies the most likely answer. Where a systematic wrong answer is more likely than the right one, more votes lower accuracy (set p below the wrong answer's share in the selector chart).
    Scaling best-of-n against a reward model without limit
    Past the peak the proxy score keeps rising while true quality falls, and nothing in the proxy signals it.
    Forgetting that sequential tokens are serial
    Doubling the thinking budget doubles the time to the first answer token; no amount of hardware parallelism removes it.

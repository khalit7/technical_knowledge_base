Data quality per parameter: Microsoft Phi

Phi tests one hypothesis: most of a web corpus is noise, so a small model trained on a little extremely high-signal text can match a much larger model trained on the raw web. "Textbook quality" means two things: web documents filtered by a classifier that scores reasoning and pedagogical value rather than fluency, and a corpus that is mostly synthetic, exercises, worked solutions and explanations generated by a larger model so that each example demonstrates a reasoning step rather than stating a fact (how such data is made: {{Synthetic data|n:3c65c17b0d0d8171b5d9e36e05b7a5d0}}). Phi-1 and Phi-2 showed the effect at 1B to 3B ({{Phi-2, 2.7B|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/phi-2-the-surprising-power-of-small-language-models/}}); Phi-4 (14B, December 2024) reached 84.8% on MMLU and beat considerably larger models on maths ({{Phi-4 technical report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.08905}}), followed by Phi-4-mini (3.8B) and the Phi-4-reasoning variants ({{Phi-4-reasoning|https://huggingface.co/microsoft/Phi-4-reasoning}}).

The cost of the bet. A model trained on benchmark-shaped synthetic tasks is strongest on benchmark-shaped tasks: Phi is consistently weaker on long-tail world knowledge, on messy real inputs and on anything phrased unlike a textbook exercise, and because the generator has itself seen the evaluations, contamination is hard to rule out. Used as intended it remains the default dense family for local and edge deployment in 2026, under MIT, as permissive as licensing gets ({{Phi-4 on Hugging Face|https://huggingface.co/microsoft/phi-4}}). Where Phi-4 sits on memory and compute is in the two-lever chart below.

Other small dense models, and Microsoft's in-house frontier line (volatile, as of 2 November 2026)

OpenBMB MiniCPM5-2B (September 2026) is a dense 2.5B Apache 2.0 on-device model averaging 53.9 across 34 benchmarks, the lab's own figure ({{MiniCPM5-2B|https://huggingface.co/openbmb/MiniCPM5-2B}}). Hugging Face SmolLM3 (3B) sits at the same size and is valued for its documentation; it is under open process.

Microsoft's MAI team trains in-house frontier models to reduce dependence on OpenAI. MAI-Base-2 (described 15 October 2026) is a base model, not yet callable: a mixture of experts in every layer with about 80B active parameters and a vision module, trained on 50T tokens (about a fifth of them for vision) for 38 days on 16,000 GB200s with the Muon optimiser. Coverage disagrees on its total size (2T in SiliconANGLE, 2.8T elsewhere), and Microsoft's claim of 4.2 to 12.3 times less pre-training compute than DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Base is a scaling-law extrapolation, not a controlled comparison. Post-training is under way and the weights are to be licensed to "trusted partners" through Azure, with no price or date ({{SiliconANGLE|https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/16/microsoft-reveals-cutting-edge-mai-base-2-model-with-2t-parameters/}}).

Microsoft's small-model side also ships agents: Mage-VL-Cowork (October 2026, Apache 2.0) is a 4B vision-language policy that chooses between GUI actions and shell commands with no external orchestrator, at 66.0% on OSWorld-Verified with a 50-step budget, a two-run average Microsoft reports as the best among 4B models ({{model card|https://huggingface.co/microsoft/Mage-VL-Cowork}}).

Memory per token: NVIDIA Nemotron and the hybrid camp

NVIDIA ships open models as demand generation for GPUs: anything that makes it easier to build with language models sells more of them, so models, datasets and recipes are given away ({{NVIDIA Nemotron|https://developer.nvidia.com/nemotron}}). The design choice that distinguishes the Nemotron 3 line (Nano, Super, Ultra) and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, per their model cards, is that they are Mamba-2 hybrids rather than transformers. A state-space layer carries a fixed-size state through the sequence instead of a cache that grows with it; Mamba-2 makes the recurrence selective (its parameters are computed from the current input, so the layer chooses what to keep) and trains it as structured matrix multiplications on the tensor cores ({{Mamba|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752}}, {{Mamba-2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21060}}). A few attention layers stay because a compressed state is bad at exact recall, the same argument MiniMax reached from the other direction. How these layers work in general, and where the state against KV crossover falls for other models: {{Beyond attention|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8142bc9ecac1fc366966}}.

The number that matters is the cache. Only attention layers store a key and a value for every past token. Nemotron 3 Nano has 6 attention layers among 52 (23 Mamba-2, 23 MoE); Qwen3-30B-A3B, the transformer with the same 30B total and 3B active, has 48. So per token Nano keeps 6 × 2 × 128 × 2 × 2 = 6 KiB against Qwen's 48 × 4 × 128 × 2 × 2 = 96 KiB. Predict before it plays: at a short prompt, which holds more memory?

  1. Two stacks, layer by layer. Qwen3-30B-A3B (back row) has 48 layers, each an attention block followed by a mixture-of-experts block. Nemotron 3 Nano (front row) has 52: 23 Mamba-2 (green), 23 MoE (grey, which cache nothing) and only 6 attention layers (blue). Both are 30B-class models with about 3B active.
  2. Token 1. Every attention layer stores a key and a value for every token it has seen: 4 key-value heads × 128 numbers × 2 (key and value) × 2 bytes = 2 KiB per layer for Qwen, 96 KiB per token across 48 layers; with 2 key-value heads Nano's attention layers take 1 KiB each, 6 KiB across six. Each Mamba-2 layer instead sets up a fixed state at the first token, 64 heads × 64 × 128 numbers in float32 (2 MiB) plus a 36 KiB convolution buffer: 46.8 MiB across the 23. The dots are the new keys and values streaming in.
  3. 512 tokens: about level. Qwen holds 48 MiB, Nano 49.8 MiB: for a short prompt the fixed state is the larger cost. The two are level at about 533 tokens (the 47 MiB state divided by the 90 KiB per-token difference); past that the transformer's cache grows 16 times faster (96 KiB a token against 6 KiB).
  4. 4,096 tokens. 384 MiB against 70.8 MiB, 5.4× less. The green columns have not moved; only the six attention layers grow, so they shrink against the new tallest column.
  5. 131,072 tokens, the longest Qwen validates (with YaRN): 12 GiB per sequence against 814.8 MiB, 15.1× less.
  6. 1,048,576 tokens, Nano's supported maximum (arithmetic only for Qwen, which is not validated this far): 96 GiB, more than an entire 80 GB H100, against 6.05 GiB. The ratio approaches 96 ÷ 6 = 16, the per-token ratio, because the fixed state stops mattering at long context: what decides it is how few layers keep a cache at all.
  7. What fits on one H100. At 131,072 tokens, after the BF16 weights (61 GB for Qwen, 63 GB for Nano) one 80 GB H100 has about 19 GB and 17 GB left: room for 1 sequence of Qwen's cache, against 19 sequences of Nano's. "Sequences that fit" is arithmetic (free memory ÷ cache per sequence) and ignores activations and framework overhead.

Layer counts, heads and state sizes are from each config.json ({{Qwen3-30B-A3B|https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-30B-A3B/blob/main/config.json}}, {{Nemotron 3 Nano|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-BF16/blob/main/config.json}}); caches in BF16 (2 bytes), Mamba state in float32 as the config sets it; one column per layer, all on one scale that is reset at each step (the tallest column is named on the stage). Defaults reproduce the claim that a hybrid's cache falls "from hundreds of KiB to a few KiB", independently: Llama 3.1 70B (80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 × 2 × 2) gives 320 KiB, Qwen3-30B-A3B 96 KiB, Nemotron 3 Nano 6 KiB, Nemotron 3 Super 8 KiB, IBM Granite 4.0-H-Small 16 KiB, all from config.json (src/recompute.py).

Why it matters. For agentic serving, where thousands of long sequences must be resident at once and memory rather than FLOPs is the limit, a cache 15 times smaller changes deployment economics more than a few benchmark points do. The fixed state is the price; the saving comes from how few layers keep a per-token cache at all.

Four other labs bet on the same constraint, each for its own buyer:

  • IBM Granite 4 (from October 2025): small hybrid Mamba/transformer enterprise models from 3B to 32B under Apache 2.0; Granite 4.0-H-Small is 32.2B with 4 attention layers among 40 ({{model card and config|https://huggingface.co/ibm-granite/granite-4.0-h-small}}). IBM sells the compliance around it, under auditability.
  • Liquid AI LFM: deliberately non-transformer models for phones and laptops, where RAM and battery rather than throughput are the limits. LFM blocks are short-range gated convolutions with a few attention layers, so most of the network mixes information locally at fixed cost and memory does not grow with context as a full KV cache does. The largest, LFM2.5-24B-A2B (13 October 2026), is 24B total and 2B active ({{Liquid AI|https://www.liquid.ai/blog/lfm2-5-24b-a2b}}); it is the most credible part of the post-transformer fringe, alongside the SSM hybrids and the text-diffusion experiments, of which Inception's Mercury 3 (19 October 2026) is the largest commercial one: a 1M-token context, 1,068 tokens per second on NVIDIA GPUs by Inception's own figure, $0.20 / $0.70 per million tokens, API only, and no published benchmark scores ({{Inception|https://www.inceptionlabs.ai/blog/introducing-mercury-3}}).
  • AI21 Jamba combines Mamba layers, attention layers and mixture-of-experts in one architecture, one of the first to ship very long context at a memory footprint a single node could hold (Jamba 1.5 Large, 399B, August 2024, {{card|https://huggingface.co/ai21labs/AI21-Jamba-Large-1.5}}).
  • TII Falcon (Abu Dhabi) was one of the earliest credible open-weights providers, with Falcon 40B and 180B and the RefinedWeb corpus, which showed heavily filtered web data could replace curated corpora ({{RefinedWeb|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01116}}). Its current Falcon-H models are hybrid SSMs up to 34B under the Falcon LLM licence ({{Falcon-H1-34B|https://huggingface.co/tiiuae/Falcon-H1-34B-Instruct}}).
Nemotron and LFM releases, NVIDIA's reward models and the Hugging Face deal (volatile, as of 2 November 2026)

Nemotron 3: Nano (31.6B total, 3.5B active, 15 December 2025, {{model card|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-BF16}}), Super (120B / 12B, {{card|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B-BF16-MTPv2}}) and Ultra (550B / 55B, June 2026, {{card|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B-A55B-BF16}}). Nemotron 3.5: Lightning (30B / 3B, 11 August 2026, OpenMDW-1.1 licence, {{card|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Lightning-30B-A3B-BF16}}); from 15 October Super VL, an open 120B multimodal agent model with 12B active, and Bolt, a hybrid MoE with 9B of 74B active and native 2-bit weights so that it fits in 24 GB on an RTX card or a DGX Spark. NVIDIA reports about 26T tokens of pre- and continued training for Super VL, 3.8M post-training samples and RL in 100 open environments, and claims it matches its largest reasoning models at a fifth of the size and nearly five times the output speed; those are NVIDIA's figures, and the licence and layer design of the two October models were not stated in the coverage read ({{SiliconANGLE|https://siliconangle.com/2026/10/15/nvidia-expands-nemotron-llm-family-with-hyper-efficient-models-for-agentic-systems/}}). Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe (28 October 2026, OpenMDW-1.1) carries the hybrid into speech: about 30B total and 4B active, a speech encoder in front of a hybrid Mamba-transformer MoE, first among open weights on the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard's English average at 5.33% word error rate ({{Hugging Face|https://huggingface.co/blog/nvidia/nemotron-transcribe-granary}}).

NVIDIA also publishes open post-training datasets and reward models (the HelpSteer line). A reward model scores candidate responses so that reinforcement learning can optimise against it; open ones are rare because they encode a lab's preference data. It also ships Llama-Nemotron distills, Llama bases repackaged with NVIDIA's post-training.

NVIDIA is buying the place most open weights are distributed from: in September 2026 it agreed to acquire Hugging Face for $12.93 billion, closing expected in the first half of 2027 subject to regulatory approval ({{TechCrunch|https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/03/nvidia-confirms-it-will-buy-hugging-face-for-12-9-billion/}}). Jensen Huang has promised that the platform stays open and that Nvidia compute will not be required to use it, which is a commitment rather than a structural guarantee.

Liquid LFM2 and LFM2.5: LFM2-8B-A1B is an 8.3B MoE with 1.5B active; LFM2.5-1.2B appeared in January 2026; both under Liquid's LFM Open License, where quality per FLOP and a small resident footprint decide whether a feature can run locally at all ({{LFM2-8B-A1B|https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-8B-A1B}}, {{LFM2.5-1.2B|https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-1.2B-Instruct}}). LFM2.5-24B-A2B has 40 layers, 64 experts with 4 active, gated short convolutions plus grouped-query attention, 131K context, 33T training tokens, $0.10 / $1.00 per million tokens, or 184 tokens per second on an M5 Max laptop. Liquid's own comparison against Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is mixed: far ahead on AutomationBench (27.69 against 8.72), well behind on SWE-Bench Verified (51.0 against 66.2). LFM3 (27 October 2026, LFM Open License v1.0) puts six 2B-class models (text, vision, audio, omni and two decision models) on one 1.73B backbone of 24 layers: 13 gated short convolutions, 5 sliding-window attention and 6 global attention layers, with a 256K context ({{model card|https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM3-1.7B}}).

Recursive Flint (23 October 2026, Apache 2.0) aims at the same laptop niche from the MoE side: 40B total and 2.5B active, up to 512K context, shipped in NVFP4 (within 0.7 points of 16-bit by Recursive's figure) and run at more than 125 tokens per second on an M4 Pro laptop; Recursive names its harnesses but publishes no scores ({{Recursive|https://recursive.com/updates/introducing-recursive-flint}}).

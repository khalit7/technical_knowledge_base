Data quality per parameter: Microsoft Phi

Phi tests one hypothesis: most of a web corpus is noise, so a small model trained on a little extremely high-signal text can match a much larger model trained on the raw web. "Textbook quality" means two things: web documents filtered by a classifier that scores reasoning and pedagogical value rather than fluency, and a corpus that is mostly synthetic, exercises, worked solutions and explanations generated by a larger model so that each example demonstrates a reasoning step rather than stating a fact (how such data is made: Synthetic data). Phi-1 and Phi-2 showed the effect at 1B to 3B (Phi-2, 2.7B); Phi-4 (14B, December 2024) reached 84.8% on MMLU and beat considerably larger models on maths (Phi-4 technical report), followed by Phi-4-mini (3.8B) and the Phi-4-reasoning variants (Phi-4-reasoning).

The cost of the bet. A model trained on benchmark-shaped synthetic tasks is strongest on benchmark-shaped tasks: Phi is consistently weaker on long-tail world knowledge, on messy real inputs and on anything phrased unlike a textbook exercise, and because the generator has itself seen the evaluations, contamination is hard to rule out. Used as intended it remains the default dense family for local and edge deployment in 2026, under MIT, as permissive as licensing gets (Phi-4 on Hugging Face). Memory per token: NVIDIA Nemotron and the hybrid camp

NVIDIA ships open models as demand generation for GPUs: anything that makes it easier to build with language models sells more of them, so models, datasets and recipes are given away (NVIDIA Nemotron). The design choice that distinguishes the Nemotron 3 line (Nano, Super, Ultra) and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, per their model cards, is that they are Mamba-2 hybrids rather than transformers. A state-space layer carries a fixed-size state through the sequence instead of a cache that grows with it; Mamba-2 makes the recurrence selective (its parameters are computed from the current input, so the layer chooses what to keep) and trains it as structured matrix multiplications on the tensor cores (Mamba, Mamba-2). A few attention layers stay because a compressed state is bad at exact recall, the same argument MiniMax reached from the other direction. How these layers work in general, and where the state against KV crossover falls for other models: Beyond attention.

The number that matters is the cache. Only attention layers store a key and a value for every past token. Nemotron 3 Nano has 6 attention layers among 52 (23 Mamba-2, 23 MoE); Qwen3-30B-A3B, the transformer with the same 30B total and 3B active, has 48. So per token Nano keeps 6 × 2 × 128 × 2 × 2 = 6 KiB against Qwen's 48 × 4 × 128 × 2 × 2 = 96 KiB. Predict before you play: at a short prompt, which holds more memory?

The hybrid starts heavier, then its cache grows 16 times slower

One sequence grows from 1 to 1,048,576 tokens through both models, per layer, to one scale; it waits until you press play or step. Layer counts, heads and state sizes are from each config.json (Qwen3-30B-A3B, Nemotron 3 Nano); caches in BF16 (2 bytes), Mamba state in float32 as the config sets it. "Sequences that fit" is arithmetic (free memory ÷ cache per sequence) and ignores activations and framework overhead. Defaults reproduce the claim that a hybrid's cache falls "from hundreds of KiB to a few KiB", independently: Llama 3.1 70B (80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 × 2 × 2) gives 320 KiB, Qwen3-30B-A3B 96 KiB, Nemotron 3 Nano 6 KiB, Nemotron 3 Super 8 KiB, IBM Granite 4.0-H-Small 16 KiB, all from config.json (src/recompute.py).

What it shows. At a short prompt the hybrid holds more, because 23 Mamba-2 layers each set up a fixed 2 MiB state; the two are level at about 533 tokens. Past that the transformer's cache grows 16 times faster, and at 131,072 tokens it is 12 GiB per sequence against 0.8 GiB. For agentic serving, where thousands of long sequences must be resident at once and memory rather than FLOPs is the limit, that changes deployment economics more than a few benchmark points do. The fixed state is the price; the saving comes from how few layers keep a per-token cache at all.

Auditability and on-prem: Cohere and IBM

Cohere sells to enterprises that will not send their data to a public API, and its design choices follow from that (Cohere research).

  • Grounded generation with citations. Command R and R+ were the first models explicitly post-trained so that, given retrieved documents in context, the model answers from them and emits inline references to the passages it used: a reviewer can check each claim against its source and an unsupported sentence becomes visible. That objective differs from ordinary instruction tuning, and it made them the default RAG models for a period (Command R+ was 104B under CC-BY-NC 4.0, Cohere).
  • Sized to the customer's server. Command A (March 2025) is 111B and dense: Cohere's card says it is "deployable on just two GPUs" (Command A model card). A dense model has predictable memory and no router, where an MoE of equal quality needs every expert resident and a router that complicates multi-GPU serving. The arithmetic adds a caveat the card leaves out: 111B × 2 bytes = 222 GB in 16-bit, more than two 80 GB cards hold, so "two GPUs" holds at 8-bit (111 GB). The flagship has since moved to Command A+ (May 2026), a 218B total, 25B active MoE under Apache 2.0 (Command A+ model card), so Cohere now accepts the MoE serving cost too.
  • Multilingual data from people, not translation. Aya (Cohere Labs: Aya 23, Aya Expanse, Aya Vision) builds instruction data across languages through a large annotator community rather than by machine-translating English datasets, which is what usually leaves "multilingual" models fluent but culturally hollow.
  • Agents inside the customer's network. North is the secure agent platform, running against internal systems in the customer's environment. Its single-task specialist, North Small Translate (September 2026), is a 218B total, 25B active MoE built only for translation, a reminder that specialists at mixture-of-experts scale are still economically viable against generalists.

IBM Granite runs on the same architectural logic as Nemotron Nano (above: a constant-size state for most layers lets long documents be processed on modest hardware). What IBM adds is the compliance surface: an ISO-certified governance process for how the models were built, published training-data provenance, and indemnification, which is what actually unblocks a bank or a hospital (IBM Granite). Its function-calling quality per parameter is strong, which matters because the enterprise use is structured tool calls rather than open-ended chat. It is chosen for the paperwork as much as for the model.

Bits per weight move a model left and right; active parameters move it up and down

Weights in memory = parameters × bits ÷ 8 (decimal GB); the KV cache and activations come on top. Compute per token ≈ 2 × active parameters FLOPs, the standard estimate for a forward pass. Red lines are single-card or single-node memory: RTX 5090 32 GB, H100 80 GB, B200 180 GB (a DGX B200's 1,440 GB ÷ 8), 8 × B200. Picking a model with a published hardware claim switches to the precision that claim implies and checks it. Defaults reproduce Prism's "5.9 GB" and "nine times smaller" for Bonsai 2 by construction (27B × 1.76 ÷ 8 = 5.94 GB; 16 ÷ 1.76 = 9.09), Cohere's W4A4 North Small Translate at 218 × 4 ÷ 8 = 109 GB (one B200 or two H100s), and Tencent's 1.56 TB for Hy4 at about 2.03 bytes per parameter, which shows that release is essentially all 16-bit.

What it shows. The levers are independent: North Small Translate stores 218B parameters but computes like a 25B model, and at 4 bits fits one B200; Bonsai 2 keeps all 27B active but stores them in 6 GB.

Distribution and billing: Amazon Nova and Bedrock

Nova is Amazon's closed in-house family, available only through Bedrock. The first generation (December 2024) was a capability ladder, Micro, Lite, Pro and Premier, differing in size, latency and price, so an application can route cheap turns to Micro and hard ones to Pro without changing vendor. Two siblings make distinct design choices. Nova Sonic is speech-to-speech: audio in, audio out, with no transcribe, generate, synthesise pipeline, which removes the latency of three sequential models and keeps the prosody, interruption and tone that a text bottleneck discards. Nova Act is trained to drive a browser, emitting UI actions (click, type, scroll) against a page rather than prose about it. The Nova 2 generation (from December 2025) is current, with Premier retired in favour of Nova 2 Lite (AWS: Nova 2 Lite, Bedrock model catalog 2026).

Nova is competent mid-tier and priced to undercut; Amazon's real position is elsewhere. Bedrock is the aggregator: one API, one bill, one IAM policy and one VPC boundary across models from Anthropic, Meta, Mistral, Cohere and others, which is what large enterprises buy. Trainium, Amazon's training accelerator, is the compute behind Project Rainier for Anthropic, which makes Amazon a supplier of an alternative to Nvidia rather than only a customer. Microsoft's Azure plays the same game, serving everyone's models regardless of its own: the strategy is to own the distribution layer and treat the model layer as replaceable. NVIDIA's Hugging Face purchase (above) is the open-weights version of it.

NaiveAI: the same base with no full attention at all

Naive-N0.5-Flash (27 September 2026, MIT) is 309B total / 15.5B active with a 1M-token context, built on Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.5 base (Hugging Face, NaiveAI research). Its design choice is the attention: at a million tokens the base's few global-attention layers dominate decoding, so NaiveAI replaced each of them with DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA), in which a lightweight indexer scores the whole history and the layer attends only to the selected tokens (how DSA works: Attention variants). Adapting the model to the new layers was one objective of continued pretraining.

Before and after: the nine global layers become sparse, so no layer reads the whole context

Eight six-layer modules, each five sliding-window layers then one global layer, with the first layer of the first module also global: 39 sliding-window and 9 DSA layers across 48 in Naive-N0.5-Flash, against 39 sliding-window and 9 full-attention layers in MiMo-V2.5 (MiMo-V2.5 card; positions from NaiveAI's card, which states the global layers were replaced). SWA uses a 128-token window; DSA selects the top 2,048 tokens. NaiveAI also swaps DSA's original MLA for grouped-query attention with four KV groups. The indexer still scans the full history and the full KV cache is kept: sparse attention cuts attention compute and memory reads, not the cache.

Openness itself: Ai2 OLMo, the fully open recipe

"Open weights" and "open source" are used interchangeably and are not the same thing. A typical open-weights release (Llama, Qwen, Mistral, DeepSeek) gives you one artefact: the final parameter tensors, a model card and a table of scores; everything that produced them stays inside the lab. OLMo, from the Allen Institute for AI (Ai2), established the practice of releasing the production process rather than the product (OLMo 1 paper: "unlike most prior efforts that have only released model weights and inference code, we release OLMo alongside open training data and training and evaluation code"). Ai2 calls the whole path the model flow: "every stage, checkpoint, dataset, and dependency required to create and modify it" (Olmo 3 blog). It was the only line doing so until September 2026, when IFM's K2 Horizon joined it (below): two members on two continents under different funders, a norm forming rather than a curiosity. What OLMo ships that others withhold, with the Olmo 3 32B release as the example:

1. The corpus and its tooling

The pretraining corpus (Dolma) as downloadable documents, plus the deduplication, filtering, decontamination and tokenisation pipeline, so the corpus can be rebuilt or varied, not merely inspected. Olmo 3's pool is 9.31T tokens; heuristic web filtering took 252.6B documents to 38.7B, removing 84.9%, in about 1,030 hours on EC2 instances (Olmo 3 report, Tables 4 and 36).

2. The mixture and its order

The proportions of each source and the curriculum across stages, the part labs guard hardest: two models with identical architectures and different mixtures are different models. Drawn in the Inspect an open run tab. The released mixes are "reconstructions of the data sampled during our actual training runs" (report, note to Table 4).

3. Training code and full configs

So a run can be reproduced rather than approximated: the OLMo-core trainer, open-instruct for post-training, OLMES for evaluation, and every stage's batch size, learning rate and parallelism (report, Tables 34 and 35).

4. Intermediate checkpoints

The most valuable item and the rarest. The Olmo 3 32B base repository has 718 checkpoint branches: 655 from pretraining, 49 from midtraining, 14 from long-context extension (Hugging Face, counted 1 October 2026). An open-weights release has one.

5. Training logs

Loss curves, spikes, restarts and what was changed when; the failures are as informative as the final number and almost never published. Ai2's Weights & Biases projects are public, 69 runs for Olmo 3 32B alone (W&B ai2-llm).

6. The post-training stack

Tulu 3 for OLMo 2, Dolci for Olmo 3: the SFT datasets, preference data, RL prompt sets and recipe code (OlmoRL). Dolci "provides separate mixes for each stage of post-training: SFT, DPO, and RLVR" (Olmo 3 blog).

What the extra artefacts let you do

None of this matters if you only run inference; it matters for research. Pick a question to see which artefacts it needs and whether an open-weights release could answer it.

The two that matter most: contamination becomes checkable, because without the corpus every benchmark number is unfalsifiable in principle, and here you can grep for the eval set (decontamination in general: Decontamination, PII and governance); and interventions get a real control, because a fork of a released OLMo checkpoint with the released mixture is a controlled experiment, while fine-tuning someone's closed-corpus weights is not.

The price: capability

Publishing your corpus means you cannot use data you have no clear right to publish, and that alone caps OLMo against labs that train on whatever they can reach; Ai2 also does not "collect from sites that explicitly disallow it" (Olmo 3 blog). On Artificial Analysis's index the gap is wide, though the bars mix that constraint with size and age:

What the open runs teach (1): OLMo 2's stability fixes, checked from the logs

OLMo 2 (7B, 13B and 32B, trained on 4T, 5.6T and 6.6T tokens in total; OLMo 2 report) documented training-stability fixes other labs treat as tribal knowledge: reordered normalisation (the norm moves from the input of the attention and feed-forward blocks to their output, so the residual stream stays unnormalised) and QK-norm (queries and keys normalised before their dot product, which bounds the attention logits whose unbounded growth is a standard cause of mid-run divergence), alongside a z-loss term, a plain normal initialisation, AdamW epsilon 10⁻⁸, no weight decay on embeddings and filtering of repeated n-grams. What the open runs teach (2): save the best data for the end

OLMo 2 and Olmo 3 pretrain in two stages: bulk pretraining on the broad web mixture (OLMo 2 Mix 1124, 3.9T tokens, over 95% web), then a short anneal on Dolmino, a much smaller high-quality mix (filtered web, FLAN, peS2o papers, Wikipedia, Stack Exchange and a 10.7B-token maths set) fed while the learning rate decays to zero (report, Tables 4, 5 and 13). Tulu 3, the open post-training recipe that shipped with OLMo 2, and the 32B's claim

Tulu 3 shipped alongside as the open post-training recipe, first applied to Llama 3.1 bases: supervised fine-tuning, then DPO (the policy is optimised directly on pairs of preferred and rejected responses, so no separate reward model and no rollouts), then RLVR (the reward is a mechanical check of the final answer, a maths result or unit tests, rather than a learned preference model that style can game), with all data and code (Tulu 3; the stages in depth: Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO, RLVR). The OLMo 2 1B and 32B used GRPO for the RL step (OLMo 2 paper page). The 32B was claimed as the "first fully-open model (all data, code, weights, and details are freely available) to outperform GPT3.5-Turbo and GPT-4o mini" (Ai2, March 2025).

What the open runs teach (3): Olmo 3, a reasoning model from 6x fewer tokens

Instead of a model, Ai2 published the path: "checkpoints from each major training milestone: the base pretrained model, the mid-trained model after targeted skill enhancement, the long-context-extended version, plus post-training checkpoints for the Olmo 3-Think, Olmo 3-Instruct, and Olmo 3-RL Zero flows", so a researcher can "fork the model at whatever point best fits" (Olmo 3 blog). At launch (20 November 2025) that meant Base and Think at 7B and 32B, Instruct at 7B and RL-Zero; Instruct 32B followed with 3.1.

  • Base. Dense, 32 and 64 layers; the 32B uses grouped-query attention (40 query, 8 key-value heads); sliding-window attention on 3 of 4 layers with a 4,096-token window; QK-norm and output norms carried over from OLMo 2 (report, Table 33). Pretrained on Dolma 3 Mix, midtrained on Dolmino, then extended from 8,192 to 65,536 tokens of context on Longmino.
  • Think. SFT on Dolci-Think; then DPO with "delta learning": chosen answers from Qwen3-32B thinking, rejected ones from the much weaker Qwen3-0.6B, because fine-tuning on the strong answers alone hurt (report, Table 21); then RLVR with OlmoRL, whose inflight weight updates and continuous batching made RL "4x more efficient" (blog). The first fully open 32B reasoning model with the entire RL pipeline public.
  • Cost. About 56 days from the start of training to the evaluated Think 32B on a dedicated cluster of 1,024 H100s (report, section 2.4): 1,024 GPUs × 56 days × 24 h = 1,376,256 GPU-hours; × $2 per hour = $2.75M (both the price and the total are the report's).

Ettin, ladder scaling, the testbed and provenance: how the open stack gets used

Ettin: one variable at a time. Ettin (Johns Hopkins with LightOn, July 2025) trains paired encoders and decoders from 17M to 1B parameters on up to 2T tokens with one ModernBERT-style recipe built from open OLMo-family data (DCLM with Dolma 1.7 sources, then Dolmino-style sources for the later phases), with only the attention pattern and objective differing (Ettin paper). Encoders and decoders had never been compared with data, scale, recipe and tokeniser all held fixed, so every earlier comparison confounded the objective with everything else. With only the objective varying, encoders win on classification and retrieval, decoders on generation, and continued training across objectives does not close either gap: "a 400M encoder outperforms a 1B decoder on MNLI, and vice versa for generative tasks". For choosing an embedding backbone against a generative one, the pretraining objective, not size, decides (Ettin paper page; objectives in general: Families and objectives). Ettin also releases 200+ checkpoints and the training order segmented by checkpoint, the OLMo practice carried over.

How the stack gets used.

  • The standard testbed for interventions. Per-stage checkpoints make the family the default base for data ablations, RL variants and interpretability work that needs a training trajectory; OlmoTrace, in Ai2's playground, traces parts of a response back to training documents (blog).
  • Ladder scaling. Fit scaling curves on many small controlled runs to predict a large model's downstream accuracy before paying for it: model and data size predict a loss, the loss predicts task accuracy, and the ladder costs 1% of the compute of the 7B (4T tokens) and 13B (5T tokens) targets it predicts (paper). That needs dozens of runs with a fixed recipe and open data, which only this stack provides.
  • Provenance. US-origin fully open matters politically as well as scientifically: an answer to Chinese open weights for auditable government and regulated deployments, where a buyer who must answer "what is in this model" has an answer. It is no longer the only such answer: K2 Horizon gives Gulf-funded buyers a second fully open provenance chain.

Prime Intellect: training across the internet

INTELLECT models are proofs of concept for decentralised training: one model trained across clusters in different places joined by ordinary internet links rather than a datacentre fabric. Synchronising gradients over such links every step is hopelessly slow, so it needs low-communication algorithms that let workers take many local steps and synchronise rarely. INTELLECT-1 (10B, November 2024, Apache 2.0, with data and checkpoints released) used DiLoCo: each node takes 100 inner steps alone, then all nodes average the change in their weights (the pseudo-gradient, sent as int8) once, and an outer optimiser applies it. Predict: synchronising every step over these links, what share of the time are the GPUs busy?

Before and after: syncing once per 100 steps keeps GPUs busy 83 to 96% of the time

Inputs from the INTELLECT-1 technical report, Table 2: 100 inner steps take 38 minutes on an 8 × H100 node; the median all-reduce of the int8 pseudo-gradient takes 103 s (USA), 382 s (USA and Europe) or 469 s (global). Compute utilisation = 38 min ÷ (38 min + all-reduce). Defaults reproduce the report's 95.7%, 85.6% and 83.0% independently (the global case computes to 82.9%, a rounding difference). The sync-every-step run is this page's extrapolation, labelled as such: the fp32 gradient is 4 times the int8 payload (10B × 4 bytes = 40 GB), and its all-reduce is assumed to take 4 times as long. Communication falls 100 × 4 = 400 times. It waits until you press play or step.

What it shows. Synchronising every step over internet links leaves the GPUs idle about 95% of the time (5.2% utilisation in the USA case); DiLoCo keeps them busy 83 to 96% of the time by synchronising once per 100 steps and sending a quarter of the bytes. The models are not competitive on quality; the point is that frontier-scale training need not be geographically centralised, which matters strategically far more than the checkpoints do. Hugging Face SmolLM3 and the smaller specialists

Hugging Face SmolLM3 (3B, July 2025, Apache 2.0) is valued for its documentation rather than its capability: every stage of the recipe, the data mixture and the ablations are published, making it the reference for how a competent 3B-class model is built (SmolLM3 blog, model). Its one architectural choice to know: NoPE (no positional encoding) in a fraction of layers, which get no explicit position signal and rely on the causal mask alone, a choice associated with better generalisation beyond the trained context (Positional encodings).

  • Nous Research Hermes: post-training specialists who take open bases and produce instruct and reasoning variants with their own data and recipes. They publish datasets and methodology, so the Hermes line is a usable control for "how much of this model's behaviour came from post-training".
  • Upstage Solar popularised depth up-scaling: duplicate a slice of an existing base model's layers to build a deeper network, then continue pretraining so the seams heal, producing a larger model without training one from scratch. SOLAR 10.7B took two copies of a 32-layer base, dropped 8 layers from each, and joined them: 2 × (32 − 8) = 48 layers (SOLAR paper).
  • Reka builds natively multimodal models (text, image, audio and video in one encoder path) from a small team. Reka, AI21 and Upstage are niche but recur in evaluations.

Not a single model: orchestrators and structured-decision models

Two 2026 products from the long tail are sold as models and are not models in the sense the rest of this page uses the word; each moves the question a deployment answers, and the second became a category within a month.

Three things sold behind one endpoint: a model, an orchestrator, a decision model

Structure as each vendor describes it (Sakana, TypeSafe); no routing data or internals are published, so the diagram shows the shape of the call, not measured behaviour.

Sakana AI: Fugu. Fugu Max and Fugu Ultra v2 (10 to 11 September 2026; a first Fugu Ultra appeared in June) are a learned orchestrator: Fugu reads an incoming query, builds an agentic scaffold for it on the fly, and routes the work across a pool of other models behind one OpenAI-compatible API. It was trained by combining fine-tuning, evolutionary search and reinforcement learning, drawing on two ICLR 2026 papers: TRINITY, which assigns participating models Thinker, Worker or Verifier roles, and The Conductor, which learns coordination strategies by RL rather than having them specified (Sakana, 10 min). If a routed ensemble sold as one endpoint holds the Pareto frontier, "which model" stops being the question a deployment answers and "which orchestrator" replaces it, the same boundary shift ARC Prize's Provider Adapter produced from the benchmark side (on Topic: benchmarks). The scaffold rather than the checkpoint is the subject of Topic: agentic-harnesses.

TypeSafe AI: Jev. Jev (early access 15 September 2026) is what TypeSafe calls a "System One model". It does not generate prose: it takes unstructured state and returns type-safe structured values with calibrated confidence scores, sampling outputs in parallel rather than one token at a time, so a type error is impossible by construction rather than caught by validation. Its training objective is the part worth tracking: Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions, optimising for honest probability estimates on structured decisions rather than for human preference, a different objective from both RLHF and RLVR (TypeSafe, 15 min). If a large share of production calls are structured-decision calls wearing a text interface, this is a different product shape, not a smaller model. Everything is vendor-reported with no independent evaluation, and a 1,282-point Hacker News thread the same week claimed prior art on non-autoregressive decision models trained with RL, so the novelty of the category is contested.

The decision-model tier. Within three weeks of Jev, three larger companies shipped the same shape. Cloudflare Clef and Clef-flash (1 October 2026, Apache 2.0) put a rank-256 LoRA and a routing head on frozen Qwen3.8-27B and Qwen3.5-9B backbones with 64K context (Cloudflare). Amazon Strands Decider 2B is an open (Apache 2.0) decision model for Amazon's Strands agent framework. OpenAI's Decisions API (public beta 6 October) puts the shape behind an endpoint, POST /v1/decisions on GPT-6 Luna, billed $0.10 per million input tokens with no output charge (OpenAI: GPT family). None has an independent evaluation yet; the common bet is that routing and classification calls should be priced on input alone.

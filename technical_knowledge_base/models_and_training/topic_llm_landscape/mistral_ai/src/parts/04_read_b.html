Mixtral: Mistral 7B with eight feed-forward networks

Mixtral 8x7B (December 2023) is Mistral 7B's block with one change that mattered and one that is easy to miss ({{Mixtral paper|@mixp}}; {{config.json|@cfgx7}}). The change that mattered: each layer's feed-forward network becomes 8 experts of the same shape plus a router that sends every token to the top 2, mixing their outputs by a softmax over the two kept scores. The one easy to miss: it dropped the sliding window for full attention over 32k tokens. Everything else (width 4,096, 32 layers, 32 query and 8 key-value heads) is Mistral 7B's. Only the feed-forward networks are copied, so "8x7B" is 46.7B total and 12.9B active, not 56B; the sparsity ratio is 3.6. Routing and load balancing in general are on {{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}}.

  1. Mistral 7B: 32 identical blocks. Each block is an attention layer (41.9M parameters, the thin orange slab) under a feed-forward network (176.2M, blue). Slab thickness is to scale, so four fifths of every block is feed-forward. A token climbs all 32.
  2. Inside one block. 32 query heads (orange) share 8 key-value heads (teal), four queries to each, so the cache stores a quarter of what one key-value head per query would. Each key-value head reads only the last 4,096 of the 32,768 positions drawn: the window that the rolling buffer in Fig. 1 holds.
  3. Mixtral: the feed-forward network, eight times. Mixtral keeps the attention exactly (41.9M) but drops the window, so every key-value head reads all 32,768 positions. The feed-forward network becomes 8 experts, each the shape of Mistral 7B's: 3 × 4,096 × 14,336 = 176.2M.
  4. The router keeps 2 of 8. A linear router (4,096 × 8 = 32,768 parameters) scores the eight experts; the top 2 run and a softmax over the two kept scores mixes their outputs. In this layer 352.3M parameters are read and 1,057M sit idle for this token; the next token may pick another pair.
  5. Both models to scale. Mixtral's 32 layers, one attention slab and eight expert slabs each, beside Mistral 7B's tower: 46.7B stored and 12.9B read per token (the blue slabs), against 7.24B. The 0.26B of embeddings are in the sums but not drawn. Memory pays for eight experts, compute for two.

Parameter counts summed from the configs ({{Mistral 7B|@cfg7}}, {{Mixtral 8x7B|@cfgx7}}); they reproduce the Mixtral paper's 47B and 13B independently. Which experts the router picks is illustrative.

What it bought. Mixtral "outperforms or matches Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 across all evaluated benchmarks" at the per-token compute of a 13B model, under Apache 2.0 ({{paper|@mixp}}), which made open sparse mixture-of-experts models mainstream. Mixtral 8x22B (April 2024) scaled the same design to 141B total and 39B active with a 64k context ({{Mistral|@x22}}).

The experts are not topic experts

Mistral's own routing analysis is the finding people still cite. Expert distributions over ArXiv, PubMed, PhilPapers and the rest of The Pile are nearly identical; the router shows "structured syntactic behavior", so tokens such as self in Python or "Question" in English "often get routed through the same expert". The paper's own Figure 8 shows it.

Consecutive tokens are assigned the same expert far more often than chance in the middle and late layers. That matters for serving: a hot pair of experts can over-subscribe one GPU, and the repetition can be exploited for caching. The share of repeats by layer and dataset (the paper's Table 5, against the random baseline) is charted on the {{Mixtral of Experts paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba72af0ac91ddc6b3}}, which owns the routing evidence.

The current line: granular MoE and big dense models side by side

Eight large experts are now the classic baseline, not the frontier: the Chinese open-weight labs moved to hundreds of small experts with an always-on shared one, and Mistral followed. It now ships both architectures and picks per product, which makes it a natural experiment.

  • Mistral Large 3 (December 2025, Apache 2.0): a "granular" mixture of experts, 675B total and 41B active (673B and 39B of language model plus a 2.5B vision encoder), 256k context, image input, trained from scratch on 3,000 Nvidia H200 GPUs ({{Mistral|@m3}}; {{model card|@l3card}}). Its {{params.json|@cfgl3}} is DeepSeek-V3's skeleton: width 7,168, 61 layers, 128 heads with multi-head latent attention (a 512-number latent plus a 64-number position key), first 3 layers dense, one shared expert. The difference is the experts: 128 of width 4,096 with 4 active, where {{DeepSeek-V3|@dsv3}} has 256 of width 2,048 with 8 active, the same routed parameters per layer cut into half as many, twice as wide. So Large 3 is not a scaled-up Mixtral: Mixtral survives in the name "MoE", not in the shape. The FP8 checkpoint runs on one node of eight B200s or H200s, the NVFP4 one on a node of H100s or A100s; at launch it debuted second among open non-reasoning models on LMArena, by Mistral's report.
  • Mistral Small 4 (16 March 2026, Apache 2.0): 119B total, 128 experts with 4 active plus one shared, latent attention with a 256-number latent, 256k context, image input ({{Mistral|@s4}}; {{params.json|@cfgs4}}). It merges three former lines into one checkpoint: instruct (Small), reasoning (Magistral) and agentic coding (Devstral), with a reasoning_effort setting of none or high instead of separate models.
  • Mistral Medium 3.5 (April 2026, modified MIT): the other way, a dense 128B model with grouped-query attention (96 query heads, 8 key-value heads), 256k context, a vision encoder trained from scratch and the same reasoning setting, open weights ({{model card|@m35card}}). Its text {{config|@cfgm35}} is identical to Devstral 2's (width 12,288, 88 layers, feed-forward 28,672): same shape, plus the vision encoder. Mistral reports 77.6% on SWE-bench Verified.
  1. One layer of each, to one scale. Every tile's area is its parameter count. A Large 3 layer (from the fourth on; the first 3 are dense) holds 128 routed experts of 3 × 7,168 × 4,096 = 88.1M plus one shared, 11.55B with its attention (orange); a Small 4 layer holds 128 experts a seventh of the size of Mixtral's (25.2M against 176.2M) plus one shared, 3,275M; a Medium 3.5 layer is one dense block of 3 × 12,288 × 28,672 = 1,057M, 1,384M with attention.
  2. A token in the two MoE layers. Each router keeps 4 of its 128 experts and the shared expert always runs: Large 3 reads 628M of a layer's 11.55B, Small 4 reads 154M of 3,275M, about 5% each. Which four are lit is illustrative.
  3. Medium 3.5 reads everything. One token touches all 1,384M of a dense layer, 2.2 times what it touches in a Large 3 layer, and Medium 3.5 has 88 layers to Large 3's 61.
  4. Whole models, volume to scale. Footprint and height per layer share one scale, so volume is parameters (layers only; embeddings and vision encoders left out). Large 3: 671.5B stored and 38.1B read per token (the solid column, its 3 dense layers at the base); Small 4: 117.9B and 5.6B; Medium 3.5: 121.8B, all read. Small 4 and Medium 3.5 take about the same memory; in the layers a token through Medium 3.5 does 22 times the work.
  5. The cache follows the attention. Latent attention caches one vector per layer, 512 + 64 numbers on Large 3 and 256 + 64 on Small 4; grouped-query attention caches keys and values for 8 heads of 128 in each of Medium 3.5's 88 layers. In 16 bits that is 68.6 KiB, 22.5 KiB and 352 KiB per token, and one 256K sequence holds 17.2 GiB, 5.6 GiB and 88 GiB (the teal columns, height to scale). Cache sizing in general is on {{Long context|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5}}.

Layer sums from the configs ({{Large 3|@cfgl3}}, {{Small 4|@cfgs4}}, {{Medium 3.5|@cfgm35}}) with the formulas of the Weights tab; Large 4 is not drawn (the card gives its expert counts but no widths).

Why both? Memory is priced on total parameters and compute on active ones. Small 4 and Medium 3.5 occupy about the same memory (119B against 128B), but a token through Small 4 touches 6B to 8B parameters and a token through Medium 3.5 touches all 128B, 16 to 21 times more work. Sparse wins when throughput per GPU is the bill; dense keeps every parameter engaged on every token, avoids routing and all-to-all communication, and is simpler to fine-tune and serve, the trade Mistral made for its agentic coding flagship. The cache tells the same story (Fig. 3, step 5): the MoE models use latent attention and the dense one does not; every model is a preset in the {{KV-cache calculator|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5#t-kv}}.

Predict: which bar moves when you change the precision? Memory for the weights is M = Ptotal · s (s bytes per parameter: 2 at BF16, 1 at FP8, 0.5 at 4 bits); forward compute is Ctok ≈ 2 · Pactive FLOPs per token, one multiply and one add per active parameter, ignoring attention over the context.

Which "active" count? Small 4's three figures, summed from the config (depth)

Mistral states Small 4's active parameters three ways: 6B per token "(8B including embedding and output layers)" in its {{announcement|@s4}}, and 6.5B on the {{model card|@s4card}}. Summing the config: the layers alone are 5.56B, 6.10B with the output layer and 6.53B with the vision encoder too, so 6B and 6.5B both reproduce; no sum reaches 8B (everything included is 7.06B). The same convention (output layer counted, input embedding not, since looking up a row is not a multiplication) reproduces Large 3's 39B for the language model exactly (39.0B); adding the vision encoder gives 41.6B against the stated 41B. The Weights tab sums every model every way.

Medium 3.5's dates: weights and API arrived on 28 to 29 April ({{docs|@m35doc}}); Mistral's announcement followed on 22 May, when it replaced Medium 3.1 and Magistral in Mistral's assistant and Devstral 2 in its coding agent ({{Mistral|@m35post}}). Devstral 2 (9 December 2025) is 123B under the modified MIT licence, with Devstral Small 2 at 24B under Apache 2.0, both 256k context, reported at 72.2% and 68.0% on SWE-bench Verified ({{Mistral|@dev2}}); both are retired from the API (Small 2 on 31 March 2026, Devstral 2 on 31 July) with Medium 3.5 as the replacement ({{catalogue|@models}}), and the weights remain on Hugging Face.

Lineage: specialists, then consolidation

The order explains the current shape. In 2024 and 2025 Mistral shipped a model per job: a flagship that started API-only (Mistral Large 1, February 2024; Large 2 in July 2024 released its weights under the research-only Mistral Research License, with a paid licence for commercial self-deployment, {{Mistral|@large2}}), price tiers (Small 3 and 3.1 back on Apache 2.0, Medium 3 on the API), and specialists: Codestral trained for fill-in-the-middle (completing inside a file given both the prefix and the suffix, the shape editor autocomplete needs), Ministral edge models, Pixtral for vision (from September 2024, an image encoder feeding the language decoder so images and text interleave in one context), Voxtral for speech (July 2025), Magistral (June 2025), its first reasoning models, and Devstral (May 2025), tuned for the agent loop of reading a repository, editing files and running tests inside a scaffold. Mistral 3 (2 December 2025) brought Large 3 plus Ministral 3 at 3B, 8B and 14B, each in base, instruct and reasoning variants, all multimodal and Apache 2.0 (85% on AIME 2025 for the 14B reasoning variant, by Mistral's report, {{Mistral|@m3}}); Devstral 2 followed a week later with the Vibe command-line coding agent.

In 2026 the specialists folded back into generalists: Small 4 absorbed Magistral and Devstral behind a reasoning setting, Medium 3.5 replaced them in Mistral's own products, and vision is built into Large 3, Small 4 and Medium 3.5; every Pixtral, Magistral and Devstral version is in the deprecated table of {{Mistral's catalogue|@models}}. Specialists remain only where the job is different in kind: speech, document OCR, formal proofs (Leanstral), safety (Shieldstral) and robotics (Robostral Navigate).

Release notes: every release, dated and sourced (volatile detail; a dot opens its card)

Large 4, a trillion parameters

In July 2026 Mistral opened partner early access to a model Mensch called a "fat but sparse" family ({{Tech Times|@tt}}); on 6 October it became Mistral Large 4 ("Le Chonk"), a public API preview of a natively multimodal mixture of experts covering more than 160 languages and trained on 3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs ({{Mistral|@l4}}). The weights followed on 29 October 2026 as Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B, under the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License, a modified MIT: free to use, fine-tune and sell, except that a model-as-a-service business needs a separate commercial licence from Mistral ({{Mistral|@l4oss}}, {{model card|@l4hf}}). The card fixes the shape: about 1T parameters in total and about 49B active per token (52B counting the embedding and output layers, hence "A52B"), a granular MoE of 128 routed experts with 4 active plus 1 shared, and a 1M-token context ({{model card|@l4hf}}). The attention is the Large 3 recipe pushed for a million tokens, per vLLM's write-up of the config: latent attention (MLA) with a DeepSeek-style sparse indexer (DSA) that keeps the top 2,048 keys per query, and nine of the 72 layers reading layer 35's latent cache instead of writing their own, so 63 layers hold a cache ({{vLLM|@l4vllm}}; the mechanisms are on {{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}} and {{Long context|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5}}). It also ships its own draft model, "DSpark": three MLA layers inside the checkpoint that propose a block of three tokens for speculative decoding ({{model card|@l4hf}}; mechanism on {{Speculative decoding|n:3f35c17b0d0d81e086fde85940ebac1c}}), and an NVFP4 checkpoint for Blackwell GPUs. It is Mistral's strongest model on the Artificial Analysis index by far, and still below the best Chinese open-weight models (scores below).

Large 4's scores, vendor and independent (volatile, as of 2 November 2026)

Mistral's own: DeepSWE 1.1 61.7%, Terminal-Bench 4.0 28.3%, AutomationBench 59.9%, and 3.74 in Surge AI's blind human evaluation against Claude Opus 5's 4.22 and GLM-5.3's 3.60 ({{Mistral|@l4}}). Independent: Artificial Analysis scores the preview 38 on Intelligence Index v4.3 at $1.13 per index task, listing a 524K context ({{Artificial Analysis|@aal4}}); Vals AI's fixed mini-swe-agent harness gives 27.78% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 against 65.15% for Claude Opus 5.5 ({{Vals AI|@valstb}}). Those are preview readings. For the open-weight release, Artificial Analysis measures 26 on its Intelligence Index v5 (a new scale introduced on 27 October 2026, not comparable with the v4.3 figure) at $2.06 per index task ({{Artificial Analysis|@aal4}}); Mistral, citing Artificial Analysis, reports 41% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 (28% for the preview) and 48% on the Coding Agent Index, about level with GLM-5.3 ({{Mistral|@l4oss}}). vLLM measures about 2.9 accepted draft tokens per DSpark step and about twice the output throughput on eight-way tensor parallelism, its figures on its own benchmark ({{vLLM|@l4vllm}}).

The 2026 specialists: Shieldstral, Robostral Navigate, Leanstral, speech and OCR (depth)

Shieldstral 1.0 (4 August 2026, Apache 2.0): a guard model (text and image input, 32k context, public preview) that takes a safety policy written in natural language as a question and answers yes or no, covering prompt moderation, response moderation, prompt-response pairs and refusal detection ({{model card|@shield}}). Because the policy is an input rather than a fixed list of harm categories trained into the weights, a policy change needs no retraining, and because it is a separate model, the policy lives outside the generator. Mistral says it "matches models up to 7x its size on text safety" and sets a new state of the art on multimodal moderation ({{Mistral|@shieldn}}); it does not win every text benchmark (85.0 on BeaverTails against 85.9 for Qwen3Guard-8B, {{Hugging Face|@shieldhf}}). Size: 3.8B on the docs card, "3B" in the post and the repository name (built on Ministral 3 3B).

Robostral Navigate (8 July 2026, licence not named): an 8B model that takes images from a single RGB camera plus a plain-language instruction ("leave the lobby, walk through the corridor, enter the supply room and stop to face the second shelf") and outputs navigation actions: a target point in the image with an orientation, or a displacement in the robot's frame. Trained entirely in simulation on about 2.4 million trajectories across 350,000 scenes, then refined with online reinforcement learning; Mistral reports 76.6% success on unseen environments of the R2R-CE vision-and-language navigation benchmark ({{Mistral|@robo}}). The announcement names no licence and links no weights.

Leanstral (March 2026) and Leanstral 1.5 (July 2026), Apache 2.0: open-weight Lean 4 proof generation built on the Small 4 family, 119B total and 6B active by the blog (6.5B on the card) ({{Leanstral|@lean}}; {{Leanstral 1.5|@lean15}}; {{Hugging Face|@leanhf}}). Leanstral 1.5 solves 587 of 672 PutnamBench problems by Mistral's report. Both versions are retired from the API (30 June and 30 September 2026); the weights remain.

Speech and OCR: Voxtral TTS (March 2026, open weights under a non-commercial licence) and the Voxtral transcription models; OCR 4 (June 2026), replaced by OCR 4.1 in July.

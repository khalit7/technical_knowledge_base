The idea in one screen

Mistral is a fast follower: it takes an efficiency idea that already works elsewhere (a sliding window with shared key-value heads, sparse experts, then DeepSeek's latent attention and fine-grained experts), ships it early at a size people can run on their own hardware, and decides what you may do with each model by putting it on one rung of a four-rung licence ladder.

Mistral AI is a Paris lab founded in April 2023 by Arthur Mensch (from Google DeepMind) and Guillaume Lample and Timothée Lacroix (both from Meta) (Wikipedia). Its bet is that a cheaper model with open weights and EU jurisdiction wins the deals raw benchmark position does not: sovereign and defence work, regulated on-premises installs, anyone who cannot send tokens to a US API. Mensch, July 2026: "we do not yet own the best language models, but we've constantly reduced that gap" (Tech Times). It is the European counterweight to the Chinese open-weight labs, alongside Ai2 and the Institute of Foundation Models (the page's own positioning, not a sourced ranking); how it compares with every other lab is on the landscape root.

You need: grouped-query attention, sliding windows and latent attention from Attention variants; total against active parameters and routing from Mixture of experts; GRPO from Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO, RLVR. This page says what Mistral chose and what it bought; those pages teach the mechanisms. Whole-model cache sizing at any context is in the KV-cache calculator, where every Mistral model is a preset.

The rolling buffer stops at 4,096 positions; the other two keep growing

"Full MHA" is a hypothetical Mistral 7B with 32 key-value heads. Bytes assume 16-bit keys and values. Defaults reproduce the paper's 8x saving at 32k and its 131K "theoretical attention span" (32 × 4,096 = 131,072, sixteen times the 8,192-token training context) independently.

Mistral 7B: a sliding window and shared key-value heads

Mistral 7B (September 2023) attacked the KV cache twice. Grouped-query attention: 32 query heads share 8 key-value heads, so the cache stores a quarter of what multi-head attention (MHA) would. Sliding-window attention: each layer attends only to the last W = 4,096 tokens, implemented as a rolling buffer that overwrites the token that just left the window. Windows stack, so after k layers a token can be influenced by about k · W earlier tokens, the way stacked convolutions grow a receptive field; anything beyond the window arrives only through what the intermediate states carried, so exact long-range recall is weaker than with full attention. Both mechanisms, and the reach stepper, are on Attention variants; here is what they bought on Mistral's own shape.

Concretely (Mistral 7B paper, Table 1; config.json): width 4,096, 32 layers, head dimension 128, feed-forward width 14,336, 32 query heads, 8 key-value heads, window 4,096, context length 8,192, vocabulary 32,000, Apache 2.0. One token leaves 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 131,072 bytes, 128 KiB, of 16-bit keys and values. At 32,768 tokens a full cache would need 4 GiB; the rolling buffer holds only min(32,768, 4,096) positions, 512 MiB: "On a sequence length of 32k tokens, this reduces the cache memory usage by 8x, without impacting the model quality" (paper).   1. Tokens 1 to 4,096 arrive. Each token leaves one key and one value in each of 32 layers, for 8 key-value heads of 128 numbers: 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 bytes = 131,072 bytes, 128 KiB. Until the window is full the rolling buffer fills like any cache: 4,096 tokens, 512 MiB. One square is 32 MiB, 256 positions.
  2. Token 4,097 overwrites token 1. Position i (counting from 0) is written to slot i mod W. Position 4,096 lands in slot 4,096 mod 4,096 = 0, over the oldest token, which has just left every layer's window. The buffer is a fixed ring and never holds more than 4,096 positions.
  3. To 8,192 tokens, the context Mistral 7B was trained on. The second lap overwrites every slot (colour shows the lap that wrote it). Still 512 MiB; the dashed outline is what a cache without the window would hold by now, 1 GiB.
  4. To 32,768 tokens. Eight laps. Without a window the cache would hold 32,768 positions, 4 GiB; the rolling buffer still holds 4,096, 512 MiB. That is the 8x saving the paper reports for a 32k sequence.
  5. How far back can the last token reach? Each layer looks back 4,096 positions, but layer 2 reads layer-1 states that already summarise the 4,096 before them, like stacked convolutions. After k layers the reach is about k × 4,096: 8 layers cover all 32,768, and 32 layers reach 131,072, the paper's "theoretical attention span". It is an upper bound: anything beyond the window arrives only through what the intermediate states carried, so exact long-range recall is weaker than with full attention.
  6. The two savings multiply. GQA divides the per-token cost by 4 (8 key-value heads instead of 32); the window caps the positions at 4,096, a factor of 8 at this length. Together: 512 MiB against 16 GiB for full multi-head attention, 32 in all. Full MHA with no window would need more than the model's own 14.5 GB of weights.
  1. Tokens 1 to 4,096 arrive. Mixtral kept Mistral 7B's attention shape (32 query heads sharing 8 key-value heads), so a token still costs 128 KiB, but it dropped the window. The first 4,096 tokens look exactly like the rolling buffer: 512 MiB.
  2. Token 4,097 is appended. Without a window nothing is overwritten: position 4,096 gets a new slot and the cache keeps growing by 128 KiB per token.
  3. To 8,192 tokens. 1 GiB, twice the rolling buffer.
  4. To 32,768 tokens. 4 GiB, the "without the window" line of the worked example: 32,768 × 128 KiB. Mixtral supports "a fully dense context length of 32k tokens".
  5. How far back can the last token reach? Every layer reads every earlier position directly, so recall is exact at any distance inside the context. That is what the window gave up, and why Mixtral and the field moved away from windowing every layer.
  6. Compared with the other two. Eight times the rolling buffer at 32k, a quarter of full multi-head attention. GQA alone saves the factor of 4 at every length; the window's saving grows with the sequence.
  1. Tokens 1 to 4,096 arrive. A hypothetical Mistral 7B with one key-value head per query head: 2 × 32 × 32 × 128 × 2 = 524,288 bytes, 512 KiB per token. 4,096 tokens already need 2 GiB.
  2. Token 4,097 is appended. No window and no sharing: every token keeps 32 heads of keys and values in every layer.
  3. To 8,192 tokens. 4 GiB.
  4. To 32,768 tokens. 16 GiB: four times the full GQA cache, and more than the 14.5 GB the model's weights take in 16-bit. Decoding reads this whole cache for every new token, which is why the cache, not the arithmetic, limits throughput.
  5. How far back can the last token reach? Every layer reads every earlier position directly.
  6. Compared with the other two. Thirty-two times the rolling buffer with GQA: the factor of 4 from sharing key-value heads times the factor of 8 from the window at 32k.

In symbols: btok = 2 · L · nkv · dh · s bytes per token (key and value, layers, key-value heads, head dimension, bytes per number), and the buffer holds M(n) = btok · min(n, W); the reach bound is R(k) ≈ k · W, an upper bound on influence, not a guarantee the information survived. The general formula and every model's cache are on Long context.

What it bought. Mistral 7B outperformed "the best open 13B model (Llama 2) across all evaluated benchmarks, and the best released 34B model (Llama 1) in reasoning, mathematics, and code generation" (paper), which established the lab, and it became a common small base for fine-tuning (unconfirmed as "the default"). What did not last: Mistral itself dropped the window in Mixtral, which supports "a fully dense context length of 32k tokens" (Mixtral paper); every current Mistral model uses full grouped-query or latent attention. Treat the window as a 2023 Mistral choice, not a family trait.

Parameter count and the 131K reach, checked (depth)

Mistral's announcement calls it a 7.3B-parameter model (Mistral); summing the table gives 7.24B (32 layers of 218.1M plus 0.26B of input and output embeddings), about 14.5 GB at 16 bits.

The 131K reach does not mean Mistral 7B handles 131K-token inputs: it is a theoretical bound on how far information can propagate through 32 windowed layers, and the model was trained on 8,192-token contexts.

Mixtral: Mistral 7B with eight feed-forward networks

Mixtral 8x7B (December 2023) is Mistral 7B's block with one change that mattered and one that is easy to miss (Mixtral paper; config.json). The change that mattered: each layer's feed-forward network becomes 8 experts of the same shape plus a router that sends every token to the top 2, mixing their outputs by a softmax over the two kept scores. The one easy to miss: it dropped the sliding window for full attention over 32k tokens. Everything else (width 4,096, 32 layers, 32 query and 8 key-value heads) is Mistral 7B's. Only the feed-forward networks are copied, so "8x7B" is 46.7B total and 12.9B active, not 56B; the sparsity ratio is 3.6. Routing and load balancing in general are on Mixture of experts.

  1. Mistral 7B: 32 identical blocks. Each block is an attention layer (41.9M parameters, the thin orange slab) under a feed-forward network (176.2M, blue). Slab thickness is to scale, so four fifths of every block is feed-forward. A token climbs all 32.
  2. Inside one block. 32 query heads (orange) share 8 key-value heads (teal), four queries to each, so the cache stores a quarter of what one key-value head per query would. Each key-value head reads only the last 4,096 of the 32,768 positions drawn: the window that the rolling buffer in Fig. 1 holds.
  3. Mixtral: the feed-forward network, eight times. Mixtral keeps the attention exactly (41.9M) but drops the window, so every key-value head reads all 32,768 positions. The feed-forward network becomes 8 experts, each the shape of Mistral 7B's: 3 × 4,096 × 14,336 = 176.2M.
  4. The router keeps 2 of 8. A linear router (4,096 × 8 = 32,768 parameters) scores the eight experts; the top 2 run and a softmax over the two kept scores mixes their outputs. In this layer 352.3M parameters are read and 1,057M sit idle for this token; the next token may pick another pair.
  5. Both models to scale. Mixtral's 32 layers, one attention slab and eight expert slabs each, beside Mistral 7B's tower: 46.7B stored and 12.9B read per token (the blue slabs), against 7.24B. The 0.26B of embeddings are in the sums but not drawn. Memory pays for eight experts, compute for two.

Parameter counts summed from the configs (Mistral 7B, Mixtral 8x7B); they reproduce the Mixtral paper's 47B and 13B independently. Which experts the router picks is illustrative.

What it bought. Mixtral "outperforms or matches Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 across all evaluated benchmarks" at the per-token compute of a 13B model, under Apache 2.0 (paper), which made open sparse mixture-of-experts models mainstream. Mixtral 8x22B (April 2024) scaled the same design to 141B total and 39B active with a 64k context (Mistral).

The experts are not topic experts

Mistral's own routing analysis is the finding people still cite. Expert distributions over ArXiv, PubMed, PhilPapers and the rest of The Pile are nearly identical; the router shows "structured syntactic behavior", so tokens such as self in Python or "Question" in English "often get routed through the same expert". The paper's own Figure 8 shows it.

Consecutive tokens are assigned the same expert far more often than chance in the middle and late layers. That matters for serving: a hot pair of experts can over-subscribe one GPU, and the repetition can be exploited for caching. The share of repeats by layer and dataset (the paper's Table 5, against the random baseline) is charted on the Mixtral of Experts paper page, which owns the routing evidence.

The current line: granular MoE and big dense models side by side

Eight large experts are now the classic baseline, not the frontier: the Chinese open-weight labs moved to hundreds of small experts with an always-on shared one, and Mistral followed. It now ships both architectures and picks per product, which makes it a natural experiment.

  • Mistral Large 3 (December 2025, Apache 2.0): a "granular" mixture of experts, 675B total and 41B active (673B and 39B of language model plus a 2.5B vision encoder), 256k context, image input, trained from scratch on 3,000 Nvidia H200 GPUs (Mistral; model card). Its params.json is DeepSeek-V3's skeleton: width 7,168, 61 layers, 128 heads with multi-head latent attention (a 512-number latent plus a 64-number position key), first 3 layers dense, one shared expert. The difference is the experts: 128 of width 4,096 with 4 active, where DeepSeek-V3 has 256 of width 2,048 with 8 active, the same routed parameters per layer cut into half as many, twice as wide. So Large 3 is not a scaled-up Mixtral: Mixtral survives in the name "MoE", not in the shape. The FP8 checkpoint runs on one node of eight B200s or H200s, the NVFP4 one on a node of H100s or A100s; at launch it debuted second among open non-reasoning models on LMArena, by Mistral's report.
  • Mistral Small 4 (16 March 2026, Apache 2.0): 119B total, 128 experts with 4 active plus one shared, latent attention with a 256-number latent, 256k context, image input (Mistral; params.json). It merges three former lines into one checkpoint: instruct (Small), reasoning (Magistral) and agentic coding (Devstral), with a reasoning_effort setting of none or high instead of separate models.
  • Mistral Medium 3.5 (April 2026, modified MIT): the other way, a dense 128B model with grouped-query attention (96 query heads, 8 key-value heads), 256k context, a vision encoder trained from scratch and the same reasoning setting, open weights (model card). Its text config is identical to Devstral 2's (width 12,288, 88 layers, feed-forward 28,672): same shape, plus the vision encoder. Mistral reports 77.6% on SWE-bench Verified.
  1. One layer of each, to one scale. Every tile's area is its parameter count. A Large 3 layer (from the fourth on; the first 3 are dense) holds 128 routed experts of 3 × 7,168 × 4,096 = 88.1M plus one shared, 11.55B with its attention (orange); a Small 4 layer holds 128 experts a seventh of the size of Mixtral's (25.2M against 176.2M) plus one shared, 3,275M; a Medium 3.5 layer is one dense block of 3 × 12,288 × 28,672 = 1,057M, 1,384M with attention.

  3. Medium 3.5 reads everything. One token touches all 1,384M of a dense layer, 2.2 times what it touches in a Large 3 layer, and Medium 3.5 has 88 layers to Large 3's 61.
  4. Whole models, volume to scale. Footprint and height per layer share one scale, so volume is parameters (layers only; embeddings and vision encoders left out). Large 3: 671.5B stored and 38.1B read per token (the solid column, its 3 dense layers at the base); Small 4: 117.9B and 5.6B; Medium 3.5: 121.8B, all read. Latent attention caches one vector per layer, 512 + 64 numbers on Large 3 and 256 + 64 on Small 4; grouped-query attention caches keys and values for 8 heads of 128 in each of Medium 3.5's 88 layers. In 16 bits that is 68.6 KiB, 22.5 KiB and 352 KiB per token, and one 256K sequence holds 17.2 GiB, 5.6 GiB and 88 GiB (the teal columns, height to scale). Cache sizing in general is on Long context.

Why both? Memory is priced on total parameters and compute on active ones. Small 4 and Medium 3.5 occupy about the same memory (119B against 128B), but a token through Small 4 touches 6B to 8B parameters and a token through Medium 3.5 touches all 128B, 16 to 21 times more work. Sparse wins when throughput per GPU is the bill; dense keeps every parameter engaged on every token, avoids routing and all-to-all communication, and is simpler to fine-tune and serve, the trade Mistral made for its agentic coding flagship. The cache tells the same story (Fig. 3, step 5): the MoE models use latent attention and the dense one does not; every model is a preset in the KV-cache calculator.

Predict: which bar moves when you change the precision? Memory for the weights is M = Ptotal · s (s bytes per parameter: 2 at BF16, 1 at FP8, 0.5 at 4 bits); forward compute is Ctok ≈ 2 · Pactive FLOPs per token, one multiply and one add per active parameter, ignoring attention over the context.

Lineage: specialists, then consolidation

The order explains the current shape. In 2024 and 2025 Mistral shipped a model per job: a flagship that started API-only (Mistral Large 1, February 2024; Large 2 in July 2024 released its weights under the research-only Mistral Research License, with a paid licence for commercial self-deployment, Mistral), price tiers (Small 3 and 3.1 back on Apache 2.0, Medium 3 on the API), and specialists: Codestral trained for fill-in-the-middle (completing inside a file given both the prefix and the suffix, the shape editor autocomplete needs), Ministral edge models, Pixtral for vision (from September 2024, an image encoder feeding the language decoder so images and text interleave in one context), Voxtral for speech (July 2025), Magistral (June 2025), its first reasoning models, and Devstral (May 2025), tuned for the agent loop of reading a repository, editing files and running tests inside a scaffold. Mistral 3 (2 December 2025) brought Large 3 plus Ministral 3 at 3B, 8B and 14B, each in base, instruct and reasoning variants, all multimodal and Apache 2.0 (85% on AIME 2025 for the 14B reasoning variant, by Mistral's report, Mistral); Devstral 2 followed a week later with the Vibe command-line coding agent.

In 2026 the specialists folded back into generalists: Small 4 absorbed Magistral and Devstral behind a reasoning setting, Medium 3.5 replaced them in Mistral's own products, and vision is built into Large 3, Small 4 and Medium 3.5; every Pixtral, Magistral and Devstral version is in the deprecated table of Mistral's catalogue. Specialists remain only where the job is different in kind: speech, document OCR, formal proofs (Leanstral), safety (Shieldstral) and robotics (Robostral Navigate).

Large 4, a trillion parameters

In July 2026 Mistral opened partner early access to a model Mensch called a "fat but sparse" family (Tech Times); on 6 October it became Mistral Large 4 ("Le Chonk"), a public API preview of a natively multimodal mixture of experts covering more than 160 languages and trained on 3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs (Mistral). The weights followed on 29 October 2026 as Mistral-Large-4-1T-A52B, under the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License, a modified MIT: free to use, fine-tune and sell, except that a model-as-a-service business needs a separate commercial licence from Mistral (Mistral, model card). The card fixes the shape: about 1T parameters in total and about 49B active per token (52B counting the embedding and output layers, hence "A52B"), a granular MoE of 128 routed experts with 4 active plus 1 shared, and a 1M-token context (model card). The attention is the Large 3 recipe pushed for a million tokens, per vLLM's write-up of the config: latent attention (MLA) with a DeepSeek-style sparse indexer (DSA) that keeps the top 2,048 keys per query, and nine of the 72 layers reading layer 35's latent cache instead of writing their own, so 63 layers hold a cache (vLLM; the mechanisms are on Attention variants and Long context). It also ships its own draft model, "DSpark": three MLA layers inside the checkpoint that propose a block of three tokens for speculative decoding (model card; mechanism on Speculative decoding), and an NVFP4 checkpoint for Blackwell GPUs. It is Mistral's strongest model on the Artificial Analysis index by far, and still below the best Chinese open-weight models (scores below).

The 2026 specialists: Shieldstral, Robostral Navigate, Leanstral, speech and OCR (depth)

Shieldstral 1.0 (4 August 2026, Apache 2.0): a guard model (text and image input, 32k context, public preview) that takes a safety policy written in natural language as a question and answers yes or no, covering prompt moderation, response moderation, prompt-response pairs and refusal detection (model card). Because the policy is an input rather than a fixed list of harm categories trained into the weights, a policy change needs no retraining, and because it is a separate model, the policy lives outside the generator. Mistral says it "matches models up to 7x its size on text safety" and sets a new state of the art on multimodal moderation (Mistral); it does not win every text benchmark (85.0 on BeaverTails against 85.9 for Qwen3Guard-8B, Hugging Face). Robostral Navigate (8 July 2026, licence not named): an 8B model that takes images from a single RGB camera plus a plain-language instruction ("leave the lobby, walk through the corridor, enter the supply room and stop to face the second shelf") and outputs navigation actions: a target point in the image with an orientation, or a displacement in the robot's frame. Trained entirely in simulation on about 2.4 million trajectories across 350,000 scenes, then refined with online reinforcement learning; Mistral reports 76.6% success on unseen environments of the R2R-CE vision-and-language navigation benchmark (Mistral). The announcement names no licence and links no weights.

Leanstral (March 2026) and Leanstral 1.5 (July 2026), Apache 2.0: open-weight Lean 4 proof generation built on the Small 4 family, 119B total and 6B active by the blog (6.5B on the card) (Leanstral; Leanstral 1.5; Hugging Face). How Mistral trains: Magistral's recipe

Mistral publishes weights first and papers rarely, so most of its recipes have to be inferred from the artefacts. Two things are documented, both in the Magistral paper.

Pure RL for the big model. Magistral Medium was trained from Mistral Medium 3 by reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) alone: no learned reward model, and no reasoning traces distilled from someone else's reasoner. A rollout is scored by mechanically checking its final answer (a maths result by a rule-based checker with SymPy, or code against 20 unit tests), so style cannot game the reward the way it can game a preference model. The algorithm is GRPO (score each of a group of answers to one prompt against the group's mean instead of a learned critic; the full objective is on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO, RLVR), with Mistral's own changes:

  • No KL penalty to the starting model: Mistral found the policy diverges regardless, and the reference copy was not worth its compute.
  • Clip-higher: the upper clipping threshold εhigh raised to between 0.26 and 0.28 for Medium, 0.3 for Small, so rare but useful tokens gain probability faster. PPO's original setting is 0.2 on both sides (PPO paper); the paper does not state its lower bound. A token at probability 0.01 may rise to 0.0128 in one update at 0.28, against 0.012 at 0.2.
  • Length-normalised loss over all tokens of all answers in a group, so long answers do not dominate; the advantage is simply ri − μ (no division by the group's spread), normalised again across each minibatch.
  • Zero-advantage filtering: groups where every answer got the same reward carry no signal and are dropped.
  • Reward: 0 if the format is wrong (one <think> block, the answer in \boxed{} or a fenced code block); otherwise 0.1 for the format, a further 0.9 for a correct answer, a soft length penalty of up to −0.1 that grows linearly once an answer passes lmax − lcache (raised from 16k to 32k tokens during Medium's run), and 0.1 more when problem, reasoning and answer share a language (10% of problems were translated into French, Spanish, Italian, German, Chinese and Russian).

Predict: what happens to the group if every answer is correct? One group of eight answers to the same problem: toggle what each got right and watch the rewards, the advantages and the filter.

Distillation for the small one. Magistral Small (24B, Apache 2.0) was not pure RL: it was cold-started with supervised fine-tuning of Mistral Small 3 on reasoning traces from Magistral Medium (correct traces from Medium's RL run, plus Medium's answers to OpenThoughts and OpenR1 code prompts, plus 10% general instruction data), then trained with RL on top. Distillation as a method is on Distillation and small language models.

What it bought, on the paper's own evaluation: AIME 2024 pass@1 went from 26.8% for Mistral Medium 3 to 73.6% for Magistral Medium (90.0% by majority vote over 64 samples), and Magistral Small reached 70.7%, against 65.4% with the distilled traces alone. Magistral no longer ships: reasoning is now a setting on Small 4 and Medium 3.5, and every Magistral version is deprecated. Reasoning modes and adjustable effort in general are on the landscape root.

Reward rules from the Magistral paper, section 2.2; the group of eight and the answers are illustrative (the paper does not state the group size). "Half into the penalty zone" is an answer halfway between lmax − lcache and lmax. Standard GRPO divides by the group's standard deviation; Magistral does not, and normalises across the minibatch instead.

Which Mistral model when

Not a frontier lab on capability, but one of the leading Western open-weight providers and the EU's strategic champion: you choose it for the licence, the jurisdiction or the price per token, not for a leaderboard.

  • Mistral's most capable model: Large 4, through the API or as weights. It buys the best Mistral scores by a wide margin and costs a trillion parameters of memory (the NVFP4 checkpoint is the smallest) and, for a model-as-a-service business, a separate licence; Weights covers the models up to Large 3.
  • The most capable weights under Apache 2.0: Large 3, if you have a full eight-GPU node: no licence conditions, 675 GB of FP8 weights.
  • Cheap, fast, multimodal, with a reasoning switch: Small 4: 6B-active decode cost and a small cache, but memory for all 119B parameters.
  • Agentic coding and tool use, self-hosted: Medium 3.5, unless your company is over the $20M monthly revenue line; then Devstral Small 2 (Apache 2.0, 24B, weights only now) is the unencumbered option, or Medium 3.5 through the API.
  • On a phone or laptop: Ministral 3 at 3B or 8B. A guard model in front of any generator: Shieldstral, with your policy written as text. Code completion in an editor: Codestral, through the API.

Common misconceptions, each answered in one line (depth)
  • "8x7B means 56B parameters": only the feed-forward networks are replicated; attention and embeddings are shared, so it is 46.7B total and 12.9B active.
  • "Mixtral's experts are topic experts": Mistral's own routing analysis found syntactic rather than topical specialisation, and strong repetition between consecutive tokens.
  • "Everything Mistral ships is Apache 2.0": Medium 3.5 and Devstral 2 are under a modified MIT licence with a $20M monthly revenue exception, Voxtral TTS is non-commercial, and Codestral, OCR and the embedding models have no public weights.
  • "Magistral was all pure RL": Magistral Medium was; Magistral Small started from Magistral Medium's traces.

Weights: every model rebuilt from its config

Every number here is summed from the model's published configuration (layer count, widths, heads, experts) rather than copied from a headline, so you can see which headline counts what, and what each model's weights take to hold.

The cache, in one line. Mistral's two latent-attention models cache far less than its dense one: one 256K sequence holds 5.6 GiB on Small 4 (22.5 KiB per token) and 17.2 GiB on Large 3 (68.6 KiB), against 88 GiB on Medium 3.5 and Devstral 2 (352 KiB per token over 88 layers). Every Mistral model here is a preset in the KV-cache calculator, which adds the cache, the context and the GPUs to these weights; Mistral 7B's rolling buffer is worked on the Reading tab. This tab is the tool across every model; the Reading tab uses its sums.

1. Where the headline parameter count comes from

Try: Small 4 is selected. Untick the input embedding: the active sum lands on the model card's 6.5B; untick the vision encoder as well and it lands on the blog's 6B. Nothing reaches the blog's 8B. Then pick Devstral 2 and Medium 3.5: the same text model, counted two ways.

Sums: attention projections, norms, routers and feed-forward or expert weights per layer from the config, plus the embedding tables; the vision projector's shape (a 2 × 2 patch merger and a two-layer adapter) is assumed, so vision totals carry a few hundredths of a billion of uncertainty. Verdict: ✓ inside the stated figure's rounding, ≈ within 1%, ✗ beyond. These reproduce Mistral's figures independently, not by construction; where none does (Small 4's 8B), the table says so.

2. What the weights take to hold

Try: FP8 is selected: Large 3's 675 GB of weights fit one 8 × H200 node, as its model card states; switch to 4-bit and Medium 3.5 fits one H100.

Grey: weights at the chosen precision (Mistral's stated totals; Ministral 3 at the computed totals). The KV cache for each sequence, activations and framework overhead come on top, so real deployments need headroom. GPU sizes: H100 80 GB, H200 141 GB. Defaults reproduce, independently, the model card's statement that FP8 Large 3 runs on one node of eight H200s.

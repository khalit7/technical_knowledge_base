0. In one screen

After heuristic filters and deduplication, a web pipeline still has to decide which of the surviving pages are worth training on. Today that is done by a model that scores every page and a cut that keeps the top slice. This page takes the scorers apart on one real Common Crawl file and measures what each keeps, drops and gets wrong.

  • It learns style as well as content. DCLM's classifier pushes hardest toward "high quality" on code fences, "Why" and "Answer:", and toward "crawl" on bylines, years and typographic punctuation.   • Other selectors behave differently. Perplexity favours predictable text, templates included; DSIR's weights depend strongly on document length; gradient methods are the most direct and the most expensive (sections 7 to 9).
  • Every filter has a demographic. FineWeb-Edu under-keeps entertainment, sport and commerce; DCLM keeps 18% of .uk pages against 26% of .com (section 11). Published audits found filters favouring wealthier, urban and anglophone writing.
  • Only training says which filter is better. Agreement with people did not predict it in DCLM's study; controlled training or annealing runs do (section 12).

1. What "quality" means, operationally

A web crawl is mostly text you would not want a model to imitate: shop pages, cookie banners, spun spam, lists of links. The cheap heuristic filters of the Pipeline lab (word counts, punctuation, repeated lines; owned in depth by Extraction and filtering) remove the obvious junk. What survives still varies enormously, and since about 2023 every competitive open corpus adds a model-based quality filter: a model gives each document a number, and a rule keeps the documents above a cut-off. Data selection is the wider name for any procedure that picks a subset of a pool to train on.

There is no definition of quality behind these filters other than what their scorer was built to prefer. Every method on this page is one of five answers to the question "quality according to what?":

Two consequences run through the whole page. First, the reference decides the bias: a classifier trained to tell Reddit explanations from the crawl will prefer whatever Reddit explanations look like, including their punctuation and their country (section 4 measures both). Second, a filter is judged by the model you train on its output, not by agreement with people: DCLM found that filters agreeing best with human judgements did not give the best models (section 12), so the only real test is a training run, and the evidence on this page separates what was measured here (who gets kept and dropped, on real documents) from what was published (what the kept data does to a model).

The running example
One Common Crawl file from CC-MAIN-2024-30 (July 2024), taken through a FineWeb-style datatrove pipeline by the root page: 20,991 responses become 2,617 English documents after extraction, language ID, heuristic filters and MinHash (the root's run0, see the root's Pipeline lab). Every classifier on this page scores those same 2,617 documents. Larger samples are used where a question needs them: 50,000 FineWeb rows of the same crawl, 232,000 pages graded by Llama 3 for FineWeb-Edu, 1,000 Nemotron-CC documents with their published quality levels, and 300 documents read and labelled by hand.

Everything labelled measured was run on an Apple M1 Pro, CPU only, 4 threads at low priority on a shared machine, on 2026-10-14. Counts and scores are deterministic and transfer to any machine; timings are rough and do not transfer to a GPU cluster (section 13 gives the cluster arithmetic).

2. Reference-set classifiers from zero: how fastText scores a page

The problem. You have billions of documents and a few hundred thousand examples of text you like (the reference set, or positives). You want a scorer cheap enough to run on every document of a crawl. The answer since GPT-3 has been a linear classifier on word features, trained to separate the reference set from random crawl documents (the negatives). DCLM's classifier, the one behind DCLM-baseline and OLMo 2's pretraining mix, is a fastText model: Facebook's library for linear text classifiers with hashed word n-grams (Joulin et al. 2016).

How it works, read from fastText v0.9.2's source (the version every pipeline here uses) and checked number for number below:

  1. One line of text. fastText reads one line at a time, so the document's lines are first joined with a space: " ".join(text.strip().splitlines()) (DCLM's enricher, baselines/mappers/enrichers/quality_prediction_enrichers_calc_fasttext.py:49 at commit 361714b; Dolma's tagger does the same). The Python binding appends a newline, which becomes the end-of-sentence token </s>.
  2. Tokens are what lies between ASCII spaces, tabs and newlines (dictionary.cc:208-232). No lowercasing and no punctuation splitting: Why, why and why? are three different words.
  3. Features. Every token found in the vocabulary contributes its own row (DCLM's model has 3,777,339 words). Every pair of neighbouring tokens contributes a hashed bigram: the two tokens' 32-bit FNV-1a hashes are combined as h = h1 * 116049371 + h2 and reduced modulo 2,000,000 buckets (dictionary.cc:312-322). Unknown words still take part in bigrams, and unrelated bigrams that land in the same bucket share a row.
  4. Average. The document vector is the plain mean of all feature rows, 100 numbers each (model.cc:42-50). A 50-word page and a 5,000-word page both end up as one averaged vector.
  5. Score. Two output vectors, one per label (__label__hq, __label__cc); a softmax over the two dot products gives P(hq). With two labels this is a logistic regression: logit = (whq - wcc) · mean of rows.
  6. Cut. DCLM keeps a document when P(hq) ≥ 0.018112 (baselines/baselines_configs/fasttext_filter.yaml:8), the value that keeps the top 10% of its pool.

Because step 5 is linear, every feature's push, its row dotted with (whq - wcc), adds up exactly to the document's logit after dividing by the number of features. This page rebuilds DCLM's prediction from the weights this way for all 2,617 documents; the largest difference from fastText's own output is 1.2e-05, which is fastText adding 10-5 inside its log (loss.cc std_log). That decomposition drives the animation below and the Classifier replay tab.

A trap found while rebuilding it
The first rebuild was wrong on some documents by up to 0.84 in probability. Two details of the C++ matter: tokens are split on ASCII whitespace only (Python's str.split() also splits on non-breaking spaces), and the 32-bit word hashes are stored in a std::vector<int32_t> (dictionary.cc:382), so a hash above 231 becomes negative and is sign-extended when widened to 64 bits for the bigram formula. Any reimplementation of fastText features (in Rust, Spark or a GPU kernel) has to copy both, or its bigram buckets silently differ.

The cost

Training is minutes on a laptop: DCLM's recipe at full size (200,000 positives and 200,000 negatives, with the smaller bucket table of section 3) took 82 s here on 4 threads measured, rough. Scoring is a hash lookup and a 100-dimensional average, about 2.2 ms per document per core, so a whole crawl is a CPU job, not a GPU job. The memory is the catch: the 2,000,000 bigram buckets plus a vocabulary of millions of words make DCLM's file 2.39 GB, loaded once per worker process (DCLM's README asks for --ray_num_cpus 2 per task for this reason).

GPT-3's version: logistic regression and a random keep

GPT-3 (Brown et al. 2020, Appendix A) trained a logistic regression on Spark's standard tokenizer and HashingTF features, with WebText, Wikipedia and a web books corpus as positives and unfiltered Common Crawl as negatives. It did not keep a top slice. It kept a document when np.random.pareto(α) > 1 - score with α = 9, "to take mostly documents the classifier scored highly, but still include some documents that were out of distribution". NumPy's pareto draws from a Lomax distribution with P(X > x) = (1 + x)-α, so the chance of keeping a document with score s is

3. Which reference set? DCLM's ablation, rebuilt in miniature

The classifier's architecture barely matters next to its positives. DCLM held everything else fixed and swapped the reference set (Table 5, 7B-1x scale: 6.9B parameters, 138B tokens, each run about 3,700 H100 hours, published):

Core (22-task centered accuracy) and MMLU, from DCLM Table 5. "GPT-3 Approx" = Wikipedia + OpenWebText2 + RedPajama books, as in GPT-3. The paper's "3.5 point lift" is OH-2.5 + ELI5 against GPT-3 Approx (41.0 - 37.5). The last two rows keep 15% and 20% instead of 10%.

Rebuilt here. Training 7B models is out of reach on a laptop, but the classifiers are not. Five fastText classifiers were trained with the hyperparameters stored in DCLM's own model file (100 dimensions, learning rate 0.05, 5 epochs, word bigrams, 2,000,000 buckets; DCLM's training script defaults to a learning rate of 0.1 and its README says defaults were kept, so the file is the reliable source), changing only the positives, all against random RefinedWeb pages, plus a GPT-3-style logistic regression against raw Common Crawl. One concession to the shared machine: with most memory held by another topic's GPU job, a 2,000,000-bucket model with a vocabulary of 1.4 million words was swapped out while scoring, so the classifiers below use 500,000 buckets and drop words seen fewer than 3 times. Measured on the same 50k OH-2.5 + ELI5 training set, the two settings rank run0 at Spearman 0.999 and share 96% of their top 10%. Training took 56 to 97 s per 100,000-example classifier on 4 threads measured, rough. Departures from DCLM, said plainly: OpenWebText stands in for OpenWebText2 (no longer distributed in the Pile mirror), Project Gutenberg chunks for RedPajama books, a Hugging Face ELI5 mirror without vote scores for DCLM's vote-filtered Reddit dump, and real RefinedWeb for DCLM's reproduction of it.

Does the rebuild match DCLM's classifier? The OH-2.5 + ELI5 classifier trained here at DCLM's full size ranks run0 at Spearman 0.92 with the published one, and their top-10% sets share 68% (Jaccard) measured. Same recipe, similar data, different random draw and a different ELI5 source: close but not identical, which is itself the lesson that a "DCLM-style filter" is defined by its exact training data, not by its recipe.

Three readings. First, the reference sets disagree with each other about as much as FineWeb-Edu disagrees with DCLM: pairwise overlaps of the 50k classifiers' top 10% are 6% to 40%, where random sets would share 5%. Second, and the most instructive result on this page: the human-style reading and the training result point in opposite directions. On the uniform hand-labelled sample, the Wikipedia, OpenWebText and GPT-3-approx classifiers keep documents a reader calls worth training on 74% to 81% of the time, against 52% to 59% for the three OH-2.5 + ELI5 classifiers, and they rank Llama 3's educational grades worse (AUC 0.62 to 0.71 against 0.75 to 0.77). Yet DCLM's 7B training runs put OH-2.5 + ELI5 5 to 6 Core points ahead of them (Table 5). The Wikipedia-style classifiers keep tidy, encyclopaedic pages that look right to a reader; the OH-2.5 + ELI5 classifiers keep question-and-explanation pages that, by DCLM's measurement, train better. Each precision rests on 15 to 29 labelled documents, so treat a 20-point gap as real and a 5-point gap as noise; DCLM's own human study (section 12) found the same disconnect. Third, the GPT-3-style classifier, trained against raw crawl text, barely discriminates among pages that already passed heuristic filters: its median score on run0 is 0.68 and its stochastic rule would keep about a third of them.

Dialect. On 50,000 FineWeb pages, DCLM's cut keeps 26% of .com pages and 28% of .us, against 18% of .uk, 17% of .au and 12% of .nz. Spelling explains a small part of that: replacing British spellings with American ones (from a fixed list of 88 pairs, nothing else changed) raised the score of 70% of the 2,102 edited British-domain pages and moved 13 into the keep set; the reverse edit on American pages moved 93 out measured. The FineWeb-Edu encoder showed no such effect (mean change at most 0.006 points of a 0 to 5 score). Typography and topic mix probably account for more of the gap; the Bias browser shows the breakdown.

The synthetic data sibling's finding, tested with pairs. The Synthetic data page found that Nemotron-CC's LLM rewrites of high-quality pages pass the FineWeb-Edu cut more often than real high-quality pages do, comparing different documents. So the sibling's finding holds for some styles and not others: on the same content, "extract knowledge" rewrites raise the FineWeb-Edu score for most pages and roughly double the share passing its cut, knowledge lists raise it less, and concise distillations, question-and-answer pairs and Wikipedia-style rewrites barely move it. Part of the sibling's gap therefore comes from which pages Nemotron-CC chose to rewrite, part from the format itself, and how much depends on the scorer: quality scores of synthetic and real text are not on the same scale

5. LLM-graded classifiers: FineWeb-Edu and the Nemotron-CC ensemble

The problem with reference sets is that you can only ask "does it look like X". The second family asks a question instead: write a rubric, have a large LLM grade a few hundred thousand pages against it, then train a small model to imitate the grades on the whole crawl. The LLM is the annotator; the small model is a distilled classifier.

FineWeb-Edu, step by step

  1. Rubric. An additive 0 to 5 "educational value" prompt aimed at primary to grade-school teaching, chosen "to avoid having the LLM favor highly technical pages like arXiv abstracts" (the verbatim prompt is on the root's Quality lab).
  2. Annotator. Llama-3-70B-Instruct graded 460,000 random pages of CC-MAIN-2024-10 (FineWeb paper, Section 4, published).
  3. Student. A linear regression head on the frozen Snowflake arctic-embed-m encoder (110M parameters), trained 20 epochs on 410,000 grades, checkpoint chosen on 50,000 held out. Input: the first 512 tokens.
  4. Cut. Round the predicted score; keep ≥ 3. The paper reports "F1 82%" for that binary decision. On 1,500 annotation rows the root measured a positive-class F1 of 72% and a macro F1 (average of both classes) of 85%, so the published 82% is closest to the macro average measured.
  5. Cost. 6,000 H100 GPU hours to score FineWeb's 15T tokens published; here 0.14 s per document on 4 CPU threads rough, which is why scoring 50,000 FineWeb rows with it is left to the GPU queue (section 13).

Two properties of the student follow from its tokenizer, and the page measured both: it is uncased (lowercasing all 300 test documents changed no score by more than 4.8e-07) and whitespace-blind (collapsing a page to one paragraph changed nothing), while turning every line into a bullet raised the score of 81% of documents (section 4). It reads content and some layout, not typography.

What it buys published: on 1.71B models trained on 350B tokens, MMLU 33% to 37% and ARC 46% to 57% against FineWeb, and FineWeb-Edu reaches FineWeb's final MMLU with about 10 times fewer tokens. The threshold 3 was chosen over 2 on aggregate benchmarks (the paper gives the comparison only as a plot, Fig. 17). WebOrganizer's analysis (section 11) shows what that choice means in content: Science & Tech. rises from 4.1% to 15.8% of the data and Entertainment falls from 8.1% to 1.6%.

Nemotron-CC: three graders, percentile buckets and a maximum

NVIDIA's argument (Nemotron-CC, 2024) is that FineWeb-Edu and DCLM each keep about 10% of the crawl, too few unique tokens for a 15T-token run, and that they keep different 10% (Table 8: 10.1% of the union kept by both, the root measured 14.4% on our documents). So it trains two FineWeb-Edu-style regressors on other annotators (Mixtral 8x22B and Nemotron-4-340B; the FineWeb-Edu classifier itself was left out for licence reasons) and adds DCLM's fastText. Each classifier's score is turned into a rank bucket 0 to 19 (bucket 19 = its top 5%), a document gets the maximum of its three buckets, and buckets are grouped into five levels by annealing tests: 19 high, 18 medium-high, 12 to 17 medium, 7 to 11 medium-low, 0 to 6 low (Table 2). Taking the maximum is a deliberate union: a page any one grader loves is promoted.

Rebuilt here. NVIDIA released both regressors, so the whole ensemble runs on CPU. On 1,000 Nemotron-CC documents of CC-MAIN-2024-30 (200 per published level), with percentile cut-offs estimated from those documents re-weighted to the published level shares, the rebuilt ensemble puts 84% of documents in exactly their published level and 100% within one level measured. Each member alone does worse (48% to 50% exact), and the maximum is set by the Mixtral grader for 40%, DCLM for 38%, the Nemotron-4 grader for 22% of documents. The two regressors agree with each other more than with DCLM (Spearman 0.93 between them, 0.40 with DCLM, on run0).

Rows: the level Nemotron-CC published. Columns: the level the rebuilt ensemble assigns. Departures, said plainly: the real percentiles came from a whole crawl after jusText extraction; ours come from 1,000 documents weighted by Table 2's token shares as if they were document shares. Model revisions: nvidia/nemocurator-fineweb-mixtral-edu-classifier 768fe255, nemotron-4 variant 84231629, scored as NeMo Curator 1.4.0 does (stages/text/classifiers/fineweb_edu.py:98-126).

Applied to run0 with the same cut-offs, the ensemble would place 277 of the 2,617 documents in "high" and 467 in the top two levels, against 159 kept by FineWeb-Edu and 573 by DCLM. That is the recall argument in numbers: the union of three graders keeps more, and Nemotron-CC then decides by annealing how much of each level to train on (its Table 9: the ensemble labels 25% of tokens high quality against 9% to 14% for single classifiers, at equal or better accuracy on 1T-token 8B runs, published).

6. Choosing the threshold

A classifier gives scores; the threshold (or cut-off) turns them into a decision, and it is a separate choice with its own failure modes.

  • A probability is not a quality level. fastText's P(hq) is a softmax trained on a balanced set, not a calibrated probability on the crawl. DCLM's cut, 0.018112, keeps documents the classifier thinks are 98% likely to be crawl. Cuts are therefore set as percentiles of a pool: "keep the top 10%". On 18,478 held-out pages of the real RefinedWeb, the same classifier's 90th percentile is 0.043, and DCLM's cut keeps 15.5% of them, not 10% measured: DCLM's pool had been through DCLM's own extraction, heuristics and Bloom-filter dedup, and its percentile does not travel even to a close cousin.
  • A percentile depends on the pool. The same 0.018112 keeps 25% of already-filtered FineWeb documents and 22% of run0's, not 10%: the cleaner the pool, the more a fixed cut keeps. Re-derive cuts on the pool you are filtering.
  • Stricter was better at DCLM's scale. Keeping the top 10% beat 15% and 20% at 7B-1x: Core 41.0, 39.8, 38.7 (Table 5, published). FineWeb-Edu chose an integer score of 3 over 2 the same way.
  • But strictness costs tokens. A 10% filter of a 15T-token crawl leaves 1.5T tokens. A model that needs more must repeat them, and repeated data loses value (section 10). Nemotron-CC's buckets exist so that the threshold can be chosen per training stage instead of once.
  • Sample or cut? GPT-3 kept documents at random in proportion to a function of the score (section 2), DSIR samples by weight (section 8), QuRating found sampling with temperature 2 better balanced than top-k. Top-k maximises average score and minimises diversity.

7. Perplexity filters

Idea. Train a language model on text you trust and score documents by how surprised it is. Perplexity is exp of the mean negative log-likelihood per token: a perplexity of 20 means the model was, on average, as unsure as if choosing among 20 equally likely tokens. CCNet (2019) used a 5-gram KenLM model trained on Wikipedia per language and split documents into head, middle and tail at the 30th and 60th percentiles; the root's Quality lab runs CCNet's own English model on run0.

Measured here with a small transformer instead: Pythia-160M (trained on the Pile) over the first 512 tokens of every run0 document. Its median perplexity is 29.5; its ranking agrees with CCNet's 5-gram model at Spearman 0.46, and with the quality classifiers only weakly (0.29 with FineWeb-Edu, 0.19 with DCLM). Low perplexity selects predictable text, and on the web the most predictable text includes boilerplate, templates and lists: of the 10% lowest-perplexity documents, 5 of 12 of those hand-labelled were judged not worth training on, against 4 of 18 in the middle band (small numbers: read it as a direction, not a rate) measured.

Evidence published: in DCLM's comparison, keeping low-perplexity documents under a 154M model trained on Wikipedia, books and peS2o scored 29.0 Core against 27.5 for no model-based filter and 30.2 for fastText (Table 4). Ankner et al. 2024 found the best band depends on the pool: with a 125M reference model, keeping the highest-perplexity half of the Pile and the middle half of Dolma helped 1B and 3B models (Pile 3B: 18.63 to 20.67 average), and the pruned data had higher held-out perplexity while scoring better downstream, a warning against judging a filter by upstream perplexity. Small reference models pruning for 30 times larger ones worked.

8. Importance resampling (DSIR): select by matching a distribution

The problem. A classifier answers "is this like the reference?" one document at a time, then a top-k cut keeps the most reference-like ones. If the reference is narrow, top-k collapses onto its most typical documents. Importance resampling instead asks for a sample whose overall distribution matches a target distribution, the textbook way to turn draws from one distribution into draws from another.

How it works (Xie et al. 2023; code read at p-lambda/dsir commit 4802a1c, data_selection/hashed_ngram_dsir.py):

  1. Features. Lowercase, split with NLTK's word-punct tokenizer, and hash every unigram and bigram into one of 10,000 buckets with sha256 (lines 22-50). A document becomes 10,000 counts.
  2. Two bags of n-grams. Count buckets over the target set and over the raw pool and normalise: ptarget and praw, two probability vectors (lines 186-216). That is the whole "training": counting.
  3. Weights. A document's log importance weight is the sum over its n-grams of log ptarget - log praw (line 120): how much more likely the target makes this bag of n-grams than the pool does.
  4. Resample. Add Gumbel noise to the log weights and keep the top k (base.py:236-242): exactly a sample without replacement in proportion to the weights. With no noise it would be plain top-k.
log w(x) = Σj cj(x) · (log ptarget,j - log praw,j)
cj(x)
count of hashed n-gram bucket j in document x
keep
top k of log w + Gumbel(0, 1) noise

Run here on 50,000 FineWeb rows of the running crawl as the pool, with three targets: Wikipedia, OH-2.5 + ELI5 (DCLM's positives) and FineWeb-Edu. Keeping 10% by DSIR toward a target and 10% by a fastText classifier trained on the same target overlap by 13% (Jaccard, OH-2.5 + ELI5) measured. The largest difference is length. The weight is a sum over n-grams, not an average, so its size scales with the document: toward Wikipedia and toward OH-2.5 + ELI5, where the average web n-gram is less likely under the target than in the pool, every extra n-gram pushes the weight down, and DSIR picks short pages (Spearman between log weight and length -0.68 and -0.89; median 615 and 231 hashed n-grams in the selected 10% against 855 in the pool). Toward FineWeb-Edu the sign flips and it picks long pages (median 1,627). Divided by length, the weight is nearly independent of it (Spearman -0.10 for Wikipedia). fastText averages, so it has the opposite habit of judging a 30-word page as confidently as a 3,000-word one. The package filters very short examples (perexample_metadata_filter, line 127) and the paper's general-domain run removed short and repetitive documents first, partly for this reason.

Evidence published: selecting from the Pile toward Wikipedia plus books, a BERT-style model pretrained on DSIR's selection reached 82.3 GLUE average against 80.3 for random selection and 79.9 for GPT-3-style classification (Table 3), and DSIR's resampling beat its own top-k variant by about 0.9 points. Selecting 100M documents from the Pile took 4.5 hours on one 96-core CPU node. The paper also proposes KL reduction (how much closer the selection is to the target in hashed n-gram space than a random selection is) as a cheap proxy and reports Pearson r = 0.82 with downstream accuracy over 8 methods. Its limits show in later comparisons: on 1B-scale pretraining DSIR toward Wikipedia scored below uniform sampling (QuRating Table 1: 42.9 against 44.9), and for instruction tuning it fell below random (LESS Table 3). A bag of n-grams captures topic and register, not whether a page is correct or well argued.

9. Influence and gradient-based selection: select by what training on it would do

Every method so far scores text by its surface. The most direct question is different: if the model trained on this example, how much would its loss on the tasks I care about go down? That quantity is the example's influence. To first order, one gradient step on example x changes the loss on target example z by about -η ∇L(x) · ∇L(z): an example helps when its gradient points the same way as the target's.

LESS, step by step

LESS (Xia et al. 2024) makes that practical for instruction tuning (code read at princeton-nlp/LESS commit 8abf962):

  1. Warm up. LoRA-train the model on a random 5% of the candidate pool for 4 epochs, saving each epoch. Off-the-shelf gradients performed worse; the warm-up is required (Table 5).
  2. Gradient features. For every candidate, compute its LoRA gradient (with Adam's preconditioning) at each checkpoint and shrink it with a random projection to 8,192 numbers (default proj_dim=[8192], less/data_selection/collect_grad_reps.py:143). Random projection keeps inner products approximately (the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma), so similarities survive the compression.
  3. Score. Cosine similarity between each candidate's features and the gradients of a few examples of the target task, summed over checkpoints. Cosine, not the inner product: gradient norms shrink with sequence length, so inner products would favour short examples (Appendix H).
  4. Select the top 5% and fine-tune on it.

Result published, Llama-2-7B, 5% of a 270k-example pool (Table 2): MMLU 50.2 (random 5%: 46.5, full pool: 51.6), TydiQA 56.2 (52.7, 54.0), BBH 41.5 (38.9, 43.2). Selection with a 7B model transferred to 13B and to Mistral-7B. Cost: 6 GPU hours of warm-up and 48 hours of gradient features on one A100 for the pool, plus 17.7 GB of stored features (Table 4). That is affordable for a fine-tuning pool and hopeless for a 15T-token crawl.

Making it cheap enough for pretraining

MATES (Yu et al. 2024) measures true influence on a few thousand examples (take one training step on the example, see how a reference loss moves), trains a BERT-base model to predict it for the whole pool, and repeats every 10,000 steps because what helps changes as the model learns. On 1B models trained on 25B C4 tokens it scored 47.5 average against 46.4 random, with selection costing 11.5% of total FLOPs (Table 1). published

When gradient methods are worth it
Use them when the target is narrow and known (a benchmark family, a domain, an instruction-tuning mixture) and the pool is small enough that a backward pass per example is cheap relative to the training run. For general pretraining at trillions of tokens, the classifiers of sections 2 to 5 dominate in practice because they cost a hash lookup or a small encoder pass. Not run here: gradient selection needs per-example backward passes through a language model, a GPU job.

10. Selection for small models and for large ones

The tension. A strict filter raises the value of each token; it also shrinks the number of unique tokens. A small model trained on a modest budget never runs out, so stricter is better. A large model trained on trillions of tokens runs out and must repeat its data, and repeated tokens are worth less each time.

  • Repetition decays published: Muennighoff et al. (arXiv 2305.16264) fit effective data D' = U + U R* (1 - e-R/R*) with R* = 15.4 for R repeats of U unique tokens; up to about 4 epochs repeated data is nearly as good as new (4 epochs are worth 3.7 fresh ones), after about 16 it is worth little (the Data mixing child page owns this law).
  • The best filter depends on compute published: Goyal et al. (arXiv 2404.07177, CLIP on DataComp, not LLMs) found the top 10% pool best at low compute and the top 30% best at high compute, with the top 10% falling behind the 30 to 40% pool after its fourth epoch: "data curation cannot be compute agnostic".
  • Nemotron-CC's answer is to keep more and rank it: 6.3T tokens in five levels, with the high levels repeated and rephrased (the Synthetic data page) rather than discarding 90%. Its 8B model on 15T tokens used 7.2T Nemotron-CC tokens (published).
  • Small proxies still choose well. DCLM's rankings of curation methods at 400M, 1B and 3B correlate with their 7B-1x ranking at Pearson r = 0.838, 0.956 and 0.982 (Section 3.2), and a 125M model's perplexity prunes usefully for a 3B one (section 7). What transfers is the ranking of filters at a fixed keep rate; the right keep rate does not transfer, because it depends on how many tokens the large run needs.

How strict can you afford to be? Unique tokens, epochs and effective data

Pool defaults: FineWeb's 15T GPT-2 tokens after its heuristic filters and dedup. Effective data uses Muennighoff et al.'s fit (R*D = 15.39, App. A; formula above), the same constant the Data mixing page uses. Derived, not measured: it says how much a filter's token budget is worth once repeated, not whether the filter's tokens are better.

11. Whom filters disadvantage: topic, format, dialect

A quality filter is a choice about which writing counts. The question for an engineer is not whether a filter is biased (every one is) but which kinds of page it under-keeps, by how much, and whether that matters for the model you want. Three ways to measure it, all used on this page:

  1. Label the pool, then compare keep rates per label. WebOrganizer (Wettig et al. 2025) released classifiers for 24 topics and 24 formats; run here on all 2,617 run0 documents, they agree with the hand labels on 78% of topics and 65% of formats (200 documents; the hand labels used the same category names) measured. Good enough for keep rates by category, not for single documents.
  2. Controlled edits: change one property of the same text and rescore (section 4: typography, spelling, layout, and LLM rewrites of the same page).
  3. Proxies at scale: top-level domain as a rough stand-in for country, on 50,000 pages.

Topics measured, keep rate at each filter's own cut among topics with at least 30 run0 documents. FineWeb-Edu (keeps 6% overall) keeps most of Science & Tech. 39%; Health 21%; Education & Jobs 11% and least of Travel 0%; Fashion & Beauty 1%; Entertainment 1%. DCLM (keeps 22%) keeps most of Science & Tech. 41%; Software Dev. 31%; Politics 31% and least of Art & Design 11%; Fashion & Beauty 13%; Home & Hobbies 13%. Formats: FineWeb-Edu keeps most of Knowledge Article 49%; Nonfiction Writing 18%; Tutorial 17%, least of Comment Section 0%; User Review 0%; Legal Notices 0%; DCLM most of Knowledge Article 52%; Comment Section 43%; Nonfiction Writing 43%, least of About (Org.) 8%; News (Org.) 9%; Content Listing 10%. The direction matches WebOrganizer's whole-corpus measurement (FineWeb-Edu up-weights Science & Tech. 3.8x and History 4.0x, cuts Entertainment to 0.2x; DCLM up-weights Q&A Forum 3.2x and Comment Section 1.5x, cuts Product Page to 0.2x, Table 9, published).

Region and dialect. On 50,000 FineWeb pages DCLM keeps 28% of .us and 26% of .com pages but 18% of .uk, 17% of .au and 12% of .nz; .in pages are kept at 28%. The spelling swap (section 4) shows a causal but small part of that gap. Published audits found larger, systematic effects with GPT-3-style filters: school newspapers from richer, more educated, urban places score higher (Gururangan et al. 2022), and self-descriptions of realtors, quilters and crafters are dropped more than those of journalists and data scientists (Lucy et al. 2024).

What the hand labels add. In the uniform random sample of 200, 64 documents were judged worth training a general model on, 49 unsure and 87 not (labelled by the agent that built this page, first 1,000 characters each, 2026-10-14). The root's stratified 100 labels found that about a third of the documents both FineWeb-Edu and DCLM drop are fine, half of them news; the Threshold explorer shows precision and recall of every classifier against the random sample, and the Bias browser lists the labelled documents behind every cell.

What this does and does not show
Keep rates per category are measured; whether under-keeping a category hurts a model is not, and the published ablations suggest it often does not hurt benchmarks (FineWeb-Edu's gains come with heavy topic skew). It matters when the model's users write or ask about the dropped categories: a model trained on DCLM-baseline sees less British-spelled and typographically formatted text, a model trained on FineWeb-Edu sees little entertainment, sport or commerce. Decide which skew you accept and check it, rather than inheriting one.

12. How a selection method is judged: DCLM's benchmark

Everything this page measures (who is kept, who is dropped, how classifiers agree) is evidence about a filter's behaviour. Whether the filter is good is a different question that only training answers. DataComp-LM (DCLM, 2024) turned that into a benchmark, and its design is the template for judging any filter:

  1. Fix the pool. DCLM-Pool is all Common Crawl before 2023, re-extracted with resiliparse: 200B documents, 240T GPT-NeoX tokens. Each competition scale gets a random subset as its pool.
  2. Fix the training. Architecture, optimiser and hyperparameters are frozen per scale, so the only variable is the data. Scales (Table 1): 400M-1x (412M parameters, 8.2B tokens, 26 H100 hours), 1B-1x (1.4B, 28.8B, 240 h), 3B-1x (2.8B, 55.9B, 740 h), 7B-1x (6.9B, 138B, 3,700 h), 7B-2x (6.9B, 276B, 7,300 h). "1x" means Chinchilla's 20 tokens per parameter.
  3. Fix the evaluation. 53 downstream tasks. Core is the average over 22 tasks chosen because they give a low-variance signal even at small scale, each rescaled so 0 is random guessing and 1 is perfect (centered accuracy). Extended averages all 53. MMLU 5-shot is reported separately.
  4. Two tracks. Filtering: select from the pool only. Mixing: also add outside sources (Wikipedia, code, books).

Why it works: rankings transfer. Ten methods ranked at 400M-1x, 1B-1x and 3B-1x correlate with their 7B-1x ranking at Pearson r = 0.838, 0.956 and 0.982, so most of the search can run at 1B-1x (240 H100 hours per data point). What it does not give: a noise floor. The paper reports no spread of Core across seeds; it says only that Core's tasks were chosen for low variance and that two deduplication pipelines landing 0.2 points apart were within noise. Read differences under about half a point as unresolved.

What it found about filters published, 1B-1x, Core (Table 4): RefinedWeb reproduction 27.5; top 20% by PageRank 26.1; SemDedup 27.1; a classifier on BGE embeddings 27.2; AskLLM 28.6; perplexity 29.0; top-k average logits 29.2; fastText OH-2.5 + ELI5 30.2. And on human judgement (Appendix N): 16 annotators labelled about 500 documents (71% agreement between annotators); the best and worst fastText filters agreed with the majority label equally (about 73% ROC-AUC), and AskLLM agreed best (about 82%) yet gave one of the weakest models (about 28.5 Core against more than 31). Agreement with people does not predict usefulness, which is why this page's hand labels are used to describe what filters drop, never to rank filters.

How to test your own filter, the DCLM way, on a budget
  1. Hold the pool, tokenizer, model and steps fixed; vary only the filter.
  2. Compare at equal training tokens, not equal documents, and train past the point where benchmarks leave chance (FineWeb used 1.71B models on 28B tokens; DCLM's 1B-1x is 28.8B).
  3. Include the no-filter baseline and a random subset of the same size: a filter has to beat both.
  4. Run at least two seeds of the baseline to see the noise before believing a gap.
  5. For a cheaper read, anneal: continue a partly trained model on a mix containing the candidate data (Llama 3 used 30% new data for 40B tokens; Nemotron-CC 34% for 50B; Ultra-FineWeb 30% for 10B, about 110 H100 hours against about 1,200 for a 1B run from scratch, published).

At scale. Scoring is embarrassingly parallel. Rough arithmetic for a 15T-token crawl of about 15 billion documents (about 1,000 tokens each): DCLM's fastText at 2.2 ms per document is about 9,000 CPU core-hours; a 110M encoder at about 1,000 documents per second per H100 (FineWeb-Edu's 6,000 H100 hours for 15T tokens implies about 700) is thousands of GPU hours. Practical rules:

  • Load the model once per worker process, not per document (2.39 GB for DCLM's fastText; datatrove's FastTextClassifierFilter does this).
  • Write scores as a column next to the document and threshold in a second pass: the cut will change, the scores will not (DCLM's README recommends exactly this).
  • Pin the preprocessing with the model: line joining, truncation length, lowercasing. Section 4 shows typography alone moves 205 of 2,617 decisions.
  • Compute percentile cuts on the pool you filter, after dedup, and record them with the model revision.
  • Keep a stratified sample of what each filter drops and read it before every release.

Common mistakes

Reading P(hq) as a calibrated probability
fastText's probability comes from a classifier trained on a balanced reference-versus-crawl set. DCLM keeps pages at P(hq) ≥ 0.018. Set cuts as percentiles of the pool you filter, and re-derive them when the pool changes (section 6).
Changing the preprocessing between training and scoring
Joining lines with "" instead of " " flips 115 of run0's 2,617 DCLM decisions (the root's finding); normalising curly quotes adds 205 keeps; lowercasing adds 299 (section 4). The model file is half the classifier; the text normalisation is the other half. Version them together.
Assuming two "quality" classifiers measure the same thing
FineWeb-Edu and DCLM keep sets overlap by 14% of their union on run0 and 10% on a whole crawl (Nemotron-CC Table 8). They encode different references: school teaching material and instruction-style answers. Pick deliberately, or ensemble (section 5).
Judging a filter by human agreement or by perplexity
DCLM's AskLLM agreed with human annotators best and trained one of the weakest models; Ankner et al.'s best pruning raised held-out perplexity. Judge by a controlled training run or an annealing test (section 12).
Using the strictest threshold for every model size
Top 10% wins when tokens are plentiful relative to the run. At trillions of tokens the same filter forces many epochs; check unique tokens against the token budget first (section 10).
Ignoring who gets filtered out
Reference sets carry dialect, topic and typography. On this page's measurements British spellings and curly quotes are scored lower by DCLM's classifier; on published measurements GPT-3-style filters favour urban and wealthier schools' newspapers and drop pages about real estate and crafts (section 11). Measure your filter's drop rates by topic, format and region before shipping it.
Reimplementing fastText features "close enough"
Python's split() is not fastText's tokenizer and Python integers do not overflow like C++ int32. Both mistakes changed scores by up to 0.84 here (section 2). Verify a port against the library on thousands of documents before trusting it.
Training the classifier on data that overlaps its evaluation
If negatives come from the same crawl file you later score, the classifier has seen those pages labelled "crawl". This page drew negatives from RefinedWeb and from other WET files, never from run0's file.

Interview questions

Why do pipelines use fastText rather than a transformer for quality filtering at crawl scale?
Cost: a hash lookup and a 100-dimensional average per document, about 2.2 ms on one CPU core here, against about 0.14 s for a 110M encoder on 4 threads. On 15 billion documents that is CPU-days against thousands of GPU hours. DCLM also found fastText beat embedding classifiers, AskLLM and perplexity filters on Core (Table 4).
DCLM's reference set is instruction data plus ELI5 answers. Why not Wikipedia?
Because it trained better models: at 7B-1x, Core 41.0 with OH-2.5 + ELI5 against 35.7 with Wikipedia and 37.5 with a GPT-3-style mix (Table 5). One reading: question-and-explanation text is closer to what benchmarks ask. The price is the reference's style biases, e.g. a preference for code fences and question words and against bylines and curly quotes (section 4).
What does GPT-3's np.random.pareto(9) > 1 - score rule do?
It keeps a document with probability (2 - s)-9: always at score 1, 2.6% at 0.5, 0.2% at 0. It is soft selection that keeps some out-of-distribution text for diversity, unlike a hard top-k.
How would you choose the threshold for a new quality classifier?
Score a representative sample of the deduplicated pool, express candidate cuts as percentiles, compute tokens kept at each against the training budget and the epochs implied, read samples just above and below each cut, and run small controlled training or annealing tests at two or three keep rates. Record the cut with the model revision and preprocessing.
How is DSIR different from a classifier, and when would you use it?
DSIR fits two bags of hashed n-grams (target and pool) and resamples by their likelihood ratio; there is no discriminative training, and Gumbel top-k makes it a weighted sample, not a top slice. Use it to shift a pool toward a domain cheaply (continued pretraining on a domain), not to judge quality. Watch its length bias: the weight is a sum over n-grams.
What is LESS and why is it not used to filter Common Crawl?
Gradient-based selection: warm up with LoRA, store random-projected Adam gradients per example, select examples whose gradients align (cosine) with a target task's. It needs a backward pass per example (48 A100 hours for a 270k-example pool), so it suits fine-tuning pools, not trillions of tokens.
Nemotron-CC takes the maximum of three classifiers' buckets. Why maximum, not average?
To raise recall: the classifiers prefer different pages (only about 10% overlap), and a page any of them rates in its top 5% is likely useful. The max widens the high-quality set from 9 to 14% of tokens to 25% with equal or better downstream accuracy (Table 9). Averaging would demote pages that only one grader recognises.
System design: design the quality-selection stage for a 100 TB, 15T-token English web pretraining corpus.
Inputs: deduplicated, language-filtered documents in Parquet shards with ids. (1) Score every document with a cheap model (fastText, CPU fleet, model loaded once per worker, scores written as columns), plus one encoder grader on GPUs if budget allows (a 110M encoder is about 6,000 H100 hours for 15T tokens). (2) Store scores, not decisions; compute percentile cuts per crawl on the deduplicated pool. (3) Bucket documents by score (or by an ensemble maximum) instead of a single keep/drop, so later stages choose how much of each bucket to train on. (4) Validate with annealing tests per bucket on a partly trained model; pick per-stage thresholds against the token budget and an epoch cap of about 4. (5) Audit: drop rates by topic, format, language variety and region on a labelled sample each release; pin model revisions and preprocessing. Trade-offs to state: strictness against unique tokens; one reference's bias against an ensemble's cost; GPU graders' accuracy against CPU fastText's throughput.
A teammate reports their new filter beats DCLM's by 0.4 Core at 1B-1x. What do you ask?
Same pool, same tokens, same training seed policy? How many seeds of the baseline, and what is their spread (DCLM publishes none, and dedup variants 0.2 apart were called noise)? Does it hold at 3B-1x? What keep rate, and is the comparison at equal tokens? Is any benchmark data leaking through the reference set?

What published studies found

  • published WebOrganizer (Wettig et al. 2025, Table 9): FineWeb-Edu's selection raises Science & Tech. from 4.1% to 15.8% of the data and History from 2.3% to 9.0%, and cuts Entertainment from 8.1% to 1.6% and Sports & Fitness from 7.3% to 1.5%; DCLM's raises Comment Section (8.3% to 12.4%), Q&A Forum (0.8% to 2.6%) and Creative Writing (1.9% to 5.4%) and cuts Product Page (11.5% to 2.8%).
  • published Gururangan et al. 2022: a GPT-3-style filter scores U.S. high-school newspaper articles higher in larger, wealthier, more educated and urban places (each of 14 points more urban, 17 points more college graduates, double the home value: about +1 point of score), and prefers news and sports topics over food and holidays.
  • published Lucy et al. 2024 (AboutMe, 10.3M self-description pages): Wikipedia- and WebText-referenced filters keep news and media and drop real estate, home, fashion and cleaning businesses; lower-prestige occupations (realtor, quilter, crafter) are dropped more; English language ID removes Eastern Asian pages most.

Times are for a careful read. Papers marked vendor come from teams that sell data curation; every paper here reports its own results on its own benchmarks.

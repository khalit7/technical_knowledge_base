11. How a selection method is judged: DCLM's benchmark

Everything this page measures (who is kept, who is dropped, how classifiers agree) is evidence about a filter's behaviour. Whether the filter is good is a different question that only training answers. DataComp-LM (DCLM, 2024) turned that into a benchmark: fix the pool, the model, the training recipe and the evaluation, vary only the data. Its comparison of filter families is the one table every later paper quotes:

In words: RefinedWeb reproduction 27.5; top 20% by PageRank 26.1; SemDedup 27.1; a classifier on BGE embeddings 27.2; AskLLM 28.6; perplexity 29.0; top-k average logits 29.2; fastText OH-2.5 + ELI5 30.2.

Why the benchmark works: rankings transfer. Ten methods ranked at 400M-1x, 1B-1x and 3B-1x correlate with their 7B-1x ranking at Pearson r = 0.838, 0.956 and 0.982, so most of the search can run at 1B-1x (240 H100 hours per data point). What it does not give: a noise floor. The paper reports no spread of Core across seeds; it says only that Core's tasks were chosen for low variance and that two deduplication pipelines landing 0.2 points apart were within noise. Read differences under about half a point as unresolved.

Agreement with people does not predict usefulness published, Appendix N: 16 annotators labelled about 500 documents (71% agreement between annotators); the best and worst fastText filters agreed with the majority label equally (about 73% ROC-AUC), and AskLLM agreed best (about 82%) yet gave one of the weakest models (about 28.5 Core against more than 31). That is why this page's hand labels are used to describe what filters drop, never to rank filters.

The benchmark's design: pool, scales, tasks, tracks (DCLM Sections 2 and 3)
  1. Fix the pool. DCLM-Pool is all Common Crawl before 2023, re-extracted with resiliparse: 200B documents, 240T GPT-NeoX tokens. Each competition scale gets a random subset as its pool.
  2. Fix the training. Architecture, optimiser and hyperparameters are frozen per scale, so the only variable is the data. Scales (Table 1): 400M-1x (412M parameters, 8.2B tokens, 26 H100 hours), 1B-1x (1.4B, 28.8B, 240 h), 3B-1x (2.8B, 55.9B, 740 h), 7B-1x (6.9B, 138B, 3,700 h), 7B-2x (6.9B, 276B, 7,300 h). "1x" means Chinchilla's 20 tokens per parameter.
  3. Fix the evaluation. 53 downstream tasks. Core is the average over 22 tasks chosen because they give a low-variance signal even at small scale, each rescaled so 0 is random guessing and 1 is perfect (centered accuracy). Extended averages all 53. MMLU 5-shot is reported separately.
  4. Two tracks. Filtering: select from the pool only. Mixing: also add outside sources (Wikipedia, code, books).
How to test your own filter, the DCLM way, on a budget
  1. Hold the pool, tokenizer, model and steps fixed; vary only the filter.
  2. Compare at equal training tokens, not equal documents, and train past the point where benchmarks leave chance (FineWeb used 1.71B models on 28B tokens; DCLM's 1B-1x is 28.8B).
  3. Include the no-filter baseline and a random subset of the same size: a filter has to beat both.
  4. Run at least two seeds of the baseline to see the noise before believing a gap.
  5. For a cheaper read, anneal: continue a partly trained model on a mix containing the candidate data (Llama 3 used 30% new data for 40B tokens; Nemotron-CC 34% for 50B; Ultra-FineWeb 30% for 10B, about 110 H100 hours against about 1,200 for a 1B run from scratch, published).

At scale. Scoring is embarrassingly parallel. Rough arithmetic for a 15T-token crawl of about 15 billion documents (about 1,000 tokens each): DCLM's fastText at @@cost_ft_ms@@ ms per document is about @@cpu_hours_ft@@ CPU core-hours; a 110M encoder at about 1,000 documents per second per H100 (FineWeb-Edu's 6,000 H100 hours for 15T tokens implies about 700) is thousands of GPU hours. Practical rules:

  • Load the model once per worker process, not per document (2.39 GB for DCLM's fastText; datatrove's FastTextClassifierFilter does this).
  • Write scores as a column next to the document and threshold in a second pass: the cut will change, the scores will not (DCLM's README recommends exactly this).
  • Pin the preprocessing with the model: line joining, truncation length, lowercasing. The model file is half the classifier; the text normalisation is the other half. Joining lines with "" instead of " " flips 115 of run0's 2,617 DCLM decisions (the root's finding); typography alone moves 205 (section 3).
  • Compute percentile cuts on the pool you filter, after dedup, and record them with the model revision.
  • Keep a stratified sample of what each filter drops, and its drop rates by topic, format and region, and read them before every release.

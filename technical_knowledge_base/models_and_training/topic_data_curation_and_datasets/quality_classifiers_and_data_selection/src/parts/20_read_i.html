10. Selection for small models and for large ones

The tension. A strict filter raises the value of each token; it also shrinks the number of unique tokens. A small model trained on a modest budget never runs out, so stricter is better. A large model trained on trillions of tokens runs out and must repeat its data, and repeated tokens are worth less each time.

  • Repetition decays published: Muennighoff et al. (arXiv 2305.16264) fit effective data D' = U + U R* (1 - e-R/R*) with R* = 15.4 for R repeats of U unique tokens; up to about 4 epochs repeated data is nearly as good as new (4 epochs are worth 3.7 fresh ones), after about 16 it is worth little (the {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}} child page owns this law).
  • The best filter depends on compute published: Goyal et al. (arXiv 2404.07177, CLIP on DataComp, not LLMs) found the top 10% pool best at low compute and the top 30% best at high compute, with the top 10% falling behind the 30 to 40% pool after its fourth epoch: "data curation cannot be compute agnostic".
  • Nemotron-CC's answer is to keep more and rank it: 6.3T tokens in five levels, with the high levels repeated and rephrased (the {{Synthetic data|n:3c65c17b0d0d8171b5d9e36e05b7a5d0}} page) rather than discarding 90%. Its 8B model on 15T tokens used 7.2T Nemotron-CC tokens (published).
  • Small proxies still choose well. DCLM's rankings of curation methods at 400M, 1B and 3B correlate with their 7B-1x ranking at Pearson r = 0.838, 0.956 and 0.982 (Section 3.2), and a 125M model's perplexity prunes usefully for a 3B one (section 7). What transfers is the ranking of filters at a fixed keep rate; the right keep rate does not transfer, because it depends on how many tokens the large run needs.

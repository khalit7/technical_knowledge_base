1. Domains, weights and epochs, from zero

A domain is any group of documents you choose to treat as one pile: a source (Wikipedia, GitHub), a type (maths web pages), a quality bucket (Nemotron-CC's "high"), a language, or a cluster of embeddings (CLIMB clusters documents instead of trusting source labels). The grouping is a choice, and every method below optimises weights over whatever groups you give it.

A mixture weight wi is the fraction of training tokens drawn from domain i; the weights sum to 1. With a total budget of D tokens, domain i supplies wi·D tokens. If the domain holds Ui unique tokens, the model reads it wi·D / Ui times: its number of epochs. Upweighting a small domain is the same act as repeating it.

epochsi = wi · D / Ui
wi
weight of domain i (share of training tokens)
D
total training tokens
Ui
unique tokens available in domain i

The Pile (EleutherAI, 2020) is the clearest published example of hand weights, because its Table 1 lists every component's raw size, weight and epochs. Its authors "attempted to upweight academic texts the most" and smaller sets, "strictly disallowed any data more than 3 epochs and avoided having any data with more than 2 epochs" ({{The Pile, Table 1 and Appendix D.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.00027}}) published. Sizes are bytes (GiB), not tokens; bytes per token differ by domain, so byte weights are close to but not equal to token weights.

Flattening the natural shares: temperature sampling and UniMax

Taking each domain in proportion to its size (its natural share pi) lets the largest pile dominate. Multilingual models fixed this with temperature sampling: raise every share to the power 1/τ and renormalise. τ = 1 keeps natural shares; large τ approaches uniform.

qi = pi1/τ / Σj pj1/τ
pi
natural share of domain i (its size over the total)
τ
temperature: mBERT 1.43, XLM 2.00, XLM-R and mT5 3.33 (UniMax paper, Table 1); some papers write α = 1/τ, so mT5's "α = 0.3"

The flaw is that temperature fixes the shares without looking at the budget: a long run reads the smallest domains many times. UniMax (Chung et al. 2023) starts from the budget instead: go through the domains from smallest to largest, give each an equal share of what is left unless that exceeds N epochs of it, in which case give it exactly N epochs ({{UniMax, Algorithm 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09151}}). With N = 1 nothing repeats. On mT5-style models up to the XXL size UniMax beat τ = 1 and τ = 3.33 on TyDi QA and WMT21 at every scale, and in the N ablation N = 1 was best "but the effect is small" (Table 2) published.

2. How many times can a domain repeat?

The problem. Upweighting a scarce domain means reading it again, and the second reading teaches less than the first. How much less decides how hard you can push a good small domain.

How it was measured. Muennighoff et al. trained over 400 models from 10M to 9B parameters for up to 1,500 epochs on C4 and fitted a data-constrained version of the Chinchilla law. The idea: replace the token count by an effective token count D′ in which each repetition is worth a little less than the one before, decaying exponentially ({{Muennighoff et al. 2023, §3 and Appendix A|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264}}).

D′ = UD + UD · RD* · (1 − e−RD/RD*)
UD
unique tokens
RD
repetitions, that is epochs − 1
RD*
fitted "half-life" constant: 15.387756 (Appendix A), so about 16 epochs
D′
effective tokens: the fresh data that would be worth as much

Curve: D′/UD from the formula with RD* = 15.387756 derived. Dashed: what the same tokens would be worth if fresh. The ceiling, 1 + RD* = 16.39 unique-data equivalents, is never exceeded however long you train.

What the numbers say. Four epochs are worth 3.73 fresh epochs, 93% of face value; sixteen are worth 10.6, 66%; past about 40 epochs each extra one is worth under 8% of a fresh epoch (the slope of D′ is e−R/R*, 0.079 at R = 39) derived. The paper's own measurement matches: an 8.7B model trained four epochs over 44B unique tokens ended "with only 0.5% higher validation loss" than one epoch over 178B, and downstream "differences in downstream performance are insignificant for up to around 4 epochs" (§6; the downstream result is §7, Figure 6) published.

Correction to the old page and to a common reading of T5
The old root said a corpus can be repeated "a few dozen times at most". That came from T5's Table 9, where a truncated C4 repeated 64 times cost almost nothing; it was a 220M model trained 235 tokens, small enough not to memorise. At larger scale the measured limit is about 4 epochs nearly free and about 16 useful; the parent page's section 7 says the same.

Where real recipes sit. The Pile capped at 3 epochs. OLMo 2's mid-training mixes repeat their small sources up to 4 times (Table 13: StackExchange Q&A, Wikipedia and the maths mix at "400" source percent in the 300B mix). DeepSeekMath's continued pretraining read its 120.2B-token maths corpus about 2.3 times (56% of 500B; the {{DeepSeekMath paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} derives it). SmolLM2 limited code to 10% in stage 1 "to ensure approximately 4 epochs over 11T tokens" (§4.2). The planner tab computes epochs and D′ for any mix.

Predict: you can double a 50B-token maths set's weight, which takes it from 2 to 4 epochs. How much more effective maths data do you get?
About 1.9 times derived: D′ is 1.97 × 50B at 2 epochs and 3.73 × 50B at 4, a ratio of 1.89. Repeats up to about 4 epochs are nearly as good as fresh data. The cost shows up later: going from 16 to 32 epochs adds only 3.75 unique-data equivalents (10.58 to 14.34).

What does not transfer: RD* was fitted on C4 and OSCAR web text with GPT-2-style models; high-quality maths or code may decay at a different rate, and the fit says nothing about memorisation or benchmark contamination, which grow with repetition. The paper also found, in its 84B-token experiments, that perplexity filtering helped while deduplication did not help downstream (§7), a result the {{Deduplication|n:3f95c17b0d0d8171ac17e43ab5ea276f}} page discusses.

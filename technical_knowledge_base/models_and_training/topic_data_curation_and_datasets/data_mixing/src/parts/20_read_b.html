2. How many times can a domain repeat?

Upweighting a scarce domain means reading it again, and the second reading teaches less than the first. Muennighoff et al. trained over 400 models from 10M to 9B parameters for up to 1,500 epochs on C4 and fitted a data-constrained Chinchilla law: replace the token count by an effective count D′ in which each repetition is worth a little less than the one before, decaying exponentially ({{Muennighoff et al. 2023, §3 and Appendix A|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264}}).

Predict: you can double a 50B-token maths set's weight, which takes it from 2 to 4 epochs. How much more effective maths data do you get?
About 1.9 times derived: D′ is 1.97 × 50B at 2 epochs and 3.73 × 50B at 4, a ratio of 1.89. Repeats up to about 4 epochs are nearly as good as fresh data. The cost shows up later: going from 16 to 32 epochs adds only 3.75 unique-data equivalents (10.58 to 14.34).

Four epochs are worth 3.73 fresh epochs, 93% of face value; sixteen are worth 10.6, 66%; past about 40 epochs each extra one is worth under 8% of a fresh epoch (the slope of D′ is e−R/R*, 0.079 at R = 39) derived. The paper's measurement agrees: an 8.7B model trained four epochs over 44B unique tokens ended "with only 0.5% higher validation loss" than one epoch over 178B, and "differences in downstream performance are insignificant for up to around 4 epochs" (§6; downstream in §7, Figure 6) published.

Correction to the old page and to a common reading of T5
The old root said a corpus can be repeated "a few dozen times at most". That came from T5's Table 9, where a truncated C4 repeated 64 times cost almost nothing; it was a 220M model trained 235 tokens, small enough not to memorise. At larger scale the measured limit is about 4 epochs nearly free and about 16 useful; the parent page's section 7 says the same.

Where real recipes sit. The Pile capped at 3 epochs. OLMo 2's mid-training mixes repeat their small sources up to 4 times (section 5). DeepSeekMath's continued pretraining read its 120.2B-token maths corpus about 2.3 times (56% of 500B; the {{DeepSeekMath paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}} derives it). SmolLM2 limited code to 10% in stage 1 "to ensure approximately 4 epochs over 11T tokens" (§4.2). The {{Token-budget planner|#t-plan}} computes epochs and D′ for any mix.

What the repetition law does not cover

RD* was fitted on C4 and OSCAR web text with GPT-2-style models; high-quality maths or code may decay at a different rate, and the fit says nothing about memorisation or benchmark contamination, which grow with repetition. The paper also found, in its 84B-token experiments, that perplexity filtering helped while deduplication did not help downstream (§7), a result the {{Deduplication|n:3f95c17b0d0d8171ac17e43ab5ea276f}} page discusses.

3. Choosing weights with small proxy models

The only direct test of a mixture is to train the real model on it, and that is the run you cannot afford twice. Every method here trains proxy models (small, short runs) and transfers something from them: weights read off one proxy (DoReMi), or a fitted function from mixture to loss (mixing laws, RegMix). Each assumes the transfer holds; the evidence for that assumption is what to read.

3a. DoReMi: reweight towards the domains where a proxy lags a reference

Xie et al. (2023; {{arXiv 2305.10429, §2 and Algorithm 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10429}}):

  1. Reference model. Train a small model (280M in the paper) on reference weights, usually the natural shares. Its loss on a token estimates how learnable that token is at this scale.
  2. Proxy with Group DRO. Train a second small model from scratch. Every step: take a batch with an equal number of examples from each domain; compute the excess loss per token, clipped at 0; average it per domain (λi); multiply each domain weight by eη·λi, renormalise, and mix in a little uniform (smoothing c); update the proxy on the loss weighted by these domain weights. Group DRO (distributionally robust optimisation over groups, Sagawa et al. 2020) names this minimax game: the weights chase the worst domain, the model tries to fix it.
  3. Big model. Average the domain weights over all proxy steps and train the large model on that mixture.
αt = (1 − c) · normalise(αt−1 · eη·λt) + c/k,   result = (1/T) Σt αt
λt[i]
mean over domain i's tokens in the batch of max(proxy loss − reference loss, 0)
η, c
step size 1 and smoothing 10−3 "in all our experiments" (§2)
k
number of domains

Why the excess and not the plain loss: a domain that stays hard for the reference too (very high entropy, like noisy web text) has low excess, and so does a domain the proxy has already learned (low entropy, like boilerplate code); weight flows to domains that are learnable but not yet learned.

The update, run on real data. This page ran Algorithm 1 exactly on the five running-example domains: a 0.79M-parameter reference trained 4,000 steps on the pools' natural shares, then the Group DRO proxy for 4,000 steps against it, on the Apple M1 Pro GPU measured, one seed.

Predict: which domain does DoReMi move weight away from on these five?
Web text, from 17% to 2.6%, while code and maths rise to about 38% each: the opposite of the paper's Pile result, where diverse web text rose to 60.6%. Excess is the proxy's loss minus a reference trained on the same data; code and maths have the most predictable tokens, where a trained reference is far ahead of a young proxy, while web text is high-entropy, so the reference is not much better than the proxy and the excess is small. The proxy itself ended with a held-out loss 0.35 to 0.39 nats worse than the reference on every domain, including code and maths, which it weighted most. A likely reason (not tested here): when α concentrates on one domain, each step's gradient comes mostly from 6 of the 30 sequences, a noisier, effectively smaller batch. DoReMi never uses the proxy as a model, only its weights. With a 280M reference trained 200k steps, as in the paper, the balance can differ; this is one small run, one seed, 4,000 steps.

Not run here, and why
A RegMix-style sweep (48 tiny proxies on random mixtures of these five domains plus anchors) and a rank check at a larger size were designed (proxy_mix.py sweep) and dropped on 2026-10-15 as hours of GPU time that would re-measure what is published: RegMix's 1M-to-1B rank correlation of 97.12 (Table 2; 98.76 for the exponential law refitted in 3c), and DoReMi's own transfer from 280M to 8B (+6.5 points) and at matched sizes (Figure 5). For the same reason no main model was trained on the averaged weights, so DoReMi's actual claim, that such a model beats the natural-share reference, is not run here. The CPU smoke test that came first (60 steps; about 6,400 tokens per second on 4 CPU threads against about 14,700 on the GPU) is recorded in data/smoke.json.

In this toy (natural-like reference, transfer on) web loses most weight, from 0.40 to about 0.29; code (easy, learned fast) also loses, from 0.15 to about 0.11; wiki, maths and papers gain. That is the paper's own hypothesis about which domains can be downweighted (the lowest- and highest-entropy ones), reproduced by construction of the toy's curves, not as evidence. With a uniform reference and transfer off, identical curve shapes put the average close to the reference. Three lessons: DoReMi's answer is an average over a noisy trajectory (the README: "we expect the domain weights during training to be somewhat noisy and the averaged domain weights to be mostly smooth"); it depends on the reference (iterated DoReMi re-trains the reference on the last answer until weights change by less than 10−3, which took 3 rounds on the GLaM dataset); and it optimises worst-case excess loss, not average loss, so it can make a domain worse in exchange for others, as the before/after bars show for web and code.

What the paper found published: on The Pile, weights from a 280M proxy put 60.6% on Pile-CC (from 11.2%) and cut ArXiv, PubMed Central and StackExchange to under 2% each (Table 1). An 8B model trained on them gained 6.5 points of average one-shot accuracy on five generative tasks (TriviaQA, Natural Questions, WebQuestions, SQuAD v2, LAMBADA) and reached the baseline's final accuracy at 75k of 200k steps, "2.6x faster" (§3.2). Finding the weights cost 8% of the 8B run's FLOPs (§3). With proxy and main model the same size (280M to 1B) the gain was about 2 points and 4 times faster on average (Figure 5); a 1B proxy did worse than a 280M one for the 8B model, which the authors attribute to the Group DRO optimiser (Figure 6).

3b. Data Mixing Laws: fit loss as a function of the weights

Ye et al. (2024; {{arXiv 2403.16952|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16952}}) trained small models on many mixtures and found that, at fixed model size and tokens, the validation loss on each domain i follows

Li(r) = ci + ki · exp( Σj tij rj )
rj
weight of training domain j
ci, ki, tij
fitted constants; tij < 0 means training on j lowers loss on i

They chose this form over three alternatives by fit quality (§3.2), then nested it with scaling laws in training steps and model size: fit the mixing law on small, short runs, extrapolate each run's loss along its own scaling curve, and predict a large model's loss for any mixture. Optimising RedPajama's mixture this way for a 1B model trained on 100B tokens reached the performance of the default mixture trained 48% longer (abstract) published.

3c. RegMix: many tiny runs and a regression

Liu et al. (2024; {{arXiv 2407.01492|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01492}}) skip the functional form and treat mixture search as ordinary regression:

  1. Sample 512 mixtures over The Pile's 17 available domains from a Dirichlet distribution whose concentration is the natural token distribution times a random scale from 0.1 to 5.0, so both extreme and near-uniform mixtures appear and no tiny domain gets an impossible share (§3.1).
  2. Train a 1M-parameter model (non-embedding) on 1B tokens of each.
  3. Fit a regression from the 17 weights to a target, the Pile-CC validation loss: linear, or LightGBM (gradient-boosted trees).
  4. Score 100,000 random mixtures with the regression, average the best 128, and train the big model on that mixture.

The bet is rank invariance: the order of mixtures by loss is the same at 1M and at 1B. Table 2 tests it on unseen mixtures: Spearman ρ between predicted and true loss of 98.45 (1M), 98.64 (60M) and 97.12 (1B on 25B tokens) for LightGBM, 90.08, 89.26 and 88.01 for linear published. RegMix released every run (512 training runs plus 256 unseen at 1M, 256 at 60M and 64 at 1B), so the bet can be checked here: fit a mixing law to the 1M runs in your browser and test it on the 1B runs.

At 1B parameters and 25B tokens, the RegMix mixture scored 47.3 average over 14 tasks against 45.1 for "Human" (the document-count weights above) and 46.8 for DoReMi's weights, using 3.5e18 FLOPs to find its mixture against DoReMi's 3.7e19, about 10% published (Table 4). The same table has a column worth more attention than it gets: training on Pile-CC alone also scored 46.8, tying DoReMi with zero search cost, and was best on 5 of 14 tasks. RegMix's own mixture is 87% Pile-CC. Its Figure 5 reads the same way: across 64 1B runs, low web-text (Pile-CC) loss correlated with downstream scores more than low Wikipedia loss.

Predict: a linear regression of loss on mixture weights is used to pick the best mixture directly (its minimum). Where does that minimum land?
On a corner. A linear function over the simplex (weights summing to 1) is minimised at a vertex: 100% of the domain with the lowest coefficient. That is why RegMix averages the top 128 of 100,000 sampled mixtures instead of taking an argmin, and why the curved forms (LightGBM, the exponential law) are preferred. For the GitHub validation loss as target, the exponential law says GitHub itself matters most, by far (exponent −58.5 relative to the domain that helps least, against −8.6 for the next): code is learned from code. Switch the model to linear and the top coefficient becomes Enron Emails, a domain with 0.2% of the tokens: few runs gave it any weight, so its coefficient is poorly determined, and with weights that sum to 1 a linear coefficient is only meaningful relative to the others. Read fitted coefficients as a fit, not as causes. For Pile-CC (RegMix's target) the exponential law also puts Pile-CC first, which is why the recommended mixture is mostly web text.

3d. The check: do these methods beat simple baselines?

Aioli (Chen, Hu et al. 2024; {{arXiv 2411.05735|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05735}}) re-ran the methods side by side on 160M models over six subsets of SlimPajama against stratified sampling (equal weight per group): "no existing method consistently outperforms a simple stratified sampling baseline in terms of average test perplexity".

Most methods lose to equal weights in some settings; DoReMi loses by up to 6.9 perplexity. Aioli Table 2: difference in average test perplexity from stratified (negative is better) published, third party for the methods it re-runs. GS: grid search; DML: Data Mixing Laws; A: ArXiv, B: Books, GH: GitHub, SE: StackExchange, W: Wikipedia, CC: CommonCrawl. Methods could use up to 10 extra runs. Aioli itself estimates the mixing law's parameters online during the run and adjusts the weights; it beat stratified on 6 of 6, by 0.27 perplexity on average (abstract). All at 160M parameters and 5K to 40K steps: small-scale evidence.

Aioli's diagnosis: every method implicitly assumes a mixing law (a relationship between proportions and loss) and fits its parameters badly; DoReMi's two large failures (GitHub/C4 +5.3, CommonCrawl/GitHub/Wikipedia +6.9) are where that assumption broke.

Other methods, each with its authors' evidence (AutoScale, ODM, Skill-It, DoGE, CLIMB)

Published, not re-checked here published:

  • AutoScale ({{arXiv 2407.20177|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20177}}): "data mixtures that perform well at smaller scales may not retain their advantage at larger scales"; predicts how the optimal composition shifts with budget; 28% faster perplexity reduction than baselines on GPT-2 Large.
  • ODM, online data mixing ({{Albalak et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02406}}): a multi-armed bandit (EXP3) picks domains during training with loss as the reward; reached the next best method's final perplexity with 19% fewer iterations.
  • Skill-It ({{Chen et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14430}}): skills learned in an order; samples prerequisite skills first.
  • DoGE ({{Fan et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.15393}}): bi-level optimisation of weights by gradient alignment with a target; beat baselines on SlimPajama in its own tests, but beat stratified in only 1 of 6 Aioli settings.
  • CLIMB ({{NVIDIA, arXiv 2504.13161|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13161}}): embeds and clusters a crawl into 20 groups instead of trusting source labels, then searches mixtures with proxies and a predictor; a 1B model on 400B tokens of the result beat Llama-3.2-1B by 2.0%, and the released ClimbMix is 400B tokens.

Named in the old page and not verified here, so not described: MDE, UtiliMax/MEDU, RegMix-D, "Data Mixing Agent". They may exist; this page found no primary source it could check by its date.

3. Choosing weights with small proxy models

The problem. The only direct test of a mixture is to train the real model on it, and that is the run you cannot afford twice. Every method in this section trains proxy models (small, short runs) and transfers something from them: weights read off one proxy (DoReMi), or a fitted function from mixture to loss (mixing laws, RegMix). Each makes an assumption that the transfer holds; the evidence for each assumption is the thing to read.

3a. DoReMi: reweight towards the domains where a proxy lags a reference

How it works (Xie et al., 2023; {{arXiv 2305.10429, §2 and Algorithm 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10429}}):

  1. Reference model. Train a small model (280M in the paper) on some reference weights, usually the natural shares. Its loss on a token estimates how learnable that token is at this scale.
  2. Proxy with Group DRO. Train a second small model from scratch. Every step: take a batch with an equal number of examples from each domain; compute the excess loss per token, proxy loss minus reference loss, clipped at 0; average it per domain (λi); multiply each domain weight by eη·λi, renormalise, and mix in a little uniform (smoothing c); then update the proxy on the loss weighted by these domain weights. Group DRO (distributionally robust optimisation over groups, Sagawa et al. 2020) is the name for this minimax game: the weights chase the worst domain, the model tries to fix it.
  3. Big model. Average the domain weights over all proxy steps and train the large model on that mixture.
αt = (1 − c) · normalise(αt−1 · eη·λt) + c/k,   result = (1/T) Σt αt
λt[i]
mean over domain i's tokens in the batch of max(proxy loss − reference loss, 0)
η, c
step size 1 and smoothing 10−3 "in all our experiments" (§2)
k
number of domains

Why the excess and not the plain loss: a domain that stays hard for the reference too (very high entropy, like noisy web text) has low excess, and so does a domain the proxy has already learned (low entropy, like boilerplate code); weight flows to domains that are learnable but not yet learned. The {{DoReMi step by step|#t-dr}} tab animates one run of this update on toy losses, step by step, and compares the big model trained on the reference weights with the one trained on DoReMi's weights.

The official implementation does exactly this in log space (sangmichaelxie/doremi at commit 7cde52d, doremi/trainer.py, lines 252 to 259):

curr_domain_scores = torch.clip(scores[domain_mask][perdomain_scores_mask], min=0).mean() # 252: clip, then mean per domain ... log_new_train_domain_weights = torch.log(train_domain_weights) + self.args.reweight_eta * self.model.perdomain_scores # 257 log_new_train_domain_weights = log_new_train_domain_weights - torch.logsumexp(log_new_train_domain_weights, dim=0) # 258 train_domain_weights = (1-self.args.reweight_eps) * torch.exp(log_new_train_domain_weights) + self.args.reweight_eps / len(log_new_train_domain_weights) # 259

Two details the paper does not state: a domain absent from a batch keeps its previous score (line 254), and the README calls --reweight_eps the "update rate" with default 1, while the code uses reweight_eta as the step size and reweight_eps as the smoothing; the run script passes eta 1.0 and eps 1e-3 (scripts/runs/run_pile_doremi120M.sh, lines 70 to 71), matching the paper.

What it found published: on The Pile, weights from a 280M proxy put 60.6% on Pile-CC (from 11.2%) and cut ArXiv, PubMed Central and StackExchange to under 2% each (Table 1). An 8B model trained on them gained 6.5 points of average one-shot accuracy on five generative tasks (TriviaQA, Natural Questions, WebQuestions, SQuAD v2, LAMBADA) and reached the baseline's final accuracy at 75k of 200k steps, "2.6x faster" (§3.2). Finding the weights cost 8% of the 8B run's FLOPs (§3). With proxy and main model the same size (280M to 1B) the gain was about 2 points and 4 times faster on average (Figure 5); a 1B proxy did worse than a 280M one for the 8B model, which the authors attribute to the Group DRO optimiser (Figure 6).

The "Pile baseline" is not The Pile's mix
DoReMi's baseline weights, which RegMix also calls "Human", give Wikipedia 9.19% and Pile-CC 11.21%; The Pile's own Table 1 gives 1.53% and 18.11%. The paper says only that they are "computed from the default Pile dataset". The repository's README explains the method: "nopack counts the number of examples in each domain after padding each document to the context window length", so a short Wikipedia article counts as much as a long PubMed paper. The rank correlation between the two weightings over 22 domains is 0.90 derived; the paper's 256k-vocabulary baseline is not reproduced exactly by either 50k-vocabulary config in the repo (Wikipedia 8.94% nopack, 6.49% pack). So "DoReMi beats The Pile's weights" means it beats a document-count weighting, not the epochs The Pile's authors chose. The repo's own 120M round-1 run gives Pile-CC 13.8% instead of the paper's 60.6%: the weights depend strongly on proxy size, tokenizer and reference.

3b. Data Mixing Laws: fit loss as a function of the weights

Ye et al. (2024; {{arXiv 2403.16952|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16952}}) trained small models on many mixtures and found that, at fixed model size and tokens, the validation loss on each domain i follows

Li(r) = ci + ki · exp( Σj tij rj )
rj
weight of training domain j
ci, ki, tij
fitted constants; tij < 0 means training on j lowers loss on i

They chose this form over three alternatives by fit quality (§3.2), then nested it with scaling laws in training steps and model size: fit the mixing law on small, short runs, extrapolate each run's loss along its own scaling curve, and predict a large model's loss for any mixture. Optimising RedPajama's mixture this way for a 1B model trained on 100B tokens reached the performance of the default mixture trained 48% longer (abstract) published.

3c. RegMix: many tiny runs and a regression

Liu et al. (2024; {{arXiv 2407.01492|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01492}}) skip the functional form and treat mixture search as ordinary regression:

  1. Sample 512 mixtures over The Pile's 17 available domains from a Dirichlet distribution whose concentration is the natural token distribution times a random scale from 0.1 to 5.0, so both extreme and near-uniform mixtures appear and no tiny domain gets an impossible share (§3.1).
  2. Train a 1M-parameter model (non-embedding) on 1B tokens of each.
  3. Fit a regression from the 17 weights to a target, the Pile-CC validation loss: linear, or LightGBM (gradient-boosted trees).
  4. Score 100,000 random mixtures with the regression, average the best 128, and train the big model on that mixture.

The bet is rank invariance: the order of mixtures by loss is the same at 1M and at 1B. Table 2 tests it on unseen mixtures: Spearman ρ between predicted and true loss of 98.45 (1M), 98.64 (60M) and 97.12 (1B on 25B tokens) for LightGBM, 90.08, 89.26 and 88.01 for linear published. RegMix released every run, so this page refits them:

Refit with scripts/regmix_fit.py on the released CSVs (sail-sg/regmix at dd9d1c3, data/), same 512 training runs and the notebook's LightGBM settings; LightGBM , CPU, seconds measured. Linear and LightGBM land within 0.7 points of Table 2 (exact numbers depend on the solver and the early-stopping split). The paper's 1M MSE (0.13 linear, 0.04 LightGBM) does not reproduce: the refit gives 0.024 and 0.003, so the paper's MSE is on some other scale it does not state. The Data Mixing Laws form, which RegMix did not try, ranks the 1B runs better than either (ρ 98.76) with 19 parameters.

At 1B parameters and 25B tokens, the RegMix mixture scored 47.3 average over 14 tasks against 45.1 for "Human" (the document-count weights above) and 46.8 for DoReMi's weights, using 3.5e18 FLOPs to find its mixture against DoReMi's 3.7e19, about 10% published (Table 4). The same table has a column worth more attention than it gets: training on Pile-CC alone also scored 46.8, tying DoReMi with zero search cost, and was best on 5 of 14 tasks. RegMix's own mixture is 87% Pile-CC. Its Figure 5 reads the same way: across 64 1B runs, low web-text (Pile-CC) loss correlated with downstream scores more than low Wikipedia loss.

Predict: a linear regression of loss on mixture weights is used to pick the best mixture directly (its minimum). Where does that minimum land?
On a corner. A linear function over the simplex (weights summing to 1) is minimised at a vertex: 100% of the domain with the lowest coefficient. That is why RegMix averages the top 128 of 100,000 sampled mixtures instead of taking an argmin, and why the curved forms (LightGBM, the exponential law) are preferred. The {{fitter|#t-fit}} shows each model's recommended mixture.

3d. The checks: do these methods beat simple baselines?

Aioli (Chen, Hu et al. 2024; {{arXiv 2411.05735|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05735}}) re-ran the methods side by side on 160M models over six subsets of SlimPajama and compared with stratified sampling (equal weight per group). Its finding: "no existing method consistently outperforms a simple stratified sampling baseline in terms of average test perplexity". Table 2, difference in average test perplexity from stratified (negative is better) published, third party for the methods it re-runs:

GS: grid search; DML: Data Mixing Laws; A: ArXiv, B: Books, GH: GitHub, SE: StackExchange, W: Wikipedia, CC: CommonCrawl. Methods could use up to 10 extra runs. Aioli itself estimates the mixing law's parameters online during the run and adjusts the weights; it beat stratified on 6 of 6, by 0.27 perplexity on average (abstract). All at 160M parameters and 5K to 40K steps: small-scale evidence.

Aioli's diagnosis is that every method implicitly assumes a mixing law (a relationship between proportions and loss) and fits its parameters badly; DoReMi's two large failures (GitHub/C4 +5.3, CommonCrawl/GitHub/Wikipedia +6.9) are where that assumption broke. Other methods, each with its own (authors') evidence published, not re-checked here:

  • AutoScale ({{arXiv 2407.20177|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.20177}}): "data mixtures that perform well at smaller scales may not retain their advantage at larger scales"; predicts how the optimal composition shifts with budget; 28% faster perplexity reduction than baselines on GPT-2 Large.
  • ODM, online data mixing ({{Albalak et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02406}}): a multi-armed bandit (EXP3) picks domains during training with loss as the reward; reached the next best method's final perplexity with 19% fewer iterations.
  • Skill-It ({{Chen et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.14430}}): skills learned in an order; samples prerequisite skills first.
  • DoGE ({{Fan et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.15393}}): bi-level optimisation of weights by gradient alignment with a target; beat baselines on SlimPajama in its own tests, but beat stratified in only 1 of 6 Aioli settings.
  • CLIMB ({{NVIDIA, arXiv 2504.13161|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13161}}): embeds and clusters a crawl into 20 groups instead of trusting source labels, then searches mixtures with proxies and a predictor; a 1B model on 400B tokens of the result beat Llama-3.2-1B by 2.0%, and the released ClimbMix is 400B tokens.

Named in the old page and not verified here, so not described: MDE, UtiliMax/MEDU, RegMix-D, "Data Mixing Agent". They may exist; this page found no primary source it could check by its date.

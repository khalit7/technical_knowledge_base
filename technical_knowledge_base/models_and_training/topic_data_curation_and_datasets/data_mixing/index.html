The idea in one screen

A pretraining run is one token budget split across piles of text: the weight of each pile fixes how many times it is read, the order fixes what the model sees while the learning rate decays, and continued pretraining is the same split made later on a finished model, with general data replayed beside the new domain so the model does not forget.

Three decisions, drawn on two real recipes. Left: SmolLM3's 11.14T-token run from its released stage configs (shares grouped by this page; learning rate drawn as its warmup-stable-decay shape). Right: DeepSeekMath's 500B-token continued pretraining of a 7B code model, its mix as the paper states it.
  1. Weights (sections 1 to 4): English web is 70% of stage 1; each share times the budget, over the pile's size, is how many times the pile is read.
  2. Order (section 5): in the decay stage, while the learning rate falls to zero, code rises to 23% and maths to 12.5%.
  3. Continued pretraining (section 6): 56% new maths web text, and the other 44% is code, arXiv, AlgebraicStack and Common Crawl text, data close to what the base already knew.

You need: the Pretraining page for scaling laws (section 3), stages (section 4) and what the anneal buys (section 5, with the anneal toy and OLMo 2's split of its gain into learning rate and data); the warmup-stable-decay schedule and the three meanings of "annealing" are on Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers; the piles themselves on Pretraining corpora.

1. Domains, weights and epochs

A domain is whatever grouping you choose, and every method below optimises weights over the groups you give it: a source (Wikipedia, GitHub), a type (maths web pages), a quality bucket (Nemotron-CC's "high"), a language, or a cluster of embeddings (CLIMB clusters documents instead of trusting source labels). With a budget of D tokens, domain i supplies wi·D of them; if it holds Ui unique tokens, it is read wi·D / Ui times. Upweighting a small domain is the same act as repeating it.

epochsi = wi · D / Ui
wi
weight of domain i (share of training tokens)
D
total training tokens
Ui
unique tokens available in domain i

The Pile (EleutherAI, 2020) is the clearest published example of hand weights: its Table 1 lists every component's raw size, weight and epochs. Its authors "attempted to upweight academic texts the most" and smaller sets, "strictly disallowed any data more than 3 epochs and avoided having any data with more than 2 epochs" (The Pile, Table 1 and Appendix D.1) published.

Small academic sets get large weights and up to 3 epochs; the web pile gets about one. The Pile, Table 1 (arXiv 2101.00027v1), all 22 rows. Sizes are bytes (GiB), not tokens; bytes per token differ by domain, so byte weights are close to but not equal to token weights. Checks in recompute.py: raw sizes sum to 825.18 GiB, effective sizes (raw × epochs) to 1,254.20 GiB, and each weight equals its effective size over 1,254.20 within rounding derived. The † components (Books3 and four others) were taken with minimal processing; Books3 is no longer distributed for copyright reasons, which is why RegMix uses 17 of the 22.

Flattening the natural shares: temperature sampling and UniMax

Taking each domain in proportion to its size (its natural share pi) lets the largest pile dominate. Multilingual models fixed this with temperature sampling: raise every share to the power 1/τ and renormalise; τ = 1 keeps natural shares, large τ approaches uniform.

qi = pi1/τ / Σj pj1/τ
pi
natural share of domain i (its size over the total)
τ
temperature: mBERT 1.43, XLM 2.00, XLM-R and mT5 3.33 (UniMax paper, Table 1); some papers write α = 1/τ, so mT5's "α = 0.3"

Temperature fixes the shares without looking at the budget, so a long run reads the smallest domains many times. UniMax (Chung et al. 2023) starts from the budget: go through the domains from smallest to largest, give each an equal share of what is left unless that exceeds N epochs of it, in which case give it exactly N epochs (UniMax, Algorithm 1). With N = 1 nothing repeats. On mT5-style models up to XXL, UniMax beat τ = 1 and τ = 3.33 on TyDi QA and WMT21 at every scale; in the N ablation N = 1 was best "but the effect is small" (Table 2) published.

2. How many times can a domain repeat?

Upweighting a scarce domain means reading it again, and the second reading teaches less than the first. Muennighoff et al. trained over 400 models from 10M to 9B parameters for up to 1,500 epochs on C4 and fitted a data-constrained Chinchilla law: replace the token count by an effective count D′ in which each repetition is worth a little less than the one before, decaying exponentially (Muennighoff et al. 2023, §3 and Appendix A).

Predict: you can double a 50B-token maths set's weight, which takes it from 2 to 4 epochs. How much more effective maths data do you get?
About 1.9 times derived: D′ is 1.97 × 50B at 2 epochs and 3.73 × 50B at 4, a ratio of 1.89. Repeats up to about 4 epochs are nearly as good as fresh data. The cost shows up later: going from 16 to 32 epochs adds only 3.75 unique-data equivalents (10.58 to 14.34).

Four epochs are worth 3.73 fresh epochs, 93% of face value; sixteen are worth 10.6, 66%; past about 40 epochs each extra one is worth under 8% of a fresh epoch (the slope of D′ is e−R/R*, 0.079 at R = 39) derived. The paper's measurement agrees: an 8.7B model trained four epochs over 44B unique tokens ended "with only 0.5% higher validation loss" than one epoch over 178B, and "differences in downstream performance are insignificant for up to around 4 epochs" (§6; downstream in §7, Figure 6) published.

Correction to the old page and to a common reading of T5
The old root said a corpus can be repeated "a few dozen times at most". That came from T5's Table 9, where a truncated C4 repeated 64 times cost almost nothing; it was a 220M model trained 235 tokens, small enough not to memorise. At larger scale the measured limit is about 4 epochs nearly free and about 16 useful; the parent page's section 7 says the same.

Where real recipes sit. The Pile capped at 3 epochs. OLMo 2's mid-training mixes repeat their small sources up to 4 times (section 5). DeepSeekMath's continued pretraining read its 120.2B-token maths corpus about 2.3 times (56% of 500B; the DeepSeekMath paper page derives it). SmolLM2 limited code to 10% in stage 1 "to ensure approximately 4 epochs over 11T tokens" (§4.2). The Token-budget planner computes epochs and D′ for any mix.

What the repetition law does not cover

RD* was fitted on C4 and OSCAR web text with GPT-2-style models; high-quality maths or code may decay at a different rate, and the fit says nothing about memorisation or benchmark contamination, which grow with repetition. The paper also found, in its 84B-token experiments, that perplexity filtering helped while deduplication did not help downstream (§7), a result the Deduplication page discusses.

3. Choosing weights with small proxy models

The only direct test of a mixture is to train the real model on it, and that is the run you cannot afford twice. Every method here trains proxy models (small, short runs) and transfers something from them: weights read off one proxy (DoReMi), or a fitted function from mixture to loss (mixing laws, RegMix). Each assumes the transfer holds; the evidence for that assumption is what to read.

3a. DoReMi: reweight towards the domains where a proxy lags a reference

Xie et al. (2023; arXiv 2305.10429, §2 and Algorithm 1):

  1. Reference model. Train a small model (280M in the paper) on reference weights, usually the natural shares. Its loss on a token estimates how learnable that token is at this scale.
  2. Proxy with Group DRO. Train a second small model from scratch. Every step: take a batch with an equal number of examples from each domain; compute the excess loss per token, clipped at 0; average it per domain (λi); multiply each domain weight by eη·λi, renormalise, and mix in a little uniform (smoothing c); update the proxy on the loss weighted by these domain weights. Group DRO (distributionally robust optimisation over groups, Sagawa et al. 2020) names this minimax game: the weights chase the worst domain, the model tries to fix it.
  3. Big model. Average the domain weights over all proxy steps and train the large model on that mixture.
αt = (1 − c) · normalise(αt−1 · eη·λt) + c/k,   result = (1/T) Σt αt
λt[i]
mean over domain i's tokens in the batch of max(proxy loss − reference loss, 0)
η, c
step size 1 and smoothing 10−3 "in all our experiments" (§2)
k
number of domains

Why the excess and not the plain loss: a domain that stays hard for the reference too (very high entropy, like noisy web text) has low excess, and so does a domain the proxy has already learned (low entropy, like boilerplate code); weight flows to domains that are learnable but not yet learned.

The update, run on real data. This page ran Algorithm 1 exactly on the five running-example domains: a 0.79M-parameter reference trained 4,000 steps on the pools' natural shares, then the Group DRO proxy for 4,000 steps against it, on the Apple M1 Pro GPU measured, one seed.

Predict: which domain does DoReMi move weight away from on these five?
Web text, from 17% to 2.6%, while code and maths rise to about 38% each: the opposite of the paper's Pile result, where diverse web text rose to 60.6%. Excess is the proxy's loss minus a reference trained on the same data; code and maths have the most predictable tokens, where a trained reference is far ahead of a young proxy, while web text is high-entropy, so the reference is not much better than the proxy and the excess is small. In this toy (natural-like reference, transfer on) web loses most weight, from 0.40 to about 0.29; code (easy, learned fast) also loses, from 0.15 to about 0.11; wiki, maths and papers gain. That is the paper's own hypothesis about which domains can be downweighted (the lowest- and highest-entropy ones), reproduced by construction of the toy's curves, not as evidence. With a uniform reference and transfer off, identical curve shapes put the average close to the reference. Three lessons: DoReMi's answer is an average over a noisy trajectory (the README: "we expect the domain weights during training to be somewhat noisy and the averaged domain weights to be mostly smooth"); it depends on the reference (iterated DoReMi re-trains the reference on the last answer until weights change by less than 10−3, which took 3 rounds on the GLaM dataset); and it optimises worst-case excess loss, not average loss, so it can make a domain worse in exchange for others, as the before/after bars show for web and code.

What the paper found published: on The Pile, weights from a 280M proxy put 60.6% on Pile-CC (from 11.2%) and cut ArXiv, PubMed Central and StackExchange to under 2% each (Table 1). 3b. Data Mixing Laws: fit loss as a function of the weights

Ye et al. (2024; arXiv 2403.16952) trained small models on many mixtures and found that, at fixed model size and tokens, the validation loss on each domain i follows

Li(r) = ci + ki · exp( Σj tij rj )
rj
weight of training domain j
ci, ki, tij
fitted constants; tij < 0 means training on j lowers loss on i

They chose this form over three alternatives by fit quality (§3.2), then nested it with scaling laws in training steps and model size: fit the mixing law on small, short runs, extrapolate each run's loss along its own scaling curve, and predict a large model's loss for any mixture. Optimising RedPajama's mixture this way for a 1B model trained on 100B tokens reached the performance of the default mixture trained 48% longer (abstract) published.

3c. RegMix: many tiny runs and a regression

Liu et al. (2024; arXiv 2407.01492) skip the functional form and treat mixture search as ordinary regression:

  1. Sample 512 mixtures over The Pile's 17 available domains from a Dirichlet distribution whose concentration is the natural token distribution times a random scale from 0.1 to 5.0, so both extreme and near-uniform mixtures appear and no tiny domain gets an impossible share (§3.1).
  2. Train a 1M-parameter model (non-embedding) on 1B tokens of each.
  3. Fit a regression from the 17 weights to a target, the Pile-CC validation loss: linear, or LightGBM (gradient-boosted trees).
  4. Score 100,000 random mixtures with the regression, average the best 128, and train the big model on that mixture.

The bet is rank invariance: the order of mixtures by loss is the same at 1M and at 1B. Table 2 tests it on unseen mixtures: Spearman ρ between predicted and true loss of 98.45 (1M), 98.64 (60M) and 97.12 (1B on 25B tokens) for LightGBM, 90.08, 89.26 and 88.01 for linear published. RegMix released every run (512 training runs plus 256 unseen at 1M, 256 at 60M and 64 at 1B), so the bet can be checked here: fit a mixing law to the 1M runs in your browser and test it on the 1B runs.

Predict: a linear regression of loss on mixture weights is used to pick the best mixture directly (its minimum). Where does that minimum land?
On a corner. A linear function over the simplex (weights summing to 1) is minimised at a vertex: 100% of the domain with the lowest coefficient. That is why RegMix averages the top 128 of 100,000 sampled mixtures instead of taking an argmin, and why the curved forms (LightGBM, the exponential law) are preferred. 3d. The check: do these methods beat simple baselines?

Aioli (Chen, Hu et al. 2024; arXiv 2411.05735) re-ran the methods side by side on 160M models over six subsets of SlimPajama against stratified sampling (equal weight per group): "no existing method consistently outperforms a simple stratified sampling baseline in terms of average test perplexity".

4. Offline or online, and how a loader hits the weights

Offline mixing fixes the weights before training and writes (or indexes) one shuffled stream. The Pile did it literally: it drew documents at random, "weighted by the number of documents in each dataset times the number of epochs desired", and interleaved the output into 30 files (appendix on construction). Reproducible and simple; the weights cannot change. Online mixing keeps one stream per domain and lets the training loop choose the domain of every sample from the current weights. Stages need it (the weights change at a step boundary), and so do the methods that adapt during the run: DoReMi's proxy, ODM, Aioli. The cost is engineering: per-domain shards, a sampler whose state is saved with each checkpoint so a restart resumes the same sequence, and a log of the weights actually used (Storage and loading covers shards, shuffling and resumption).

Hitting the weights exactly. Sampling a domain at random for each sample matches the weights only on average; over a short window the achieved shares wander. Megatron-LM's blended dataset is deterministic: for sample n it computes, for every dataset, the error wi·n − (samples taken from i so far), and takes the next sample from the dataset with the largest error.

Random draws drift from the target; the blend is never more than one sample off. 5. Curricula, stages and mid-training

A curriculum is an order over the data. In current pretraining it is coarse: a few stages, each a block of steps with its own mixture, ending in a decay stage (mid-training, the anneal) where the learning rate falls to zero while the mix shifts to the best data: maths, code, instruction-like and question-answer text, often synthetic. 6. Continued pretraining: the same split, made later

Continued pretraining (CPT, also "continual pretraining") resumes next-token training of an existing base model on new data: a domain (finance, medicine, law, code, maths), a new language, fresher data, or longer documents. It keeps the objective and trains every parameter; what changes is the data, and the data decision is the domain share: how much of the stream is the new domain, the rest being general data replayed so the model does not forget.

Pretraining

From random weights, on trillions of tokens of broad text (Pretraining).

Mid-training, the anneal

The base-model builder's last stage: same objective, learning rate decaying, the best data up-weighted (OLMo 2: 50B tokens; section 5 here, and Pretraining, section 5).

Continued pretraining

Someone else's finished base, billions to hundreds of billions of new tokens, all parameters, same objective (DeepSeekMath: 500B tokens on a 7B base, paper page).

Fine-tuning (SFT, LoRA)

Demonstrations or preference pairs, millions of tokens; teaches format and behaviour more than knowledge (PEFT, Alignment).

Mid-training and CPT are the same machinery; the difference is who runs it. The base-model builder anneals its own run on curated data; an adapter takes a released checkpoint (usually without its optimizer state or its data) and pushes it towards a domain. Long-context extension is CPT too: the standard 128K recipes are continued pretraining on upsampled long documents with a rescaled rotary base (Positional Encodings). The training topic compares all the stages on one grid.

When it is worth it. CPT is for a large knowledge gap: a body of text the base model has barely seen, measured in billions of tokens. Fine-tuning, including LoRA, does not inject bulk knowledge well: Biderman et al. found LoRA "learns less and forgets less" than full training when continuing to pretrain on code and maths (PEFT, Against full fine-tuning). When the gap is a few documents that change often, retrieval or a fine-tune is cheaper and easier to keep current; skip CPT. A domain-specific financial-services LLM Khalid worked on followed the usual shape: select a base model by benchmark screening; continue pretraining on a mix of domain and general text; post-train; evaluate on three axes (domain, general, safety) with a private in-house benchmark. Every step of that shape is below.

6b. The two levers: replay and the re-warmed peak

Replay. Measured ranges:

  • Even 1 to 5% helps a lot. Meditron kept 1% general text, Llemma 2% (plus 3% code), SaulLM about 2%. Ibrahim et al.'s lowest setting, 1%, "significantly reduces forgetting" on both shifts, and 0.5% already removed 63% of the Pile loss rise on the weak shift derived (Table 2). The D-CPT Law runs show the same at 1.8B: training on chemistry alone raised general loss 21.2%; with 6 to 10% general data the rise was 1.6 to 4.4% derived from Table 5.
  • 10 to 30% when the shift is strong or general ability is precious. Ibrahim et al. chose 25% for English to German and 5% for the weak shift; DeepSeek-Coder-V2 kept 30% natural language over 6T tokens of code CPT (as Ibrahim et al. note in their §2; row in CPT recipes compared).
  • Domain-heavy, half and half, when domain depth is the goal. Dialpad's DACP mixed 25B tokens of business-call transcripts with 25B tokens sampled from FineWeb-Edu, 1:1, for Llama 3.1 8B and Mistral 7B v0.3 (DACP, 2025).
  • Too much replay costs adaptation. At 50%, Ibrahim's German loss rose from 1.11 to 1.22, "relatively significantly worse".

Choose replay data close to the original pretraining distribution: the closer it is, the more of the old ability it protects. Ibrahim et al. replayed the Pile itself, the base model's own data; open-weight labs rarely release their mix, so FineWeb-Edu and DCLM-baseline are the usual stand-ins, and their own domain weights (section 1) become part of your CPT mix.

The learning-rate peak. A released base has usually finished with its learning rate decayed near a small minimum. Continuing at that minimum adapts too slowly; re-warming to a high peak adapts well but makes the old-data loss spike first. Ibrahim et al.'s rule of thumb: re-warm to the order of the original peak and re-decay to the order of the original minimum; "decreasing the schedule's maximum learning rate can help reduce forgetting, whereas increasing it can improve adaptation" (§2). Gupta et al. found the same on Pythia 410M continued on SlimPajama, and that the length of the warmup barely matters (Gupta et al. 2023). Use both levers together: replay and a re-warmed, re-decayed schedule.

Corrections

The old page said re-warming "drives most forgetting". In Ibrahim et al.'s own numbers it does not, under a real shift: the 405M model trained on German at a constant learning rate equal to the old minimum, never re-warmed, still raised its Pile loss from 2.17 to 3.22; re-warming to the old peak raised it to 3.56. So re-warming added 0.34 of the 1.39 rise, 24%, and the data shift the other 76% derived (Table 12; on the weak shift, 0.02 of 0.27, 7%). What is true is narrower: re-warming alone raises the loss even with no shift at all ("every model that re-increases its learning rate ... sees an increase in loss", §7.1, on Pile continued with Pile). The toy separates the two the same way: compare "No replay, peak 0.1×" with "No replay" in 6a.

The old page also gave "an order of magnitude lower peak than pretraining" as a common heuristic. It is common practice, but not what the two controlled studies tested. eBay's e-Llama paper names two paradigms in the literature, "use the same LR_max as for the original pretraining ... or ... a smaller value, typically around 10% of the original", and chose 10% after an ablation (e-Llama, §4). Ibrahim et al. and Gupta et al. tried 0.5×, 1× and 2× the original peak; the closest thing to 0.1× in their tables is the never-re-warmed constant run at the old minimum (least forgetting, worst adaptation: German 1.21 against 1.11). Disclosed recipes span 1× (Code Llama, Meditron) to about 1/150 (DACP), with Llemma and Swallow at one third and ChipNeMo at 1/60 (CPT recipes compared). ChipNeMo's own ablation shows the risk at the top: at 3e-4 instead of 5e-6, MMLU fell from 44.6 to 26.6 (ChipNeMo). A sensible place to start pilots, not a law.

6c. Schedules that resume without re-warming

Re-warming is needed because a cosine schedule ends low. Ibrahim et al. propose "infinite" schedules instead: warm up, cool down once to a constant learning rate, stay constant across every later dataset, and decay quickly only when a model is to be released (§7.2). The checkpoint to continue from is the one before the final decay, so no re-warm is needed. This is the warmup-stable-decay (WSD) family; the WSM paper page replaces the final decay with checkpoint merging. DeepSeek did this for DeepSeek-Coder-V2: it continued DeepSeek-V2 from a checkpoint before the learning-rate decay (as Ibrahim et al. describe it, §2).

An infinite schedule leaves a constant-rate checkpoint to continue from, so there is nothing to re-warm. Shapes from Ibrahim et al.'s Equations 1 to 4 with their 405M settings (Table 14): peak 3e-4, minimum 3e-5, constant 1.65e-4 for the infinite schedule, warmup 1%. Two datasets of equal size on the same token axis; the cooldown and anneal lengths are illustrative.

The evidence is thinner than for replay: infinite schedules matched cosine at 405M on 300B tokens of SlimPajama from scratch, and across three 100B splits of the same data showed "negligible forgetting across dataset boundaries" (Figure 10), but the authors list as limitations that these runs had no distribution shift and only one size (§8).

6d. Choosing the ratio: the CMR and D-CPT laws

Grid-searching the domain share at full scale is expensive. Two 2024 papers fit scaling laws on small pilot runs and predict it instead. A "10 to 50% general data" rule of thumb also circulates without a source; treat it as folklore and fit the curve.

The CMR scaling law (Gu et al., EMNLP 2024 Findings). Llama-shaped models of 460M to 3.1B, pretrained on 200B general tokens, then continued for 20B tokens (10,000 steps × 512 × 4,096 = 20.97B derived) on finance or arXiv papers mixed with general text at a constant learning rate of 3e-5. The definitions, read from the paper:

  • a mixture ratio R (the domain share) is feasible if, by the end of training, general loss is no more than a tolerance ε above the base model's (ε = 0.05, "based on empirical results by observation") and domain loss has fallen;
  • the Critical Mixture Ratio is the largest feasible R: below it the domain data is under-used, above it general loss rises past the tolerance;
  • final domain and general losses follow L(R) = α·Rs + β in the ratio, and their changes follow power laws in tokens; the CMR itself is fitted as RCMR = α₄·Ts₄ + β₃ (Equation 8). Fit on a few short runs, solve, and spend the full budget there.

The old page reported the four predicted CMRs (29.8%, 34.9%, 41.4%, 47.8% for 460M, 940M, 1.6B and 3.1B on finance) "expressed as the general-data share". The D-CPT law, and why its "optimal" share (92%) disagrees with CMR's (30 to 48%)

The D-CPT Law (Que et al., NeurIPS 2024) adds the mixture ratio r to a Chinchilla-style law: L(N, D, r) = E + A/Nα + B·rη/Dβ + C/(r + ε)γ, fitted on Qwen1.5 0.5B, 1.8B and 4B, 0.1B to 26B tokens and six domains, and used three ways: the best domain share under a cap on general-loss damage, the best share when the domain corpus is limited (its "explicit domain-corpus-size" use), and how to split compute between model and data. Its worked example: chemistry, 1.8B, 10B tokens, general loss allowed to rise at most 3% from 2.8602.

A looser cap on general damage buys a larger domain share. Measured general loss at each domain share from Table 5, as a rise over 2.8602; the crossing is interpolated between neighbouring measurements. At the paper's 3% it falls at a domain share of 0.9245, matching the law's prediction of 0.924 independently (the law's predicted general loss there, 2.9458, against a measured 2.9445). Outside 1.6% to 4.3% the table has no points to interpolate.

Why the two "optimal" shares disagree (about 30 to 48% domain in CMR, 92% in D-CPT): they answer different questions. CMR caps the general-loss rise at an absolute 0.05 nats and requires it to be heading down by the end; D-CPT caps it at a relative 3% (about 0.086 here); and the domains, base models, budgets and learning rates differ (CMR: a constant 3e-5 over 20B tokens; D-CPT: 10B tokens on Qwen1.5). Neither ratio transfers to your run; the method does: run a few short pilots at different ratios, fit, pick the ratio, then commit the budget.

A domain that teaches general skills can exceed both. DeepSeekMath continued DeepSeek-Coder-Base-v1.5 7B for 500B tokens at 56% DeepSeekMath Corpus, 20% GitHub code, 10% arXiv, 10% Common Crawl natural language and 4% AlgebraicStack, so 44% of the stream was not new maths web text published. Its 56% domain share is above every CMR the finance fits predict (at most 47.8%, at 3.1B), and its general benchmarks still rose (MMLU 49.1% to 54.9% against the released Coder model, on its paper page): CMR's tolerance is defined on general loss, at its scales and for its domains, and maths or code can raise general scores rather than cost them.

This page could not find those studies unconfirmed. What is measured is narrower: Llama-3-SynE (Llama 3 8B, about 100B tokens) found an easy-to-difficult curriculum over its scientific data beat random sampling and the reverse order (§4.2), and used two stages (92.5B tokens at Chinese:English 2:8, then 7.5B at Chinese:English:synthetic 1:7:2). And Ibrahim et al. found that ingesting domains one after another, re-warming for each, did worse than one mixture, "of limited practical use" (Appendix A.1).
  • Model scale. The evidence that larger models forget less is mixed. Yıldız et al. found "smaller models are particularly sensitive to continual pretraining, showing the most significant rates of both learning and forgetting" (arXiv 2402.17400); the CMR study found larger models tolerate a larger domain share (29.8% at 460M to 47.8% at 3.1B). But in Ibrahim et al.'s weak shift the relative rise in Pile loss was about the same at both sizes: 12.4% at 405M and 13.1% at 10B with no replay, 2.8% and 2.3% with 5% replay derived (Table 4). Recipes that trained several sizes the same way lean towards the claim: Swallow's English average fell 5.0, 4.8 and 2.3 points at 7B, 13B and 70B ("the performance drop tends to be smaller as the model size increases", Table 4); ChipNeMo's MMLU moved −1.1, −2.0 and +0.8.
  • Tokenizer extension. For a new language or a domain the tokenizer splits badly (high fertility: many tokens per word), extend the vocabulary, train the new embeddings first in a brief embedding-only phase, then run full CPT. Chinese-LLaMA added 20,000 Chinese tokens to LLaMA's 32,000 (49,953 after merging) and, for 7B, trained only the new embeddings and output layer before the main stage (§2.2); Swallow added 11,176 Japanese subwords and cut the tokens needed for Japanese text by 56.2%; ChipNeMo added about 9K chip-design tokens, initialised from the mean of their old sub-tokens; the toy here avoided the question by writing German umlauts as two letters. Fertility and its cost are on Tokenizers.
  • Parameter-level alternatives to replay help when replay tokens are unaffordable or the original data is unknown, but replay plus the schedule remains the default answer. Elastic weight consolidation (EWC) penalises moving weights that mattered for the old data (Kirkpatrick et al. 2017). LoRA-based CPT forgets less because it learns less (PEFT). Merging: in Ibrahim et al.'s preliminary test, TIES-merging separately trained models scored below continued pretraining with replay on every comparison (10B weak shift, English average 41.94 against 47.68; Appendix A.2, Table 6), and merging a continued model back into its base "does not increase performance". Later recipes report the opposite at scale: Thomson Reuters merged its continued model with the pre-CPT checkpoint and "an appropriate merge coefficient can recover most of the general capability lost during continual pre-training" (report, §3.3), and eBay merged e-Llama with its base to set the trade-off. Treat merging as a lever worth a pilot, not a settled answer.
What it costs: two documented pipelines (Thomson Reuters, Periodic's Neon; reported figures)

Practitioners almost never publish a budget next to a recipe, which is why these two are worth more than their technical content. Their dollar figures, with what each includes, sit on the training topic's Price list; this block keeps the shape.

Thomson Reuters continued Qwen3.5-397B-A17B into Thomson-1.0, a legal, tax and news model: 200B curated tokens selected from a 19T-token pool with DatologyAI (1.05% kept derived), then DPO alignment against an open-source constitution and GSPO for context compaction and document caching (The Batch; technical report). The report's mid-training mix is "three roughly equal parts: curated proprietary documents, synthetic data generated from those documents, and curated general-capability replay data", so about a third is replay derived; the learning rate was chosen per scale by pilot sweeps; the continued model was merged with the pre-CPT checkpoint, then aligned with DPO and GSPO. Against its base it measured general average 76.7 against 73.5, with coding (39.9 against 43.9) and factuality (73.7 against 76.2) down (§3.3, Table 1). LawNext quotes Thomson Reuters saying CPT used its proprietary content "exclusively", which the report's replay third contradicts; the report is the technical source. Unusually, it continued an instruction-tuned, value-realigned checkpoint rather than a base model, and relied on the merge and the later DPO to restore behaviour: the customise-an-open-base path stated end to end (select hard from a much larger pool, keep some replay, then re-align).

Periodic's Neon makes the same argument with a different corpus. Mid-training plus reinforcement learning of Kimi K2.6 on real laboratory records took it from 2.7% to 55.3% on FrontierXRD (134 lab samples), ahead of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 on a hard scientific-analysis evaluation at lower cost per analysis reported; it is deployed in labs analysing superconductor and magnet experiments, and the final run used 1,300 H200 GPUs (Periodic, 15 September 2026). The transferable part is the structural claim: domain training on proprietary operational data, the records an organisation generates anyway rather than a corpus assembled for the purpose, establishes a Pareto-optimal cost-performance frontier against general frontier models. Most organisations sizing a domain-adaptation project already hold that data and do not think of it as a corpus.

7. Checklist for a CPT run

  1. Define target and guard metrics before training: domain evaluations, general evaluations and safety. Evaluate the base model on all of them first; that is the line forgetting is measured against.
  2. Pilot runs to pick the mixture ratio (CMR- or D-CPT-style: a few short runs, fit, predict) and the peak learning rate. Start the pilots well below the pretraining peak and include one near it, since the published sweeps (0.5× to 2×) trade adaptation against forgetting; use a WSD-style schedule if more stages will follow.
  3. Replay source as close to the original pretraining data as you can get; deduplicate the domain data against your evaluations; compute each source's epochs (section 1).
  4. Monitor both loss families during training, general and domain. Forgetting shows early: in the toy and in Ibrahim's runs the general loss jumps in the first few percent of steps, and "relative differences between [replay fractions] appear very early" (§2).
  5. Post-train afterwards, and compare against a no-CPT baseline fine-tuned the same way, to prove CPT was worth it.

Filtering and deduplication of the replay and domain data are on the Topic: data-curation-and-datasets children; the re-alignment step on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR.

Depth: common mistakes and interview rehearsal

Common mistakes (12, mixing and CPT)
Setting weights without computing epochs
A 10% weight on a 20B-token domain in a 2T-token run is 10 epochs. Compute w·D/U for every domain before training; above about 4, expect diminishing value, above about 16 near zero (section 2).
Copying a published mixture or mixture ratio
Weights depend on the domain definitions, the tokenizer, the model size and the budget: DoReMi's Pile weights changed from 60.6% Pile-CC (280M proxy, 256k vocabulary) to 13.8% (120M, 50k vocabulary), and AutoScale shows optima shifting with scale. In CPT, CMR's 30 to 48% and D-CPT's 92% domain share are both "optimal" under different tolerances, domains and budgets. Use a published mix as a starting point and fit your own on pilots.
Comparing against a strawman baseline
"Beats the Pile's default weights" meant beating a document-count weighting (section 3a), and "Pile-CC only" tied DoReMi in RegMix's own Table 4. Always include stratified sampling, natural shares and the single best domain among your baselines.
Optimising the loss of one domain and calling it quality
RegMix optimised Pile-CC validation loss because it correlated best with its downstream tasks; on other tasks (code, maths) a different target would win. Choose the target from the evaluation you care about, and check the correlation on your own runs.
Crediting the anneal's data for the learning-rate decay
A decay stage changes the data and the learning rate at once. OLMo 2's ablation found most of the knowledge gain came from the decay alone; only the maths gain was the data (Pretraining, section 5). Run the decay on the unchanged mix as a control.
Letting the loader change the epochs
Interleaving with an "all exhausted" strategy repeats small streams; restarting without the sampler's state replays or skips data. Log achieved token counts per domain, not just the configured weights.
Testing a mixture on a single seed
Small proxy runs are noisy: Aioli's differences are tenths of a perplexity point. Several seeds per mixture, or many mixtures and a regression (RegMix's answer), before trusting a ranking.
Continuing the chat model instead of the base
Instruction following erodes within a billion tokens (Jindal et al.: up to 10 points). Continue the base, then post-train, or add the instruction residual back.
No general evaluation until the end of a CPT run
Forgetting happens in the first percent of steps. Track a general held-out loss from step zero, next to the domain loss.
Blaming the learning rate for all the forgetting
Under a strong shift most of the damage is the data: Ibrahim's never-re-warmed run still lost 76% as much. Lowering the peak trades adaptation for retention; replay is the lever that is nearly free.
Comparing losses across tokenizers or scales
Loss per token depends on the tokenizer, and a 0.2 rise means different things at 405M and 10B. Compare relative changes against the same model's base, on the same validation set.
Reading a headline budget as a run cost
Thomson Reuters' $40M is a two-year programme including staff; the final run was $450K. Say which one you compare.
Interview rehearsal (6 questions)
Why not just train on the natural shares of your corpus?
Size is not value: the biggest pile (web) drowns small high-value ones (code, maths, encyclopedic). But upweighting a small pile repeats it, so the choice is a trade between value per token and repeats; compute epochs and use a cap (UniMax) or a fitted method.
Explain DoReMi in three sentences.
Train a small reference model on default weights. Train a small proxy with Group DRO: each step, upweight domains (multiplicatively) by the proxy's excess loss over the reference, clipped at zero, and weight the proxy's loss by them. Average the weights over the run and train the big model on that mixture.
What is the rank-invariance assumption and how would you test it?
That the order of mixtures by loss is the same for tiny and large models. Train a handful of large runs on mixtures the regression ranks differently and compute the Spearman correlation between predicted and true order (RegMix: 0.97 from 1M to 1B on its data; check yours).
You have 50B tokens of excellent maths and a 4T-token run. How do you use them?
Mostly in the decay stage, where they can be a large share of a short run; up to about 4 epochs costs little, so a 200B-token anneal could take 50% maths at 2 epochs or 25% at 1. Keep a small share in the stable phase if the model must not lose maths early. Validate with an anneal ablation and a decay-only control.
How do you pick the replay ratio for a legal-domain continued pretraining?
Run 3 or 4 short CPT runs at different domain shares, fit general and domain loss against share and tokens (CMR law), and take the largest share keeping general loss within your tolerance; replay data as close as possible to the base model's pretraining mix. Refit if the base model or the budget changes.
System design: plan the data mixture for a 3T-token pretraining run from six sources, with a fixed budget of 2% of the run's compute for mixture search.
Inventory: unique tokens per source after dedup and decontamination, with the tokenizer of the run. Constraints: max epochs per source (4 for anything that matters), minimum shares the product needs (code, languages). Search: RegMix-style, a few hundred proxy runs of about 1M non-embedding parameters on random Dirichlet mixtures inside the constraints, targets chosen by correlation with the eval suite; fit a nonlinear regressor and the exponential law, compare their recommendations; confirm the top 2 or 3 with a few 1B-scale runs (rank check). Stages: stable phase on the searched mix, a decay over the last 5 to 10% with the best sources upweighted to their epoch caps, chosen by anneal ablations from a mid-run checkpoint. Loader: per-source shards, deterministic blending, sampler state in checkpoints, per-source token logs. Risks: rank flips at scale, contamination through upweighted small sources, and the anneal hiding a weak base mix.

What the recipes share

  • Almost everyone replays. General shares run from 1% (Meditron) to 80% (Llama-3-SynE, whose "general" is English kept while adding Chinese); the median of the 13 that state one is 10% derived. Only Chinese-LLaMA describes none.
  • Peaks vary a hundredfold. From the base's own peak (Code Llama, Meditron) to 1/150 of it (DACP); the most common choices are one third and one tenth.
  • Few measure forgetting. Seven of the fourteen report a general benchmark before and after (DeepSeek-Coder-V2, DeepSeekMath, ChipNeMo, Swallow, e-Llama, Thomson Reuters, Llama-3-SynE); the largest drops are about five or six points on one benchmark (NaturalQuestions 53.4 to 47.5 for DeepSeek-Coder-V2, e-Llama 8B's leaderboard 17.2 to 12.6), and some general scores rise, nothing like the collapse of the no-replay runs in Reading, section 6a.
  • Token budgets range from 24B (ChipNeMo) to 6T (DeepSeek-Coder-V2, continued from a checkpoint before its learning-rate decay, the infinite-schedule idea in practice).
  • From scratch is the exception. BloombergGPT trained 50.6B parameters from scratch on 569B tokens, about half financial; since 2023 the domain models in the table all start from an open base.

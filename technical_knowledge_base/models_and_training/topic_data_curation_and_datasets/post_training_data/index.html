The idea in one screen

Every post-training row is a prompt plus a signal of what good looks like: a written answer, a comparison of two answers, a checker program, or a test suite in a sandbox. The data is exactly as good as that signal, so building these sets is making the signal cheap (a stronger model writes it, a model judges it, a program checks it) and then checking the signal itself.

Four kinds of row: the same prompt slot, a different signal, and the way each went wrong when measured here

Blue: the signal the model learns from. Orange: the failure measured on this page (sections 1, 5, 6 and 7). A pretraining corpus can be noisy because noise averages out over trillions of tokens; post-training is a few hundred thousand to a few million rows, and a wrong row teaches the wrong thing directly.

1. SFT data: who writes the answers

A pretrained model continues text; a few hundred thousand examples of "request, then a good answer" teach it the format, the tone and when to stop. The question has always been where the answers come from: people are slow and expensive; models are fast and cheap but pass on their mistakes and their terms of use.

Answers moved from people to models, and sets grew from 1,000 rows to over 2 million

Rows on a log axis, by release date; colour says who wrote the answers. published sizes (each with its paper or card in the table below). The two dots at 1,000 rows, LIMA (2023) and s1K (2025), carry the counter-lesson: the "Superficial Alignment Hypothesis" (the format is learned from very few examples) and, for reasoning, Qwen2.5-32B tuned on 1,000 verified traces "exceeds o1-preview on competition math questions by up to 27%".

Self-Instruct's filter: the same candidates with and without it

Self-Instruct starts a pool with 175 human-written tasks and asks the model for new ones, 8 pool instructions per prompt (6 human, 2 generated). A candidate joins only if its ROUGE-L with every instruction already there is below 0.7: F = 2 × LCS / (words in a + words in b), where LCS is the longest common subsequence of words. Below, the same stream of real candidates (Dolly instructions, written by people and never filtered) goes into the pool with the filter on and off.

human seed (175)admitted candidaterejected candidate

measured On whole sets: the filter would reject 3,833 of Dolly's 15,011 instructions (25.5%; replaying it in file order rejects 2,672). Alpaca, filtered this way, has 0 pairs above 0.7 but 1,501 instructions with a neighbour at exactly 0.7: the fingerprint of a rule that rejects only when the score is strictly greater (generate_instruction.py line 196 at commit 761dc5bf). Even 4 of the 175 human seeds have a neighbour above 0.7 ("Write a conversation based on the given facts." and "Write a cover letter based on the given facts.").

The filter measures wording, not task
Short instructions cross 0.7 easily: "When did World War II finish?" and "When did World War II begin?" score 0.83 (two Dolly instructions). Two different tasks in the same template are rejected; a paraphrase of the same task passes. It keeps a pool lexically varied, which is what it was for; duplicate tasks need near-duplicate detection on prompt and answer together (MinHash, embeddings: Deduplication). Its cost is one LCS per pool member per candidate, quadratic in the pool; Alpaca's code ran it with a process pool.

2. Distillation: the teacher's errors and terms travel with its answers

Almost every open SFT and preference set since Alpaca is distilled: prompts from people or templates, responses from a teacher model. The student inherits the teacher's style and errors (a filter for "As an AI developed by OpenAI" exists because students learned to say it; the root's Post-training data lab runs Ai2's identity and cutoff filters), and the teacher's terms of use travel with its outputs. 3. Reasoning traces: rejection sampling, and who checks the checker

To teach a long chain of thought by SFT you need traces that end in the right answer: a good-looking trace with a wrong answer teaches the wrong habit. So reasoning SFT data is made by rejection sampling: several traces per problem from a strong model, kept when a checker accepts the final answer. OpenR1-Math-220k is the open example: DeepSeek-R1 on 400k NuminaMath 1.5 problems, two (sometimes four) traces each, 16k-token limit, kept when Math-Verify accepts the answer or, for 12% of samples, when Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct as a judge does (card). measured on 12,358 R1 generations for 6,000 of its problems:

The thinking is about 89% of the tokens (median); the answer after </think> is a short summary that repeats the result. That matters twice: SFT trains on all of it (section 4), and a checker that reads the whole text can pick up an intermediate guess from the thinking (section 6).

Is the second checker right? Math-Verify (Feb 2025) accepted 7,975 (64.5%) of the 12,358 generations. The Llama judge ran only on rows Math-Verify rejected and said yes on 2,701 of 3,545 (76%). Thirty of those rescues, read by hand (seeded sample, labels in src/notes/judge_labels.md, an agent's reading):

Most judge rescues are format mismatches, not wrong answers

An option letter against the option's value, a solution set written another way, a label garbled when it was extracted from the source solution. Two of 30 looked wrong, so the judge's error rate on rescues is somewhere between about 2% and 21% (95% Wilson interval): a judge pass rescues real data and lets some wrong traces in.

Predict: if you rerun Math-Verify today (0.8.0) on the same answers, how often does it agree with the verdict recorded in February 2025?
95.9% (on the answer after </think>; 501 disagreements: today's version accepts 336 answers the old one rejected, and rejects 165 it accepted). A checker is code with versions; a dataset's correctness flags are only as current as the checker that wrote them. Record the checker's version next to every verdict, and re-run the one you will train with on a sample.
Two other published trace recipes (DeepSeek-R1's SFT data, OLMo 3's thinking SFT)

DeepSeek-R1's SFT data: rejection sampling from its own RL checkpoint, "retain only the correct ones", filter "chain-of-thought with mixed languages, long paragraphs, and code blocks"; about 600k reasoning samples (Table 5: 626,933 math, code, STEM and logic) plus about 200k others (177,812). OLMo 3's thinking SFT reuses OpenThoughts3 traces, regenerates truncated ones with QwQ-32B and keeps code traces that pass tests written by GPT-4.1 (arXiv 2512.13961, 4.2.1).

4. What a row becomes: one conversation, one training example

A row is a list of turns; the model reads one string of tokens. A chat template renders the turns into that string with headers that say who speaks, and a loss mask decides which tokens are graded. The mechanics are owned elsewhere: how masked tokens drop out of the loss, and packing, on Alignment (section 1, its loss-mask widget); template syntax and special tokens on Tokenizers. Here is what they do to one real row of the Tulu 3 sample, rendered with open-instruct's "tulu" template and tokenized with the Qwen3 tokenizer.

Before: the row as stored. After: the string the model trains on, with only the answer graded

How much of a row is trained measured: the same template and masking on every set (at most 3,000 rows each, seed 0), share of all tokens that get loss.

Trained tokens per row run from 14% to 98%: budget in trained tokens, not rows

In a reasoning set nearly every token is the model's own trace, so nearly every token is trained (98%). In an agent trajectory the observations (file contents, test output) are most of the tokens and are masked: only 14% is the agent's. Two sets with the same number of rows can carry five times different amounts of training signal, so a mixture balanced by rows is not balanced by signal.

How open-instruct builds the mask, and three ways it goes wrong (code at pinned commits)

open-instruct renders the conversation with its "tulu" template (<|user|>, <|assistant|> headers, end-of-text token after each answer; dataset_transformation.py lines 260 to 279 at commit 72f46040), then, for each non-assistant message, renders the conversation up to that message and masks that span of tokens, including the <|assistant|> header that follows it (finetune.py lines 401 to 444 at the Tulu 3 release commit b0899767; today's code builds the same labels another way, lines 1598 to 1728). Rows padded, not packed.

Template drift: the base model's tokenizer ships one template, the training script uses another, the serving stack a third; whitespace and the end-of-turn token are the usual differences. Render a row with each and diff the strings. No end token: if the token after the answer is masked or missing, the model never learns to stop. Masking by string search: finding "assistant" in the text to start the mask breaks on any answer that contains the word; render prefixes, as open-instruct does.

5. Preference data: pairs, judges and the length trap

SFT can only copy; pairs teach a model to prefer the better of answers it can already write (DPO and relatives, or a reward model). Whatever separates chosen from rejected is what the model learns, intended or not.

Does on-policy help? Tulu 3's ablation (Figure 11, read from figure): 8B SFT model 58.2; DPO on 100k off-policy pairs 60.0; on 100k pairs with one on-policy answer 60.7; on the combined 120k 61.2 (arXiv 2411.15124, section 5). On-policy pairs show the model its own likely mistakes; here that is worth less than a point, and the mixture is best.

Binarization: the same ratings, different pairs

UltraFeedback gives four rated answers per prompt; DPO needs one pair, and the rule that picks it is a data decision. Hugging Face's ultrafeedback_binarized card says it took the highest overall_score and a random other answer (the Zephyr paper, trained on it, says "highest mean score"); Argilla found overall_score is a critique score that "will give a 10 to responses that get a 1" in some rows, and rebuilt the pairs from the mean of the four aspect ratings, dropping ties. measured Across all 10,000 prompts the two common rules pick the same chosen answer for only 5,461 of the 9,935 prompts where both apply (55.0%; Argilla reported "a different chosen response in ~30K examples (out of ~63K)" for the whole set). Length: the signal nobody asked for

Within each UltraFeedback prompt, GPT-4's mean rating correlates with the answer's length at +0.33 on average (Spearman, 9,931 prompts with untied ratings and lengths) measured; the longest of the four answers is (one of) the top-rated in 46% of prompts, against 25% by chance. Per aspect:

Longer answers are often more complete, so this is not proof of bias; but a model trained on these pairs can lower its loss by writing more. Published: always choosing the longer answer is right 55.7%, 59.6% and 63.1% of the time on WebGPT, Stack Exchange and RLCD preferences, and a reward of length alone reproduces most of RLHF's measured gains on two of them (Singhal et al., Table 5 and Table 2). Across eight sets, the share of pairs whose chosen answer is longer (Qwen3 tokens; 50% means length says nothing):

Six of eight preference sets favour the longer answer, human-labelled ones included

6 of 8 sets lean towards the longer answer, most strongly Dolci-Instruct-DPO (67%); the exceptions are HH-RLHF harmless and Tulu 3 persona IF, where the chosen answer is more often the shorter one (plausibly because a safe refusal is short, and a constraint such as a word limit rewards brevity; not tested here). Human labels lean too: HH-RLHF helpful is at 59%.

Predict: OLMo 3's delta-learning pairs (Qwen3-32B chosen, Qwen3-0.6B rejected) have no judge at all. Is the chosen answer longer more or less often than in GPT-judged pairs of the same set?
More often. In the 5,000-pair sample, delta pairs have the chosen answer longer in 73% of 2,389 pairs, GPT-judged pairs in 62% of 2,413 (Qwen3 tokens; measured). Removing the judge does not remove the length signal: the larger model simply writes more, so length enters through the generator. OLMo 3 also caps the length difference at 100 tokens for its Instruct chat and multi-turn DPO subsets (OLMo 3 paper), which limits this.

The fixes are in the data or the loss: cap the length difference (OLMo 3, 100 tokens for chat), balance pairs by length, rate verbosity separately (HelpSteer2), or length-normalise DPO (Tulu 3). A small open judge is mostly inconsistent rather than biased by position: shown the same pair twice, Qwen3-4B gives the same verdict in only 67% of 300 pairs; it scores the first-shown answer higher in 55% of untied judgements (a mild position bias); it agrees with GPT-4's chosen answer in both orders for 53%; and when its verdict is consistent it prefers the longer answer in 66% of 194 pairs. Judging each pair in both orders and keeping only consistent verdicts is the cheap fix; it doubles the cost and here drops a third of the pairs.

Not run here: generating our own delta pairs (a large and a small Qwen3 answering the same prompts). 6. Verifiable-reward data: a prompt, a label and a checker

RL needs a reward for every sampled answer, millions of times. Where an answer can be checked mechanically (a number, a passing test, a formatting rule), RLVR uses a program, so a row is a prompt, a label (reference answer, tests, constraint parameters) and the name of a checker. The model learns whatever the checker rewards: a false negative (a right answer scored 0) pushes it away from correct behaviour, a false positive pays it for a wrong one. How the reward drives the update, and what verifier errors do during RL, is on RL for LLMs (section 5); here the question is whether the data's labels and checkers are right.

Math: extracting the answer is the hard part

A checker extracts an answer from free text and compares it with the label (as strings after normalising LaTeX, or symbolically). The last-number rule fails on anything that is not a single number (fractions, intervals, option letters); exact \boxed{} fails on \dfrac against \frac; R1 almost never writes the Minerva sentence. For IF rows, Ai2's count matches the sum of partial credit (share of constraints met per rollout), not the number of rollouts meeting every constraint; the small gap is consistent with open-instruct's later fix to its word-counting checkers (commit 72f46040, "Fix IFEvalG word-counting checkers").

Not run here: a local model (Qwen3-1.7B) answering these problems. Two sets of real model answers (DeepSeek-R1's in OpenR1 and OLMo 3's stored rollouts) already show every kind of disagreement; a small local model would add messier answers at a GPU cost judged not worth it (decision of 2026-10-15).

Difficulty: keep the prompts the model sometimes solves

A prompt the model always solves gives every rollout the same reward, so under group-relative methods (GRPO and relatives) its advantage is zero and it teaches nothing; a prompt it never solves does the same. OLMo 3 generated eight rollouts per prompt from the initial checkpoint and removed "all samples that the model easily solves (that is, those with a pass rate greater than 62.5%)" (Sec. 4.4.2).

The 62.5% cut is visible in the released data: nothing above 5 of 8

measured Over all 81,378 math, code and IF rows with a recorded pass rate, the highest is 0.625 (5 of 8), and 13,856 rows (17.0%) have a pass rate under 1/16 (in effect none of eight right), kept although they give no signal at the start of training: the 7B run filtered only from above.

Code: tests are labels, and labels can be weak

A code row is a problem, hidden tests (input, expected output) and a sandbox that runs the program. Passing the gold solution does not make a suite strong: on hard problems TACO's tests accept wrong solutions often enough that "false positive rate" exceeds 90% (HardTests, arXiv 2505.24098). Mutation testing measures it: for 300 seeded problems, the gold solution and up to four mutants (one small change each: flip a comparison, add 1 to a constant, swap + and −, swap min and max) run against the problem's tests (up to 50) in a sandbox with no network and no file writes. A mutant that passes every test is, to the reward, the gold solution.

About one mutant in five survives every test; single-test problems are the weakest

Of 870 mutants of 295 gold solutions, 167 (19%) passed every test, in 112 problems. Read by hand (20 survivors, src/notes/code_survivors.md): 10 change the output on some valid input (one prints a-b as a+b and passes both of its tests), 7 touch a boundary that valid inputs may never reach, 3 are equivalent. So roughly one mutant in ten is a wrong program the reward would pay for. Single-test problems are the weakest: 41 of 70 had a survivor, against 43 of 154 with more than 20 tests. The fix is in the data: generated inputs checked against the gold solution (HardTests, CodeContests+), or tests chosen because they kill mutants.

How many problems the tests themselves cost (open-r1's Python set)

open-r1's Python set went from 35,735 problems (from PrimeIntellect's SYNTHETIC-1 pool) to 27,839 after 8-gram decontamination, to 15,068 in its "decontaminated-tested" version, where every gold solution passes its own tests (test_reward = 1 on every row measured; the card does not say how the others failed). The mutation test above samples this tested version.

Instruction-following constraints

IFEval defined 25 constraint types a program can check ("answer in all lowercase", "at least 300 words", "end with this exact phrase") over 541 prompts (arXiv 2311.07911); RLVR sets attach such constraints to ordinary prompts (Tulu 3's 14,973 IF rows, one each; Ai2's IF_multi_constraints_upto5, 95,373 rows with up to five, used for OLMo 3's RL). The reward is partial credit: open-instruct's IFEvalVerifier returns the share of constraints met (ground_truth_utils.py lines 310 to 355 at 72f46040), not IFEval's all-or-nothing score. On OLMo 3's IF rollouts measured, 7,545 rollouts meet all their constraints 370 times, partial credit sums to 2,655.0, and Ai2 recorded 2,645 correct: a rollout meeting three of five constraints earns 0.6.

Public prompt pools and instruction-following constraint sets (published sizes)

published GSM8K and MATH (7,473 and 7,500 training problems); DeepScaleR, about 40,000 competition problems; DAPO-Math-17k, 17K prompts each with an integer answer (so last-number checking is enough); Big-Math, over 250,000 questions with verifiable answers; Guru, 92K verifiable examples across math, code, science, logic, simulation and tables (arXiv 2506.14965); for code, TACO (26,443 problems) and KodCode (447K question, solution and test triplets).

Tulu 3's IF rows use 24 constraint types, about 620 rows each (measured, root lab); IF_multi follows IFBench's training data, which pairs Tulu 3 SFT prompts with "at least one and up to n constraints" and adds 29 new constraint types (arXiv 2507.02833). IFEval scores in a strict and a loose mode (the loose one also tries the answer without its first line, last line or markdown asterisks).

7. Environments and trajectories: the row is a task

For agents a row is a task the model acts in. A software-engineering (SWE) task is a repository at a commit, an issue, a container image that can run the tests, and the tests that decide success: FAIL_TO_PASS (tests the fix must make pass) and PASS_TO_PASS (tests that must keep passing). The model explores and edits through tools; its trajectory (actions, observations) is recorded and the tests score it. How harnesses and rollouts run is on Harnesses for training and evaluation (its Environment lab tab, and section 6 for turning rollouts into training data); this section is about the rows.

Newer ways to manufacture environments (published 2025 to 2026; not run here)

Answer first, question second: ToolGrad builds a valid chain of tool calls first and only then writes the user query for it, reaching a 99.8% pass rate on ToolBench's 16k+ real APIs against 63.8% for query-first search; Gemma 3 12B fine-tuned on 500 of its examples scored 83.1 on BFCL against Gemini 2.5 Pro's 83.2 (arXiv 2508.04086). The half that must be correct is constructed, not sampled and then checked. A trajectory contains its environment: Terminal-Universe replays the file operations recorded in agent trajectories to rebuild their workspaces, lets an agent fill in missing files and dependencies, and grows tasks from them: 37.3k task-sufficient environments; SFT of Qwen3.5-27B on them gained 11.9 points on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (arXiv 2609.04148). Reshape an existing environment: EnvHarness wraps a static environment in plug-in components, written by an LLM from the policy's diagnosed failures, keeping the original verifier (up to 9.0 points on held-out instances; arXiv 2608.19880). A fourth data type sits beside instructions and traces: operational know-how, such as Repo-To-Skill's AREX-Skill Library of 5,000+ verified skills distilled from 1,000 ML repositories, which made a GPT-5.5 research agent score 134.3% higher on MLE-bench (relative) at a fixed budget (arXiv 2609.02749).

8. Quality control: one prompt pool, three stages

The pretraining steps (dedup, decontamination, filtering) all apply, with three differences: rows are few, so each one matters; prompts are reused on purpose across stages; and the evaluations that will be reported are known in advance, so contamination is both easier to check and more damaging.

A prompt travels: the same pool feeds SFT, preference and RL, each with a different signal

measured Every row of IF_multi_constraints_upto5 (OLMo 3's RL constraints) records the SFT row its base prompt came from: of 95,373 rows, 92,617 come from Tulu 3 SFT subsets (Persona Hub 24,995, Evol CodeAlpaca 11,243, Aya 10,502, ...), and all 493 whose id is in our 5,000-row SFT sample start with that SFT row's prompt.

That is by design: one pool, a different "what good looks like" per stage. It has two consequences. A prompt contaminated in one stage is contaminated in all of them, so decontaminate once, at the pool. And an RL prompt can be a jailbreak attempt with a formatting rule bolted on: 10,741 rows (11%) come from the two adversarial safety subsets (WildJailbreak, WildGuardMix); rewarding "answer in lowercase" on a harmful request rewards compliance with the format, so the safety behaviour has to come from elsewhere.

Depth

Mistakes that are easy to make with this data (each tied to a measurement above)
  • Trusting a dataset's correctness flags. They were written by a checker at a version: Math-Verify today disagrees with OpenR1's February 2025 verdicts on 501 of 12,358 answers, and an LLM judge accepts 76% of what Math-Verify rejected (section 3).
  • Reading "chosen" as "better". It is "rated higher by this judge under this binarization rule". Ties, identical pairs and length effects are in the data (section 5); check the margin and length distributions before training.
  • Assuming tests the gold solution passes are good tests. 19% of mutants survived all tests on the sampled code problems (section 6). Add tests that kill mutants, or generate inputs.
  • An n-gram contamination rule on instruction-following data flags the constraint template, not the request (section 8): strip the constraint text first.
Design rehearsal: the post-training data pipeline for a 7B reasoning model (interview-style synthesis of the page)

Prompt pool first: sources per skill (math, code, science, chat, IF, safety, tool use), each with licence and origin recorded; exact and near dedup across sources; decontamination of prompts against every evaluation you will report, by 8-gram overlap plus an embedding pass for paraphrases, removing rows, not only datasets. SFT: generate several traces per prompt with a permissively licensed teacher; keep those whose answers pass a checker (math), tests (code) or constraint verifiers (IF); filter teacher artefacts, language mixing and truncation; budget by trained tokens. Preference: on-policy samples from the SFT model plus a stronger model; judge with a rubric and both orders, or use delta pairs; cap length differences; drop ties. RL: prompts with labels in canonical form; checkers tested on reference solutions and on model outputs before use; offline difficulty filter (pass rate between 0 and about 60% over 8 rollouts). Throughout: per-subset counts at every step, a read sample, the checker and generator versions stored per row, and ablations per subset, because quality is measured by the model trained on it. Scale: generation dominates cost (millions of long traces); checking and dedup are cheap CPU jobs.

The minimum to store per row: source dataset and row id, generator model and version, judge or checker and version, licence, the prompt's origin (to trace reuse across stages), decontamination result, and the date. Every later filter depends on these.

A distilled model claims to be another company's assistant: the teacher's identity statements were in the responses. Filter identity and knowledge-cutoff patterns (Ai2's open-instruct scripts do), add hard-coded identity rows for your own model, and check by sampling identity questions after training.

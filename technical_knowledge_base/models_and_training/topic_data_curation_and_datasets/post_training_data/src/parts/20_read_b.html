1. SFT data: who writes the answers

A pretrained model continues text; a few hundred thousand examples of "request, then a good answer" teach it the format, the tone and when to stop. The question has always been where the answers come from: people are slow and expensive; models are fast and cheap but pass on their mistakes and their terms of use.

Answers moved from people to models, and sets grew from 1,000 rows to over 2 million

Rows on a log axis, by release date; colour says who wrote the answers. published sizes (each with its paper or card in the table below). The two dots at 1,000 rows, LIMA (2023) and s1K (2025), carry the counter-lesson: the "Superficial Alignment Hypothesis" (the format is learned from very few examples) and, for reasoning, Qwen2.5-32B tuned on 1,000 verified traces "exceeds o1-preview on competition math questions by up to 27%".

Self-Instruct's filter: the same candidates with and without it

Self-Instruct starts a pool with 175 human-written tasks and asks the model for new ones, 8 pool instructions per prompt (6 human, 2 generated). A candidate joins only if its ROUGE-L with every instruction already there is below 0.7: F = 2 × LCS / (words in a + words in b), where LCS is the longest common subsequence of words. Below, the same stream of real candidates (Dolly instructions, written by people and never filtered) goes into the pool with the filter on and off.

Alpaca, filtered this way, has @@si_alp_above@@ pairs above 0.7 but @@si_alp_exact@@ instructions with a neighbour at exactly 0.7: the fingerprint of a rule that rejects only when the score is strictly greater (generate_instruction.py line 196 at commit 761dc5bf). Even @@si_seed_above@@ of the 175 human seeds have a neighbour above 0.7 ("Write a conversation based on the given facts." and "Write a cover letter based on the given facts.").

The filter measures wording, not task
Short instructions cross 0.7 easily: @@si_dolly_short_example@@. Two different tasks in the same template are rejected; a paraphrase of the same task passes. It keeps a pool lexically varied, which is what it was for; duplicate tasks need near-duplicate detection on prompt and answer together (MinHash, embeddings: {{Deduplication|n:3f95c17b0d0d8171ac17e43ab5ea276f}}). Its cost is one LCS per pool member per candidate, quadratic in the pool; Alpaca's code ran it with a process pool.

Today's SFT sets are mixtures, built skill by skill

The skills a program can check grow fastest

Every row of three Ai2 mixtures, counted by the dataset's own source column (Hugging Face statistics over the full train split, fetched 2026-10-15; the skill grouping is ours, the counts are not). Tulu 3 and OLMo 2 used the same mixture (one hard-coded identity subset differs). OLMo 3's instruct mixture is more than twice as large and adds tool use (@@mx_dolci_tool@@ rows), verifiable reasoning and logic puzzles. Math is over a third of Tulu 3; in OLMo 3 verifiable reasoning, puzzles and code with tests are the largest groups. A generated answer that can be checked can be kept only when it is right, which makes those rows cheap to trust.

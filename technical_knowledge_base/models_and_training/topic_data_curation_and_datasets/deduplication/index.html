The web repeats itself. The same cookie banner sits on thousands of sites, a news story is syndicated to hundreds, a shop renders the same template for every product, and Common Crawl recrawls the same pages every month. Deduplication finds those copies and decides which to keep. This page teaches it from zero: what the evidence says it buys, then every method a pretraining pipeline uses, each with the problem it solves, how it works, what it costs, when it matters and how to check it, measured on the topic's running example.

The running example, used on every tab of the root page: one WARC file (Common Crawl's raw archive of HTTP responses) from the July 2024 crawl CC-MAIN-2024-30, 20,991 responses, taken through a FineWeb-style datatrove pipeline (run0). Its stages: 19,241 extracted documents (text out of HTML), 2,692 filtered documents (English, passed the quality rules; this is what reaches dedup), 2,617 final. Everything labelled measured was run on an Apple M1 Pro, CPU only, in October 2026; published numbers are the authors' own, at their scale, with the table they come from; derived numbers show their formula; vendor marks a claim by someone selling the tool. The three things to remember
  1. Duplication is about scope. One WARC file has 2.8% near-duplicate documents; a whole crawl, then all crawls, have far more. DCLM measured that a global MinHash would still remove 85% of its finished, already deduplicated DCLM-baseline (published Table 23). Every number on this page says over what scope it was measured.
  2. Removing copies cuts memorisation strongly and wasted compute clearly; its effect on benchmark scores is small and sometimes negative. The evidence in section 2 is consistent on the first and mixed on the second.
  3. Each method sees a different unit, so they are run in sequence, cheapest first: exact hashes, then MinHash on whole documents, then (optionally) suffix arrays on spans, then semantic dedup where paraphrase is the problem. The Method comparer runs every combination on the same 2,692 documents.

2. Does deduplication help? The evidence, table by table

Three separate claims are often merged into "dedup makes models better". Each has different evidence.

Claim 1: duplicated text is memorised, and dedup cuts that sharply

Memorisation here means a model reproducing a stretch of its training text verbatim. The evidence is strong and consistent.

  • Lee et al. (2021, ACL 2022) trained 1.5B-parameter models on C4 and on two deduplicated versions: NearDup (MinHash on space-separated 5-grams, 9,000 hashes, candidates confirmed by Jaccard > 0.8 and edit similarity > 0.8) and ExactSubstr (suffix arrays, every repeated span of 50 or more tokens removed). Unprompted, the original model emitted memorised text in 1.926% of its output tokens after one epoch, against 0.189% and 0.138% for the deduplicated ones (Table 4): ten times less. The same table at two epochs: 1.571%, 0.264%, 0.168%.
  • Kandpal, Wallace and Raffel (2022) measured how memorisation scales with the number of copies: a sequence present 10 times in training is generated about 1,000 times more often than one present once (slopes above 1 on a log-log plot, Figure 1). On Lee et al.'s models, the deduplicated model generated 68,090 training sequences against 1,427,212 for the original, about 21 times fewer (Table 1), and two of three membership-inference attacks lost most of their power (AUROC 0.76 to 0.67 and 0.86 to 0.68; the third, 0.88 to 0.87, barely moved).
  • Carlini et al. (2022, ICLR 2023) found memorisation grows log-linearly with model size, with the number of duplicates and with prompt length. On Lee's deduplicated C4 model, sequences repeated fewer than 35 times were memorised at 1.2% against 3.6% for the original; but for sequences repeated more than about 100 times dedup "does not help", because the deduplication had missed them (Section 5.2).

Claim 2: repeated data wastes compute and can hurt

  • Hernandez et al. (Anthropic, 2022) trained models on 90% unique data plus 10% made of a small subset repeated many times. Their abstract: performance of an 800M-parameter model "could be degraded to that of a 2x smaller model (400M params) by repeating 0.1% of the data 100 times". The body puts the loss "nearly as high as for the 340M" model; both are read from a figure (arXiv 2205.10487). The damage comes from a few heavily repeated items, exactly what MinHash's largest clusters are.
  • Muennighoff et al. (2023) showed that repeating a whole dataset up to about 4 epochs costs almost nothing (see the data mixing page): uniform repetition is not the problem; skewed repetition is.

Claim 3: dedup improves benchmark scores

FineWeb's per-crawl finding, read carefully

FineWeb first deduplicated all 96 crawls together, newest crawl first, so a page that appeared in a later crawl was removed from every earlier one. That removed "as much as 90%" of the oldest crawls and left 4T tokens, and models trained on it improved little over no dedup. On the oldest crawl, 2013-48, the about 31B tokens that survived scored worse than the about 460B tokens removed (deduplicated on their own to 171B). Their hypothesis: the pages that never reappear in later crawls are disproportionately low quality, so global dedup "upsamples lower-quality data"; the gain of dedup comes from removing the huge clusters, and deduplicating clusters smaller than about 100 "can harm performance" (Section 3.4). Per-crawl MinHash gave 20T tokens and matched RefinedWeb; lighter global passes on top (URL dedup, which would remove 71.5% of tokens; line dedup 77.8%; 3-line dedup 80.9%) were all worse (Appendix E.3).

Read the arithmetic
"10% kept, 90% removed" is rounded: 31 / (31 + 460) = 6.3% kept. The paper's text points to Figure 4 for a comparison that is in Figure 3, and the figure values are not given as numbers. What it shows: at FineWeb's scale, what survives global dedup is a biased sample. What it does not show: that duplicates are good; the authors still deduplicate inside each crawl.

How much duplication is left in the corpora people train on

Implied duplicate shares: 85%, 80%, 74%. The text rounds these to "around 80%".
  • Zyphra (Zyda-2, 2024): "we believe we have found 80% duplicates" in DCLM, and "about 80% for both" DCLM and FineWeb-Edu in the technical report, with MinHash on character 25-grams, 128 hashes, 8 bands (about 85% Jaccard); "80%" is not defined further, and they did not deduplicate DCLM internally for the release because their first ablations showed "only a small effect".

About the same. 3. Exact dedup: hashes of URLs, documents, paragraphs and lines

The problem. The cheapest duplicates are byte-identical: the same page fetched twice, the same file mirrored, the same "Home | About | Contact" line on every page of a site.

How it works. A hash function maps any text to a fixed-size number (64 or 128 bits) so that equal inputs always give equal numbers and different inputs almost never do. Hash every unit, keep the first unit with each hash value, drop the rest. On one machine the "seen" set is a hash table; on many machines, items are routed by their hash (all items whose hash starts with the same bits go to the same worker, a shuffle), so each worker deduplicates its own share and no worker needs the whole set.

The unit decides what you catch. Common Crawl already removes repeated URLs inside one crawl, so URL dedup only matters across crawls (Llama 3 keeps the most recent version of each URL across its whole dataset, Section 3.1.1). Document hashes catch mirrors and empty or error pages. Line and paragraph hashes catch boilerplate, and also strip structure (table separators, list markers, repeated headings). Normalising before hashing (lowercase, no punctuation, digits as 0) catches a few more.

import hashlib, re
def h(s):                       # 8 bytes of SHA-1 is plenty below ~10^9 items (see the calculator)
    return hashlib.sha1(s.encode("utf-8")).digest()[:8]
def norm(s):                    # light normalisation: case, punctuation, digits, spaces
    s = re.sub(r"\d", "0", s.lower())
    return " ".join(re.sub(r"[^\w\s]", " ", s).split())

seen_doc, seen_norm, seen_line = set(), set(), set()
dup_doc = dup_norm = lines = dup_lines = 0
for t in docs:                  # run0's 19,241 extracted documents
    k = h(t);       dup_doc += k in seen_doc;   seen_doc.add(k)
    k = h(norm(t)); dup_norm += k in seen_norm; seen_norm.add(k)
    for line in t.split("\n"):
        if line.strip():
            lines += 1; k = h(line); dup_lines += k in seen_line; seen_line.add(k)

How big must the hash be?

Two different items with the same hash are a collision: the second one is wrongly dropped. With n distinct items and a b-bit hash, the expected number of colliding pairs is about n2 / 2b+1 (the birthday problem: there are n2/2 pairs and each collides with probability 2−b).

derived expected colliding pairs = n(n−1)/2 · 2−b, and memory for a plain set = n × b/8 bytes (a hash table needs 1.5 to 2 times more). A 32-bit hash is unusable past a few tens of thousands of items; 64 bits is fine for documents and marginal for 1012 lines.

Line and span dedup in practice

  • CCNet (used by Llama 1 and the first Dolma) hashes paragraphs within groups of shards of one crawl; in Dolma it "removed approximately 70% of paragraphs, primarily consisting of headers and navigation elements" (Dolma Section 5.1).
  • Llama 3 removes "lines that appeared more than 6 times in each bucket of 30M documents", and notes this removes "frequent high-quality text" too, yet "our empirical evaluations showed strong improvements" (Section 3.1.1). A count threshold rather than "keep the first" keeps a line that recurs a few times, such as a common quote.
  • Dolma v1, all with its Bloom filter (section 8): exact URL dedup removed 53.2% of documents, exact document dedup 14.9% of the rest, exact paragraph dedup 18.7% of paragraphs (Section 5.4). Dolma 3 (2025): exact dedup removed 67% of the pool after heuristic filtering, before MinHash.
  • C4 removed every repeated three-sentence span; datatrove's SentenceDedup implements it (the root lab measured it: 70 of 2,692 documents dropped, 24 shortened).

When it matters: always, first: it is a single pass and removes the bulk at web scale. How to check it: count items per hash and read the 50 most frequent; for a line rule, sample removed lines from pages you consider good (Llama 3's own warning).

4. MinHash and LSH, from zero

The problem. Most web duplicates are not byte-identical: a syndicated article with a different header, a product page that differs by one name, a forum page with a different visitor count. We want pairs whose text is mostly the same, among 109 documents, without comparing 5×1017 pairs.

Step 1: documents as sets of shingles

Normalise the text (lowercase, no punctuation, numbers normalised), split it into words, and take every run of n consecutive words: a shingle (FineWeb uses n = 5; Gopher 13; NeMo Curator uses 24 characters instead of words). A document becomes the set of its shingles, each kept once. Short shingles make unrelated documents look alike (common phrases); long ones make a single changed word break many shingles.

Step 2: Jaccard similarity

The Jaccard similarity of two sets is |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|, the shared shingles over all distinct shingles. Two forum pages from the running example share 43 of 73 distinct shingles: J = 0.589. "Near duplicate" means J above a threshold such as 0.75 or 0.8.

Step 3: the min-hash trick, and why it works

Pick a random hash function h that maps every shingle to a number, and keep each document's smallest value, minh(A). Then

P[ min h(A) = min h(B) ] = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| = J(A, B)

Proof (Broder 1997, Theorem 1): look at the smallest value over the union A ∪ B. Every shingle of the union is equally likely to own it. The two minimums are equal exactly when that shingle is in both sets, which happens for |A ∩ B| of the |A ∪ B| shingles.

With k independent hash functions, each document becomes a signature of k minimums, and the share of positions where two signatures agree is an unbiased estimate of J. The number of agreements is binomial, so the estimate's standard deviation is √(J(1−J)/k): at J = 0.75 and FineWeb's k = 112, 0.041. Halving the noise costs four times the hashes.

measured dots: the standard deviation of the estimate for 24 real pairs, each hashed with 2,000 independent families (the MinHash step by step tab shows the full histograms); derived lines: √(J(1−J)/k). The binomial model holds to within a few thousandths at every k.

In practice the "random functions" are a cheap family: datatrove hashes each shingle once to 64 bits (xxhash), then computes (a·x + b) mod (261−1) for 112 random pairs (a, b) in one vectorised line (minhash.py:182). Because the product is computed in unsigned 64-bit arithmetic it wraps around before the modulus, so the family is not exactly the textbook one; the measurements above (and the root lab's estimate-vs-truth chart) show it does not matter.

Step 4: locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) with bands

Signatures make each comparison cheap, but there are still too many pairs. LSH avoids pairwise comparison: cut the k = b×r values into b bands of r rows, and for each band group documents by the band's r values (a sort or a hash table). Two documents that agree on all r values of at least one band become a candidate pair.

P(candidate | J = s) = 1 − (1 − sr)b

A band matches with probability sr (all r rows equal, each with probability s). It fails with 1−sr; all b bands fail with (1−sr)b. The curve is an S whose steep part sits near s ≈ (1/b)1/r. FineWeb's 14 × 8 gives 56%, 77%, 92%, 98.8% at 0.70, 0.75, 0.80, 0.85, the numbers its Appendix E.1 states (recomputed here).

Rows sharpen, bands widen. More rows per band make the S steeper and push it right (fewer false candidates, more misses); more bands push it left (higher recall, more candidates, more hashes). Choosing them is a trade-off with a standard rule: datasketch picks the (b, r) that minimises a weighted sum of the area under the S left of the target (false positives) and above it right of the target (false negatives). With 128 hashes and equal weights it picks 11 × 11 for a 0.75 target and 9 × 13 for 0.8 (derived with its own code). The Band designer runs that rule and checks every choice against real pairs.

When it matters: every web pipeline. Cost: computing signatures is documents × shingles × k multiply-adds, embarrassingly parallel; the grouping is one sort per band (a shuffle across machines); memory is k × 8 bytes per document (112 hashes: 112 GB per 109 documents of 64-bit values). How to check it: sample candidate pairs in several similarity bins and read them; compute the true Jaccard of each candidate (cheap: candidates are few); recompute the S-curve and confirm it reproduces any probabilities a paper states before copying its setting (RefinedWeb's "20 buckets of 450" does not; section 12).

5. From pairs to clusters: union-find, chaining, and which copy to keep

The problem. LSH outputs pairs. Removing "one document of every pair" is ill-defined when pairs overlap (A~B and B~C), so pipelines treat candidates as edges of a graph and remove all but one document per connected component (a set of documents linked by any chain of edges).

How it works. Union-find (also called disjoint-set) keeps a forest: every document points to a parent, and a tree's root represents its set. find(x) follows parents to the root; union(x, y) points one root at the other. Two tricks make it nearly constant time per operation: path compression (after a find, point every node on the path straight at the root) and union by size (the smaller tree joins the larger, so trees stay shallow). datatrove's MinhashDedupCluster does exactly this in a Python dict (minhash.py:537-556), then removes every node that is not its root (579-583).

A detail that saves work: per band, datatrove sorts the signatures and emits only neighbouring equal pairs (minhash.py:471-480), so a group of m identical band values gives m−1 edges (a path), not m(m−1)/2. On the running example the 14 bands wrote 810 pair records for only 94 distinct pairs; union-find over them gives 31 clusters and removes 75 documents, and replaying it operation by operation reproduces run0's remove file exactly. The Union-find clusters tab animates those operations.

Chaining: clusters are not similarity groups

A component can join two documents that are not similar at all, through intermediate documents. In the running example's largest cluster (25 pages from 25 sites whose extracted text is one cookie-consent notice), a page with two sentences about a stove before the same banner has Jaccard 0.49 with the kept page; it joined through other banner pages and was removed. Over all 31 clusters the lowest similarity between two members of one cluster is 0.49.

The fix: check the true similarity inside clusters
Dolma 3 computes the true Jaccard similarity inside each cluster and removes only documents at 0.8 or more (allenai/dolma3 pool README). Applied against the kept root on the running example, that check removes 65 instead of 75: the 10 that come back are pages that joined through chains. Lee et al. confirmed candidates with Jaccard and edit similarity > 0.8; RefinedWeb skipped verification to favour recall ("our main concern is improving recall", Appendix G.3.1).

Which copy survives?

datatrove keeps the union-find root, whichever set won the size comparisons: on the running example the kept page is the longest member in 23 of 31 clusters and the lowest document index in 30. FineWeb describes it as "one randomly chosen document" per cluster. Other pipelines choose on purpose: Llama 3 keeps the most recent version of each URL; Dolma 3 keeps the most recent copy; Zyda-2 kept the copy from the source it ranked highest (FineWeb-Edu-2, then DCLM, then Zyda-1, then Dolma-CC). A good default for web text: the most recent crawl (fresher content, working links), then the longest extraction.

Keep the count. FineWeb-2 stores each kept document's cluster size as metadata, so a later step can repeat documents the web repeated ("rehydration", section 12).

Connected components at scale

  • One machine. datatrove's clustering step must run as a single task (assert world_size == 1, minhash.py:533); its example gives that task 2 CPUs with 70 GB each, 140 GB in total, and 90 hours (examples/minhash_deduplication.py:66-84). measured the same dict structure costs about 140 bytes per document that appears in any pair (tracemalloc, 106 nodes in clusters of 4), so 140 GB holds about 109 duplicated documents. A numpy parent array costs 4 to 8 bytes per document instead: scipy's connected_components labelled 107 nodes with 5×106 random edges in about 2.5 s (rough: one core, shared machine).
  • Many machines. Union-find is sequential; distributed systems use label propagation instead. The text-dedup Spark script implements Kiveris et al.'s "large-star / small-star" alternation (each round, every node links to the smallest label in its neighbourhood; about O(log2 n) rounds; minhash_spark.py:38-134 at tag 2023.09.20). NeMo Curator 1.4.0 runs cuGraph's multi-GPU weakly connected components (fuzzy/connected_components.py:20-21, 146).
  • Giant components. A boilerplate cluster can reach a million documents (Zyphra reports a DCLM cluster "close to 1 million documents"); a chain through templates can merge unrelated sets. Inspect the largest components before removing anything.

6. SimHash: one 64-bit fingerprint per document

The idea (Charikar 2002; named and scaled by Manku, Jain and Das Sarma at Google, 2007). Give every shingle a 64-bit hash. For each of the 64 bit positions, count +1 for every shingle whose hash has that bit set and −1 otherwise; the fingerprint's bit is 1 where the count is positive. Similar documents share most shingles, so most counts keep their sign and the fingerprints differ in few bits. Similarity is read as the Hamming distance (number of differing bits).

Why it works. Each bit is the sign of a random ±1 projection of the document's shingle vector, so two documents disagree on a bit with probability close to θ/π (exactly θ/π for Gaussian projections), where cos θ = |A ∩ B| / √(|A| |B|) (Charikar, Section 3). Expected Hamming distance = 64θ/π: 32 for unrelated documents, 0 for identical ones.

At Google's scale: "for a repository of 8 billion pages, 64-bit simhash fingerprints and k = 3 are reasonable", with precision and recall both "near 0.75" at k = 3 on their hand-tagged sample (Manku et al., Sections 3 and 4.1). Finding all fingerprints within 3 bits without comparing pairs uses permuted tables: split the 64 bits into blocks; any two fingerprints within 3 bits agree exactly on some choice of blocks, so each table sorts the fingerprints by one choice and only fingerprints sharing it are compared (their Example 3.1: 20 tables, at most 8 fingerprints per probe; or 4 tables, up to 256K per probe). Eight bytes per document against 896 for a 112-value MinHash signature.

measured On the running example's 2,692 filtered documents, with the same datatrove shingles MinHash uses: the 3,602,411 pairs with no shared shingle have mean Hamming distance 31.99 and standard deviation 4.00, exactly the 32 and 4 of a coin-flip model; the closest of them is 13 bits apart. At Manku's k = 3, SimHash finds 147 of the 308 pairs with Jaccard ≥ 0.8 (48%), all near-identical; it needs k = 8 to reach 91% (280), and makes its first candidate below Jaccard 0.5 at k = 13. Measured Hamming distance tracks 64θ/π well at the extremes and runs above it for cosine 0.6 to 0.9 (for example 13.7 measured vs 10.7 expected at cosine 0.8 to 0.9): the θ/π law is exact for Gaussian random projections, while SimHash uses ±1 hash bits on sets of 50 to 200 shingles, where the sign of a short sum ties and flips more often (a likely reason; not tested further here).

When it fits: near-identical detection with a tiny index (crawlers, URL-level recrawl detection), and as a cheap first pass. When it does not: catching documents that are 75% to 90% the same, where MinHash's banding gives a sharp, tunable threshold; text-dedup's benchmark on the CORE dataset found the same (MinHash duplicate recall 0.94 against 0.73 for SimHash, README at commit 7538f3e). The Method comparer shows SimHash at k = 3 removing a single document that exact hashing had not already removed.

7. Suffix arrays: exact substrings, the 50-token rule, and what they cost

The problem. MinHash judges whole documents. A 600-byte disclaimer pasted into a million otherwise different pages leaves each page unique by Jaccard, yet the model reads the disclaimer a million times.

How it works. Concatenate the corpus into one long string, with a unique separator before each document so no match can cross a boundary. A suffix array lists every starting position of that string, sorted by the text that follows it. Identical spans then sit next to each other in the sorted order, so one pass over neighbouring entries finds every span of length L or more that occurs at least twice anywhere, without comparing documents. The root's Dedup lab builds a small suffix array live in the browser.

Construction

Sorting n suffixes naively costs O(n log n) comparisons of long strings. SA-IS (Nong, Zhang and Chan, 2009) builds the array in linear time: it classifies each position as S-type or L-type (is its suffix smaller or larger than the next one), sorts only a subset of "LMS" substrings (at most half the positions), recursively solves the smaller problem, and induces the order of every other suffix from those in two linear scans. Google's deduplicate-text-datasets (Lee et al.'s tool) vendors an SA-IS implementation with 64-bit indices (src/table.rs:52-62, 115, 391-511 at 4e9888ac). For files larger than memory allows, scripts/make_suffix_array.py builds arrays of chunks in parallel and merges them with a heap; chunks overlap by HACK = 100000 bytes (line 23), so the merge is only correct when no repeated span is longer than that, which holds for documents of normal length.

The 50-token rule

Lee et al. remove every repeated span of 50 or more tokens: their plot of matches against length bends at about 10 tokens, and reading matches of 25 tokens found no false positives, so they doubled it (Appendix B). With --tokenize the tool stores GPT-2 token ids as 2 bytes each, so 50 tokens is a threshold of 100 bytes; on raw text the same rule is about 50 × 4.68 = 234 bytes on the running example (4.68 bytes per GPT-2 token, measured). RefinedWeb used Lee et al.'s 50 tokens after MinHash; Dolma 3 used 500 bytes.

measured Both forms on the running example's 2,692 filtered documents: bytes at 230 mark 2.50% of the text (146 documents touched); tokens at 50 mark 2.36% (144 documents, all among the 146); the share cut per document correlates at 0.997. One quirk: the tool compares bytes, so in token mode a match can start in the middle of a 2-byte token id; 18 of 216 ranges did. Use an even threshold and align ranges to token boundaries when you cut.

"Remove" means every copy
The tool's self-similar output lists every occurrence of a repeated span, the first included (main.rs:900-922), so deleting the ranges deletes the text everywhere. Keeping one copy (Dolma 3: "we ensure we keep at least one copy") removes 1.08% instead of 2.50% here. Lee et al. also ran the whole process twice, because cutting spans creates new repeats across the cut points (README line 246).

What it costs

  • measured (rough: shared machine, one core for make, low priority): building the array for 13 MB, 54 MB and 162 MB of FineWeb text took about 7 s, 47 s and 310 s, with peak memory 15, 11 and 7 bytes per text byte, and an array file of 3 to 4 bytes per text byte (the tool stores the smallest pointer width that addresses the file: 5 bytes at about a terabyte, 6 at hundreds of terabytes).
  • published Lee et al.: one machine with 96 cores and 768 GB built C4's array (350 GB) in under 12 hours, about 1,000 CPU-hours; finding the duplicates took under an hour more; Wiki-40B (4 GB) took 2.3 minutes. They describe an "8x space overhead" and a 1.5 TB array for C4; 1.5 TB over 350 GB is 4.3 times, so the 8 times is the 8-byte in-memory index per byte, not the file on disk. The README asks for more than 600 GB of RAM and 1 TB of disk for C4.
  • So suffix arrays run per shard at web scale: Nemotron-CC ran exact substring dedup "over eighths of snapshots", Dolma 3 on 56 shards.

Repeated spans grow with the scope

Two readings. First, MinHash leaves span-level repetition behind: FineWeb's output, already deduplicated per crawl, still has 0.30% of its text in repeated spans of 230 bytes within its first 4,000 rows, 0.46% within 16,000 and 0.55% within 48,000 (more WARC files, more chances for a shared disclaimer). Second, the curated corpora sit low at equal sample size (16,000 documents): FineWeb-Edu 0.21%, DCLM-baseline 0.18%, Nemotron-CC high 0.08%, against 2.50% in the running example before MinHash. A sample this small says little about a whole corpus (DCLM-baseline is 85% duplicates by a global MinHash); it says that a random slice of these corpora carries little boilerplate.

When it matters: after MinHash, for boilerplate inside unique pages (cookie text, legal footers, shop policies); RefinedWeb, Nemotron-CC and Dolma 3 use it, FineWeb and DCLM do not. DCLM's Table 17 found suffix arrays alone the strongest single step at 1B scale (26.6 Core, 33% removed). How to check it: read the 50 longest and the 50 most repeated spans, and count documents left with almost nothing (the tool can empty a page that was all boilerplate).

8. Bloom filters: exact dedup in fixed memory, and DCLM's near-dup variant

The problem. Exact dedup of paragraphs or n-grams across a corpus needs a "seen" set of 1011 to 1013 items: 8 bytes each is terabytes.

How it works. A Bloom filter is an array of m bits and k hash functions. Adding an item sets its k bits; testing checks whether all k are set. It never misses an item it has seen, but can say "seen" for a new item whose bits were all set by others (a false positive), with probability about (1−e−kn/m)k after n items. The best k is (m/n) ln 2, which gives m/n = −ln p / (ln 2)2 bits per item for a target rate p: 9.6 bits at 1%, 14.4 at 0.1%, 57.5 at 10−12 (derived). In dedup a false positive drops one unique item, which is acceptable at 1%.

The root's Dedup lab ran the Dolma toolkit's filter on the running example's 743,644 lines with filters from 64 KiB to 64 MiB: false positives matched the streaming formula within about 1% (1 MiB, 17 bits per distinct line: 11 to 14 lines wrongly dropped), and it found that --dedupe.min_words above 0 silently turns paragraph dedup off in dolma 1.2.1 (deduper.rs:339-353).

DCLM's BFF: near-duplicate detection with a Bloom filter

DCLM-baseline was deduplicated with a modified "Big Friendly Filter": split each document into paragraphs, cut each paragraph into 13-grams, and test every 13-gram against one filter holding all 13-grams seen so far. A paragraph is removed when more than 80% of its 13-grams were seen; a document when more than 80% of all its 13-grams were (published DCLM Appendix L; settings min and max n-gram 13, threshold 0.8, false-positive rate 0.01). Unlike MinHash it compares a document with the whole corpus seen so far, not with other documents, in one streaming pass.

  • Memory decides the shard. DCLM: a 1T-token corpus with a false-positive rate of 10−12 "would require 6.5TB of RAM" (1012 13-grams at 57.5 bits each is 7.2 TB, or 6.5 TiB: it reproduces in binary units); at 0.01, 1.2 bytes per 13-gram. The DCLM pool was about 70 TB at that point, so they ran 100 shards of about 700 GB each, deduplicating within each shard only.
  • Removal grows with the shard. Table 22: the same filter removes 29% of 114B tokens, 35% of 227B, 40% of 341B, 43% of 455B and 46% of 569B. This is consistent with a global MinHash still removing 85% of DCLM-baseline (Table 23): most duplicates sit across shards.
  • Quality was not hurt. At 7B parameters and 2x tokens, the Bloom filter and MinHash plus suffix array scored within 0.2 Core points of each other (45.3 vs 45.5, Table 19), and the Bloom filter is "vastly more efficient" than a MinHash and suffix-array pipeline.
  • Dolma 1.7 used the same idea as "fuzzy deduplication": a paragraph's score is the share of its 13-grams repeated across Dolma CC, RefinedWeb and C4; documents with a length-weighted score above 0.3 and paragraphs above 0.8 are removed: 48% of Dolma CC tokens, 10% of C4, 12% of RefinedWeb (Ai2 blog, April 2024).

How to check it: run the filter on a sample where an exact Python set gives the true answer, and count the difference (the root lab's method); log the fill ratio of the filter at the end of each shard.

9. Semantic dedup: SemDeDup and D4

The problem. Two pages can say the same thing in different words and share no 5-word phrase: Jaccard near 0. Paraphrase-level redundancy is common in synthetic data, instruction sets and spam.

How it works. An encoder (a model that maps a text to a vector so that texts with similar meaning land close together; the RAG topic covers them) embeds every document. Comparing all pairs is again quadratic, so SemDeDup (Abbas et al. 2023) first clusters the vectors with k-means, then inside each cluster removes a document whose cosine similarity to a kept one is at least 1−ε. NeMo Curator 1.4.0 runs this on GPUs with embeddinggemma-300m, 100 clusters and ε = 0.01 by default, keeping the members farthest from the centroid first ("hard"; stages/text/deduplication/semantic.py:80-92).

What the papers report

  • SemDeDup (arXiv 2303.09540): on LAION image-text pairs (k = 50,000 clusters), removing 37% caused no drop and removing 50% cost under 0.5% of zero-shot accuracy. On C4 with OPT-125M (k = 11,000), keeping 80% after SemDeDup matched the perplexity of keeping 96.1% after NearDup. The training-time saving is stated three ways: 15% (introduction), 10% to 15% (Section 5.2), 5% (Figure 8's example). Perplexity at 4% pruned (Table A12): baseline 38.95, NearDup 39.46, random 39.51, SemDeDup 39.35 on C4: pruning anything raised perplexity a little.
  • D4 (Tirumala et al. 2023, arXiv 2308.12284): SemDeDup first (keep 75%), then "SSL prototypes": k-means again and drop the documents closest to their centroid, the most typical ones, until the target size. At 6.7B parameters and 100B tokens: about 20% fewer steps to reach the baseline's perplexity and up to 2% better average downstream accuracy. Table 1 (1.3B, 40B tokens): D4-selected 20B tokens repeated twice beat 40B random tokens once on non-web perplexity (16.10 vs 16.27) and instruction perplexity (13.85 vs 14.19). Selection always hurt perplexity on a held-out web snapshot: it trades web typicality for diversity.
  • DCLM Table 4: SemDedup used as the selection step scored 27.1 Core against 27.5 for no model-based selection: as a quality filter on its own it did not help.

Measured on the running example: does the encoder matter?

The shape of a distributed MinHash job

  1. Signatures, embarrassingly parallel: each worker reads a shard and writes, for every band, (band hash, document id). datatrove writes one file per band per worker.
  2. Group by band value: a shuffle (all records with the same band hash go to the same worker), or, as datatrove does, each band's files sorted on disk and merged with a heap; workers split each band's hash range (minhash.py:391-399).
  3. Connected components over the candidate edges: single-machine union-find (datatrove), label propagation in Spark or Dask (text-dedup, TxT360), or cuGraph on GPUs (NeMo Curator).
  4. Filter: every worker drops the ids on its remove list as it rewrites its shard.

11. Dedup and benchmark contamination

Contamination means test items (benchmark questions and answers) in the training data; a model that has seen them scores higher without being more capable. The decontamination page owns detection and removal; here is what dedup has to do with it.

  • The overlap is real between standard splits. Lee et al. found near-duplicates in the training set for 4.6% of C4's validation examples, 14.35% of RealNews', 4.92% of LM1B's and 0.72% of Wiki-40B's (Table 2). A published Transformer-XL trained on LM1B has perplexity 10.11 on LM1B validation examples that have training copies against 23.58 on the rest; GROVER-XL on RealNews, 7.68 against 9.45 (Table 5). Their policy: keep the test copy, drop the training copy.
  • Within-corpus dedup reduces, but never removes, a copied test item: it keeps one copy by design. Removing benchmark text needs a dedup across two sets: the training corpus against the evaluation sets. The same machinery does it: datatrove's MinHash can deduplicate against a prebuilt index (index_folder), Google's tool has across-similar, and Dolma seeds its Bloom filter with test items.
  • Fewer copies, less memorisation of whatever leaked (Kandpal et al.: generation rate grows super-linearly with the number of copies), so dedup lowers how much a leaked item inflates a score even when it cannot remove it.
  • No source measures a percentage reduction in benchmark contamination from dedup alone (searched; not confirmed). The decontamination page measured the running example and whole FineWeb shards directly against seven benchmarks.

13. Common mistakes

Measuring the duplicate rate on a sample and calling it the corpus rate
Duplicates are pairs: a random 1% sample keeps only 0.01% of the pairs. The running example's one WARC has 2.8% near-duplicate documents; DeepSeek found 22.2% inside one dump and 89.8% across 91. Measure at the scope you will deduplicate, or estimate how the rate grows with scope (section 7's chart).
Treating cluster membership as similarity
Connected components chain: a stove page with Jaccard 0.49 to the kept page was removed with a cookie-banner cluster. Check the true Jaccard against the kept document (Dolma 3), and read the largest clusters.
Copying (b, r) from prose
RefinedWeb's "20 buckets of 450" and Dolma 3's "14 bands of size 5 ... same as FineWeb" are wrong as written. Recompute the S-curve and check it reproduces the probabilities the paper states.
Deleting suffix-array ranges and losing the first copy
The ranges cover every occurrence. Keep one if the span is content (a quote, a licence you want once); remove all if it is boilerplate. On the running example the choice changes the bytes removed from 1.08% to 2.50%.
Using a semantic threshold tuned on another encoder
Median cosine of unrelated pages is 0.05 for MiniLM and 0.49 for bge-small on the same documents. ε = 0.01 means different things for each. Calibrate on labelled pairs.
Deduplicating globally and assuming the result is better
FineWeb's global dedup kept a biased, worse remainder of old crawls. Keep cluster sizes, compare per-crawl and global versions with a small ablation, and let mixing set repetition.
Trusting a flag you have not tested
In dolma 1.2.1, --dedupe.min_words above 0 turns paragraph dedup off (root lab). Run every tool on a sample where a plain Python set gives the right answer.
Expecting within-corpus dedup to decontaminate
It keeps one copy of every test item it finds. Decontaminate against the evaluation sets separately.

14. Interview questions, with short answers

Prove that the min-hash collision probability equals Jaccard similarity.
Under a random permutation the minimum over A ∪ B is equally likely to be any element of the union; the two minimums agree iff that element is in A ∩ B. So P = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|.
How many hashes do you need to estimate J to ±0.02?
The estimate is binomial, sd = √(J(1−J)/k). At J = 0.8 and two standard deviations within 0.02: k ≥ 4 × 0.16 / 0.0004 = 1,600. For a yes/no decision with LSH you need far fewer, because the S-curve, not the estimate, decides.
Choose b and r for a 0.8 threshold with 128 hashes.
Steep point near (1/b)1/r ≈ 0.8. datasketch's rule (minimise weighted areas of false positives and negatives) gives 9 × 13; RedPajama v2 ships exactly that. More rows, sharper and fewer false positives; more bands, higher recall.
Why union-find, and what goes wrong with it?
It turns candidate pairs into groups in near-linear time (path compression, union by size). Chaining joins dissimilar documents through intermediates; giant components appear on boilerplate. Verify inside clusters and inspect the largest.
MinHash or SimHash for an LLM corpus?
MinHash: a tunable, sharp threshold at 0.7 to 0.9. SimHash at 64 bits and k = 3 finds near-identical pages with 8 bytes per document; on the running example it found 48% of pairs with Jaccard ≥ 0.8. ROOTS used SimHash and saw false positives on long documents.
When would you add a suffix-array pass?
When boilerplate spans survive inside unique documents (cookie text, disclaimers), after MinHash, per shard, at about 50 tokens (230 bytes of English) or 500 bytes, keeping one copy of content spans. Memory: the text plus 8 to 16 bytes per byte while building.
Does dedup improve benchmarks?
It cuts memorisation about tenfold (Lee et al.) and waste from skewed repetition (Hernandez et al.); benchmark gains are small and mixed (Pythia: no clear benefit; Muennighoff: worse on C4, better on OSCAR; DCLM: +0.3 to +2.1 Core at 1B). Global dedup of an already filtered set showed no gain (FineWeb, FineWeb-Edu).
System design: deduplicate 100 Common Crawl snapshots (about 1010 documents) for a pretraining run.
1. URL dedup across snapshots keeping the newest capture (a sorted join on URL). 2. Exact document hashes (or a Bloom filter at 0.1%: about 18 GB for 1010 items). 3. How the rule chooses

datasketch's MinHashLSH (v2.0.0, lsh.py:21-48) tries every b and r with b × r ≤ the number of hashes and keeps the pair that minimises wfp · (area under the S-curve left of t) + wfn · (area above it right of t), weights 0.5 and 0.5 by default. The areas treat every similarity as equally likely, which real data is not: on the web almost all pairs sit near 0, so a false-positive area that looks small still multiplies by billions of pairs. That is why the counters below report both the measured pairs and the expected candidates over all 3,622,086 pairs of the running example, and why production pipelines add a true-Jaccard check on candidates (Dolma 3) or accept a low r because candidates are cheap to verify.

10. Recipes, rehydration and contamination

What production corpora do. Almost every recipe runs exact dedup first and MinHash second; they differ in scope, in whether candidates are verified, and in which copy survives. FineWeb runs MinHash 14 × 8 inside each crawl with no verification; Dolma 3 runs MinHash 26 × 11 over 32 shards, checks true Jaccard 0.8 inside clusters, keeps the newest copy, then a keep-one suffix array; DCLM-baseline uses only a Bloom-filter BFF within 100 shards; TxT360 and Llama 3 deduplicate globally. The full table, with sources, is below.

Two settings that do not mean what their prose says
RefinedWeb writes "20 buckets of 450 hashes", but its own probabilities (76% at 0.75, 99.4% at 0.8) only come out of 450 bands of 20 rows; read literally, 20 bands of 450 rows would match only identical shingle sets (the root lab measured 143 candidates, all with Jaccard 1). Lee et al. and D4 use the same reversed naming. Dolma 3's README says its PDF data used "the same parameters as FineWeb ... 14 bands of size 5", which is not FineWeb's 14 × 8. Rehydration: dedup gives the counts, mixing decides

TxT360 deduplicated 99 crawls globally and kept each document's duplicate count, then upsampled heuristically: weight 3 for 2 to 5 copies, 5 for 5 to 100, 8 for 101 to 1,000, 10 above 1,000. FineWeb-2 made it data-driven: per language, measure the filtering rate of documents by cluster size (as a quality proxy), give weight 10 to the cluster size the filters remove least, weight 1 to sizes removed more often than average, and interpolate; for French the curve is U-shaped, with both unique documents and the most repeated 0.1% filtered more than average ({{FineWeb-2 Section 4.5|https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20920}}). Its card warns: "If you do not upsample based on these weights, dataset performance may be lower." The {{data mixing page|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}} covers repetition budgets.

Dedup does not decontaminate

Contamination means benchmark items in the training data; the {{decontamination page|n:3f95c17b0d0d8100b7e4e0f96074c309}} owns detection and removal. Within-corpus dedup keeps one copy of every test item it finds, by design; removing benchmark text needs a dedup across two sets, the training corpus against the evaluation sets, with the same machinery (datatrove's MinHash against a prebuilt index, index_folder; Google's tool's across-similar; Dolma seeds its Bloom filter with test items). What dedup does buy: fewer copies, less memorisation of whatever leaked (Kandpal et al.: generation rate grows super-linearly with copies).

How much test text sits in standard training sets (depth)

Lee et al. found near-duplicates in the training set for 4.6% of C4's validation examples, 14.35% of RealNews', 4.92% of LM1B's and 0.72% of Wiki-40B's (Table 2). A published Transformer-XL trained on LM1B has perplexity 10.11 on LM1B validation examples that have training copies against 23.58 on the rest; GROVER-XL on RealNews, 7.68 against 9.45 (Table 5). Their policy: keep the test copy, drop the training copy. No source measures a percentage reduction in benchmark contamination from dedup alone (searched; not confirmed). The decontamination page measured the running example and whole FineWeb shards directly against seven benchmarks.

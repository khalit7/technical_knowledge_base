3. MinHash and LSH: near copies without comparing pairs

The problem. Most web duplicates are not byte-identical: a syndicated article with a different header, a product page that differs by one name, a forum page with a different visitor count. We want pairs whose text is mostly the same among 109 documents, without comparing 5×1017 pairs.

A concrete pair. Normalise two forum pages from the running example (lowercase, no punctuation, numbers normalised), split them into words, and take every run of 5 consecutive words, each kept once: their shingle sets share 43 of 73 distinct shingles, a Jaccard similarity of 43/73 = 0.589. "Near duplicate" means J above a threshold such as 0.75 or 0.8. (FineWeb uses 5-word shingles; Gopher 13; NeMo Curator 24 characters. Short shingles make unrelated documents look alike; long ones let one changed word break many shingles.) Step through what MinHash does with this pair:

Why the minimum works

Pick a random hash function h over shingles and keep each document's smallest value, minh(A). Then

P[ min h(A) = min h(B) ] = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| = J(A, B)

Proof (Broder 1997, Theorem 1): look at the smallest value over the union A ∪ B. Every shingle of the union is equally likely to own it. The two minimums are equal exactly when that shingle is in both sets, which happens for |A ∩ B| of the |A ∪ B| shingles.

With k hash functions each document becomes a signature of k minimums, and the share of positions where two signatures agree estimates J without bias. The count of agreements is binomial, so the estimate's standard deviation is √(J(1−J)/k): at J = 0.75 and FineWeb's k = 112, 0.041. Halving the noise costs four times the hashes.

The noise follows the binomial law at every signature size

measured dots: the standard deviation of the estimate for 24 real pairs, each hashed with 2,000 independent families; derived lines: √(J(1−J)/k). The binomial model holds to within a few thousandths at every k.

LSH: bands turn the estimate into a cheap yes/no

Signatures make each comparison cheap, but there are still too many pairs. Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) avoids comparing pairs at all: cut the k = b×r values into b bands of r rows, and per band group documents by the band's r values (a sort or a hash table). Two documents equal on all r values of at least one band become a candidate pair.

P(candidate | J = s) = 1 − (1 − sr)b

A band matches with probability sr; all b bands fail with (1−sr)b. The curve is an S whose steep part sits near s ≈ (1/b)1/r. FineWeb's 14 × 8 gives 56%, 77%, 92%, 98.8% at 0.70, 0.75, 0.80, 0.85, the numbers its Appendix E.1 states (recomputed here).

Rows sharpen, bands widen. More rows per band make the S steeper and push it right (fewer false candidates, more misses); more bands push it left (higher recall, more candidates, more hashes). Predict, then move the target from 0.75 to 0.9 with 112 hashes: does the rule pick more bands or fewer?

The rule is datasketch's: with 128 hashes and equal weights it picks 11 × 11 for a 0.75 target and 9 × 13 for 0.8 (derived with its own code); FineWeb's 14 × 8 is not the rule's optimum for 0.75 (it sits lower, catching more). measured dots: real pairs with Jaccard ≥ 0.01 ( identical shingle sets plus with 1,024-bit equality masks from a hash family drawn with seed 2, independent of the one used to find the pairs), candidate rate per 0.05-wide bin, bands as consecutive slices; derived line and expected counts: 1−(1−sr)b summed over every pair's true Jaccard.

Predict: on the running example, FineWeb's 14 × 8 meets 308 pairs with true Jaccard ≥ 0.8. How many does it miss?
None. 92% is the chance at exactly 0.8; most real pairs above 0.8 are far above it (143 of the 308 have identical shingle sets, where the chance is 100%), and pairs between 0.8 and 0.85 have 92% to 98.8%. The root lab measured 0 misses and 0 candidates below Jaccard 0.5 on all 3,622,086 pairs. Recall depends on the similarity distribution of your data, not just on the curve.

Cost: signatures are documents × shingles × k multiply-adds, embarrassingly parallel; the grouping is one sort per band (a shuffle across machines); memory is k × 8 bytes per document (112 hashes: 112 GB per 109 documents of 64-bit values). How to check it: sample candidate pairs in several similarity bins and read them; compute the true Jaccard of each candidate (cheap: candidates are few); recompute the S-curve and confirm it reproduces any probabilities a paper states before copying its setting (RefinedWeb's "20 buckets of 450" does not; section 10).

How the band rule chooses, and the presets' sources (depth)

datasketch's MinHashLSH (v2.0.0, lsh.py:21-48) tries every b and r with b × r ≤ the number of hashes and keeps the pair that minimises wfp · (area under the S-curve left of t) + wfn · (area above it right of t), weights 0.5 and 0.5 by default. The areas treat every similarity as equally likely, which real data is not: on the web almost all pairs sit near 0, so a false-positive area that looks small still multiplies by billions of pairs. That is why the designer reports both the measured pairs and the expected candidates over all 3,622,086 pairs of the running example, and why production pipelines add a true-Jaccard check on candidates (Dolma 3) or accept a low r because candidates are cheap to verify.

Presets: published FineWeb paper Appendix E.1 and datatrove minhash.py:51-54; Dolma 3 pool README (allenai/dolma3 @ 1a9daced); NeMo Curator 1.4.0 fuzzy/workflow.py:75-83 (character 24-grams, so not directly comparable with word 5-grams); datasketch defaults lsh.py:149-151; text-dedup's Spark script defaults (tag 2023.09.20, minhash_spark.py:365-370); RefinedWeb Appendix G.3.1 read as 450 bands of 20 (the only reading that reproduces its own probabilities).

The same pair hashed 2,000 times: estimates and candidate rates (depth)

MinHash is a random estimate: a different set of hash functions gives a different signature. We took 24 real pairs from the running example, from Jaccard 0.30 to 0.96, and hashed each with 2,000 independent families of 1,024 functions (datatrove's arithmetic, seeds 1,000 to 2,999). Each family gives one estimate (the share of equal minimums) and one yes/no for "becomes a candidate".

Setting

measured dots: share of the 2,000 hash families in which the pair became a candidate (bands are consecutive slices of the signature, as datatrove's np.split); derived line: 1−(1−sr)b. 26 × 11 is Dolma 3's web setting; 20 × 5 and 9 × 13 are two other shapes for comparison.

In practice the "random functions" are a cheap family: datatrove hashes each shingle once to 64 bits (xxhash), then computes (a·x + b) mod (261−1) for 112 random pairs (a, b) in one vectorised line (minhash.py:182). Because the product is computed in unsigned 64-bit arithmetic it wraps around before the modulus, so the family is not exactly the textbook one; the measurements above (and the root lab's estimate-vs-truth chart) show it does not matter.

Running it with the real tool (code and output)
from datatrove.pipeline.dedup.minhash import MinhashConfig, MinhashDedupSignature
from datatrove.utils.hashing import HashConfig
import numpy as np
cfg = MinhashConfig(hash_config=HashConfig(precision=64))   # FineWeb: 5-grams, 14 bands x 8 rows, seed 1
sig = MinhashDedupSignature(output_folder="/dev/null", config=cfg)
sa, sb = sig.get_shingles(a), sig.get_shingles(b)           # a, b: the two forum pages
A, B = set(sa.ravel().tolist()), set(sb.ravel().tolist())
print(f"shingles {len(A)} and {len(B)}, shared {len(A & B)}, Jaccard {len(A & B) / len(A | B):.3f}")
eq = np.concatenate(sig.get_signature(sa)) == np.concatenate(sig.get_signature(sb))
print(f"equal minimums {eq.sum()} of 112, estimate {eq.mean():.3f}")
bands = eq.reshape(14, 8).all(axis=1)
print("bands where all 8 agree:", (np.nonzero(bands)[0] + 1).tolist(), "-> candidate:", bool(bands.any()))
s = len(A & B) / len(A | B); print(f"P(candidate) at this similarity: {1 - (1 - s**8)**14:.3f}")

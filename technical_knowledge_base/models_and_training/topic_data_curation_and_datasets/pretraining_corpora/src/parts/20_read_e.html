3. The lineage, generation by generation

The open corpora form a family tree with one root (Common Crawl) and four generations. Each generation changed one or two things about how the crawl is turned into training text, and each change came with an experiment that justified it. The {{Lineage|#t-pclin}} tab draws the whole tree with every corpus's full card; this section tells the story and the experiments, with the numbers checked against the papers' own tables.

Generation 1 (2019 to 2021): rules on one crawl, and hand-built mixtures

C4 (Google, October 2019) took one month of Common Crawl's WET text (April 2019) and applied a handful of rules: keep lines that end in terminal punctuation and have at least five words, keep pages with at least three sentences, drop pages containing a word from a bad-words list, "lorem ipsum" or a curly bracket (a proxy for code), drop cookie and "javascript" lines, and keep only one copy of any repeated three-sentence span ({{T5 paper, section 2.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683}}; thresholds confirmed in {{TFDS c4_utils.py|https://github.com/tensorflow/datasets/blob/master/tensorflow_datasets/text/c4_utils.py}}). The result was 364,868,892 English documents, 156B words by spaCy's count. Its paper also ran the first clean filtering experiment, at T5-Base size and 34B training tokens:

Filtering won on all seven columns against the 8 times larger unfiltered set (and the baseline never repeats data, so the extra volume was not needed). The paper's prose says the unfiltered set is "the worst in every task"; its own table says six of seven: on SuperGLUE, Wikipedia alone (68.01) is below it (68.04). The same paper's Table 9 is the first repetition experiment: truncating C4 so it repeats 64 times cost almost nothing, 1,024 times cost 3.7 GLUE points (83.28 to 79.55) and 4.6 SQuAD points; the {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}} page owns repetition.

Two years later, an independent audit of C4 ({{Dodge et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08758}}) found what the bad-words rule cost: it removed documents in African American English and Hispanic-aligned English at 42% and 32%, against 6.2% for white-aligned English, and much LGBTQ-related text. A filter that looks neutral can select dialects and topics, which is why {{Quality classifiers and data selection|n:3f95c17b0d0d810baaabe41da6c9c50f}} measures what filters drop by topic and dialect.

The Pile (EleutherAI, December 2020) took the opposite view: diversity by hand. Twenty-two sources (its own Common Crawl extraction, PubMed Central, Books3, OpenWebText2, arXiv, GitHub, FreeLaw, Stack Exchange, patents, Wikipedia, subtitles, IRC logs, emails...), each given a hand-set number of epochs (Wikipedia 3, PubMed 2, Pile-CC 1), 825 GiB in all ({{Pile Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.00027}}). Its size is often quoted as "300B tokens", which is Pythia's training budget; Pythia's paper gives 334B GPT-NeoX tokens, and the Pile paper itself gives no total. Its evidence was perplexity: a 1.3B model trained on the Pile scored 5.59 WikiText perplexity against 8.27 for CC-100 and 11.75 for raw Common Crawl. Its legacy is double: the idea that mixing domains helps, and a licence lesson, because Books3 (about 200,000 pirated books) was taken down in 2023 after a Danish Rights Alliance notice and sits at the centre of several lawsuits (section 7).

The closed models of the time published mixture tables without the data: GPT-3 (2020) drew 60% of its 300B tokens from filtered Common Crawl (410B available, so 0.44 epochs) and 22% from WebText2 (19B, so about 3 epochs); LLaMA 1 (2023) drew 67% from Common Crawl through CCNet, 15% from C4, and 4.5% each from GitHub, Wikipedia and books ({{GPT-3 Table 2.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165}}, {{LLaMA Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.13971}}). GPT-3's table does not add up internally: weight times 300B divided by tokens gives 3.5 epochs for WebText2 and 3.0 for Wikipedia, against 2.9 and 3.4 printed.

Generation 2 (2022 to 2023): reproduce the closed recipes, then "web only"

ROOTS (BigScience, 2022) built BLOOM's 1.6 TB in 46 languages with a data card per source and per-language thresholds set by native speakers, a governance model more than a recipe. RedPajama v1 (Together, April 2023) rebuilt LLaMA 1's undisclosed mix from its paper (1.2T tokens), and SlimPajama (Cerebras) deduplicated it globally to 627B, a first look at how much of a multi-source mix is repeated. Dolma (Ai2, from August 2023) released a documented 3T-token multi-source corpus with its toolkit.

RefinedWeb (TII, June 2023) made the claim that defined the next generation: web data alone, filtered and deduplicated hard enough, matches curated mixtures. Its pipeline re-extracted text from the WARC HTML with trafilatura, used fastText language ID at 0.65, Gopher's quality and repetition rules, line-level fixes, MinHash with 9,000 hashes and exact substring deduplication of any repeat over 50 tokens, and no learned quality filter. The robust lesson is "filter and deduplicate the web well", less "web beats curated".

Generation 3 (2024): every step ablated, and learned quality filters

FineWeb (Hugging Face, April 2024; same lead author as RefinedWeb) rebuilt the RefinedWeb approach in the open {{datatrove|https://github.com/huggingface/datatrove}} library on every crawl and published an ablation for each step, at 1.71B parameters and 28B tokens per filtering test. Three findings changed practice: extracting from WARC with trafilatura beat Common Crawl's WET text clearly; deduplicating each crawl separately beat deduplicating across all crawls, because global deduplication kept mostly the rare (and worse) documents (in their 2013-48 study, the 31B tokens kept by global dedup trained a worse model than the 171B tokens it had removed, once those were deduplicated per crawl); and C4's terminal-punctuation rule helped but threw away about 30% of tokens, so it was dropped for three custom rules ({{FineWeb paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557}}, sections 3.2 to 3.5; the plots have no tables). It now covers 110 crawls (v1.4.0, July 2025); its card says "more than 18.5T" GPT-2 tokens, and its per-crawl table sums to 18,306.0B, because the table's printed total of 18,527.0B counts CC-MAIN-2021-43 twice (two identical rows of 221.0B; checked 2026-10-14).

Then the field moved from rules to learned quality filters: a classifier trained to say what "good" text looks like, used to keep the top slice. Two recipes won. FineWeb-Edu asked Llama-3-70B-Instruct to grade 460,000 pages from 0 to 5 for educational value, trained a small regression head on those grades, and kept pages scoring 3 or more (8% of FineWeb, 1.3T tokens). DCLM (June 2024), a benchmark for data with fixed models and evaluations, found that a cheap fastText classifier trained to tell instruction-like text (OpenHermes 2.5 and top Reddit ExplainLikeImFive answers) from ordinary web text, keeping the top 10%, beat every other filter it tried:

The paper's "+3.5 Core over conventional choices" is Table 5 at 7B (41.0 against 37.5 for a GPT-3-style classifier) and checks. Why these filters work, and what they are biased towards, is {{Quality classifiers and data selection|n:3f95c17b0d0d810baaabe41da6c9c50f}}.

Nemotron-CC (NVIDIA, December 2024) argued the other side of filtering: DCLM keeps 10% of the web, which is fine for a 1T-token run and runs out for a 15T-token one. It scored pages with an ensemble of three classifiers into 20 buckets, kept the medium buckets, rewrote the low ones with an LLM instead of deleting them, and turned high-quality pages into question-answer pairs and summaries, for 4.4T real plus 1.9T synthetic tokens. Its headline: the high-quality subset beat DCLM by 5.6 MMLU points (59.0 against 53.4) at 8B parameters and 1T tokens, exactly as its table says; the full corpus, built for long runs, scores 0.4 points below DCLM on MMLU (53.0) and slightly above on the 10-task average (57.8 against 57.0) ({{Nemotron-CC Table 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02595}}). Alongside them, RedPajama v2 released a raw 30T-token pool with 46 precomputed quality signals per document, Zyda and Zyda-2 cross-deduplicated the best open sets against each other, and TxT360 deduplicated globally but kept each page's duplicate count so the user can up-weight popular pages.

Generation 4 (2025 to 2026): bigger pools, synthetic text, PDFs, labels and licences

The newest corpora are less single datasets than pools with labels from which a mix is drawn. Dolma 3 (Ai2, October 2025) is a 9.31T-token pool built by deduplicating 104 crawls three ways (exact, MinHash, and suffix arrays for repeated spans of 500 bytes or more) and bucketing every page by topic and quality; Olmo 3's 5.93T-token mix up-samples the high-quality buckets and adds 805B tokens of scientific PDFs read by an OCR model. Essential-Web (June 2025) labels 24T tokens on 12 taxonomy dimensions so that a subset is a query. Nemotron-CC v2 and v2.1 (August and December 2025) add 2024 to 2025 crawls and several trillion LLM-written tokens, and move behind a training-only agreement. FinePDFs (September 2025) mines 3T tokens from PDFs, a source the HTML pipelines ignored; FineWeb2 carries the FineWeb recipe to 1,868 language-script pairs (section 6). At the other end, Common Pile v0.1 (June 2025) keeps only public-domain and openly licensed text, and shows how little of the web that leaves: 15.2B tokens of licence-verified web pages (section 7).

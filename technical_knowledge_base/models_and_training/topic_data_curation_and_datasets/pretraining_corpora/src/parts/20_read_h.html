6. Beside the web: code, maths, papers and books, other languages

A general web corpus is most of a pretraining mix but rarely all of it. Modern open recipes add code (about 7 to 24% of tokens), maths (about 3 to 14%), scientific text and, for multilingual models, other languages, often weighted up near the end of training (the {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}} page owns how much and when). Each of these has its own corpora, with their own extraction problems and their own licence questions.

Code

Code corpora are built from repositories, not crawls, and licences can be read from the repository itself, so code is the one domain where licence filtering is routine. The Stack v1 (BigCode, November 2022) kept only files whose repository carried a permissive licence, excluded the roughly 81% of repositories with no detectable licence, deduplicated files near-exactly (about 40% were duplicates) and introduced an opt-out for authors. The Stack v2 (February 2024) moved to the Software Heritage archive, grew to 67.5 TB raw over 600+ languages, and changed the rule: permissive and unlicensed files are kept, copyleft and commercial licences dropped ({{StarCoder2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.19173}}); its "900B tokens" is StarCoder2-15B's training set with extras such as pull requests, notebooks and arXiv, not the archive's size. Its content is not on Hugging Face at all: the dataset lists file ids, and the files come from Software Heritage's S3 bucket under an agreement. Stack-Edu (Hugging Face, February 2025) applied the FineWeb-Edu idea to code with one classifier per language and lifted MultiPL-E pass rates by 4 to 8 points in a 1.7B annealing test (Python 20.7 to 25.6); its card and paper say 125B tokens while its own per-language table sums to 156.8B. Code also needs decontamination against code benchmarks: The Stack v2 removed files containing HumanEval, MBPP and APPS problems.

Maths

Maths pages are rare on the web and fragile under extraction: an extractor that drops MathJax or flattens LaTeX turns a derivation into noise. The lineage is a sequence of better recall and better extraction. OpenWebMath (2023) wrote a maths-aware extractor and filtered all of Common Crawl to 14.7B tokens. DeepSeekMath (2024, not released) seeded a fastText classifier with OpenWebMath, recalled pages, added whole domains with many maths pages, and iterated four times to 120B tokens. FineMath (Hugging Face, December 2024) re-extracted FineWeb's candidate pages from WARC and kept LLM-graded pages (34B tokens at 3+, about 10B at 4+); MegaMath (2025) went to 371.6B tokens with synthetic data; Nemotron-CC-Math (August 2025) re-rendered pages with a text browser and had an LLM rewrite them into clean LaTeX (133B tokens).

Scientific papers, PDFs and books

Papers and books are long, well-edited text, which is why every mixture since the Pile has included them, and they are where copyright is most concentrated. peS2o (Ai2) cleaned 39M open-access papers and abstracts from Semantic Scholar (42B whitespace words, not model tokens). arXiv LaTeX sources appear in RedPajama, Proof-Pile-2 and Dolma, but arXiv's default licence does not grant reuse to third parties, so only a minority of arXiv full texts is openly licensed. The newest source is PDFs: {{FinePDFs|https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceFW/finepdfs}} (Hugging Face, September 2025) extracted 2.9T tokens from PDFs found in 105 crawls, routing each PDF between a text-layer extractor and an OCR model, and recommends keeping PDFs under a quarter of a mix; Ai2's Olmo 3 put 805B tokens of OCR-read scientific PDFs into its mix (13.6%), and later redacted some ("[REMOVED]" in the 7B mix). For books, Books3 (pirated, in the Pile and LLaMA 1) is gone and is the centre of the litigation in section 7; what remains openly usable is public domain: PG-19 (Gutenberg books before 1919), Common Pile's pre-1929 books and Library of Congress scans. Institutional Books 1.0 (Harvard, 2025, 983,004 public-domain volumes, 242B tokens) shows the difference between public-domain text and the release terms: its early-access terms forbid commercial use, including training, without an agreement.

Other languages

English is about 42% of Common Crawl's pages (41.86% in CC-MAIN-2026-39 by Common Crawl's own {{language statistics|https://commoncrawl.github.io/cc-crawl-statistics/plots/languages}}; Russian 6.7%, German 5.7%, Japanese 5.2%, Spanish 4.7%, French 4.7%); the often-quoted "45%" is CulturaX's English share of tokens, a different measure. The multilingual lineage: mC4 (2020, C4's rules in 101 languages, 6.3T tokens), CC-100 (XLM-R, 2019), OSCAR (whose access is suspended as of October 2026), CulturaX (2023, mC4 plus OSCAR cleaned, 6.3T tokens), MADLAD-400 (Google, 419 languages, audited per language; its card's licence field says ODC-By while its text says CC BY 4.0), HPLT v2 and v3 (EU, adding Internet Archive crawls, about 30T tokens in v3), and FineWeb2 (Hugging Face, December 2024): 1,868 language-script pairs, counted in words rather than model tokens because no single tokenizer is fair across scripts. FineWeb2's French ablation is a compact lesson in what helps outside English: language ID alone 18.3, plus deduplication 18.0 (no gain), plus per-language filters 21.9, plus "rehydration" (re-adding documents in proportion to how often they were duplicated, with per-language weights) 23.6 ({{FineWeb2 paper|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20920}}, Table 30). Its paper also warns that for 70% of language-script pairs, more than half the documents come from Bible- or Wikipedia-related domains: for most languages the web is thin.

Wikipedia appears in nearly every mix and is small (Olmo 3 uses 2.51B tokens of English Wikipedia). Its licence is CC BY-SA, share-alike: a redistributed, processed copy must stay CC BY-SA, which is why mixes that bundle it under ODC-By still say the source licence applies.

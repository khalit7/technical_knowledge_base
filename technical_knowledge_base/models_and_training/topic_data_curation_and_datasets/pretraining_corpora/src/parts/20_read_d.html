2. Reading a corpus card: five questions

A pretraining corpus is a fixed, versioned collection of documents released for training language models, with a dataset card (the README on its Hugging Face page, or a paper) that says what is in it. Every comparison on this page asks the same five questions of each card, and most mistakes people make with corpora come from skipping one of them.

  1. Source. Which crawls (by id), which other sources (code from GitHub or Software Heritage, papers, books, Wikipedia), and up to which date. A corpus built from crawls up to 2022 knows nothing of 2024.
  2. Size, and in what unit. Documents, bytes, or tokens, and if tokens, which tokenizer. FineWeb's 18.5T are GPT-2 tokens, DCLM's 240T pool and its 3.8T baseline are GPT-NeoX tokens, C4's 156B are spaCy word tokens, and Nemotron-CC's paper does not say which tokenizer its 6.3T uses. Also: total or unique? A corpus deduplicated within each crawl but not across crawls can be mostly near-copies of itself (below).
  3. Recipe. Extraction (WET text, or HTML re-extracted with trafilatura, resiliparse or jusText), language identification and threshold, heuristic filters, deduplication method and scope (per crawl or global), model-based quality filter and threshold, decontamination, PII handling, and synthetic data if any. {{Extraction and filtering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81579b32c07c92b7472b}}, {{Deduplication|n:3f95c17b0d0d8171ac17e43ab5ea276f}} and {{Quality classifiers and data selection|n:3f95c17b0d0d810baaabe41da6c9c50f}} explain each step; this page says which corpus used which.
  4. Licence and access. The licence field on the card, the terms underneath it (Common Crawl's, Software Heritage's, an NVIDIA data agreement), and whether downloading needs a click-through or a manual approval (section 7).
  5. Version and evidence. Corpora change after release (FineWeb grew from 15T to 18.5T tokens over five versions and dropped domains after a takedown notice), so cite the version; and the evidence for it is usually its authors' own ablation, which section 5 reads critically.

The same text, counted four ways

A tokenizer cuts text into the units a model reads ({{Tokenizers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817187bcd94a1b0f15ea}} explains how). A bigger vocabulary packs more characters into each token, so the same corpus has fewer tokens under it, and the gap depends on the text: code, maths and non-English text are where tokenizers differ most. Total tokens and unique tokens

Deduplication scope changes what "size" means. FineWeb and DCLM deduplicate within each crawl (or in shards), not across all crawls, on purpose: FineWeb's ablations found that global deduplication over all crawls kept mostly worse documents (section 3). The consequence is that the same popular page, captured in many crawls, survives many times. NVIDIA's Nemotron-CC paper estimated unique tokens after a global fuzzy deduplication: FineWeb-Edu-2 5.4T total, 1.1T unique; DCLM 3.8T total, 1.0T unique; FineWeb-Edu 1.3T, 0.2T unique ({{Nemotron-CC Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2412.02595}}, independent of HF and DCLM, vendor for its own row), hence its remark that both "contain roughly 80% fuzzy duplicates". Whether that matters depends on your budget: a 100B-token run never sees most copies; a 15T-token run sees each popular page many times, which is repetition, with the costs the {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}} page measures.

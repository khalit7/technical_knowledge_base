5. How corpora are compared, and how to read the comparisons

There is no way to measure a corpus's quality by looking at it. The field's method is a data ablation: train the same small model with the same settings on a fixed number of tokens from each candidate corpus, then score every model on the same benchmark suite. Whatever differs in the scores is put down to the data. FineWeb used a 1.71B-parameter model on 28B tokens for each filtering decision and 350B tokens for corpus comparisons; DCLM fixed five scales from 412M parameters on 8.2B tokens to 6.9B on 276B, with 53 evaluations; NVIDIA used 8B models on 1T tokens with only the web part of the mix swapped. A run at FineWeb's comparison scale costs thousands of GPU-hours, which is why almost all published comparisons come from the corpus builders themselves.

Pick a study. The highlighted bar is the study's own corpus (a vendor number); every other bar is an independent measurement of somebody else's corpus. All cells are copied from the paper's table or released file; nothing is read off a plot.

Five things to check before believing a comparison

  1. Who ran it. Every corpus wins its own paper. The vendor result is still evidence, but the independent rows in someone else's table are better evidence about those rows. The one third-party study found that trains on Nemotron-CC-HQ, DCLM and FineWeb-Edu side by side ({{open-sci-ref|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09009}}, 1.7B on 1T tokens) puts them in that order, 0.66, 0.57, 0.56, and two of its authors also wrote DCLM.
  2. Which harness and which task format. The same two models swap places between evaluation codebases: DCLM-Baseline beats FineWeb-Edu by 13.5 MMLU points under DCLM's 5-shot LLM Foundry setup (50.8 against 37.3 at 7B, 280B tokens), yet DCLM's own appendix reports that under Hugging Face's cloze-style lighteval the two "perform quite similarly", and Hugging Face's SmolLM2 runs put FineWeb-Edu ahead on MMLU (37.5 against 35.5). Multiple-choice formats reward a model that has learned to answer A/B/C/D; cloze formats reward knowledge expressed in running text.
  3. Whether the filter was aimed at the benchmark. A classifier trained on instruction-style or "educational" text selects for what MMLU and ARC test. FineWeb-Edu's own card says higher thresholds help MMLU and ARC and hurt HellaSwag and PIQA; Nemotron-CC-HQ's lead in the third-party run sits mostly in MMLU and CommonsenseQA (without those two tasks: 0.679, 0.652, 0.638, a much smaller gap). That is not cheating, but it means "better corpus" partly means "better at these tests".
  4. Small margins against noise. FineWeb's final scores put FineWeb, RefinedWeb, C4 and Dolma within 0.84 points of each other, from one training run each. Few corpus papers report how much the same run moves with another seed or another data sample, so a gap under about a point at these scales is best read as a tie ({{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}} shows how to put error bars on such numbers).
  5. Scale and repetition. A filter that keeps 10% of the web wins a 100B-token comparison where nothing repeats, and may lose a 15T-token run that has to repeat its best data many times. NVIDIA's argument for Nemotron-CC (more unique tokens for long runs) is a claim about a regime most comparisons never reach; the full Nemotron-CC loses 0.4 MMLU to DCLM at 1T tokens and is designed to win at 15T.
Predict: in DCLM's own appendix, when the 7B models trained on DCLM-Baseline and on FineWeb-Edu are scored with Hugging Face's lighteval instead of LLM Foundry, what happens to DCLM's 13.5-point MMLU lead?
It nearly disappears: the appendix says the two models "perform quite similarly" under lighteval, while DCLM is "better by close to 10 points" in LLM Foundry (DCLM, appendix G). The data did not change; the way the question was asked did.

Pretraining corpora: lineage, licences and what to use

Where pretraining text comes from (Common Crawl), how the open corpora descend from it from C4 to FineWeb, DCLM, Dolma 3 and Nemotron-CC, the code, maths, papers, books and multilingual corpora beside them, what their sizes and licences really mean, and which to use for a budget. The idea in one screen

Almost every open pretraining corpus is the same raw Common Crawl put through a different recipe, plus code, maths, papers and books beside it. Corpora differ in what they keep (a tenth of the crawl, or a hundredth), what they count it in (whose tokenizer), and what their licence covers (the compilation, never the pages); which one to use is decided mostly by your token budget.

Pretraining corpus
a fixed, versioned set of documents released for pretraining, described by a dataset card (its Hugging Face README or paper).
Common Crawl
a free, roughly monthly snapshot of a few billion web pages; the raw material of nearly every open web corpus.
WARC, WET
a crawl's raw HTTP responses, and Common Crawl's own extracted text of them.
Learned quality filter
a classifier trained to say what "good" text looks like, used to keep the top slice.
Per-crawl dedup
removing duplicates within each crawl but not across crawls, so popular pages survive many times.
Pool
a large labelled corpus from which a training mix is drawn, rather than a mix itself (Dolma 3, Nemotron-CC's buckets).

1. Common Crawl: the raw material

Common Crawl is a non-profit (a California 501(c)(3) founded in 2007) that has crawled the public web since 2008 and gives every crawl away: "more than 10 petabytes", each crawl "typically containing more than two billion web pages" (Common Crawl, About, checked 2026-10-14), free on AWS Open Data at s3://commoncrawl/ and over plain HTTPS at https://data.commoncrawl.org/ with no account (Get started). Each crawl is a time slice of a few weeks named CC-MAIN-YYYY-WW (year and ISO week), about one a month in 2026. Its crawler, CCBot, reads each site's /robots.txt before fetching. A crawl is a sample of the web, not a mirror, and two crawls overlap heavily, which is why corpora built from many crawls must deduplicate across them (Deduplication owns how).

Most of a crawl's bytes are not text: WET is about 7% of WARC

Compressed TiB per file type of CC-MAIN-2026-39, as printed on its file listing (checked 2026-10-14). WARC (Web ARChive, an ISO standard): each URL's request, response (HTTP headers and raw HTML, byte for byte) and metadata records, about 0.9 GB per gzip file, 100,000 files per crawl. WAT: JSON metadata per record (headers, <head> fields, every link). WET: the visible text Common Crawl's own converter pulled out. C4 was built from WET; FineWeb, RefinedWeb and DCLM re-extracted text from the WARC HTML themselves, because extraction quality turned out to matter (Extraction and filtering). The URL and columnar indexes let you fetch one page without the rest (closed block below).

Crawls shrank to about 2 billion captures in 2025, and each URL appears about three times

Common Crawl's own statistics per crawl (monthly.csv in cc-crawl-statistics, commit 9ff265d, read 2026-10-14); hover or tap a bar. Crawls were mostly 2.6 to 3.5 billion page captures from 2017 to early 2025 (blue); from CC-MAIN-2025-21 on (orange) 2.0 to 2.6 billion, and every 2026 crawl is between 1.97 and 2.33 billion. Summed over all 128 index collections: 339.8 billion page captures but an estimated 103.5 billion distinct URLs (cumulative.csv, HyperLogLog, about 1% error), so each URL was captured about 3.3 times: the duplication every multi-crawl corpus removes.

The crawlable web is shrinking: more sites block AI crawlers each year

measured here Sites blocking GPTBot from the whole site rose from 2.7% (May 2023, all of it from "block every crawler" rules) to 5.6% (September 2026), and CCBot from 2.6% to 4.9%. Method: 20 robots.txt files per crawl, evenly spaced through each crawl's file list, 41,653 hosts' files in all, parsed with Python's urllib.robotparser; blocked means the crawler may fetch neither / nor a typical page path; Apple M1 Pro (CPU), src/scripts/robots_optout.py. These are shares of sites (a one-page blog counts as much as a news site), so they sit below token-weighted figures; sample error about 0.5 points.

robots.txt is a request, not an access control. Common Crawl obeys it, so a site that blocks CCBot drops out of future crawls; old crawls stay downloadable (an inference: nothing published says they are altered). Since September 2025 Common Crawl also publishes an Opt-Out Ledger of legal removal requests (announcement). The independent, token-weighted measurement is Longpre et al. 2024, Consent in Crisis: among the most-used domains of C4, RefinedWeb and Dolma, 20 to 33% of tokens were fully restricted by April 2024, against under 3% a year earlier.

Common Crawl does not licence the content. Its terms of use (updated 2024-03-07) license access to the service; the pages "may be subject to separate terms", and "by using the Crawled Content, you agree to respect the copyrights and other applicable rights of third parties". Every corpus built from it inherits this: an ODC-By or CC BY 4.0 card licenses the compilation, not each page (section 7).

Fetch one page from a crawl without downloading a terabyte (the index, the code, its output)

Each WARC record is its own gzip member, so any record can be cut out of a 0.9 GB file with an HTTP range request if you know its byte offset. The URL index (a CDX index, one line per capture, sorted by a reversed-host key such as org,python)/) gives file name, offset and length. The columnar index is the same table as Parquet files that Spark, Athena or DuckDB can query with SQL ("all captures from *.gov.uk with MIME text/html") (cc-index-table). The script runs with curl, gunzip and Python 3; its two requests were run here on 2026-10-14 (the retry loop was added after the index server started failing later that day):

src/scripts/cc_one_record.sh
#!/bin/sh
# Find one page in a Common Crawl crawl and fetch only its record (no account, no key), as shown on the page.
# 1. ask the URL index where the capture lives (file, byte offset, length)
CRAWL=CC-MAIN-2026-39
# the index server is busy at times (502/504, or an empty reply): retry a few times
for i in 1 2 3 4 5; do
  curl -s "https://index.commoncrawl.org/$CRAWL-index?url=www.python.org/&output=json&limit=1" > cdx.json
  grep -q '"offset"' cdx.json && break; sleep 5
done
cat cdx.json
# 2. fetch only those bytes from the 1 GB WARC file and decompress the one gzip member
python3 - <<'PY'
import json, subprocess
c = json.loads(open('cdx.json').readline())
off, ln = int(c['offset']), int(c['length'])
url = 'https://data.commoncrawl.org/' + c['filename']
subprocess.run(['curl', '-s', '-r', f'{off}-{off + ln - 1}', url, '-o', 'record.warc.gz'], check=True)
PY
gunzip -c record.warc.gz > record.warc
wc -c record.warc.gz record.warc
head -c 600 record.warc
Its output (recorded 2026-10-14; long lines cut)


The record is 10,644 bytes out of a file of 921,782,734 bytes: you read 0.001% of the file. The WARC header says when and from where the page was fetched and carries a SHA-1 payload digest (also in the index, so identical pages across crawls can be spotted without reading them); then the HTTP headers exactly as sent, then 52,590 bytes of HTML for a page whose visible text is a few kilobytes. The index server is often busy (502 and 504 errors and empty replies here, hence the retries), and some URLs are simply not in a given crawl ("No Captures found" for two Wikipedia pages tried in CC-MAIN-2026-39). The topic's Pipeline lab tab runs the whole funnel on a real WARC file.




Crawl details: CCBot, segments, sizes per capture, and when statistics disagree (provenance)


A web crawler keeps a list of URLs to visit (the frontier), downloads each page, stores what came back, and adds the page's links to the frontier. Common Crawl's FAQ describes CCBot as "a Nutch-based web crawler that makes use of the Apache Hadoop project"; it obeys Crawl-delay, honours nofollow (CC FAQ) and crawls from published IP ranges so sites can tell it from impostors (CCBot page). The 2026 crawls were weeks 04, 08, 12, 17, 21, 25, 30, 34 and 39; each crawl is split into 100 segments of 1,000 files. Besides WARC, WAT and WET, a crawl publishes the robots.txt files CCBot fetched, the non-200 responses (redirects, 404s) and the two indexes.


Compressed WARC per crawl did not shrink with the page count: 99.25 TiB for 3.35 billion pages in CC-MAIN-2023-50, 82.79 TiB for 2.17 billion in CC-MAIN-2026-39, so about 33 KB per capture then and 42 KB now (division of the published figures). The statistics disagree with the crawl pages for some early crawls (CC-MAIN-2013-20: 1.80 billion in the statistics, "2.00 billion" on its page), because the page repeats the original announcement while the statistics are recomputed from the index: quote the source with the number.


Longpre et al.'s full figures: about 1% of tokens across these corpora were fully restricted in mid-2023 and 5 to 7% in April 2024; the jump in the top 2,000 domains per corpus ("Head") came "directly after the introduction of GPTBot and Google-Extended" (August and September 2023). By crawler, OpenAI's agents were blocked on 25.9% of Head C4 tokens, Anthropic's and Common Crawl's on 13.3%, Google-Extended on 9.8% (their Figure 1). Their abstract says "28%+" and their introduction "~25%+" for the most critical domains: different populations, so quote the population with the number. The site-level sample here is closer to their whole-corpus figures than to the Head ones.

The same text, counted four ways


FineWeb's 18.5T are GPT-2 tokens, DCLM's 240T pool and 3.8T baseline are GPT-NeoX tokens, C4's 156B are spaCy word tokens, and Nemotron-CC's paper does not say which tokenizer its 6.3T uses. A bigger vocabulary packs more characters per token, but how much depends on the text (Tokenizers owns why).


Predict: FineWeb's card says 18.5 trillion GPT-2 tokens. Counted with Qwen3's tokenizer (three times the vocabulary), how many tokens is that?




About 17.9T, only 3% fewer. On FineWeb's English web text GPT-2 packs 4.64 characters per token and Qwen3 4.79: Qwen3's extra vocabulary is spent on other languages and code. On FineWeb2's French the gap is 1.25 times, and on The Pile's mixture 1.13 times.

The open corpora form one family tree rooted in Common Crawl. Each generation changed one or two things about turning the crawl into training text, each with an experiment behind it. The tree draws all of them; tap a row for its full card.

Generation 1 (2019 to 2021): rules on one crawl, hand-built mixtures


C4 (Google, October 2019) took one month of WET text (April 2019) and applied a handful of rules (terminal punctuation, at least three sentences, a bad-words list, no curly brackets, one copy of any repeated three-sentence span): 364,868,892 English documents, 156B words by spaCy's count (T5 paper, section 2.2). Its experiment was the first clean filtering test: filtered C4 beat the 8 times larger unfiltered set on all seven benchmark columns (83.28 against 81.46 GLUE). An independent audit (Dodge et al. 2021) found the bad-words rule removed African American English and Hispanic-aligned English at 42% and 32%, against 6.2% for white-aligned English: a filter that looks neutral can select dialects (Extraction and filtering owns the rules).


The Pile (EleutherAI, December 2020) took the opposite view, diversity by hand: 22 sources each given a hand-set number of epochs (Wikipedia 3, PubMed 2, Pile-CC 1), 825 GiB in all (Pile Table 1); the hero's first bar. Its legacy is double: mixing domains helps, and a licence lesson, because Books3 (about 200,000 pirated books) was taken down in 2023 after a Danish Rights Alliance notice and sits at the centre of several lawsuits (section 7).



Generation 2 (2022 to 2023): reproduce the closed recipes, then "web only"


First the open world rebuilt closed recipes: RedPajama v1 reproduced LLaMA 1's mix (1.2T tokens), Dolma documented a 3T-token multi-source corpus. Then RefinedWeb (TII, June 2023) made the claim that defined the next generation: web data alone, filtered and deduplicated hard enough (trafilatura on WARC, Gopher rules, MinHash and exact-substring dedup, no learned filter), matches curated mixtures. Read its margins, though: +2.8 points over the Pile at 1B but only +0.5 over C4, and C4 put through RefinedWeb's own pipeline scored above RefinedWeb. The robust lesson is "filter and deduplicate the web well".



Generation 3 (2024): every step ablated, and learned quality filters


FineWeb (Hugging Face, April 2024; same lead author as RefinedWeb) rebuilt that approach in the open datatrove library on every crawl and published an ablation per step (1.71B parameters, 28B tokens per test). Three findings changed practice: extract from WARC, not WET; deduplicate each crawl separately, not globally; and drop C4's terminal-punctuation rule (it helped but threw away about 30% of tokens) for three custom rules (FineWeb paper). It now covers 110 crawls (v1.4.0, July 2025), "more than 18.5T" GPT-2 tokens.


Then learned quality filters won. FineWeb-Edu had Llama-3-70B-Instruct grade 460,000 pages for educational value, trained a small regressor on the grades, and kept scores of 3 or more: 8% of FineWeb, 1.3T tokens. DCLM (June 2024), a benchmark for data with fixed models, found that a cheap fastText classifier trained to tell instruction-like text (OpenHermes 2.5, top r/ExplainLikeImFive answers) from ordinary web text, keeping the top 10%, beat every other filter it tried (30.2 Core against 27.5 with no model-based filter, DCLM Table 4; Quality classifiers and data selection has every row and why they work). Nemotron-CC (NVIDIA, December 2024) argued the other side: 10% of the web is fine for a 1T-token run and runs out for 15T. It scored pages with three classifiers into 20 buckets, kept the medium ones, rewrote the low ones with an LLM and turned high ones into question-answer pairs: 4.4T real plus 1.9T synthetic tokens. Its high-quality subset beat DCLM by 5.6 MMLU points (59.0 against 53.4, 8B, 1T tokens; Nemotron-CC Table 5).



Generation 4 (2025 to 2026): labelled pools, synthetic text, PDFs and licences


The newest corpora are pools with labels from which a mix is drawn. Dolma 3 (Ai2, October 2025): a 9.31T-token pool from 104 crawls, deduplicated three ways (exact, MinHash, suffix arrays for repeats of 500 bytes or more) and bucketed by topic and quality; Olmo 3's 5.93T mix up-samples the best buckets and adds 805B tokens of OCR-read science PDFs. Nemotron-CC v2 and v2.1 (August and December 2025) add 2024 to 2025 crawls and several trillion LLM-written tokens, behind a training-only agreement. FinePDFs (September 2025) mines 3T tokens from PDFs; FineWeb2 takes FineWeb's recipe to 1,868 language-script pairs (section 6). At the other end Common Pile v0.1 (June 2025) keeps only openly licensed text and shows how little web that leaves: 15.2B tokens (section 7).



The other corpora of each generation (ROOTS to Essential-Web)


Generation 1. The Pile's evidence was perplexity: a 1.3B model trained on it scored 5.59 WikiText perplexity against 8.27 for CC-100 and 11.75 for raw Common Crawl.


Generation 2. ROOTS (BigScience, 2022) built BLOOM's 1.6 TB in 46 languages with per-language thresholds set by native speakers, a governance model more than a recipe. RedPajama v1 (Together, April 2023) rebuilt LLaMA 1's undisclosed mix from its paper (1.2T tokens); SlimPajama (Cerebras) deduplicated it globally to 627B. Dolma (Ai2, from August 2023) released a documented 3T-token multi-source corpus with its toolkit.


Generation 3. Alongside FineWeb, DCLM and Nemotron-CC: RedPajama v2 (a raw 30T-token pool with 46 quality signals per document), Zyda and Zyda-2 (cross-deduplicated open sets), TxT360 (global dedup keeping each page's duplicate count, so popular pages can be up-weighted). The full Nemotron-CC, built for long runs, scores 0.4 below DCLM on MMLU (53.0) and slightly above on the 10-task average (57.8 against 57.0) at 8B and 1T tokens.


Generation 4. Essential-Web (June 2025) labels 24T tokens on 12 taxonomy dimensions so that a subset is a query.

4. One crawl file, what each corpus kept


The topic's running example is one raw WARC file from the July 2024 crawl (Topic: data-curation-and-datasets, CC-MAIN-2024-30, segment 1720763514387.30, file 00000): 20,991 HTTP responses, of which 20,617 are HTML pages with status 200. FineWeb and FineWeb-Edu record the source WARC file of every document, so their documents from this file can be matched to the raw pages by URL. Predict before stepping: of the 20,617 pages, how many end up in FineWeb-Edu?


A tenth survives FineWeb's rules; under 1% survives the educational filter














One cell per HTML page, in the order the crawler stored them. FineWeb kept 2,423 (11.8%), FineWeb-Edu 159 (0.77%). Run here on CPU, the published classifier gives 161 pages a score of 3 or more (it reproduces FineWeb-Edu's 159 within 2 pages, independently). The last step adds the Nemotron-CC pages found by streaming its files.


Three things to take from it. The bulk of the loss happens before any learned filter: FineWeb's rules, language ID and deduplication already drop about nine pages in ten (the topic's Pipeline lab measures where, stage by stage). An educational filter at threshold 3 keeps 6.6% of what FineWeb kept, so such a corpus is small by design: the 1.3T against 18.5T of section 3. And moving the cut from 3 to 2 multiplies what survives by about four (the toggle), the trade Nemotron-CC made explicit: for a long run, more medium-quality tokens can beat repeating a small high-quality set.


Nemotron-CC keeps a different set. Only 62% of the Nemotron-CC pages found from this file are also in FineWeb: different extraction (jusText instead of trafilatura) and heuristic filters applied only to its lower buckets keep different pages. Its total is lower than FineWeb's 2,423, not higher, because Nemotron-CC deduplicated globally across its 99 crawls, so a page already captured earlier survives under the earlier crawl's record. A per-file view measures what each pipeline did with one crawl, not how big each corpus is.

Nemotron-CC v1's files are split by quality bucket, not by source WARC, so finding this file's pages means reading the crawl's whole share. Each record carries the warc_record_id of its page, so the files can be streamed and matched without storing them (src/scripts/nemo_scan.py). In about two hours, 166 of its 410 real-text files for CC-MAIN-2024-30 were read (40%; 61 GB streamed; round-robin over the five buckets; the low bucket completely), finding 442 of this file's pages. 5. How corpora are compared, and how to read the comparisons


A corpus's quality cannot be seen by reading it. The method is a data ablation: train the same small model with the same settings on a fixed number of tokens from each candidate, score every model on the same suite, and credit the difference to the data. Scales range from FineWeb's 1.71B parameters on 28B tokens per filtering decision (350B for corpus comparisons) and DCLM's five fixed scales (412M on 8.2B tokens to 6.9B on 276B, 53 evaluations) to NVIDIA's 8B models on 1T tokens. Such runs cost thousands of GPU-hours, so almost every published comparison comes from a corpus's own builders.


Every corpus wins its own paper


Pick a study. The highlighted bar is the study's own corpus (a vendor number); every other bar is an independent measurement of somebody else's corpus. Cells copied from each paper's table or released file, never read off a plot (src/make_ablations.py).


Five checks before believing a comparison:


    

  1. Who ran it. The one third-party run of Nemotron-CC-HQ, DCLM and FineWeb-Edu side by side (open-sci-ref, 1.7B on 1T tokens) puts them in that order, 0.66, 0.57, 0.56; two of its authors also wrote DCLM.
    2. 

  2. Which harness and task format. DCLM-Baseline beats FineWeb-Edu by 13.5 MMLU points under DCLM's 5-shot LLM Foundry setup (50.8 against 37.3 at 7B, 280B tokens), and Hugging Face's SmolLM2 runs put FineWeb-Edu ahead (37.5 against 35.5). Multiple-choice formats reward a model that has learned to answer A/B/C/D; cloze formats reward knowledge in running text.
    3. 

  3. Whether the filter was aimed at the benchmark. FineWeb-Edu's card says higher thresholds help MMLU and ARC and hurt HellaSwag and PIQA; Nemotron-CC-HQ's third-party lead sits mostly in MMLU and CommonsenseQA (without them: 0.679, 0.652, 0.638). "Better corpus" partly means "better at these tests".
    4. 

  4. Small margins against noise. FineWeb's final scores put FineWeb, RefinedWeb, C4 and Dolma within 0.84 points, from one run each; a gap under about a point at these scales is a tie (Eval statistics puts error bars on such numbers).
    5. 

  5. Scale and repetition. A filter keeping 10% of the web wins a 100B-token comparison where nothing repeats and may lose a 15T-token run; the full Nemotron-CC loses 0.4 MMLU to DCLM at 1T tokens and is designed to win at 15T.
    6. 



Predict: in DCLM's own appendix, when the 7B models trained on DCLM-Baseline and on FineWeb-Edu are scored with Hugging Face's lighteval instead of LLM Foundry, what happens to DCLM's 13.5-point MMLU lead?




It nearly disappears: the appendix says the two models "perform quite similarly" under lighteval, while DCLM is "better by close to 10 points" in LLM Foundry (DCLM, appendix G). The data did not change; the way the question was asked did.


Contamination of the benchmarks themselves is Decontamination, PII and data governance.

7. Licences: four layers, read from the bottom


Not legal advice. What the dataset cards and terms say, verbatim, and what courts decided as of 2026-10-14, from published summaries. The governance side (opt-outs, data cards, provenance) is Decontamination, PII and data governance.


"What licence is FineWeb under?" has four answers, one per layer, and a corpus is only as usable as its most restrictive layer. A field on a higher layer cannot remove an obligation from the layer below.


The licence field covers only the third layer
Pick a corpus to fill its four layers from its card (src/data/corpora.json).


    

  1. The content. Each page, book, paper or file belongs to its author; a crawl does not change that. Some content carries an open licence (Wikipedia's CC BY-SA, a repository's MIT licence, a CC BY paper); most web pages carry none, which means all rights reserved.
    2. 

  2. The crawler's or archive's terms. Common Crawl's terms of use; Software Heritage's agreement for The Stack v2; the Internet Archive's terms for HPLT.
    3. 

  3. The dataset licence, covering the compilation (selection, cleaning, metadata). ODC-By 1.0 (FineWeb, C4, Dolma, RefinedWeb) and CC BY 4.0 (DCLM) allow redistribution with attribution; CC0 (HPLT's packaging) waives even that. NVIDIA's Data Agreement for Model Training (Nemotron-CC v2 and later, Nemotron-CC-Math) allows training your own models and forbids redistributing, reselling or relicensing the data. Some fields are wrong: Ultra-FineWeb is tagged Apache 2.0 though it is a filtered subset of ODC-By FineWeb.
    4. 

  4. The access gate. None (most), a click-through (The Stack, Nemotron-CC-Math, CulturaX), or manual approval (Nemotron-CC v2 and v2.1). Gates attach terms that bind what you do afterwards.
    5. 



Two corpora mark the extremes. Common Pile v0.1 (June 2025) keeps only public-domain text and licences meeting the Open Definition (CC BY, CC BY-SA, CC0, permissive software licences), checks against "licence laundering" (sites relabelling others' content), and keeps the licence per document: 8 TB in all, but only 15.2B tokens of licence-verified web pages, because almost no web page states an open licence. Its 7B Comma models are, by its authors' account, competitive with LLaMA 1 and 2 7B at similar budgets (vendor). At the other end, Darwin-CC (2026) is tagged CC BY 4.0 but derived from Nemotron-CC: if from v2 or later, NVIDIA's agreement forbids exactly that redistribution; which version it used is not stated.


What the courts add, without a legal opinion: how the data was acquired mattered as much as what was done with it (pirated books cost Anthropic a US$1.5B settlement, while training on lawfully acquired books was held fair use in Bartz v. Anthropic); memorisation of whole works is the risk European courts picked up (GEMA v. OpenAI); and keeping per-document provenance (source, crawl, licence field, date) is what lets you remove a source later.

Correction to the old page's 150M to 350M recipe
The old page recommended "FineWeb-Edu (the 1.3T score>=3 cut)" for a 150M to 350M model, said SmolLM2-135M and 360M trained on 2T and 4T tokens (correct: SmolLM2 paper), and that "SmolLM2 converged to" a 50/50 FineWeb-Edu and DCLM mix. Interview rehearsal (six questions, answers inside)


What is the difference between WARC, WAT and WET, and which would you build a corpus from?
WARC holds the raw HTTP responses (headers and HTML), WAT JSON metadata and links, WET Common Crawl's extracted text. Build from WARC: your own extractor (trafilatura, resiliparse) keeps more of the main text and less boilerplate, and FineWeb's ablation found WARC extraction clearly better; WET is fine for quick experiments. WET is about 7% of the WARC bytes.


FineWeb deduplicates each crawl separately. Why not deduplicate across all crawls?
In FineWeb's ablations, global MinHash over all crawls removed up to 90% of old crawls and what survived was mostly rare, lower-quality text; the 31B tokens kept by global dedup in the 2013-48 crawl trained a worse model than the 171B tokens it removed. Per-crawl dedup kept popular pages several times, which acted as useful up-weighting. The cost is repetition at very large budgets.


DCLM keeps the top 10% by a fastText classifier. What is the classifier trained on, and why might it inflate benchmark scores?
Positives: OpenHermes 2.5 instructions plus top r/ExplainLikeImFive answers; negatives: ordinary web pages. It selects text that reads like questions answered clearly, which resembles benchmark items (MMLU-style questions). That is part of why it wins MMLU-heavy evaluations; it beat every alternative filter in DCLM's Table 4 (30.2 Core against 27.5 for no learned filter).


How would you compare two candidate corpora fairly?
Same model, tokenizer, hyperparameters and token budget; several seeds or data samples to measure noise; a benchmark suite chosen before the comparison with early-signal tasks, in more than one task format; decontaminate both corpora against it; report per-task scores, not only an average; and say who ran it.


A colleague wants to train a commercial model on FineWeb-Edu, Nemotron-CC v2 and The Stack v2, and publish the cleaned data. What do you check?
FineWeb-Edu: ODC-By plus Common Crawl terms, redistributable with attribution. Nemotron-CC v2: the NVIDIA agreement allows training but forbids redistributing the data, so it cannot be in the published set. The Stack v2: content only through Software Heritage under an agreement, each file under its own licence, opt-outs to honour. Then keep provenance per document and run it past counsel.


System design: assemble the pretraining data for a 7B model trained on 4T tokens, with a commercial licence and a 3-month timeline.
Requirements. 4T training tokens; English-first with code and maths; commercially usable; reproducible; decontaminated against the evaluation suite.


Sources. Do not re-crawl: start from released, redistributable pools. Web about 80%: DCLM-Baseline (3.8T, CC BY) and FineWeb-Edu (1.3T, ODC-By), or Nemotron-CC v1 buckets; code about 10% (Stack-Edu or The Stack v2 under the Software Heritage agreement); maths about 5% (FineMath, MegaMath); papers about 5% (peS2o, FinePDFs). Check epochs: 3.2T web from 5.1T is fine, but DCLM's internal duplication means globally deduplicating it first (about 750B unique) and up-weighting deliberately.


Pipeline. Re-tokenise everything with the model's tokenizer and record counts; global MinHash dedup across sources (datatrove or NeMo Curator, sharded by hash bands); decontaminate with 13-gram matching against every evaluation set; PII scrubbing; provenance columns (source, version, URL, licence) kept per document; write tokenised shards (see Storage and loading at scale).


Validation. 1B-parameter ablations at 20 to 50B tokens for the mix and any filter you change; a held-out anneal set of the best data for the last 5 to 10%.


Cost drivers. Classifier scoring (FineWeb-Edu's classifier took 6,000 H100 hours for 15T tokens: avoid rescoring what is already scored), dedup memory, and ablation compute. Risks. Licence layers, contamination, and over-filtering for the budget.

What to train on: a starting mix for your budget and goal


Choose the model size, the training budget in tokens, the goal and the licence constraint. The tool proposes a starting mix assembled from published open recipes (each share names the recipe it comes from), then checks the one thing a mix most often gets wrong: whether each corpus is big enough for the tokens you will draw from it. Epochs = tokens drawn from a corpus divided by its published size; past about 4 epochs, repeated data is worth markedly less than fresh data (Muennighoff et al. 2023; the Data mixing page takes the curve apart). When a corpus would be repeated too often, the tool moves the excess to the next larger corpus of the same kind that your licence choice allows.


A starting point, not an optimiser. The shares are published choices, not fitted to your goal; sizes are each corpus's own published count in its own tokenizer (Reading section 2: within a few percent for English web text, more for code and other languages). Run your own small ablation before a large run.

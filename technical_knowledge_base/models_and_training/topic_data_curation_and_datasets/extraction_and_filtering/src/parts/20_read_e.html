6. Language identification

The problem. Common Crawl is multilingual and its pages do not reliably say what language they are in. An English corpus must drop everything else; a multilingual corpus must route each document to its language. Language ID runs right after extraction because it is cheap and removes most of the crawl: here 12,270 of 19,241 extracted documents (64%) are not English above the threshold (root's Pipeline lab).

How a fastText language identifier works. The text (newlines replaced by spaces: datatrove utils/lid.py:54) is split into words, and each word into its character n-grams with boundary markers: "<the>" gives "<t", "th", "he", "e>", "<th", "the", "he>" and so on. Every word and n-gram is hashed into one of a fixed number of buckets and looked up in a table of small vectors; the document's vector is their average; one linear layer and a softmax turn it into a probability for each label (Joulin et al., "Bag of Tricks", arXiv 1607.01759). It ignores word order entirely, which is why it is fast (about a millisecond for a whole web document here, a hundredth of trafilatura's time, rough) and why it is fooled by text that is not prose. A label for noise matters: a model without one must call a page of numbers some language.

The threshold, and where 0.65 comes from

A document is kept as English when the model's English probability passes a threshold. RefinedWeb introduced 0.65 for lid.176's top-language score and said why: below it "usually corresponds to pages without any natural text" (section 3.1, arXiv 2306.01116); FineWeb kept it. Others chose differently published: CCNet and Dolma 0.5 (Dolma section 5.1: it removed 61.7% of the data by bytes), mC4 0.7, Nemotron-CC 0.3 (with pycld2 as a second opinion), C4 0.99 with langdetect (TFDS c4_utils.py:116), and FineWeb2 a threshold per language: max(0.3, min(0.9, median - one standard deviation)) of that language's score distribution (FineWeb2 section 4.2, arXiv 2506.20920). On our documents that rule gives [[FW2_TH_FT]] for lid.176's English scores and [[FW2_TH_GL]] for GlotLID's derived.

Measured: two models on the same 19,241 documents. lid.176 and GlotLID agree on the top language for [[LID_AGREE_PCT]] of documents. Above 0.65, lid.176 keeps 6,971 as English and GlotLID [[GL_EN_065]]; [[LID_BOTH]] are English for both. Common Crawl's own CLD2 label in the WET header says English for [[CLD2_EN]] of them. Where the models disagree, the documents are mostly not prose: navigation-only pages, product grids, pages mixing two languages. The {{Language ID lab|#t-lid}} has the full confusion table, a threshold slider for both models and the disagreeing documents.

Code-switching and mixed pages

A document-level label hides pages that are partly in another language: a Spanish article with an English menu, an Indonesian shop with English product names, a bilingual government page. Classifying every line of at least four words with GlotLID, [[MIX20]] of the documents labelled English have at least 20% of their line characters confidently (score 0.5 or more) in another language, and [[MIX20_ALL]] of all 19,241 do measured. A document-level filter keeps or drops those lines with the rest; line-level language ID (what GlotLID's authors recommend for low-resource corpora, and what CCNet does for paragraphs) can keep the right part.

Low-resource languages

lid.176 has 176 labels, so a page in a language it does not know is forced onto the closest one it does. GlotLID gave [[GL_NOT_IN_FT]] of our documents a language that lid.176 has no label for (counting by ISO 639-3 code, macrolanguages joined) measured. But read them before celebrating coverage: the top four are Dagbani, Angika, Kirmanjki and Nigerian Pidgin, and lid.176 calls most of them English. A spot check of 24 (hand-checked) found none actually in those languages: they are navigation menus, server directory listings, calendars, timetables, product tables and code in English, Japanese, French, German and others. Text that is not prose lands on whichever small language's training data looked most like lists. This is the "noise" problem GlotLID's authors describe, and the reason FineWeb2 sets thresholds per language and runs further filters for low-resource ones. For a multilingual corpus a large label set decides whether a language exists in the data at all, which is why FineWeb2 switched to GlotLID after training models on documents chosen by each identifier: GlotLID was better on higher-resource languages and slightly behind on lower-resource ones in their runs (FineWeb2 section 4.2 and its Figure 5; plotted, not tabulated) published.

Common mistake: reading the threshold as a quality knob
Documents near 0.65 are mostly English pages that are not prose (the root's Pipeline lab read them: menus, tables, a PHP error log). Raising the threshold removes them, and the cost falls on the edges of English: Dolma audited its identifier on the International Corpus of English (spoken and written English from nine countries) and found every document kept at its threshold of 0.5 but some lost as the threshold rises, "a majority" still kept at 0.9 (Dolma Appendix G and Figure 7, arXiv 2402.00159; the per-region shares are only plotted) published. Measure what a threshold removes before using it as a quality filter; quality has its own classifiers ({{Quality classifiers and data selection|n:3f95c17b0d0d810baaabe41da6c9c50f}}).

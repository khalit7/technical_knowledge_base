6. Language identification

The problem. Common Crawl is multilingual and pages do not reliably say their language. An English corpus must drop everything else; a multilingual one must route each document to its language. Language ID runs right after extraction because it is cheap and removes most of the crawl: here 12,270 of 19,241 extracted documents (64%) are not English above the threshold.

How a fastText identifier works. Every word is broken into character n-grams with boundary markers, each word and n-gram is hashed to a row of a table of small vectors, the document's vector is their average, and one linear layer with a softmax gives a probability per language (Joulin et al., "Bag of Tricks", arXiv 1607.01759). Word order is ignored entirely, which is why it is fast (about a millisecond per web document here, a hundredth of trafilatura's time rough) and why text that is not prose fools it.

One word becomes ten hashed features; the document is their average, so word order never matters

lid.176's settings: character n-grams of 2 to 4, 2,000,000 hash buckets, 16 numbers per vector, 176 labels, read from the model file measured; the input text and the final probabilities are illustrative. Newlines are replaced by spaces before prediction (datatrove utils/lid.py:54).

The threshold, and where 0.65 comes from

A document is kept as English when the English probability passes a threshold. RefinedWeb chose 0.65 for lid.176 because lower scores "usually correspond to pages without any natural text" (section 3.1, arXiv 2306.01116), and FineWeb kept it. Others chose differently published: CCNet and Dolma 0.5, mC4 0.7, Nemotron-CC 0.3, C4 0.99 with langdetect, FineWeb2 a threshold per language. Predict before you drag: most web documents are either clearly English or clearly not, so how much does moving the threshold from 0.5 to 0.8 change the count?

Where the models disagree the documents are mostly not prose: navigation-only pages, product grids, pages mixing two languages. FineWeb2's per-language rule, max(0.3, min(0.9, median minus one standard deviation)) of that language's score distribution (FineWeb2 section 4.2, arXiv 2506.20920), gives [[FW2_TH_FT]] for lid.176's English scores here and [[FW2_TH_GL]] for GlotLID's derived.

Mixed pages. A document label hides pages partly in another language. Classifying every line of at least four words with GlotLID, [[MIX20]] of the documents labelled English have at least 20% of their line characters confidently (score 0.5 or more) in another language, and [[MIX20_ALL]] of all 19,241 do measured. Line-level language ID (GlotLID's authors' advice for low-resource corpora; CCNet does it per paragraph) can keep the right part.

Low-resource languages. lid.176 must force a language it does not know onto one it does; GlotLID gave [[GL_NOT_IN_FT]] of our documents a language lid.176 has no label for (by ISO 639-3 code, macrolanguages joined) measured. But read them: the top four are Dagbani, Angika, Kirmanjki and Nigerian Pidgin, and a spot check of 24 found none actually in those languages (hand-checked): navigation menus, directory listings, calendars, timetables, product tables and code in English, Japanese, French, German and others. Text that is not prose lands on whichever small language's training data looked most like lists.

Common mistake: reading the threshold as a quality knob
Documents near 0.65 are mostly English pages that are not prose (the root's Pipeline lab read them: menus, tables, a PHP error log). Raising the threshold removes them, but the cost falls on the edges of English: in Dolma's audit on the International Corpus of English, documents kept at 0.5 start to go as the threshold rises published. Quality has its own classifiers ({{Quality classifiers and data selection|n:3f95c17b0d0d810baaabe41da6c9c50f}}).

The models' training data, thresholds in their sources, the English audit, FineWeb2's choice (provenance)

lid.176 was trained on Wikipedia, Tatoeba and SETimes (fastText's language-identification page); GlotLID on GlotLID-C, which its paper describes as covering 1,665 languages, mostly low-resource (arXiv 2310.16248; v3 lists 2,102 labels because each is a language plus a script, such as eng_Latn, with "undetermined" labels such as und_Cyrl and a noise label zxx_Latn) published. A label for noise matters: a model without one must call a page of numbers some language.

Dolma audited its identifier on the International Corpus of English (spoken and written English from nine countries) and found every document kept at its threshold of 0.5 but some lost as the threshold rises, "a majority" still kept at 0.9 (Dolma Appendix G and Figure 7, arXiv 2402.00159; the per-region shares are only plotted).

CCNet and Dolma 0.5 (Dolma section 5.1: it removed 61.7% of the data by bytes), mC4 0.7, Nemotron-CC 0.3 (with pycld2 as a second opinion), C4 0.99 with langdetect (TFDS c4_utils.py:116). For a multilingual corpus a large label set decides whether a language exists in the data at all, which is why FineWeb2 switched to GlotLID after training models on documents chosen by each identifier: GlotLID was better on higher-resource languages and slightly behind on lower-resource ones in their runs (FineWeb2 section 4.2 and its Figure 5; plotted, not tabulated) published. Mislabelled lists and menus are the "noise" problem GlotLID's authors describe, and the reason FineWeb2 sets thresholds per language and runs further filters for low-resource ones.

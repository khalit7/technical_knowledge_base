12. Putting it in order: cost and design

Cheapest first, and keep every drop. The order is set by cost per document and by what each stage needs: the URL check reads only the URL; extraction must run before anything reads text; language ID costs about a millisecond and removes most of a multilingual crawl; rules that tokenise (Gopher with spaCy, C4 with NLTK's sentence splitter) cost more; classifiers and MinHash come last, on what survives.

Extraction costs about as much as every filter after it combined

A design that stays debuggable, the shape datatrove and the Dolma toolkit both encourage:

  1. Read WARC files in many parallel tasks; extract with resiliparse if CPU is the constraint, trafilatura with deduplicate=False if precision on articles matters more, and a separate extractor for code and maths pages.
  2. Write every document's statistics (language scores, every rule value) once, as columns, instead of filtering on the fly; thresholds then become queries you can change without re-extracting (the Dolma toolkit's "taggers then mixer" design, and how the {{Rule workbench|#t-rules}} works).
  3. Keep every dropped document with its reason (datatrove's exclusion_writer), label a sample per rule, and diff your output against a published dataset built from the same crawl.
  4. Pin versions and write down every argument, including the ones you did not change.
What transfers to a cluster, and what does not (caveat)

Every count and every kept-or-dropped decision on this page is deterministic given the same versions and inputs, and reproduces per document on a cluster (run0 does reproduce FineWeb's per-document decisions for 2,403 of the 2,425 documents FineWeb kept from this file, root's Pipeline lab). Timings do not transfer: they were taken at a load average of 20 to 90 on a laptop CPU. The per-page timeout does not transfer either: it is wall-clock time, so it drops more pages on a busier node, and the trafilatura cache depends on the order pages reach each worker.

13. Checklists, rehearsal and provenance

Common mistakes, one line each (a checklist of the sections above)
  • Training on WET text because it is already extracted: the same filters keep 877 WET documents against 2,692 from trafilatura and 2,150 from resiliparse; FineWeb and DCLM both measured WET as worse downstream. Re-extract from WARC.
  • Leaving trafilatura's deduplicate=True on in a distributed job: a hidden, order-dependent near-duplicate filter. Turn it off and deduplicate as a separate stage.
  • Expecting a general web pipeline to give you code and maths: route those pages to their own extraction (OpenWebMath's approach) and their own filters.
  • Copying an example script and assuming it reproduces the paper: datatrove's FineWeb example became multilingual when LanguageFilter's default changed in v0.3.0 (2024-08-28); its C4 defaults swap the paper's numbers. Pass every argument explicitly and diff against the published dataset.
  • Judging a rule by how much it removes: the FineWeb duplicated-line rule removed 173 documents here, and 10 of 28 labelled ones were worth keeping. Label a sample of each rule's drops, near the threshold and at random, and report the wrong-drop share with its interval.
  • Using a frozen blocklist: refresh lists, and re-check the prolific domains a list blocks.
  • Calling a word list "toxicity filtering": say what was filtered and measure what was lost.
Interview rehearsal: seven questions with short answers (optional)

Why re-extract text from WARC when Common Crawl gives you WET? WET keeps every visible string, so documents carry menus and widgets. That adds low-value tokens and distorts the statistics the filters use (duplicated lines, short lines, punctuation), so good pages are dropped and bad ones kept. FineWeb's and DCLM's ablations both show WARC extraction ahead; on our file the same filters keep 877 WET documents against 2,692 from trafilatura.

trafilatura or resiliparse for a 100-TB crawl? DCLM found them equal downstream (24.5 against 24.1 Core at 1B scale) and resiliparse about 8 times faster per core, so resiliparse when CPU is the cost. trafilatura has a slightly higher precision on news-like pages and keeps tables as rows; resiliparse keeps code and the tab structure of preformatted text. Either way, turn off trafilatura's cache and handle code and maths separately.

How does jusText decide what is boilerplate? It splits the page into blocks, classifies each from length, link density and stop-word density (good, near-good, short, bad), then revises short and near-good blocks from their nearest good or bad neighbours. It is language-specific through the stop-word list.

Why 0.65 for English language ID, and would you change it? RefinedWeb chose it for lid.176 because lower scores are mostly pages without natural text; FineWeb kept it. For one language it is reasonable; for many, a per-language threshold such as FineWeb2's (median minus one standard deviation, clipped to 0.3 to 0.9) is better, since closely related languages have lower confidence. Check what sits near the threshold before moving it.

A rule removes 10% of your documents. How do you decide whether to keep it? Read a sample of its drops near the threshold and at random and estimate the wrong-drop share with an interval; check what share of tokens it removes; then train small models with and without it (FineWeb's 28-billion-token ablations) and compare benchmark scores. A rule that removes formats (lists, code) rather than quality should be fixed, not tuned.

Line-level or document-level filtering? Line-level edits rescue good paragraphs from debris but can leave incoherent fragments and change the statistics later rules see; document-level rules are safer for coherence and lose more text. C4 did both; FineWeb kept only C4's cheap line edits; RefinedWeb dropped a document when its edits exceeded 5% of words.

Design: build the extraction and filtering stage for a 2-trillion-token English pretraining run from 20 Common Crawl snapshots. About 1.8 million WARC files (90,000 per crawl), so thousands of CPU tasks, one shard of files each, reading from object storage. Per document, in order: URL blocklist (refreshed) and word lists; extraction (resiliparse for throughput, deduplicate off), with code and maths pages routed to dedicated extractors; lid.176 or GlotLID with a checked threshold; Gopher repetition and quality rules, then C4 line edits and FineWeb's rules with explicitly set parameters; PII scrubbing. Write every statistic as columns plus the text, keep dropped documents with reasons, and log per-task stats. Then MinHash per crawl as a separate job, a quality classifier on survivors (GPU), and decontamination. Budget by measured throughput per core and by yield measured on a sample: here 13% of HTML pages and 19% of extracted characters survive the rules, before dedup and the classifier remove more, so size the number of snapshots from that, not from raw crawl size. Validate with a labelled sample per rule and small-model ablations before the full run.

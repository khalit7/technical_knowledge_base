3. Measured: the same pages through six extractors

The experiment. Take the 20,280 pages of our WARC file that pass the URL filter. Extract each with six methods, then run exactly the same filters on every result: run0's datatrove chain (lid.176 English above 0.65, Gopher repetition and quality rules, C4's rules without terminal punctuation, FineWeb's three rules). Count what comes out, and how much of it FineWeb itself kept from this file (2,425 documents; FineWeb used trafilatura with these settings). The chain run on trafilatura's text reproduces run0 exactly: 2,692 kept, 2,404 of them in FineWeb measured.

What it shows. Common Crawl's WET text has three times trafilatura's characters but survives the filters worst: its menus and widgets make the repetition and line rules fire. jusText with an English stop-word list returns nothing for most non-English pages (it acts as a second language filter) yet keeps the most English documents, because its block classification keeps short paragraphs that trafilatura's precision mode discards; only 1,946 of its 3,231 are documents FineWeb kept. Nemotron-CC made the same observation at scale: on 13 Common Crawl snapshots, jusText plus their filters gave 1,380 billion tokens and 104 billion "high-quality" tokens (FineWeb-Edu classifier score 3 or more) against trafilatura's 994 and 80 billion, and jusText without their filters 1,804 and 127 billion (Table 1, arXiv 2412.02595, published; the table's "+28.6%" and "+57.4%" are consistent with its rounded 80, 104 and 127 within rounding).

Is the text right, not just long? Counting kept documents says nothing about whether the extractor chose the right lines. For that you need pages where people marked the main content by hand. The ablations that matter, as published. FineWeb trained 1.71-billion-parameter models on 28 billion tokens of trafilatura-on-WARC text and of WET text, both with only fastText English filtering, and the WARC model was ahead throughout (FineWeb paper section 3.2 and its Figure 1, arXiv 2406.17557v2; the curves are plotted, no table, so no number is read from them here; note the section's text says "Fig. 2" for a result whose caption is Figure 1). DCLM (Table 3, 1B parameters, 1x compute) scored 24.1 Core with resiliparse, 24.5 with trafilatura and 20.7 with WET, and chose resiliparse for its speed published. So the big step is WARC instead of WET; among main-content extractors the downstream difference is small, and the choice is about cost, recall (Nemotron-CC) and what happens to code, tables and maths (section 5).

4. Why extractors fail, page by page

The root's Pipeline lab found three telling pages. Reading the HTML and the code explains each one; open them side by side in the {{Extraction comparer|#t-ext}}.

The calendar widget. An advocacy site's sidebar holds a WordPress calendar (<table id="wp-calendar">, one cell per day). WET keeps every cell: "M", "T", "W", "1", "2" ... as lines of text. trafilatura and resiliparse both drop it, for the same reason: an ancestor's class or id contains "sidebar" (trafilatura's OVERALL_DISCARD_XPATH, xpaths.py:105-130; resiliparse's sidebar_cls_regex, html2text.pyx:476, matches the list's class "sidebar_widgets").
Menu lines ending in "|". A university weather page is laid out as one HTML <table>: a menu cell and a content cell. trafilatura keeps tables and writes each cell followed by " | " and each row on its own line (xml.py:320-343), so the whole menu ("Animate: ON | OFF Switch Channel: Visible True Color ...") becomes one long line ending in "|". resiliparse writes the same cells as separate lines. Layout tables are a 1990s habit, still common on university and government sites.
The "dropped" data table. A railway reference page's mileage list is not a table at all: it is a <pre> block. Both trafilatura and resiliparse keep its two data lines; what differs is that resiliparse also keeps the heading and the "Last update" line, and only resiliparse keeps the tab between mileage and place name. Correction to the Pipeline lab's caption ("resiliparse drops the data"): neither drops it; its own line masks show both keeping lines 20 and 21 measured.

The cache that makes extraction depend on page order

datatrove's Trafilatura step calls extract(..., deduplicate=True) by default (pipeline/extractors/trafilatura.py:27,49-54). In trafilatura that switches on a process-wide least-recently-used cache of 4,096 text segments (deduplication.py:231, settings.py:191): every paragraph over 100 characters is counted, and from its fourth sighting on it is dropped (duplicate_test, lines 242-253; MAX_REPETITIONS = 2, settings.cfg:29); the same test on the whole extracted body drops entire documents (core.py:228). It is a cheap within-site boilerplate remover: a disclaimer repeated on every page of a site disappears after three pages.

Why it matters on a cluster. FineWeb split each crawl into 8,000 tasks (examples/fineweb.py:65); each task's sandbox process has its own cache, and datatrove restarts the sandbox after a timeout, which empties it. So the same page can come out differently depending on the task it lands in and on how loaded the node was. The effect is small in aggregate (1.25% of characters here) but it breaks reproducibility, and it is a near-duplicate filter nobody configured. Pass deduplicate=False and deduplicate later, on purpose ({{Deduplication|n:3f95c17b0d0d8171ac17e43ab5ea276f}}).

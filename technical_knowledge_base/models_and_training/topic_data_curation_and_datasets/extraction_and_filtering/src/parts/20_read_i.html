13. Common mistakes

Training on WET text because it is already extracted
On our file the same filters keep 877 documents from WET against 2,692 from trafilatura and 2,150 from resiliparse; FineWeb and DCLM both measured WET as worse downstream. Re-extract from WARC.
Leaving trafilatura's deduplicate=True on in a distributed job
It is a hidden, order-dependent near-duplicate filter: 310 of our pages lost all their text to it and the output changed with page order. Turn it off and deduplicate as a separate, reproducible stage.
Expecting a general web pipeline to give you code and maths
trafilatura 1.11 flattens code blocks, every main-content extractor tested but jusText deletes Wikipedia's maths, and C4's curly-bracket rule removes any page with a brace: FineWeb's 50,000-document sample contains no "{" at all. Route code and maths pages to their own extraction (OpenWebMath's approach) and their own filters.
Copying an example script and assuming it reproduces the paper
datatrove's FineWeb example became multilingual when LanguageFilter's default changed in v0.3.0 (2024-08-28); its C4 defaults swap the paper's numbers; its FineWeb duplicated-line default is 0.01, not the paper's 0.1. Pass every argument explicitly and diff against the published dataset.
Judging a rule by how much it removes
The FineWeb duplicated-line rule removed 173 documents here, and 10 of 28 labelled ones were worth keeping. Label a sample of each rule's drops, near the threshold and at random, and report the wrong-drop share with its interval.
Labelling a document from its first lines
Truncated teasers and hard-wrapped essays look alike in the first 200 characters. Look at the structure (line count, line lengths, where lines end) before deciding.
Using the language-ID threshold as a quality filter
Near 0.65 sit English pages that are not prose, and raising it also removes English varieties and mixed-language pages. Use a quality classifier for quality.
Using a frozen blocklist
datatrove's domain list is RefinedWeb's from 2023; today's UT1 adult list has 62,986 more domains. Refresh lists, and re-check the prolific domains a list blocks (RefinedWeb removed fewer than 100 benign ones by hand).
Calling a word list "toxicity filtering"
A bad-word match is mostly anatomy, medicine and everyday slang; Dodge et al. showed it removes dialects and identity mentions disproportionately. Say what was filtered and measure what was lost.

14. Interview questions, with short answers

Why re-extract text from WARC when Common Crawl gives you WET?
WET keeps every visible string, so documents carry menus and widgets. That adds low-value tokens and distorts the statistics the filters use (duplicated lines, short lines, punctuation), so good pages are dropped and bad ones kept. FineWeb's and DCLM's ablations both show WARC extraction ahead; on our file the same filters keep 877 WET documents against 2,692 from trafilatura.
trafilatura or resiliparse for a 100-TB crawl?
DCLM found them equal downstream (24.5 against 24.1 Core at 1B scale) and resiliparse about 8 times faster per core, so resiliparse when CPU is the cost. trafilatura has a slightly higher precision on news-like pages and keeps tables as rows; resiliparse keeps code and the tab structure of preformatted text. Either way, turn off trafilatura's cache and handle code and maths separately.
How does jusText decide what is boilerplate?
It splits the page into blocks, classifies each from length, link density and stop-word density (good, near-good, short, bad), then revises short and near-good blocks from their nearest good or bad neighbours. It is language-specific through the stop-word list.
Why 0.65 for English language ID, and would you change it?
RefinedWeb chose it for lid.176 because lower scores are mostly pages without natural text; FineWeb kept it. For one language it is reasonable; for many, a per-language threshold such as FineWeb2's (median minus one standard deviation, clipped to 0.3 to 0.9) is better, since closely related languages have lower confidence. Check what sits near the threshold before moving it.
A rule removes 10% of your documents. How do you decide whether to keep it?
Read a sample of its drops near the threshold and at random and estimate the wrong-drop share with an interval; check what share of tokens it removes; then train small models with and without it (FineWeb's 28-billion-token ablations) and compare benchmark scores. A rule that removes formats (lists, code) rather than quality should be fixed, not tuned.
Line-level or document-level filtering?
Line-level edits rescue good paragraphs from debris but can leave incoherent fragments and change the statistics later rules see; document-level rules are safer for coherence and lose more text. C4 did both; FineWeb kept only C4's cheap line edits; RefinedWeb dropped a document when its edits exceeded 5% of words.
Design: build the extraction and filtering stage for a 2-trillion-token English pretraining run from 20 Common Crawl snapshots.
About 1.8 million WARC files (90,000 per crawl), so thousands of CPU tasks, one shard of files each, reading from object storage. Per document, in order: URL blocklist (refreshed) and word lists; extraction (resiliparse for throughput, deduplicate off), with code and maths pages routed to dedicated extractors; lid.176 or GlotLID with a checked threshold; Gopher repetition and quality rules, then C4 line edits and FineWeb's rules with explicitly set parameters; PII scrubbing. Write every statistic as columns plus the text, keep dropped documents with reasons, and log per-task stats. Then MinHash per crawl as a separate job, a quality classifier on survivors (GPU), and decontamination. Budget by measured throughput per core and by yield measured on a sample: here 13% of HTML pages and 19% of extracted characters survive the rules, before dedup and the classifier remove more, so size the number of snapshots from that, not from raw crawl size. Validate with a labelled sample per rule and small-model ablations before the full run.

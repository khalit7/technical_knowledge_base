What the filters wrongly drop (and wrongly keep)

Method. The 2,617 documents were split into five strata by what the two classifiers do at their published cut-offs, and 20 documents were drawn from each (seed 0), so the rare disagreements get as many reads as the common case. Each was read (its first 900 characters) and labelled: would I want it in the pretraining data of a general language model? Yes means coherent, informative or well-formed natural text of any kind (news, how-to, recipes, reviews, essays, organisation pages with real content); no means commerce boilerplate, navigation, cookie banners, templated directory pages, spun or keyword-stuffed text; unsure otherwise. A second tag records the format and whether the text reads like spun or machine-generated SEO copy.

Who labelled. The agent that built this lab (Claude), on 2026-10-14; the labels, with a one-line reason each, are committed in src/qual/labels.jsonl (ids only, no text) so they can be re-labelled by a person. One labeller and 20 documents per stratum: each stratum's share has a 95% interval of roughly ±20 points. And human judgement is not the target: DCLM had 16 annotators label about 500 pages (71% inter-annotator agreement) and found that a filter's agreement with them did not predict how good a model trained on its output was (AskLLM agreed best, about 82% ROC-AUC, and trained worse models than fastText filters at about 73%; {{DCLM, Appendix on human judgement|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11794}}). So "wrongly dropped" below means "dropped although a reader would call it fine text", not "proven to hurt".

Train your own quality classifier (on a laptop CPU)

The FineWeb-Edu recipe has two expensive parts: the LLM grades (460,000 Llama-3-70B calls) and the encoder that imitates them. The grades are public, so the second part can be redone with something much cheaper. Here a fastText classifier, the same kind as DCLM's, learns the binary question "did Llama 3 give this page 3 or more?" from of the published annotations, and is tested on it never saw.

How fastText classifies, from its source (fastText v0.9.2): the text is split on whitespace (src/dictionary.cc:208-232; a newline becomes an end-of-sentence token), each word and each word pair is hashed into one of 2 million rows of an embedding table (dictionary.cc:312-323: h = h * 116049371 + hashes[j], then h % bucket), the document vector is the average of its rows (src/model.cc:42-50), and one linear layer with a softmax gives the label probabilities. No attention, no word order beyond pairs: that is why it scores a document in about 2 ms on one core and why it can be fooled by phrasing.

The 82% is most consistent with the two-class macro F1. Always ask which F1, on which class balance: 9% of pages are positives, so a classifier that drops everything has 91% accuracy.

So is an encoder worth it? On the same rows the encoder beats bigrams clearly in F1 and rank correlation, and takes about 60 times longer per document (0.14 s on 4 threads against about 2 ms on one). On our 2,617 documents our fastText ranks like the published classifier with a Spearman of (and with DCLM's), and keeps documents at its threshold. This is the trade every pipeline makes: DCLM chose fastText for the whole 240T-token pool; FineWeb-Edu paid 6,000 H100 hours for the encoder.

Nemotron-CC: keep more, sorted into buckets

Nemotron-CC scores every document with three classifiers (two FineWeb-Edu-style encoders trained on Mistral 8x22B and Nemotron-340B grades, plus DCLM's fastText), turns each classifier's ranking into 20 buckets of about 5% each, takes the maximum bucket over the three, and groups the 20 buckets into 5 quality levels by annealing an 8B model on each (published, Section 2.2). The public data is partitioned by those levels, so the first 200 documents of each level for CC-MAIN-2024-30 were scored here with the two public classifiers.

Perplexity filtering, CCNet's way

CCNet tokenises each page with SentencePiece, scores it with a 5-gram KenLM model trained on that language's Wikipedia, and computes the page's perplexity: 10 to the power of minus the average log10 probability per token, so a page the model finds predictable gets a low number. Pages are then split by per-language cut-offs into head, middle and tail. Its original English models were downloaded (4.4 GB) and its code ported line for line; the probe sentences from the model's documentation come out at 685 ("I am very perplexed") and 12,223 ("im hella trippin"), the same direction as documented.

Traps found while reproducing

  1. Newlines change DCLM's scores. fastText's Python predict refuses text containing "\n" (FastText.py:211-213), so every pipeline must flatten lines first. DCLM and the Dolma toolkit join lines with a space (dolma/taggers/quality.py:29); datatrove's FastTextClassifierFilter replaces "\n" with newline_replacement, whose default is the empty string (datatrove/pipeline/filters/fasttext_filter.py:47, 99), gluing the last word of one line to the first of the next. With the space, our scores reproduce DCLM-baseline's stored fasttext_score to within on 2,000 rows; with datatrove's default they differ by 0.07 on average and up to 0.97, and on our WARC the keep decision flips for of 2,617 documents. Pass newline_replacement=" ".
  2. "Top 10%" is a pool-relative threshold. DCLM's cut, P(hq) ≥ 0.0181, was the 90th percentile of DCLM-Pool after RefinedWeb-style filters. Applied to other data it keeps whatever fraction lands above it: of our documents, of FineWeb, of FineWeb-Edu. To reproduce a keep fraction, recompute the percentile on your own pool.
  3. Three different dataset sizes for one classifier. The FineWeb paper says 460,000 annotations with 410,000 for training and 50,000 held out; the card says it was trained on 450,000 and evaluated on a hold-out of 46,867; the annotation dataset today has 467,424 rows (8 files of 58,428). None of these is wrong on its own terms, but the published split cannot be rebuilt.
  4. F1 without a definition (section 6), and rounding: int_score is int(round(max(0, min(score, 5)))) (run_edu_bert.py:24), so "int_score ≥ 3" means score above 2.5; NeMo Curator labels a document high quality when the raw score is ≥ 2.5 (nemo_curator/stages/text/classifiers/fineweb_edu.py:117-118). Python rounds 2.5 down to 2, so the two differ only at exactly 2.5.
  5. The published scores do reproduce on CPU. On FineWeb-Edu's 159 rows from our WARC, our float32 CPU scores differ from the stored ones by at most 0.024 (the published run used bfloat16 on GPU, run_edu_bert.py:9), and 158 of 159 rounded scores match. All FineWeb-Edu documents that are also in run0's output score ≥ 2.5 here (), and only of our other documents do: our extraction, filters and scores reproduce theirs almost exactly on this file.
  6. One fastText config diverged. Unigrams with learning rate 0.1 stopped with "Encountered NaN" on the full 188k-row training file, while bigram configs trained fine; on a 20,000-row subset the same config trains. Lower the learning rate or check for extremely long lines (the longest training document has about 37,000 tokens) before concluding a feature set is worse.

Common mistakes, and interview questions

  • Reading a classifier probability as a calibrated chance. DCLM keeps pages it rates 2% likely to be "high quality". Thresholds are percentiles of a pool.
  • Copying a threshold across datasets. A cut that keeps 10% of a raw pool keeps 25% of FineWeb and 61% of FineWeb-Edu.
  • Trusting a library default for preprocessing. One empty-string default flips 4% of keep decisions here.
  • Judging a filter by its agreement with people. DCLM found no correlation between agreement with human labels and the trained model's quality. Judge filters by training small models on their output.
  • Forgetting what the reference excludes. An educational filter removes news and fiction by design; a filter trained on instructions rewards text that sounds like an answer, including generated filler and cookie banners.
  • Scoring only the start of a document. The encoder sees 512 tokens; a long page is judged on its opening.
Interview questions with short answers

Why use a fastText classifier at all when encoders are better? Cost at the scale that matters: a few milliseconds per document on one CPU core against a GPU forward pass; DCLM scored a 240T-token pool. Here the encoder agreed better with the LLM labels (F1 against ) at about 60 times the time per document, on 4 threads against one.

Your quality filter keeps 10%. Your run needs 15T tokens and you have 1.3T after filtering. What do you do? Repeat (up to about 4 epochs costs little, Muennighoff et al.), loosen the threshold, ensemble classifiers with different targets to raise recall (Nemotron-CC), and rephrase the lower tiers with an LLM instead of dropping them; check each choice with small annealing runs.

How do you know a quality filter is not throwing away something important? Stratified sample of what it drops, read and labelled; per-domain and per-format drop rates; perplexity of the trained model on held-out slices such as news, dialects and code; and ablation models trained with and without the filter.

Two classifiers keep 10% each and overlap 15%. Which do you use? Neither choice is answered by the overlap; train small models on each kept set and on the union, and compare on your target evaluations. Low overlap says the union is a cheap way to more good tokens.

What does the LLM-annotation step cost, and what is it for? Hundreds of thousands of LLM calls once (FineWeb-Edu: 460,000 pages); the labels then train a small model that is cheap enough for the whole crawl. The prompt is the real definition of quality, so read it.

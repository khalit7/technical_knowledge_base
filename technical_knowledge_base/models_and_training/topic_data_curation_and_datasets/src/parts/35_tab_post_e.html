Common mistakes

Filtering a mixture with one rule for all subsets
Rules that are right for one subset are wrong for another: "drop refusals" is right for a helpfulness subset and destroys a safety subset; "English only" deletes the multilingual one; "drop near-duplicate prompts" on RLVR's instruction-following rows would remove rows that share a base prompt but carry different constraints (the largest such cluster holds 16 rows). Count removals per subset before running anything.
Training on ties
A preference pair whose two answers got the same score teaches nothing true, and a pair with identical texts teaches noise. Check the score margin distribution of any preference set; UltraFeedback binarized has ties in this sample.
Trusting a checker you have not run on correct answers
Run the checker on reference solutions first: every rejection is a false negative the RL run will push against. Here a label format (a thousands comma) makes GSM8K prompts unwinnable under Tulu 3's checker, and the strict Minerva checker accepts many correct MATH solutions only by accident (its fallback normalises the whole response).
Reading a sample's mix as the dataset's mix
This page's SFT sample is 1,000 rows from each of five subsets; percentages here describe the sample. Recompute on the full mixture before quoting a share.

Interview questions

Design the data pipeline for an SFT mixture of one million rows.
Sources per skill (curated prompts, distilled responses, safety, multilingual), each with its licence; per-source cleaning (identity and cutoff strings, repetition, empty or truncated turns); exact then near dedup of prompts within and across sources; decontamination of prompts against every evaluation you will report (n-gram plus an embedding pass for paraphrases), with per-row removal, not only a dataset threshold; length limits matched to the training context; per-subset counts and a read sample at every step; and an ablation per subset, because quality is measured by the model trained on it.
How do you build a preference dataset, and what can go wrong?
Sample several responses per prompt (from a pool of models, or on-policy from the model being trained), score them with a judge or reward model, pair a better with a worse one. Failure modes: ties and tiny margins (noise), judge biases (length, style, position) that the pairs pass on, off-policy pairs that the model would never produce, and contaminated prompts.
What makes a good verifiable-reward prompt?
A label in a canonical form, a checker tested on correct and incorrect answers in the formats the model will actually use, a difficulty where the model sometimes succeeds (always or never solved teaches nothing), and no overlap with the evaluations.

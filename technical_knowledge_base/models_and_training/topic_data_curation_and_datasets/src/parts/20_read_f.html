7. Mixing and staging: how much of each kind, and when

A pretraining corpus is a mixture of domains: web text, code, math, scientific papers, books, encyclopedias, multilingual text, synthetic data. The mixture weights say what fraction of the training tokens each domain supplies. At a fixed budget every point of code is a point not spent on web text, so the weights move abilities directly: Llama 3's final mix was roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual tokens ({{Llama 3, Section 3.1.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}). Taking each domain in proportion to its size (its "natural" share) is rarely right: small, valuable domains get drowned. Multilingual models usually sample domain i with probability proportional to ni1/T (a temperature T above 1 flattens the mix toward small languages).

How many times can a small domain repeat? Upsampling means repeating. In the T5 experiments, repeating a truncated C4 64 times cost almost nothing, 256 times a little, and 1,024 times about 4 GLUE points and 4.6 SQuAD points, with training loss falling as the data shrank, the signature of memorisation ({{T5, Table 9|https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683}}). At larger scale the usable number is much smaller: Muennighoff et al. found up to about 4 epochs nearly as good as fresh data and meaningful gains up to about 16, after which extra repeats are worth almost nothing ({{arXiv 2305.16264|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264}}).

Choosing weights without paying for the big run. All the methods train small proxy models and transfer what they learn. DoReMi trains a 280M proxy with Group DRO (it upweights the domains where the proxy lags a reference model most) and reads the domain weights off it; an 8B model trained with them gained 6.5 points of average few-shot accuracy over The Pile's default weights and reached the baseline 2.6 times faster ({{DoReMi|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10429}}, self-reported). Data Mixing Laws fit loss as a function of the mixture proportions and extrapolate ({{arXiv 2403.16952|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16952}}). RegMix trains 512 tiny models (1M parameters, 1 billion tokens each) on random mixtures, fits a regression from mixture to loss, and picks the best predicted mixture; it matched or beat DoReMi with 10% of the compute and found web text, not Wikipedia, the domain most correlated with downstream scores ({{RegMix|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01492}}, self-reported). The sobering check: Aioli found that no existing method consistently beat simple stratified sampling (equal weight per domain) on average test perplexity ({{Aioli|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05735}}, third-party for the methods it tests). Optimal weights also shift with model size and token budget, so copying a published mix is a starting point, not an answer.

Staging: the mix changes during training. Modern recipes use a long first stage on broad data and a short final stage, called mid-training or annealing, on the best data (math, code, instruction-like and question-answer text, often synthetic), run while the learning rate decays to zero. Sometimes a long-context stage follows. The usual explanation is that scarce high-quality tokens are not diluted across trillions of others, and that what the model sees at a low learning rate is what it keeps; it is an explanation, not a proven mechanism. The effect is large and well documented:

The same OLMo 2 7B model before and after 50 billion tokens of mid-training

{{OLMo 2, Table 9|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656}}, self-reported. 50 billion tokens is about 1.2% of the run (derived: 50B / (4T + 50B)). The Dolmino mix was about half high-quality web (DCLM's fastText top 7% that FineWeb-Edu also scores 2 or more) plus instruction data, papers, encyclopedic text and math, partly synthetic (Table 13 of the same paper). GSM8K, a grade-school math test, goes from 24.1 to 67.5. Llama 3 reports the same shape and a limit: annealing an 8B model on GSM8K and MATH training data raised their validation scores by 24.0% and 6.4%, with negligible gains at 405B ({{Llama 3, Section 3.1.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}). Annealing is also the cheapest way to evaluate a dataset: take a half-trained model, decay its learning rate over a short run where the candidate dataset is a fixed share (Llama 3 used 40 billion tokens with 30% candidate data), and compare against the same run without it. Nemotron-CC ranked its quality buckets this way.

Continued pretraining: how much general data to keep. Continued pretraining (CPT) trains an existing model further on a domain (finance, law, a new language). Without some general data mixed back in (replay), it forgets. The CMR scaling law makes the ratio predictable: during CPT, general and domain loss each follow a power law in the domain share and the tokens trained, so a few short probe runs fit the curves, and the critical mixture ratio is the largest domain-data share that does not raise general loss beyond a tolerance. Predicted values grew with model size, 29.8%, 34.9%, 41.4% and 47.8% domain data for 460M, 940M, 1.6B and 3.1B models on finance, and were higher for a domain closer to the general data (36.7% for academic papers against 29.8% for finance at 460M; {{Gu et al., arXiv 2407.17467|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.17467}}). It is the method Khalid has used at work to set this ratio. The mechanics of CPT itself are on {{Continued Pretraining (CPT)|n:3c65c17b0d0d8119b8f0e801caa09474}}.

Predict: you have 20 billion tokens of high-quality math and a 2-trillion-token run. Which uses it best?
The published recipes concentrate it at the end (OLMo 2, Llama 3, SmolLM2), where 20B tokens can be a quarter or more of the final stage instead of 1% of every batch, and the annealing evidence above shows the gains. Spreading it is not wrong (it still helps), and repeating it up to about 4 times across stages is cheap. What to avoid is placing it early only, where later training dilutes it.

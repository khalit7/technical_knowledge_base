4. Deduplication: copies among billions of documents

The web repeats itself: syndicated news, mirrors, templates, boilerplate legal text, the same page crawled in many months. Duplicates waste compute, make a model memorise (reproduce training text verbatim) and leak test sets, since a benchmark question copied across many pages survives in some copy. The accuracy effect is smaller than people expect (section 1's table): deduplication is mostly about the token budget and memorisation. Four keys catch four kinds of copy:

  • Exact: a hash (xxhash, SHA-1) of each URL, document, paragraph or line; nearly free, but misses two copies of an article that differ in a date line.
  • Near-duplicate documents: MinHash estimates the Jaccard similarity of two documents' word n-gram sets from short signatures, and LSH bands the signatures so similar documents meet in a bucket without any pairwise comparison; the chance of becoming candidates is 1 − (1 − sr)b for b bands of r rows (FineWeb: 14 bands of 8 on 5-grams).
  • Repeated spans inside otherwise different pages (a disclaimer pasted into a million pages): a suffix array finds every repeated span in one pass, at a memory cost several times the text; a Bloom filter does exact membership in fixed memory and may say "seen" wrongly, never the reverse.
  • Paraphrases: semantic dedup embeds documents and drops near neighbours in a cluster (SemDeDup, D4); as a web filter in DCLM's comparison it scored 27.1 Core against 27.5 for no selection, so it is used more for synthetic and instruction data.

The cross-page point is scope. How much duplication you find depends on how far you look: MinHash inside the running example's one WARC file removes 75 of 2,692 documents (2.8%), yet 914 of the same 2,692 (34.0%) have a copy at Jaccard 0.8 or more under the same URL in at least one of four older crawls (measured, {{Dedup lab|#t-dedup}}). Wider is not always better: FineWeb deduplicated all crawls against each other, and the 2013-48 crawl's survivors scored worse than what was removed, because pages that never reappear later are disproportionately ads and keyword lists; it deduplicated each crawl on its own instead (the bars and the arithmetic are {{Deduplication|n:3f95c17b0d0d8171ac17e43ab5ea276f}}, section 1). Nemotron-CC deduplicated globally with a different pipeline and did well, so the finding is about an interaction, not a law. The framing several corpora adopted: dedup tells you the counts, mixing decides the setting. TxT360 deduplicates globally but keeps each document's duplicate count, so documents the web repeated can be upsampled on purpose (section 7).

5. Quality models: which documents are worth training on

After extraction, rules and dedup, most of what remains is grammatical, unique and still not worth much: product listings, forum chatter, thin SEO pages. Every modern corpus therefore runs a quality model, a score per document and a threshold that keeps the top slice. "Quality" means whatever the model was trained to prefer, so the useful question is always quality according to what. Three recipes are in use:

Predict: run both published classifiers at their published cut-offs on the same documents. Of all the documents that at least one of them keeps, what share do both keep?
About one in seven. On the running example's 2,617 final documents the FineWeb-Edu classifier keeps 159 and DCLM's keeps 573; 92 are kept by both, out of 640 kept by either: 14.4%, with a rank correlation of 0.44 between the two scores. On 50,000 random FineWeb documents of the same crawl the overlap is 16.0%, with rank correlation 0.41 (measured, {{Quality lab|#t-qual}}). Nemotron-CC found the same on an older crawl: the intersection is 10.1% of the union (its Table 8). Two filters this different are why an ensemble keeps more good text than either alone.

The central trade-off: per-token quality against unique tokens. FineWeb-Edu and DCLM remove about 90% of their input. That is fine for a run of a few hundred billion tokens and a problem for 15 trillion, where a small corpus must be repeated many times; and the unique count is smaller than the headline: after a global fuzzy dedup across crawls, FineWeb-Edu's 1.3 trillion tokens shrink to 0.2 trillion and DCLM-baseline's 3.8 trillion to 1.0 trillion (Nemotron-CC, Table 4). Nemotron-CC's answer: score with an ensemble of three classifiers, sort documents into quality levels ranked by short annealing runs (section 7), keep the high levels, and have an LLM rephrase the low ones instead of dropping them (section 8): in NVIDIA's 8B, 1-trillion-token runs its high-quality slice beat DCLM by 5.6 MMLU points, and the full 6.3 trillion tokens matched DCLM with four times as many unique real tokens (self-reported). Check any filter by reading a stratified sample of what it drops (the {{Quality lab|#t-qual}} labels one). This trade-off runs through sections 2, 5, 7, 8 and the calculator in section 11.

6. Decontamination: keeping the test out of the training set

Benchmarks are on the web: questions are copied into forums, study sites and repositories, and end up in the crawl. A model that trained on a test item can score by remembering it. Decontamination removes training documents or spans that overlap the test sets you will report; detecting contamination in a model you did not train belongs to {{Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}} and {{Topic: benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}}. Almost everyone uses n-gram overlap: GPT-3 used 13-grams; Llama 3 and Tulu 3 use 8-grams, and Tulu 3's rule is precise enough to reuse: a test item is matched by a training example if more than half of its tokens fall inside an 8-gram shared with it, and a whole training dataset was dropped or cleaned if it matched more than 2% of any evaluation's items ({{Tulu 3, Section 3.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}). A Bloom filter seeded with every test n-gram (the Dolma toolkit's approach) does the lookup in one streaming pass. Paraphrases defeat n-grams, so data written by a teacher model that memorised a benchmark needs embedding similarity or an LLM check on top.

The cross-page finding: web text is rarely the leak, post-training data is. Across about 360,000 web documents (the running example and samples of FineWeb, FineWeb-Edu, DCLM and Nemotron-CC), 43 share a 13-gram with one of the 29,615 benchmark items, and reading every pair finds no test question. Tulu 3's RLVR prompts contain 70 MMLU test questions word for word, which is 0.50% of MMLU and so passes Tulu 3's own 2% rule, though it is 26% of that MMLU subject; and an n-gram rule short enough to catch everything flags ordinary text, since any shared 5-gram touches 64% to 84% of web documents (measured by {{Decontamination, PII and data governance|n:3f95c17b0d0d8100b7e4e0f96074c309}} and the {{Post-training data lab|#t-post}}).

3. The pipeline: from a raw page to a training document

A raw crawl is mostly not text you want: navigation menus, cookie banners, spam, machine-generated pages, adult content, pages in other languages, and the same page many times over. The pipeline removes it in stages ordered by cost, so that the expensive stages only see what the cheap ones let through. Five of the stages are simple enough to define in one breath each; deduplication, quality models and decontamination get their own sections.

  • URL filtering. Drop pages whose domain is on a blocklist (adult and spam sites) before reading them. Costs a lookup per URL.
  • Text extraction. Turn the HTML into the page's main content: keep the article, drop the menus, footers and ads. Common Crawl's own WET text keeps everything; dedicated extractors (trafilatura, resiliparse, jusText) guess which blocks are the content. This is the most expensive cheap stage, because it parses every page.
  • Language identification. A small classifier (usually fastText's lid.176, a bag-of-character-n-grams model) gives a probability per language; FineWeb keeps pages whose English probability is at least 0.65.
  • Heuristic filters. Hand-written rules on simple statistics: too few or too many words, mean word length outside 3 to 10 characters, too many symbols, too few common words such as "the" and "and" (Gopher's rules); lines must end in punctuation (C4); too many short lines or duplicated lines (FineWeb's own three rules, found by comparing statistics of good and bad data).
  • PII scrubbing. Replace personal data with placeholders. FineWeb anonymises email addresses and public IP addresses with regular expressions and stops there; names, phone numbers and addresses need heavier tools (NER models such as Presidio) and are rarely removed from web corpora.

One real page through the pipeline: the same page as three extractors give it

An advocacy campaign page from the running example (Common Crawl CC-MAIN-2024-30, WARC file 00000; an organisation's page, chosen by the {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}}). measured: the extractors' output (trafilatura 1.11.0 with FineWeb's options, resiliparse with Dolma's settings), the lid.176 scores and datatrove's verdicts come from the Pipeline lab; the two quality scores from the {{Quality lab|#t-qual}}'s scorers; FineWeb and FineWeb-Edu membership from their rows for this file. Computed live in your browser: the rule values (Gopher thresholds as in datatrove v0.10.1; words split roughly as datatrove splits them, so a value can differ from datatrove's in the third decimal) and the 5-gram Jaccard and MinHash (112 hashes in 14 bands of 8) between the three extractions.

What the evidence says about each stage. Extraction is the stage with the largest single published effect. On DCLM's 1.4B-parameter benchmark, models trained on WET text scored 20.7 Core against 24.1 for resiliparse and 24.5 for trafilatura; WET documents are about twice as long on average (6,580 characters against 3,227 and 2,901), the difference being menus and boilerplate ({{DCLM, Tables 3 and 16|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11794}}, self-reported ablation). FineWeb found the same with its own models, and the running example shows why in counts: across four WARC files (81,738 HTML pages), the same filter chain kept 10,955 pages extracted by trafilatura, 8,676 by resiliparse and 3,526 from WET text; 9,312 of the 9,339 pages FineWeb kept from those files are among trafilatura's 10,955 (measured, {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}}; before any dedup). The cost differs a lot: resiliparse processed 4.55 MB of WARC per second per CPU core against trafilatura's 0.56, about 8 times faster for nearly the same score (same table). Heuristic filters are worth less but are cheap: C4's terminal-punctuation rule gave the largest HellaSwag boost of C4's rules in FineWeb's tests but removed about 30% of tokens; FineWeb's own three rules removed about 22% and added about 1% to the aggregate score ({{FineWeb paper, Sections 3.5 and 3.6|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557}}, self-reported).

The funnel: one WARC file measured, and FineWeb's published totals

Running example (measured, datatrove on CPU, {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}}): 20,463 HTML pages become 2,617 documents, 12.8%. Language ID removes the most (most pages are not English), then the Gopher rules. In GPT-2 tokens, the C4 and FineWeb rules keep 79.5% of the base-filtered text (FineWeb: 75%) and MinHash inside one file keeps 98.6% of the rest; FineWeb's MinHash over a whole crawl kept 55.6%, because most duplicates are in other files of the crawl. FineWeb (FineWeb paper, Sections 3.3 to 4, self-reported): base filtering (URL blocklist, English at 0.65 or more, Gopher rules), then MinHash inside each crawl, then the C4 and FineWeb rules; FineWeb-Edu is the educational-classifier slice (section 5). The two orders differ: this run applies the C4 and FineWeb rules before MinHash.

A detail that matters when you reproduce FineWeb
The FineWeb paper drops documents whose fraction of characters in duplicated lines is at least 0.1, but the FineWebQualityFilter in datatrove v0.10.1 defaults to 0.01, ten times stricter, and adds a fourth check (newline-to-word ratio above 0.3) the paper does not describe; the library's FineWeb example script uses those defaults. Checked in the source on 2026-10-14 (src/datatrove/pipeline/filters/fineweb_quality_filter.py, lines 14 to 20). A paper and its released code can disagree; when they do, your reproduction follows the code unless you change it. On the running example, the paper's 0.1 would keep 154 more documents than the default (2,846 against 2,692), and none of the 154 is in FineWeb, which holds 89% of the default's output: FineWeb's released data matches the code's 0.01, not the paper's 0.1 (measured, {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}}; 6 of 8 hand-labelled drops at 0.01 were wrong drops). The same check finds that datatrove's C4QualityFilter swaps both of C4's numbers: it defaults to at least 5 sentences per document and 3 words per line, where the T5 paper says 3 sentences and 5 words (c4_filters.py, lines 66 and 67). Two more defaults change results silently: datatrove's Trafilatura step runs trafilatura with deduplicate=True, a process-wide cache that drops any paragraph of more than 100 characters from its fourth sighting in the same worker, so the output depends on which pages a worker saw first (on the running example it emptied 310 pages, and shuffling the page order changed 38 outputs); and the shipped FineWeb example calls LanguageFilter() without a language, which keeps any language scoring above 0.65, so it is not English-only (122 non-English documents survive on our file). Both measured by {{Extraction and filtering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81579b32c07c92b7472b}} and {{Pretraining corpora|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f382b6e75707d00663}}.

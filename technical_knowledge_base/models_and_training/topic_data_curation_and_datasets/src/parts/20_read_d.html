5. Quality models: deciding which documents are worth training on

The problem. After extraction, rules and dedup, most of what remains is grammatical, deduplicated and still not worth much: product listings, forum chatter, thin SEO pages. Hand-written rules cannot say "this teaches something". Every modern corpus therefore runs a quality model: a model that gives each document a score, and a threshold that keeps the top slice. "Quality" has no definition beyond what the model was trained to prefer, so the useful question is always quality according to what. Three recipes are in use.

1. Reference against crawl

Train a binary classifier to tell a reference set you like from random crawl text, and keep the documents that look most like the reference. DCLM's reference is instruction data (OpenHermes 2.5) plus highly voted r/ExplainLikeImFive answers, its negatives random pages; it keeps the top 10%. The classifier is fastText: each word and word pair gets a learned vector, a document is the average of its vectors, and a linear layer scores it. It trains in minutes and scores on CPUs, which matters when the input is trillions of tokens.

2. An LLM grades a sample, a small model learns the grades

FineWeb-Edu: Llama-3-70B-Instruct scored 460,000 pages from 0 to 5 for educational value (with a rubric aimed at grade-school to middle-school knowledge); a linear regression head on a frozen small encoder (Snowflake-arctic-embed-m) learned to predict the score ("F1 82%" at threshold 3, which the Quality lab finds consistent with a macro-averaged F1: on 1,500 annotation rows it measured 0.85 macro and 0.72 for the positive class alone), then scored all 15 trillion tokens in 6,000 H100 GPU hours ({{FineWeb paper, Section 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557}}). Threshold 3 keeps 1.3 trillion tokens; threshold 2 keeps 5.4 trillion.

3. Perplexity under a reference model

CCNet's approach: train a small n-gram language model on Wikipedia and keep the documents it finds least surprising (low perplexity). Cheap and still used as a feature, but it prefers text that looks like Wikipedia. In DCLM's comparison it scored 29.0 Core against fastText's 30.2 (section 1's chart).

Two quality models, two different corpora: FineWeb-Edu against DCLM at equal tokens

FineWeb-EduDCLM-baseline

1.7B models trained on 350 billion tokens of each, from {{SmolLM2, Table 1 (arXiv 2502.02737)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02737}}, Hugging Face, the makers of FineWeb-Edu: self-reported for one side. FineWeb-Edu wins the knowledge benchmarks (MMLU, ARC, OpenBookQA); DCLM wins the everyday-language ones (HellaSwag, CommonsenseQA, PIQA). SmolLM2 settled on 60% FineWeb-Edu and 40% DCLM.

The chart is the most useful single fact about quality models: each one encodes a target, and the benchmarks it improves are the ones that resemble its reference. FineWeb-Edu's own paper shows the cost on the other side: its models have higher perplexity than FineWeb's on Twitter, Reddit and African-American English text, and lower on Wikipedia and academic text ({{FineWeb paper, Section 4.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557}}). A filter is also a choice of whose writing the model learns from.

Keep the top or keep more and fix it. FineWeb-Edu and DCLM remove about 90% of their input. That is fine for a run of a few hundred billion tokens and a problem for 15 trillion, where a small corpus must be repeated many times. And the unique count is smaller than the headline: after a global fuzzy dedup across crawls, FineWeb-Edu's 1.3 trillion tokens shrink to 0.2 trillion and DCLM-baseline's 3.8 trillion to 1.0 trillion (Nemotron-CC, Table 4). Nemotron-CC's answer: score with an ensemble of three classifiers, sort documents into 20 score buckets, merge them into 5 quality levels (ranked by short annealing runs, section 7), keep the high levels as they are, and have an LLM rephrase the low ones instead of dropping them ({{Nemotron-CC, arXiv 2412.02595|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02595}}). In NVIDIA's 8B-parameter, 1-trillion-token runs, its high-quality slice beat DCLM by 5.6 MMLU points, and the full 6.3 trillion tokens matched DCLM with four times as many unique real tokens; an 8B model trained for 15 trillion tokens on a weighted version reached 70.3 MMLU against 65.3 for Llama 3.1 8B under the same evaluation harness (self-reported, NVIDIA's own runs). The trade-off is the topic's central design decision: per-token quality against how many tokens you have before you start repeating.

Predict: run both published classifiers at their published cut-offs on the same documents. Of all the documents that at least one of them keeps, what share do both keep?
About one in seven. On the running example's 2,617 final documents the FineWeb-Edu classifier keeps 159 and DCLM's keeps 573; 92 are kept by both, out of 640 kept by either: 14.4%, with a rank correlation of 0.44 between the two scores. On 2,000 random FineWeb documents of the same crawl the overlap is 13.1% (measured, {{Quality lab|#t-qual}}). Nemotron-CC found the same on an older crawl: the intersection is 10.1% of the union (its Table 8). Its text also says each classifier has "a limited recall (around 10%)", but its Table 9 column is the share of tokens each labels high quality (FineWeb-Edu 8%, DCLM 11%, the three-classifier ensemble 25%), not a recall. Two filters this different are why an ensemble keeps more good text than either alone.

How to check a quality filter. Never trust the score alone. Draw a stratified sample (some documents from every score band), read them, label them yourself, and look hardest at what the filter dropped: whole topics, formats (tables, code, lists), dialects and languages vanish quietly. On the running example, a hand-labelled stratified sample (100 documents, 20 per stratum, labelled by the agent that built the Quality lab, so one reader) estimates that about 40% of what the FineWeb-Edu classifier drops, 36% of what DCLM's drops and 34% of what both drop is text worth training on; 10 of the 21 worth-keeping documents both dropped are news. In the other direction, 5 of 20 documents only DCLM keeps were not worth it (a cookie table, a navigation stub, templated SEO pages), and none of the 40 FineWeb-Edu keeps sampled measured.

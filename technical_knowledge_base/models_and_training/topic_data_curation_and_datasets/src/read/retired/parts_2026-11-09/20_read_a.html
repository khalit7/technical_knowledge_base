1. Why the data decides so much, and which stage buys what

The question: at a fixed training budget, how much does it matter which tokens you use? Across seven years of open recipes, Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han crossed each year's representative open training recipe (GPT-2 in 2019 to OLMo 2 in 2025) with each year's public corpus (OpenWebText in 2019 to Ultra-FineWeb in 2025), trained every pair at budgets up to 1019 FLOPs and read off the compute each improvement saves. Better data saved 12.0x compute, better models 3.7x: data contributed 3.24 times more, and a simple additive model of "data effect plus model effect" explains 88% of the score differences, so the two barely interact third-party ({{Patel and Han, 2026-09-08|https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/pretraining-progress-is-mostly-data}}). The caveats are the authors' own: small models, little tuning, one benchmark suite (OLMES), one seed per cell; their remark that small models gain most from data quality is an argument, not a measurement.

The cleanest evidence is a data ablation: identical small proxy models trained on data that differs in one decision. Five years before the modern corpora, the T5 paper showed the direction: an unfiltered crawl 8 times larger than C4 (6.1 TB against 745 GB) scored lower on 6 of 7 task groups at the same budget ({{T5, Table 8|https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.10683}}; its text says "every task", but on SuperGLUE its Wikipedia-only model is 0.03 lower than the unfiltered one). Data decisions are empirical: even "obviously good" steps have to be ablated. The sharpest example is FineWeb's attempt to deduplicate all 96 crawls against each other, whose model barely beat one trained on non-deduplicated data (section 4). How to read an ablation critically: check the model size, tokens, benchmarks and seeds before believing a gap; small proxies can only use benchmarks that already move at small scale, and a dataset's makers choose the benchmarks it is evaluated on (the mistakes block in section 11).

2. Where the tokens come from

Almost every open pretraining corpus starts from Common Crawl (CC), a non-profit that has crawled the web since 2008 and publishes a new crawl every month or two. One crawl comes in three file types: WARC files hold the raw HTTP responses (the HTML exactly as served), WAT files the metadata (headers, links), WET files plain text that Common Crawl extracted itself, menus and footers included. The June 2025 crawl, for scale: 2.38 billion pages, 82.73 TiB of compressed WARC, 15.62 TiB of WAT and 6.24 TiB of WET ({{Common Crawl, 2025-06-27|https://commoncrawl.org/blog/june-2025-crawl-archive-now-available}}); after FineWeb's whole pipeline it yields 152.4 billion tokens ({{FineWeb dataset card|https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceFW/fineweb}}).

The rest comes from sources with more structure and more legal care: code (The Stack v2; Stack-Edu, its educational slice), papers and PDFs (arXiv, peS2o, PDFs turned into text by OCR models; FinePDFs holds about 3 trillion tokens from 475 million PDFs), encyclopedias and books (the Books3 collection inside The Pile was taken down over copyright). A licence on a dataset (ODC-By for FineWeb and Dolma, CC-BY-4.0 for DCLM-baseline, an NVIDIA data agreement for Nemotron-CC v2) covers the compilation; it does not license each web page inside it. The Common Pile v0.1 is the attempt at 8 TB of text whose every piece is openly licensed or public domain ({{arXiv 2506.05209|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05209}}).

3. The pipeline: from a raw page to a training document

A raw crawl is mostly not text you want: menus, cookie banners, spam, machine-generated pages, adult content, other languages, and the same page many times over. Five of the stages are simple enough to define in a line each; deduplication, quality models and decontamination get their own sections.

  • URL filtering. Drop pages whose domain or URL words are on a blocklist (adult and spam sites) before reading them: a lookup per URL.
  • Text extraction. Keep the article, drop the menus, footers and ads. WET keeps everything; extractors (trafilatura, resiliparse, jusText) guess which blocks are content. The most expensive cheap stage, because it parses every page.
  • Language identification. A small classifier (usually fastText's lid.176, a bag of character n-grams) gives a probability per language; FineWeb keeps pages whose English probability is at least 0.65.
  • Heuristic filters. Hand-written rules on simple statistics: too few or too many words, mean word length outside 3 to 10 characters, too few stop words (Gopher); lines must end in punctuation (C4); too many short or duplicated lines (FineWeb's three rules).
  • PII scrubbing. Replace personal data with placeholders. FineWeb masks email and public IP addresses with regular expressions and stops there; names, phone numbers and addresses need NER models such as Presidio and are rarely removed from web corpora.

The stage with the largest published effect is extraction (section 1's table), and the running example shows why in counts: across four WARC files (81,738 HTML pages), the same filter chain kept 10,955 pages extracted by trafilatura, 8,676 by resiliparse and 3,526 from WET text, and 9,312 of the 9,339 pages FineWeb kept from those files are among trafilatura's 10,955 (measured, {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}}, before any dedup). A pipeline's library defaults also change the dataset: FineWeb's released data matches datatrove's duplicated-line default of 0.01, not the paper's 0.1, and the shipped example's language filter is not English-only.

The library defaults that silently change a FineWeb reproduction (measured detail)
The FineWeb paper drops documents whose fraction of characters in duplicated lines is at least 0.1, but the FineWebQualityFilter in datatrove v0.10.1 defaults to 0.01, ten times stricter, and adds a fourth check (newline-to-word ratio above 0.3) the paper does not describe (src/datatrove/pipeline/filters/fineweb_quality_filter.py, lines 14 to 20, read 2026-10-14). On the running example, the paper's 0.1 would keep 154 more documents than the default (2,846 against 2,692), and none of the 154 is in FineWeb, which holds 89% of the default's output: FineWeb's released data matches the code's 0.01, not the paper's 0.1 (measured, {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}}; 6 of 8 hand-labelled drops at 0.01 were wrong drops). datatrove's C4QualityFilter swaps both of C4's numbers: at least 5 sentences per document and 3 words per line, where the T5 paper says 3 sentences and 5 words (c4_filters.py, lines 66 and 67). Its Trafilatura step runs with deduplicate=True, a process-wide cache that drops any paragraph of more than 100 characters from its fourth sighting in the same worker (on the running example it emptied 310 pages, and shuffling the page order changed 38 outputs); and the shipped FineWeb example calls LanguageFilter() without a language, so it keeps any language above 0.65 (122 non-English documents survive on our file).

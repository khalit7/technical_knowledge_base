0. In one screen

A filtered, deduplicated corpus still owes three things before a model trains on it. It must not contain the tests the model will be scored on, it should not hand the model people's personal details, and its owner must be able to say where every document came from and on what terms. These are three different jobs with three different tools, and each has a characteristic way of failing quietly.

1. Contamination from zero

The problem. A benchmark is a fixed set of test items (questions with known answers) used to compare models. Its test split is meant to be unseen: the score estimates how the model does on new questions of that kind. Benchmarks are published on the web, quoted in papers, copied into study sites, forums and GitHub repositories, and from there they reach Common Crawl. A model trained on a test item can answer it from memory. Contamination is the presence of evaluation data (or something close enough to help) in the training data; decontamination is finding and removing it before training. The two sides of the problem are different jobs: the data engineer controls the training set and can search it directly (this page); someone evaluating a model they did not train can only probe the model (the {{benchmarks topic|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}} owns that side, and section 5 here covers what the training side can borrow from it).

Why it inflates scores, and by how much. The evidence comes in three strengths.

  • Deliberate, the upper bound. Yang et al. fine-tuned Llama 2 13B on rephrased copies of the MMLU test set (16 epochs) and it scored 85.9 on MMLU against 54.8 before; on rephrased GSM8K it went from 28.7 to 95.3 (arXiv 2311.04850, Tables 2 and 4, 2023). A 13-gram check found none of the rephrased items (Table 5: F1 0).
  • Accidental, measured after the fact. GPT-3's own analysis flagged 29% of PIQA and 45% of Winograd as overlapping its training data and found the clean subsets 3 and 2.6 points lower, so those two results carry an asterisk (GPT-3, Section 4). Llama 2 concluded that only HellaSwag and MMLU-Humanities had been boosted: the 70B model scored 92.2 on HellaSwag's "dirty" items against 80.0 on its clean ones (Llama 2, Appendix A.6, Table 51). Llama 3 estimated gains of 14 to 15 points on HellaSwag and 8 points on PiQA from contamination at all three sizes (Llama 3, Table 15).
  • Often none. GPT-3 also found that "in most cases performance changes only negligibly" even where overlap was high, and PaLM saw roughly as many positive as negative clean-minus-full deltas (PaLM, Section 8, Table 18). DCLM removed the MMLU and HellaSwag overlaps it detected and its 7B model's scores went up slightly (MMLU 51.8 to 52.7; DCLM, Table 7). Magar and Schwartz showed in a controlled BERT study that "memorization does not guarantee exploitation" (arXiv 2203.08242).

The honest summary: contamination can inflate a score a lot, usually inflates it a little, and the size depends on how many copies the model saw, how big the model is (Singh et al. found larger models exploit it more; arXiv 2411.03923) and whether the copy includes the answer. That is why labs decontaminate anyway: you cannot know in advance which case you are in.

Not all overlap is the same thing. What a detector reports as "contaminated" can be any of six situations, and they need different responses. The categories below are the ones used to label every flagged pair on this page.

7. Personal data in training text

What counts. Two vocabularies are in use. The US term PII (personally identifiable information) is "any data that can be used to distinguish or trace an individual's identity, either alone or in combination with other information" (NIST SP 800-122). The EU's personal data is wider: "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person", including online identifiers and anything specific to a person's physical, economic or social identity (GDPR Art. 4(1)), with health, religion, politics, sexuality, biometrics and similar "special categories" prohibited by default (Art. 9(1)). The useful engineering split is between direct identifiers (a name, an email, a phone number, an ID number) and quasi-identifiers, details that identify only in combination: Sweeney estimated that 87% of the US population was likely unique on just ZIP code, sex and date of birth (Sweeney 2000). Detectors handle direct identifiers; nothing automated handles quasi-identifiers well.

Why a language model makes it worse. Text in the training set can come back out. Carlini et al. extracted 604 memorised training examples from GPT-2, 16 of them with private individuals' contact details (arXiv 2012.07805, Sections 6.2 and 6.3); Nasr et al. extracted over 10,000 memorised examples from ChatGPT for US$200 of queries, and 16.9% of the generations they checked contained memorised PII (arXiv 2311.17035, Sections 5.3 and 5.4). Lukas et al. found that "scrubbing techniques reduce but do not prevent the risk" and that duplication of a PII string "strongly predicts leakage" (arXiv 2302.00539): deduplication is also a privacy measure.

How the detectors work. A regular expression finds strings with a fixed shape: an email has an @ and a dotted domain, a card number has 13 to 19 digits that pass the Luhn checksum, an IPv4 address has four numbers up to 255. Regexes are fast and precise on structured identifiers and blind to everything else. Named-entity recognition (NER) is a model that labels spans as PERSON, LOCATION, ORGANIZATION and so on from context; it is the only practical way to find names, and it is slow and wrong in its own ways. Microsoft's Presidio (now published from the data-privacy-stack organisation, presidio-analyzer 2.2.364, 2026-07-22) combines the two: regex recognisers with checksums and "context words" that raise a match's score, plus spaCy's en_core_web_lg NER for persons and places. Its own documentation warns "there is no guarantee that Presidio will find all sensitive information". NeMo Curator 1.4.0 has no PII stage in the library: it ships a tutorial (tutorials/text/gliner-pii-redaction) that runs NVIDIA's GLiNER-PII model, 55+ categories, on a GPU.

Recall on real text cannot be measured without annotating every document; the synthetic set is the substitute, and it flatters every detector because its PII is cleanly formatted.

Choosing. At pretraining scale the published practice is regexes for emails and IPs (FineWeb, Dolma, Apertus), because NER over trillions of tokens costs GPU-hours (StarCoder's model needed 800 A100-hours for 815 GB) and its false positives on names of places and public figures would mangle useful text. For post-training data, user-submitted text (chat logs) and code, where PII density is higher and the volume is smaller, a model-based detector earns its cost. Whatever you choose: dedupe first (repeated PII is what models memorise), mask rather than drop when the page is otherwise good, log what was masked, and give people a removal route.

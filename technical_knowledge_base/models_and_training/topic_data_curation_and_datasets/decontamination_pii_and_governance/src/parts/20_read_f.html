7. Personal data in training text

What counts. The US term PII (personally identifiable information) is "any data that can be used to distinguish or trace an individual's identity, either alone or in combination with other information" (NIST SP 800-122). The EU's personal data is wider: "any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person", including online identifiers (GDPR Art. 4(1)), with health, religion, politics, sexuality, biometrics and similar "special categories" prohibited by default (Art. 9(1)). The engineering split is direct identifiers (a name, an email, a phone number, an ID number) against quasi-identifiers, which identify only in combination: Sweeney estimated that 87% of the US population was likely unique on just ZIP code, sex and date of birth (Sweeney 2000). Detectors handle direct identifiers; nothing automated handles quasi-identifiers well.

Why a language model makes it worse. Text in the training set can come back out. Carlini et al. extracted 604 memorised training examples from GPT-2, 16 of them with private individuals' contact details (arXiv 2012.07805, Sections 6.2 and 6.3); Nasr et al. extracted over 10,000 memorised examples from ChatGPT for US$200 of queries, and 16.9% of the generations they checked contained memorised PII (arXiv 2311.17035, Sections 5.3 and 5.4). Lukas et al. found that "scrubbing techniques reduce but do not prevent the risk" and that duplication of a PII string "strongly predicts leakage" (arXiv 2302.00539): deduplication is also a privacy measure.

How the detectors work. A regular expression finds strings with a fixed shape: an email has an @ and a dotted domain, a card number has 13 to 19 digits that pass the Luhn checksum, an IPv4 address has four numbers up to 255. Regexes are fast and precise on structured identifiers and blind to everything else. NER is the only practical way to find names, and it is slow and wrong in its own ways. Microsoft's Presidio (now published from the data-privacy-stack organisation, presidio-analyzer 2.2.364, 2026-07-22) combines the two: regex recognisers with checksums and "context words" that raise a match's score, plus spaCy's en_core_web_lg NER for persons and places. Its own documentation warns "there is no guarantee that Presidio will find all sensitive information".

What the big corpora do: regexes for emails and IPs at pretraining scale (FineWeb replaces them with email@example.com or one of six non-responding IPs and runs no phone regex "due to the high false positive rate"; Dolma masks 5 or fewer spans and drops documents with 6 or more), and a trained NER model only for code (StarCoder: 800 A100-hours for 815 GB). Choosing. At pretraining scale the published practice is regexes for emails and IPs (FineWeb, Dolma, Apertus), because NER over trillions of tokens costs GPU-hours and its false positives on places and public figures mangle useful text. For post-training data, user-submitted text (chat logs) and code, where PII density is higher and the volume smaller, a model-based detector earns its cost. Whatever you choose: dedupe first (repeated PII is what models memorise), scrub the extracted text rather than raw HTML (extraction assembles addresses from separate elements, and Cloudflare-obfuscated emails survive as "[email protected]" placeholders), mask rather than drop when the page is otherwise good, log what was masked, and give people a removal route.

Toxic and unsafe content is a filter of the same shape (a word list, a classifier or an external signal, each removing some of the wrong text: C4's word list removed African-American English and text mentioning LGBTQ+ identities far more often than average, Dodge et al. 2021, arXiv 2104.08758). It runs earlier in the pipeline and is measured on run0 in section 11 of {{Extraction and filtering|n:3c65c17b0d0d81579b32c07c92b7472b}}.

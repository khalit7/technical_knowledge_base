Synthetic data: rephrasing, textbooks and model collapse

How language models are used to write pretraining data, what the evidence says it buys, how to measure a synthetic set, and when training on model output degrades the next model. Synthetic pretraining data is training text written by a language model instead of by people. A model (the generator) is given a prompt, often containing a real document or a topic (the seed), and its output is added to the corpus another model (the student) is pretrained on. This page teaches how such text is made, what the published evidence says it is worth, how to measure a synthetic set before training on it, and what happens when models are trained on model output generation after generation (model collapse). Reading time about 40 minutes, plus time with the animations (the answers folded under "Common mistakes" and "Interview questions" add about 5).

The parent page gives the two-paragraph version and a one-dimensional collapse simulation (Topic: data-curation-and-datasets, section 8); this page goes deeper and does not repeat that simulation. Post-training data (instructions, preference pairs, verifiable-reward prompts, environments), much of which is also model-written, belongs to the sibling Post-training data.

Numbers are labelled: measured means produced here in October 2026 on an Apple M1 Pro (CPU) from public samples, self-reported a dataset's or method's makers evaluating their own work, third-party someone without a stake in the result, derived computed from other numbers with the formula shown, and illustrative made up for teaching. Every measured number comes from a script in this page's src/scripts/, and the samples (24 sets of 1,000 documents) are listed in the Diversity meter.

Five things to hold on to
  • Rephrasing is the workhorse. Grounding the generator in a real document keeps the real world's variety of facts and topics; the model only fixes the form. Generation from seeds needs heavy prompt engineering to avoid writing the same text again and again (section 2).
  • Synthetic text speeds learning; it does not add knowledge. WRAP's authors: "synthetic data can not impart 'new knowledge'. It can only help pre-train faster" (section 3). Mixed with real text it helps; on its own it usually loses on knowledge benchmarks.
  • Mix, do not replace. Published mixes put synthetic text at roughly a third to a half of a stage (section 5); every result where real text was dropped entirely shows a cost somewhere.
  • Measure before training. Openings, distinct n-grams, compression ratio, near-duplicates, benchmark overlap and classifier scores take minutes on a laptop and catch most failures (section 6 and the Diversity meter).
  • Model collapse is about replacing real data. Training each generation only on the previous generation's output loses the rare things first; keeping the real data in the pool stops the slide (section 7 and the Collapse lab).

1. Why let a model write training data

The problem. A pretraining run wants more good tokens than the web holds. FineWeb-Edu's strict cut of the English web is 1.3 trillion tokens; frontier runs train on 15 trillion and more (numbers and sources on the parent page). Repeating data is nearly free for about 4 epochs and loses value fast after about 16 (Muennighoff et al., arXiv 2305.16264). Keeping lower-quality text is the other option. A generator offers a third: more tokens of the kind you lack (explanations, worked problems, questions and answers) and a better form for the tokens you have (the same facts written clearly, without menus and boilerplate).

What it costs. Generation is inference on a large model, so every synthetic token costs GPU time. WRAP generated 3 million tokens per A100 hour with Mistral-7B and estimated about 25,000 GPU hours for its 85 billion tokens (Section 7.1; 85B / 3M per hour is about 28,000, so their figure is rounded down) (arXiv 2401.16380). Cosmopedia's 25 billion tokens took "over 10k GPU hours" (Cosmopedia blog, 2024-03-20). The cost calculator in section 9 turns these into a budget.

Where it is used today. Microsoft's phi-4 (14B parameters, about 10 trillion training tokens) put 40% of its pretraining tokens on synthetic data and 15% on web rewrites (Table 5, arXiv 2412.08905). Nemotron-CC is 6.3 trillion tokens of which 1.9 trillion are rephrased (Table 4, arXiv 2412.02595). Qwen3's report says Qwen2.5 models synthesised "trillions of text tokens" of textbooks, question-answering, instructions and code (arXiv 2505.09388; no exact count given). Olmo 3's mid-training mix is largely synthetic math, code and question-answer text (parent page, section 8).

2. Generating from seeds: TinyStories, phi and Cosmopedia

The idea. Ask a strong model to write the kind of text you wish the web had more of (clear explanations, graded exercises, simple stories) and train a small model on it. The generator's knowledge does the work; the prompt only says what to write. The hard part, every team that did this reports, is diversity: a model asked a thousand times for "a story for children" writes nearly the same story a thousand times.

TinyStories: a vocabulary three-year-olds know

Eldan and Li (Microsoft Research, May 2023) asked GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 for short stories using only words "a typical 3-year-old child would understand" (arXiv 2305.07759, Section 2). Their diversity trick is the template for everything since: from a list of about 1,500 basic words, "3 words are chosen randomly (one verb, one noun, and one adjective)" and must appear in the story, and a random subset of story features (a dialogue, a plot twist, a bad ending, a moral) is required. Without it, they write, generation "even if the temperature of generation is set to a high value, will still produce a very repetitive dataset". Models under 10 million parameters, or with a single transformer block, trained on it write fluent, consistent stories, graded by GPT-4 for grammar, creativity and consistency. The dataset card lists 2,141,709 stories, licence CDLA-Sharing-1.0, plus a GPT-4-only version (dataset card).

A student trained on this learns that stories start one way, which is fine for TinyStories' purpose (a clean testbed for tiny models) and a warning for anyone using such text as general pretraining data. phi: "textbook quality" data for small models

Microsoft's phi-1 (June 2023) is the case that made the field take synthetic pretraining data seriously (Textbooks Are All You Need, arXiv 2306.11644). A 1.3-billion-parameter code model was trained on under 7 billion tokens: about 6 billion tokens of real code filtered for "educational value" (GPT-4 labelled about 100,000 files; a random forest on code-model embeddings labelled the rest), under 1 billion tokens of Python textbooks written by GPT-3.5, and about 180 million tokens of exercises with solutions (the paper also says "less than 180M" and "<200M" elsewhere). It reached 50.6% on HumanEval and 55.5% on MBPP after 4 days on 8 A100s. The filtering alone mattered: on unfiltered code a 350M model stalled at 12.19% HumanEval, on the filtered subset it reached 17.68% in fewer steps.

Because the exercises resemble HumanEval, the authors tested contamination themselves: removing between 42.5 thousand and 354 thousand of the 879.5 thousand exercises closest to HumanEval problems (by embedding and syntax-tree distance) and retraining left phi-1 at 45.1% to 50.6%, still above StarCoder-Prompted's 41.5% (Table 3). This is the right test: a synthetic set built to teach a skill will resemble the benchmark for that skill, so remove the nearest items and retrain.

phi-1.5 (September 2023) extended the recipe to general text: "roughly 20B tokens" of synthetic textbook data seeded by "20K topics", with web samples inside the prompts for variety; the model trained for 150 billion tokens, 80% synthetic (arXiv 2309.05463; Table 1 lists the data as 30B, the prose 7B plus 20B). A web-only variant trained on 95 billion tokens of filtered web text did much worse on common-sense benchmarks (ARC-Challenge 0.329 against 0.444 for phi-1.5, Table 2). phi-4 (December 2024) is the latest public recipe: 50 kinds of synthetic datasets totalling "about 400B unweighted tokens", made with multi-agent prompting, self-revision and instruction reversal (arXiv 2412.08905). Its warning is the one to remember: "Models trained only with synthetic data underperformed on the knowledge-heavy benchmarks and demonstrated increased hallucinations"; in its Table 3 the synthetic-only 13B ablation was 14.8 points worse on TriviaQA than phi-3-medium, and adding web rewrites halved that gap (to 7.7).

Cosmopedia: the open replication, and what a prompt looks like

Hugging Face's Cosmopedia (March 2024) set out to reproduce phi-1.5's data openly: over 30 million documents and 25 billion tokens written by Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, Apache-2.0 (blog, card). Curated seeds (course outlines from Stanford, Khan Academy, OpenStax, WikiHow) ran out quickly ("only 16,000 unique units from OpenStax and 250,000 from Stanford"), so over 80% of prompts are seeded with web pages: millions of web samples were clustered into 145 clusters, the educational ones kept (the blog says 112 topics, the card says 35 of 145 were discarded, which is 110), and each prompt quotes one page. Every prompt is crossed with an audience (young children, high school, college, researchers) and a style (textbook, blog post, WikiHow), "up to 12 times the number of prompts". Two lessons sit in these rows. First, the generator invents: the shipping unit adds a figure for shipping's share of CO2 emissions that the seed never gave, and the product listing becomes a unit on "materials used (cotton candy, glass, etc.)" because the brand name "Cotton Candy" was read as a material. Seeded generation imports the generator's knowledge and its mistakes; nobody checks a 30-million-document set by hand. Second, a junk seed still yields a fluent textbook: the quality filter has to act on the seed (Cosmopedia kept only educational clusters) or on the output, because fluency says nothing about value.

The diversity fix, measured. The Cosmopedia team found that changing only the audience line was "insufficient to prevent a high rate of duplicate content", and that stories kept opening with "Once upon a time" and "The sun hung low in the sky" until the prompts said not to. In the 1,000-story sample here, measured 5 of the 406 stories whose prompt says "Do not start with classic sentences like 'Once upon a time'" start that way (1.2%), against 213 of the 594 whose prompt does not (35.9%). One sentence of prompt moved the opening rate by a factor of thirty. The same sample shows what is left: 57% of the WikiHow-style documents begin "Title: How to", and 22% of the Khan Academy units begin "Welcome back to".

Share of documents that start with their set's most common three-word opening measured

1,000 documents per set (seeded samples, see the Diversity meter); openings are the first three words, lowercased, punctuation dropped. Real web text has no dominant opening; generated sets have one or several.

MinHash deduplication over Cosmopedia found "less than 1%" near-duplicates (card), and the Diversity meter agrees at sample scale (at most 0.6% of any sample here is in a near-duplicate pair). That is the trap: no two documents are copies, yet the set is narrow. Near-duplicate detection measures copies, not variety; openings, distinct n-grams and embedding spread measure variety (section 6).

Cosmopedia v2 (July 2024, used for SmolLM) replaced web clustering with a fixed list of 34,000 topics from the BISAC book classification; for each topic a search over FineWeb retrieved 1,000 seed pages, giving 39 million documents and 28 billion tokens. Its ablations on 1.8B models found curated-seed textbooks best on MMLU and ARC and a middle-school audience best on everything except MMLU (SmolLM blog). By SmolLM2 (2025) Cosmopedia v2 appears only in the last stage, at 4% of the mix (Section 4.5, arXiv 2502.02737, which gives it as 30B tokens).

Persona Hub (Tencent, 2024) attacks diversity from the other side: 1,015,863,523 "personas" inferred from web pages ("who would write or read this?") are put into prompts so the same request is written for a billion different people; 1.07 million persona-conditioned math problems took Qwen2-7B to 64.9% on MATH (arXiv 2406.20094, released subset CC-BY-NC-SA 4.0). It is mostly used for post-training data.

3. Rephrasing real documents

The idea. Instead of asking the generator to know things, give it a real web page and ask it to rewrite the page in a better form. The facts, topics and variety come from the web; the generator removes menus, fixes grammar and changes the register. This is called rephrasing (or rewriting); when the output has a new structure (questions and answers, a list of facts) it is a transformation.

WRAP: four styles, half and half with real text

Maini et al. (Apple and CMU, January 2024; ACL 2024) rephrased C4 with a frozen Mistral-7B-Instruct in four styles and trained 128M to 1.3B models on real and rephrased text sampled 1:1 (Rephrasing the Web, arXiv 2401.16380). The prompts (Appendix G, verbatim) are short:

StylePrompt (each preceded by a Vicuna-style system line)
Easy"For the following paragraph give me a paraphrase of the same using a very small vocabulary and extremely simple sentences that a toddler will understand:"
Medium"For the following paragraph give me a diverse paraphrase of the same in high quality English language as in sentences on Wikipedia:"
Hard"For the following paragraph give me a paraphrase of the same using very terse and abstruse language that only an erudite scholar will understand. Replace simple words and phrases with rare and complex ones:"
Q/A"Convert the following paragraph into a conversational format with multiple tags of "Question:" followed by "Answer:":"

Outputs were capped at 300 tokens because rephrasing longer text "often lost information". Results, read from the tables (averages of 3 runs, about 1.3B parameters, self-reported):

The paper disagrees with itself; quote the tables
  • Pile perplexity: the abstract says it "improves perplexity by more than 10% on average", the introduction and Section 4 say "~50%". Both come from figures with no table, so neither can be checked to the decimal.
  • The prose credits "the smaller C4 (85B) dataset" with 43.5% on the specialised tasks; in Table 2, 43.5 is Full C4 (170B) and Half C4 is 43.1.
  • "3x faster" (abstract) and "5x lesser data, or 3x lesser compute" (introduction) are read off Figure 1, again without a table.

The finding that has held up since is in WRAP's own discussion: "synthetic data can not impart 'new knowledge'. It can only help pre-train faster". Two more: a 1.8B rephraser (Qwen-1.8B-chat) gave rephrases "of high quality" comparable to Mistral-7B at about three times the throughput, and synthetic-only training lost perplexity on domains whose style the rephrases removed (Philpapers, Gutenberg), so the real half of the mix is not optional.

Nemotron-CC: five prompts at 1.9 trillion tokens

NVIDIA's Nemotron-CC (December 2024) applied rephrasing at corpus scale (arXiv 2412.02595). Documents had already been sorted into quality buckets by an ensemble of classifiers (the Quality classifiers page). Low-quality documents were rewritten Wikipedia-style with WRAP's prompt, so text that would otherwise be thrown away becomes usable. High-quality documents were turned into four additional forms: diverse question-answer pairs, a condensed "distilled" version, an "extract knowledge" rewrite and a knowledge list. Generator: Mistral NeMo 12B Instruct in FP8, temperature 0.5, top-p 0.9. Medium-quality documents were not rephrased "due to time and resource constraints". Each prompt type gets its own segment length (footnote 11: "512 for Wikipedia, 2,000 for Distill, 1,400 for Extract Knowledge and 1,000 for Diverse QA Pairs and Knowledge List, including tokens from the prompt"), because longer inputs gave "over-simplified outputs with reduced detail". Post-processing drops incomplete outputs and anything under 50 tokens, strips Markdown and preambles such as "Here is a paraphrased version:", and for question-answer output shuffles the pairs and appends a number of them after the source segment.

NVIDIA published the synthetic rows with the URL and WARC record id of the page each came from, so the original can be fetched from Common Crawl and compared. Nemotron-CC v2 (August 2025) switched the generator to Qwen3-30B-A3B and translated the question-answer data into 15 languages (card).

Where the evidence agrees and where it does not

Agreed: rephrased text mixed with real text beats real text alone at equal tokens; rephrased text alone does not; small generators (1B to 8B) are enough, bigger ones buy little (BeyondWeb Figure 9: 47.3, 48.8, 49.2 for 1B, 3B, 8B generators; Kang et al.; FinePhrase). Not agreed: the best synthetic share. Kang et al. find about 30% by validation loss; FinePhrase finds 60 to 80% by benchmark average; WRAP used 50%. They differ in generator, prompts, metric and scale, so the share is something to sweep for your own run, not to copy.

4. Math and code: synthesis with a checker

Math and code are where synthetic data is easiest to trust, because an executor or an answer checker can reject wrong output. Three patterns recur.

  • Generate, execute, keep what passes. phi-1's exercises (section 2); OpenCoder's annealing data (2.71 billion tokens of synthetic code snippets kept "only if they pass generated tests" and 0.91 billion of code textbooks, beside 96 billion real tokens, Table 3, arXiv 2411.04905); MegaMath's interleaved text and code blocks, generated, run and packed (50.3 billion tokens).
  • Rewrite the web into problems and solutions. MegaMath (LLM360, April 2025) turned math web pages into question-answer text with Qwen-2.5-72B-Instruct and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct; its Table 1 lists 64.5 billion synthetic tokens out of 371.6 billion (arXiv 2504.02807). In its 1B-model ablation (5B tokens, Table 6) the question-answer form beat the source web pages by a wide margin (Core average 28.3 for FineMath-4+ against 48.8 for the best prompt), and the prompt mattered almost as much as the format (39.2 for a plain prompt). MAmmoTH2's WebInstruct harvested 10 million question-answer pairs that already existed on the web (recall, extract, refine with open models), taking Mistral-7B from 11% to 36.7% on MATH without in-domain training data (arXiv 2405.03548). self-reported
  • Translate and restyle code. NVIDIA rewrote all its Python with Qwen3 32B using style-guided and self-contained rewriting prompts, checked with syntax checks and Pylint, and transpiled Python into C++, which "improved downstream C++ code-generation accuracy" (Nemotron 3 Nano report, Section 2.2.2, arXiv 2512.20848; data in Nemotron-Pretraining-Code-v2, gated).

One caution for the measurements in section 6: in the samples here MegaMath's text-and-code blocks have the lowest distinct-bigram ratio (0.26) and the highest compression ratio (5.62) of all 24 sets, and its question-answer set is next to them. Math and code repeat symbols and keywords by nature, so diversity numbers are only comparable within a domain: a math set is not "less diverse" than Wikipedia in any sense that matters.

5. How much synthetic text to mix in

Every published recipe keeps real text in the mix, and the shares vary widely. Two kinds of number are easy to confuse: what a recipe used (one team's choice for one run) and what an ablation found best (a sweep, under that paper's metric). Synthetic text is also usually repeated more than web text: phi-4's 290 billion unique synthetic tokens were seen 13.8 times each, its 1.3 trillion web tokens 1.2 times (Table 5).

    6. Checking a synthetic set before training on it

    The problem. A generator produces fluent text whatever it is asked, so reading a few samples tells you almost nothing about a set of millions. The failures are statistical: the set is narrow (the same openings, the same phrases, the same topics), it repeats itself, it contains benchmark questions the generator memorised, or it is confidently wrong. Each has a cheap measure. All of them were run here on 1,000-document samples of 24 sets (real, rephrased and generated) with scripts in src/scripts/; the full table and per-set details are in the Diversity meter.

    • Distinct n-grams (above). FineWeb's sample has distinct-2 of 0.65; TinyStories 0.33 and the Khan Academy part of Cosmopedia 0.35, both narrow by design (a small vocabulary; one lesson template).
    • Compression ratio: raw bytes divided by gzip bytes of the same 100,000 words. A compressor finds repetition at every length, so it summarises redundancy in one number and Shaib et al. found that fast compression ratios capture much of what slow n-gram homogeneity scores measure, recommending them as one of a small set of low-correlated scores to report (arXiv 2403.00553). FineWeb 2.65, TinyStories 3.33, Khan Academy units 3.42 (higher means more redundant).
    • Openings and templates: the share of documents starting with the set's ten most common three-word openings (section 2). Catches template collapse that n-gram ratios over whole documents average away.
    • Embedding spread: embed each document and average the cosine similarity between all pairs; higher means the documents are about the same things in the same way. Measured with the encoder of the FineWeb-Edu classifier (Snowflake arctic-embed-m), 300 documents per set.
    • Near-duplicates: MinHash over 5-word shingles (the Deduplication page). Necessary, never sufficient: the most templated sets here have almost no near-duplicates.
    • Benchmark overlap: the share of documents that share a 13-word sequence with a test item (MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag, GSM8K: 2.1 million distinct 13-grams). Generators memorise benchmarks; Cosmopedia's own decontamination removed, for example, 386 documents matching 41 BoolQ items from its web, Stanford and OpenStax parts (blog). In the samples here the highest synthetic rate is 0.2% of documents. Methods and their false positives are on the Decontamination page.

    A quality classifier is not a judge of synthetic text

    Pipelines filter synthetic output with the same classifiers they use on the web (REWIRE kept the top 10% of rewrites by a fastText classifier). Those classifiers were trained to recognise a style: FineWeb-Edu's on Llama-3-70B ratings of educational value, DCLM's with OpenHermes 2.5 (itself GPT-4 output) as positive examples. Measured with the FineWeb-Edu classifier (score 3 or more is FineWeb-Edu's cut): measured 5.7% of FineWeb pages pass and, as a sanity check, 100% of FineWeb-Edu pages; Cosmopedia's subsets pass at anywhere from 25% (stories) to 97% (Khan Academy units).

A pipeline that filters synthetic output by this score is therefore partly selecting formats, which may be what you want, but should be a decision.

    Model-written text in the "real" data

    FineWeb's authors tracked phrases typical of chat models ("delve", "as a large language model", "it's important to note", "rich tapestry", "intertwined", "certainly!", "dive into") across crawls and saw a "steep increase" after CC-MAIN-2023-14; they judged it did "not seem to drastically harm" quality and did not filter (FineWeb blog, section "Synthetic data"). The same phrase list, per 10,000 words, on the samples here: measured DCLM-baseline (crawls up to 2022) 0.05, Wikipedia (2023 dump) 0.04, FineWeb from the July 2024 crawl 0.52, Nemotron-CC's real high-quality bucket from the same crawl 1.99, and Cosmopedia v2 7.6. The real high-quality bucket carries about four times the rate of the unfiltered crawl: a hint, from one crawl and 1,000 documents per set, that classifiers trained on model-rated or model-written positives prefer model-written pages. Treat it as a question to test on your own data, not a result.

    7. Model collapse: what the experiments show, and what they do not

    The worry. If models are trained on text written by models, and those models write the next round of training text, errors could compound: each generation a slightly worse copy of the last. Shumailov et al. named this model collapse and defined two stages: "In early model collapse, the model begins losing information about the tails of the distribution; in late model collapse, the model converges to a distribution that carries little resemblance to the original one, often with substantially reduced variance" (Nature 631, 755 to 759, published 2024-07-24; preprint arXiv 2305.17493, May 2023). The tails are the rare events: uncommon words, rare facts, unusual styles.

    Why the tails go first: the arithmetic

    Think of the model as a table of word probabilities, refit each generation by counting the words in a sample of n words drawn from the previous model. A word with probability p is missing from the sample with probability (1 − p)n. Once missing, the refit gives it probability zero, so it can never be drawn again: in the paper's words the process is a Markov chain whose only absorbing states are distributions on a single value. Common words survive any one generation almost surely; rare words face the same small risk every generation, and the risks add up.

    Chance that one rare word survives g generations of refitting on its own samples

    derived: expected count np; chance of vanishing in one generation (1 − p)n; and an upper bound on surviving all g generations, the chance it is drawn at least once in every generation if its probability stayed at p, (1 − (1 − p)n)g. In the real process its probability also drifts up and down each generation (a random walk), which makes extinction more likely, not less; the Collapse lab simulates the full process for a thousand words at once.

    For a whole vocabulary with Zipf-shaped frequencies (1,000 word types, exponent 1.1, samples of 2,000 words), the expected number of types that appear at all in one sample is 423 (the sum of 1 − (1 − p)n over all types); after 30 generations of refitting on its own samples, the median over 50 seeded runs is 64 types left (range 54 to 78). Keeping the real sample in the pool holds it at 422 (Collapse lab).

    The experiment, exactly

    Shumailov et al.'s language-model experiment, read from the paper:

    • Model and data: OPT-125m (125 million parameters), already pretrained, fine-tuned on wikitext2 (a small Wikipedia corpus). Not pretraining from scratch.
    • Generating: for every 64-token block of the training set, the current model writes the next 64 tokens with five-way beam search (keeps the 5 most probable continuations, which favours high-probability text). The generated set is the same size as the original.
    • Generations: generation 0 is fine-tuned on real data; 9 more generations, each fine-tuned on the previous generation's output; 5 seeds.
    • Two settings: (A) 5 epochs per generation and no original data; (B) 10 epochs and a random 10% of the original data kept each generation.
    • Results: generation 0 reaches perplexity 34 on the real test set (115 zero-shot). Setting A loses "from 20 to 28 perplexity points" (the sentence is ambiguous; read from the figure it is an increase of 20 to 28, no table is given); setting B shows "only minor degradation". The perplexity of each generation's own output, scored by generation 0, piles up at low values with a longer tail of sequences "that would never be produced by the original model". A generation-9 sample degenerates into a list: "black @-@ tailed jackrabbits, white @-@ tailed jackrabbits, blue @-@ tailed jackrabbits".
    • Why it happens, they argue, is three errors that compound: statistical approximation (finite samples, the arithmetic above), functional expressivity (the model family cannot represent the truth) and functional approximation (the learning procedure is biased). The preprint names two; the Nature version three.
    What it does not show
    • Nothing about pretraining from scratch, models above 125M parameters, or real web data.
    • Nothing about keeping earlier synthetic data or all of the real data: each generation replaces the last (apart from the 10% setting, which already mostly prevents the damage).
    • Beam search, not sampling: beam search pushes output toward the most probable text, which by itself narrows the distribution.
    • Only 9 generations. Headlines that cite it for "AI trained on AI text collapses" describe a setup no lab uses.

    What a data team takes from this
    • Never drop the real data. Synthetic tokens are added to a pool that keeps all the real tokens; with a fixed token budget, cap the synthetic share.
    • Ground and verify. Rephrasing keeps the real document's tails; checkers (code execution, answer checking, fidelity checks as in Kimi K2) remove the errors that compound.
    • Watch diversity, not only quality. Low-temperature or best-of sampling buys quality at the price of the tails, exactly what collapse removes first.
    • Know what is already synthetic. Vendors measuring with commercial detectors estimate that about half of new English articles in Common Crawl samples are now model-written (Graphite, an SEO agency, May 2026 update: graphite.io; vendor, detector-based); an academic lower bound for new English Wikipedia articles in August 2024 was 4.36% (arXiv 2410.08044). No peer-reviewed estimate for Common Crawl as a whole was found. Date-stamping crawls and keeping pre-2023 snapshots is cheap insurance.

    9. Building it: a synthetic data pipeline at scale

    Put together, a production pipeline for synthetic pretraining text has these stages, each with what it costs and how to check it:

    1. Choose seeds. From the quality buckets of your web pipeline: low-quality pages to rescue by rewriting (Nemotron-CC, REWIRE), high-quality pages to transform into more forms, or a topic list (Cosmopedia v2's 34,000 BISAC topics) with retrieved seed pages. Deduplicate seeds first: duplicated seeds become duplicated outputs that MinHash will not catch once reworded. Check: topic histogram of seeds.
    2. Design prompts. Styles and formats (WRAP's four, Nemotron-CC's five, FinePhrase's tables and FAQs), audiences, personas, and explicit bans on stock openings. Check: generate 1,000 outputs per prompt and run the measures of section 6 before scaling; this is where Cosmopedia "spent most of the time".
    3. Chunk. Rewrite segments, not whole documents (WRAP: 300 tokens; Nemotron-CC: 512 to 2,000 depending on the prompt; Kimi K2: chunk by chunk with the previous chunk as context). Long inputs get summarised instead of rewritten.
    4. Generate. An inference server with large batches (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM; Nemotron-CC used TensorRT-LLM in FP8), a 1B to 8B generator for rephrasing (section 3), moderate temperature (Nemotron-CC: 0.5, top-p 0.9). Make it resumable and store the prompt id, seed id, generator and sampling settings per row.
    5. Post-process. Strip preambles ("Here is a paraphrased version:") and Markdown, drop refusals, truncated and very short outputs (Nemotron-CC: under 50 tokens).
    6. Verify. Execute code, check math answers, check fidelity to the source (Kimi K2's semantic comparison; a cheap proxy is the share of numbers and names kept, which the Rephrasing lab measures), optionally score with a classifier (knowing it favours the style, section 6).
    7. Deduplicate and decontaminate. MinHash within the synthetic set and against the real corpus; n-gram matching against every evaluation set (Cosmopedia: 10-grams then a similarity check), because generators reproduce benchmark items.
    8. Mix and test. Sweep the synthetic share on small proxy models (section 5; the Data mixing page), keep all real data in the pool, decide how many times synthetic tokens may repeat.

    What will the generation cost?

    Common mistakes

    Training on synthetic text alone
    Every comparison that tried it lost something: WRAP's synthetic-only runs on Pile domains whose style was rewritten away, phi-4's synthetic-only model on TriviaQA (−14.8) with "increased hallucinations", REWIRE's rewritten-only below real at every scale, Kang et al.'s rephrased-only no faster than web text. Mix it with real text.
    Reading "no near-duplicates" as "diverse"
    MinHash finds copies. Generated sets have almost none and can still start 84% of documents with ten openings (TinyStories here). Measure openings, distinct n-grams at a fixed budget, compression and embedding spread.
    Comparing distinct-n across samples of different sizes or domains
    The ratio falls as the sample grows, and math or code repeats symbols by nature. Fix the word budget (100,000 words here) and compare within a domain.
    Filtering synthetic text with a quality classifier and calling it checked
    Classifiers trained on model-rated or model-written positives can favour formats and styles: here every Nemotron-CC transformation of high-quality pages scored above its source pages. A score says nothing about whether the facts are right (the shipping textbook's unsourced emissions figure). Check seeds and facts, not only scores.
    Skipping decontamination because "the text is new"
    Generators reproduce benchmark items they memorised, and exercise-style data resembles benchmarks by design (phi-1's own analysis; Cosmopedia removed hundreds of matching documents). Run n-gram matching against every evaluation set, and for skill-targeted data retrain without the nearest items as phi-1 did.
    Citing model collapse against all synthetic data
    The famous experiment fine-tunes a 125M model on its own beam-search output and throws the real data away each generation. Accumulating, grounding and verifying, which is what labs do, avoids it (Gerstgrasser et al., Feng et al.). The real risks are narrower: lost tails and style, and unlabelled model text in new crawls.
    Using a bigger generator than needed
    For rephrasing, gains flatten above 1B to 8B parameters in three independent studies (BeyondWeb, Kang et al., FinePhrase), and the cost per token grows with size (33.1 million tokens per GPU-hour for a 1.7B generator against 1.1 million for a 70B one, FinePhrase Table 10). Spend the budget on prompts and seeds.
    Losing track of which model wrote what
    Generator terms differ (section 8). Without the generator, prompt and seed id on every row, a licence change or a bad prompt cannot be removed later.

    Interview questions

    Why does rephrasing help pretraining if it adds no new information?
    The facts are the same, but the form is easier to learn from: no boilerplate, consistent grammar, explanations and question-answer formats that resemble how knowledge is used later. Models reach the same loss or accuracy in fewer tokens (WRAP's speed-up; Kang et al. 5 to 10 times to the same validation loss with a third rephrased). Multiple rephrasings of the same fact also act as better repetition than repeating the original (Kimi K2's SimpleQA table).
    How would you make 10 million generated documents diverse?
    Vary the inputs, not the temperature: seed each prompt with a different real document or topic (web pages clustered or retrieved by topic), cross with audiences, formats and personas, ban stock openings in the prompt, and check openings, distinct n-grams and embedding spread on a 1,000-document pilot per prompt before scaling. Deduplicate seeds first.
    Explain model collapse in two sentences, and when it does not happen.
    Refitting a model on a finite sample of its own output loses rare events each round (a word that is not sampled gets probability zero and never returns), so the distribution narrows and drifts. It is avoided when real data stays in the training pool (accumulate), when fresh real data arrives, or when a verifier selects good samples.
    A synthetic math set lifts GSM8K by 15 points. What do you check before believing it?
    Contamination: n-gram and embedding matches between the set and GSM8K test items, and the generator's own exposure to the test set; retrain without the nearest items (phi-1's method). Then a held-out or fresh benchmark (phi-4 used the November 2024 AMC), several seeds, and whether general benchmarks dropped.
    Which generator would you pick for 500 billion tokens of rephrasing, and why?
    A permissively licensed 1B to 8B instruction model (Apache-2.0 Qwen3 or similar): quality flattens above that size in the published comparisons, and throughput, so cost, falls roughly with size. Run a small ablation of two or three generators on 1B-model proxies before committing.
    System design: build the synthetic-data stage for a 10-trillion-token pretraining run.
    Goal and budget: say 30% synthetic, 3T tokens, repeated at most 4 times, so about 750B unique synthetic tokens. Seeds: the quality buckets from the web pipeline (rewrite low and medium, transform high), deduplicated, plus topic lists for math and code. Prompts: five to ten formats, piloted on 1,000 outputs each and measured.       The four workflows

      • Replace (Shumailov et al., setting A): each generation is fit only on n samples from the previous model. A type that misses one sample is gone forever; in the long run one type remains.
      • Accumulate (Gerstgrasser et al.): each generation is fit on the real sample plus every synthetic sample so far. Real counts never leave the pool, so no type seen in the real sample can die; each new generation's noise counts for less and less.
      • Accumulate-subsample (Kazdan et al.): the pool accumulates, but each model trains on only n samples drawn from it (a fixed compute budget). Losses slow down and level off, above accumulate.
      • Fresh real data (Alemohammad et al.'s fresh-data loop; Shumailov's setting B keeps a random 10% of the original instead): each generation's n samples include a share of new draws from the real distribution, which brings dead types back.

      What this toy leaves out, on purpose: a real language model shares parameters across words, so it can give a never-seen word some probability (functional expressivity and approximation errors in Shumailov's terms, which can either soften or worsen collapse).

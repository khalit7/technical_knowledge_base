5. How much synthetic text to mix in

Every published recipe keeps real text in the mix, and the shares vary widely. Two kinds of number are easy to confuse: what a recipe used (one team's choice for one run) and what an ablation found best (a sweep, under that paper's metric). Synthetic text is also usually repeated more than web text: phi-4's 290 billion unique synthetic tokens were seen 13.8 times each, its 1.3 trillion web tokens 1.2 times (Table 5).

    6. Checking a synthetic set before training on it

    The problem. A generator produces fluent text whatever it is asked, so reading a few samples tells you almost nothing about a set of millions. The failures are statistical: the set is narrow (the same openings, the same phrases, the same topics), it repeats itself, it contains benchmark questions the generator memorised, or it is confidently wrong. Each has a cheap measure. All of them were run here on 1,000-document samples of 24 sets (real, rephrased and generated) with scripts in src/scripts/; the full table and per-set details are in the {{Diversity meter|#t-sydi}}.

    What the numbers say. Rephrasing keeps the variety of its source: Nemotron-CC's Wikipedia-style rewrites of low-quality pages have distinct-2 @@nlw_d2@@ against @@nlr_d2@@ for the low-quality pages themselves, because the information is the pages'. Generated sets are as varied as the web on n-grams when seeds are varied (Cosmopedia web_samples_v2 @@cw2_d2@@) and much less when the template is fixed (Khan Academy units, TinyStories). The diverse-QA rows look less varied (@@nqa_d2@@) because each row repeats its source and then asks about it: a measure is only meaningful against what the format must repeat.

    A quality classifier is not a judge of synthetic text

    Pipelines filter synthetic output with the same classifiers they use on the web (REWIRE kept the top 10% of rewrites by a fastText classifier). Those classifiers were trained to recognise a style: FineWeb-Edu's on Llama-3-70B ratings of educational value, DCLM's with OpenHermes 2.5 (itself GPT-4 output) as positive examples. Measured with the FineWeb-Edu classifier (score 3 or more is FineWeb-Edu's cut): measured @@fw_edu3@@ of FineWeb pages pass and, as a sanity check, @@fwe_edu3@@ of FineWeb-Edu pages; Cosmopedia's subsets pass at anywhere from @@cosmo_edu3_min@@ (stories) to @@cosmo_edu3_max@@ (Khan Academy units).

    Two readings of the bars. First, the classifier reads content, not just tone: Nemotron-CC's Wikipedia-style rewrites of low-quality pages pass at @@nemo_lq_wiki_edu3@@, the same as the low-quality pages themselves (@@nemo_lq_real_edu3@@), and Cosmopedia's web-seeded textbooks pass far less often (@@cosmo_web_v2_edu3@@ for web_samples_v2) than its textbooks seeded with real course outlines (@@cosmo_khan_edu3@@ for Khan Academy, @@cosmo_stanford_edu3@@ for Stanford). The seed's subject decides more than the textbook voice. Second, the transformations of high-quality pages all score above their sources (@@nemo_hq_real_edu3@@ for the real high-quality pages): restating facts as questions, lists or condensed prose moves documents over the cut. A pipeline that filters synthetic output by this score is therefore partly selecting formats, which may be what you want, but should be a decision.

    Model-written text in the "real" data

    FineWeb's authors tracked phrases typical of chat models ("delve", "as a large language model", "it's important to note", "rich tapestry", "intertwined", "certainly!", "dive into") across crawls and saw a "steep increase" after CC-MAIN-2023-14; they judged it did "not seem to drastically harm" quality and did not filter ({{FineWeb blog, section "Synthetic data"|https://huggingface.co/spaces/HuggingFaceFW/blogpost-fineweb-v1}}). The same phrase list, per 10,000 words, on the samples here: measured DCLM-baseline (crawls up to 2022) @@dclm_llm@@, Wikipedia (2023 dump) @@wiki_llm@@, FineWeb from the July 2024 crawl @@fw_llm@@, Nemotron-CC's real high-quality bucket from the same crawl @@nhq_llm@@, and Cosmopedia v2 @@cv2_llm@@. The real high-quality bucket carries about four times the rate of the unfiltered crawl: a hint, from one crawl and 1,000 documents per set, that classifiers trained on model-rated or model-written positives prefer model-written pages. Treat it as a question to test on your own data, not a result.

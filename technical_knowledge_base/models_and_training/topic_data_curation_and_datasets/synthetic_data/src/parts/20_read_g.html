7. Model collapse: what the experiments show, and what they do not

The worry. If models are trained on text written by models, and those models write the next round of training text, errors could compound: each generation a slightly worse copy of the last. Shumailov et al. named this model collapse and defined two stages: "In early model collapse, the model begins losing information about the tails of the distribution; in late model collapse, the model converges to a distribution that carries little resemblance to the original one, often with substantially reduced variance" ({{Nature 631, 755 to 759, published 2024-07-24|https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y}}; preprint {{arXiv 2305.17493|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17493}}, May 2023). The tails are the rare events: uncommon words, rare facts, unusual styles.

Why the tails go first: the arithmetic

Think of the model as a table of word probabilities, refit each generation by counting the words in a sample of n words drawn from the previous model. A word with probability p is missing from the sample with probability (1 − p)n. Once missing, the refit gives it probability zero, so it can never be drawn again: in the paper's words the process is a Markov chain whose only absorbing states are distributions on a single value. Common words survive any one generation almost surely; rare words face the same small risk every generation, and the risks add up.

Chance that one rare word survives g generations of refitting on its own samples

derived: expected count np; chance of vanishing in one generation (1 − p)n; and an upper bound on surviving all g generations, the chance it is drawn at least once in every generation if its probability stayed at p, (1 − (1 − p)n)g. In the real process its probability also drifts up and down each generation (a random walk), which makes extinction more likely, not less; the {{Collapse lab|#t-syco}} simulates the full process for a thousand words at once.

The experiment, exactly

Shumailov et al.'s language-model experiment, read from the paper:

  • Model and data: OPT-125m (125 million parameters), already pretrained, fine-tuned on wikitext2 (a small Wikipedia corpus). Not pretraining from scratch.
  • Generating: for every 64-token block of the training set, the current model writes the next 64 tokens with five-way beam search (keeps the 5 most probable continuations, which favours high-probability text). The generated set is the same size as the original.
  • Generations: generation 0 is fine-tuned on real data; 9 more generations, each fine-tuned on the previous generation's output; 5 seeds.
  • Two settings: (A) 5 epochs per generation and no original data; (B) 10 epochs and a random 10% of the original data kept each generation.
  • Results: generation 0 reaches perplexity 34 on the real test set (115 zero-shot). Setting A loses "from 20 to 28 perplexity points" (the sentence is ambiguous; read from the figure it is an increase of 20 to 28, no table is given); setting B shows "only minor degradation". The perplexity of each generation's own output, scored by generation 0, piles up at low values with a longer tail of sequences "that would never be produced by the original model". A generation-9 sample degenerates into a list: "black @-@ tailed jackrabbits, white @-@ tailed jackrabbits, blue @-@ tailed jackrabbits".
  • Why it happens, they argue, is three errors that compound: statistical approximation (finite samples, the arithmetic above), functional expressivity (the model family cannot represent the truth) and functional approximation (the learning procedure is biased). The preprint names two; the Nature version three.
What it does not show
  • Nothing about pretraining from scratch, models above 125M parameters, or real web data.
  • Nothing about keeping earlier synthetic data or all of the real data: each generation replaces the last (apart from the 10% setting, which already mostly prevents the damage).
  • Beam search, not sampling: beam search pushes output toward the most probable text, which by itself narrows the distribution.
  • Only 9 generations. Headlines that cite it for "AI trained on AI text collapses" describe a setup no lab uses.

What a data team takes from this
  • Never drop the real data. Synthetic tokens are added to a pool that keeps all the real tokens; with a fixed token budget, cap the synthetic share.
  • Ground and verify. Rephrasing keeps the real document's tails; checkers (code execution, answer checking, fidelity checks as in Kimi K2) remove the errors that compound.
  • Watch diversity, not only quality. Low-temperature or best-of sampling buys quality at the price of the tails, exactly what collapse removes first.
  • Know what is already synthetic. Vendors measuring with commercial detectors estimate that about half of new English articles in Common Crawl samples are now model-written (Graphite, an SEO agency, May 2026 update: {{graphite.io|https://graphite.io/five-percent/ai-now-writes-as-many-online-articles-as-humans-do}}; vendor, detector-based); an academic lower bound for new English Wikipedia articles in August 2024 was 4.36% ({{arXiv 2410.08044|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08044}}). No peer-reviewed estimate for Common Crawl as a whole was found. Date-stamping crawls and keeping pre-2023 snapshots is cheap insurance.

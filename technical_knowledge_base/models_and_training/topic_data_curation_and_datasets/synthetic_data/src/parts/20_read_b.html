Synthetic pretraining data is training text written by a language model instead of by people. A model (the generator) is given a prompt, often containing a real document or a topic (the seed), and its output is added to the corpus another model (the student) is pretrained on. This page teaches how such text is made, what the published evidence says it is worth, how to measure a synthetic set before training on it, and what happens when models are trained on model output generation after generation (model collapse). Reading time about 40 minutes, plus time with the animations (the answers folded under "Common mistakes" and "Interview questions" add about 5).

The parent page gives the two-paragraph version and a one-dimensional collapse simulation ({{Topic: data-curation-and-datasets, section 8|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa5ecda8d365ab00f}}); this page goes deeper and does not repeat that simulation. Post-training data (instructions, preference pairs, verifiable-reward prompts, environments), much of which is also model-written, belongs to the sibling {{Post-training data|n:3f95c17b0d0d81318c3ec7e4564d7b42}}.

Five things to hold on to
  • Rephrasing is the workhorse. Grounding the generator in a real document keeps the real world's variety of facts and topics; the model only fixes the form. Generation from seeds needs heavy prompt engineering to avoid writing the same text again and again (section 2).
  • Synthetic text speeds learning; it does not add knowledge. WRAP's authors: "synthetic data can not impart 'new knowledge'. It can only help pre-train faster" (section 3). Mixed with real text it helps; on its own it usually loses on knowledge benchmarks.
  • Mix, do not replace. Published mixes put synthetic text at roughly a third to a half of a stage (section 5); every result where real text was dropped entirely shows a cost somewhere.
  • Measure before training. Openings, distinct n-grams, compression ratio, near-duplicates, benchmark overlap and classifier scores take minutes on a laptop and catch most failures (section 6 and the {{Diversity meter|#t-sydi}}).
  • Model collapse is about replacing real data. Training each generation only on the previous generation's output loses the rare things first; keeping the real data in the pool stops the slide (section 7 and the {{Collapse lab|#t-syco}}).

1. Why let a model write training data

The problem. A pretraining run wants more good tokens than the web holds. FineWeb-Edu's strict cut of the English web is 1.3 trillion tokens; frontier runs train on 15 trillion and more (numbers and sources on the {{parent page|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa5ecda8d365ab00f}}). Repeating data is nearly free for about 4 epochs and loses value fast after about 16 (Muennighoff et al., {{arXiv 2305.16264|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264}}). Keeping lower-quality text is the other option. A generator offers a third: more tokens of the kind you lack (explanations, worked problems, questions and answers) and a better form for the tokens you have (the same facts written clearly, without menus and boilerplate).

What it costs. Generation is inference on a large model, so every synthetic token costs GPU time. WRAP generated 3 million tokens per A100 hour with Mistral-7B and estimated about 25,000 GPU hours for its 85 billion tokens (Section 7.1; 85B / 3M per hour is about 28,000, so their figure is rounded down) ({{arXiv 2401.16380|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16380}}). Cosmopedia's 25 billion tokens took "over 10k GPU hours" ({{Cosmopedia blog, 2024-03-20|https://huggingface.co/blog/cosmopedia}}). The cost calculator in section 9 turns these into a budget.

Where it is used today. Microsoft's phi-4 (14B parameters, about 10 trillion training tokens) put 40% of its pretraining tokens on synthetic data and 15% on web rewrites (Table 5, {{arXiv 2412.08905|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.08905}}). Nemotron-CC is 6.3 trillion tokens of which 1.9 trillion are rephrased (Table 4, {{arXiv 2412.02595|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02595}}). Qwen3's report says Qwen2.5 models synthesised "trillions of text tokens" of textbooks, question-answering, instructions and code ({{arXiv 2505.09388|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.09388}}; no exact count given). Olmo 3's mid-training mix is largely synthetic math, code and question-answer text (parent page, section 8).

1. Why let a model write training data

The problem. Frontier runs train on 15 trillion tokens and more, while FineWeb-Edu's strict cut of the English web is 1.3 trillion (numbers and sources on the {{parent page|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa5ecda8d365ab00f}}). Repeating data is nearly free for about 4 epochs and loses value fast after about 16 (Muennighoff et al., {{arXiv 2305.16264|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264}}); keeping lower-quality text is the other fallback. A generator offers a third: more tokens of the kind you lack (explanations, worked problems, questions and answers) and a better form for the tokens you have (the same facts written clearly, without menus and boilerplate).

What it costs. Every synthetic token is inference on a large model. WRAP generated 3 million tokens per A100 hour with Mistral-7B and estimated about 25,000 GPU hours for its 85 billion tokens (Section 7.1; 85B / 3M per hour is about 28,000, so their figure is rounded down; {{arXiv 2401.16380|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.16380}}). Cosmopedia's 25 billion tokens took "over 10k GPU hours" ({{Cosmopedia blog, 2024-03-20|https://huggingface.co/blog/cosmopedia}}). Section 9's calculator turns these rates into a budget; section 5 shows who pays it and how much synthetic text they mix in.

2. Generating from seeds: TinyStories, phi, Cosmopedia and instruction data

The idea. Ask a strong model for the text you wish the web had more of (clear explanations, graded exercises, simple stories, instructions with answers) and train on it. The generator's knowledge does the work; the prompt only says what to write. The hard part, every team reports, is diversity: a model asked a thousand times for "a story for children" writes nearly the same story a thousand times. So every method below is, at heart, a way to inject variety through the seed.

TinyStories: three random words per story

Eldan and Li (Microsoft Research, May 2023) asked GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 for short stories using only words "a typical 3-year-old child would understand" ({{arXiv 2305.07759, Section 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759}}). From a list of about 1,500 basic words, "3 words are chosen randomly (one verb, one noun, and one adjective)" and must appear, plus a random subset of story features (a dialogue, a plot twist, a bad ending, a moral). Without this, generation "even if the temperature of generation is set to a high value, will still produce a very repetitive dataset". Models under 10 million parameters, or with a single transformer block, trained on it write fluent, consistent stories (graded by GPT-4). The card lists 2,141,709 stories, CDLA-Sharing-1.0, plus a GPT-4-only version ({{dataset card|https://huggingface.co/datasets/roneneldan/TinyStories}}).

phi: "textbook quality" data for small models

Microsoft's phi-1 (June 2023) made the field take synthetic pretraining data seriously ({{Textbooks Are All You Need, arXiv 2306.11644|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11644}}): a 1.3-billion-parameter code model trained on under 7 billion tokens (about 6 billion of real code filtered for "educational value", under 1 billion of Python textbooks written by GPT-3.5, about 180 million of exercises with solutions) reached 50.6% on HumanEval and 55.5% on MBPP after 4 days on 8 A100s. Because the exercises resemble HumanEval, the authors removed between 42.5 thousand and 354 thousand of the 879.5 thousand exercises closest to HumanEval problems and retrained: phi-1 stayed at 45.1% to 50.6%, above StarCoder-Prompted's 41.5% (Table 3). That is the right test, because a set built to teach a skill will resemble the benchmark for that skill.

phi-1.5 (September 2023) extended the recipe to general text: "roughly 20B tokens" of textbooks seeded by "20K topics", 80% of a 150-billion-token run ({{arXiv 2309.05463|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.05463}}). phi-4 (December 2024) used 50 kinds of synthetic datasets, "about 400B unweighted tokens", made with multi-agent prompting, self-revision and instruction reversal ({{arXiv 2412.08905|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.08905}}), and gives the warning to remember: "Models trained only with synthetic data underperformed on the knowledge-heavy benchmarks and demonstrated increased hallucinations". Its synthetic-only 13B ablation was 14.8 points worse on TriviaQA than phi-3-medium, and adding web rewrites halved that gap (to 7.7; Table 3).

phi details: the filter, the counts and the web-only comparison (depth)

phi-1's code filter: GPT-4 labelled about 100,000 files for educational value; a random forest on code-model embeddings labelled the rest. The filtering alone mattered: on unfiltered code a 350M model stalled at 12.19% HumanEval, on the filtered subset it reached 17.68% in fewer steps. The exercise count is given as "about 180M", "less than 180M" and "<200M" in different places of the paper. phi-1.5's Table 1 lists the data as 30B, the prose 7B plus 20B. A web-only phi-1.5 variant trained on 95 billion tokens of filtered web text did much worse on common-sense benchmarks (ARC-Challenge 0.329 against 0.444, Table 2).

Cosmopedia: the open replication, and what a prompt looks like

Hugging Face's Cosmopedia (March 2024) reproduced phi-1.5's data openly: over 30 million documents and 25 billion tokens written by Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, Apache-2.0 ({{blog|https://huggingface.co/blog/cosmopedia}}, {{card|https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceTB/cosmopedia}}). Curated seeds ran out ("only 16,000 unique units from OpenStax and 250,000 from Stanford"), so over 80% of prompts quote a web page from an educational topic cluster, crossed with an audience (young children, high school, college, researchers) and a style (textbook, blog post, WikiHow): "up to 12 times the number of prompts". Two lessons sit in these rows. The generator invents: the shipping unit adds a figure for shipping's share of CO2 emissions that the seed never gave, and the product listing becomes a unit on "materials used (cotton candy, glass, etc.)" because the brand name "Cotton Candy" was read as a material; nobody checks a 30-million-document set by hand. And a junk seed still yields a fluent textbook, so the quality filter has to act on the seed (Cosmopedia kept only educational clusters) or on the output: fluency says nothing about value.

The diversity fix, measured. Cosmopedia's team found that changing only the audience line was "insufficient to prevent a high rate of duplicate content", and that stories kept opening with "Once upon a time" and "The sun hung low in the sky" until the prompts said not to. Cosmopedia's clusters and v2 (depth)

Seed web samples were clustered into 145 clusters and the educational ones kept (the blog says 112 topics; the card says 35 of 145 were discarded, which is 110). Cosmopedia v2 (July 2024, used for SmolLM) replaced clustering with a fixed list of 34,000 topics from the BISAC book classification; for each topic a search over FineWeb retrieved 1,000 seed pages, giving 39 million documents and 28 billion tokens. Its ablations on 1.8B models found curated-seed textbooks best on MMLU and ARC and a middle-school audience best on everything except MMLU ({{SmolLM blog|https://huggingface.co/blog/smollm}}). By SmolLM2 (2025) Cosmopedia v2 appears only in the last stage, at 4% of the mix (Section 4.5, {{arXiv 2502.02737|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02737}}, which gives it as 30B tokens).

Instructions and answers: the same problem, other seeds

Generating instruction data is the same move with a different target, and each well-known method is a different answer to "where does the variety come from". The datasets themselves, their measured properties and Self-Instruct's filter step by step are on {{Post-training data|n:3f95c17b0d0d81318c3ec7e4564d7b42}} (section 2); here is the strategy each one uses.

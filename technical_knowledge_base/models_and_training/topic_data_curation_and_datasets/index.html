Data curation is everything done to text before a model trains on it: finding it, turning it into clean text, throwing most of it away, removing copies, removing test questions, and deciding how much of each kind to show the model and when. This page teaches it from zero by following one slice of the raw web through a modern pipeline, measuring what each stage keeps and drops, and comparing it with what the published corpora kept. 1. Why the data decides so much

A token is the unit a model reads (a word piece, about four characters of English). A corpus is the set of documents it is trained on, and its size is counted in tokens. The question this section answers: at a fixed training budget, how much does it matter which tokens you use? Three pieces of evidence, from the broadest to the most controlled.

Across seven years of open recipes. Dwarkesh Patel and Jerry Han crossed each year's representative open training recipe (GPT-2 in 2019 to OLMo 2 in 2025) with each year's public corpus (OpenWebText in 2019 to Ultra-FineWeb in 2025), trained every pair at budgets up to 1019 FLOPs and read off how much compute each improvement saves. Better data saved 12.0x compute, better models 3.7x: data contributed 3.24 times more. A simple additive model of "data effect plus model effect" explains 88% of the score differences, so the two barely interact. third-party (Patel and Han, 2026-09-08). The caveats are the authors' own: small models, little tuning, one benchmark suite (OLMES), one seed per cell. Their remark that small models gain most from data quality is an argument, not a measurement: they trained only small models.

Same model, same compute, different filter: DCLM's comparison of quality filters

Within one controlled study. The chart above is the cleanest kind of evidence in this field, a data ablation: train identical small models (the proxy or ablation models) on versions of the data that differ in one decision, then compare benchmark scores. Choosing which documents to keep moves the score by 4 Core points at fixed compute; for scale, the whole gap between Common Crawl's own extracted text and a good extractor is about 3.5 points on the same scale (section 3). Five years earlier, the T5 paper had already shown the direction: an unfiltered crawl 8 times larger than C4 (6.1 TB against 745 GB) scored lower on 6 of 7 task groups at the same training budget (T5, Table 8; the paper's text says "every task", but on SuperGLUE its Wikipedia-only model is 0.03 lower than the unfiltered one).

Predict: FineWeb's team deduplicated all 96 Common Crawl snapshots against each other, the most thorough dedup possible, and trained on the result. Did it beat deduplicating each snapshot on its own?
No. Global dedup left 4 trillion tokens and a model trained on 350 billion of them barely beat one trained on non-deduplicated data; deduplicating each snapshot separately won. What survived global dedup of the oldest snapshot was disproportionately ads and keyword lists (section 4). Data decisions are empirical: even "obviously good" steps have to be ablated.

2. Where the tokens come from

Almost every open pretraining corpus starts from Common Crawl (CC), a non-profit that has crawled the web since 2008 and publishes a new crawl (also called a snapshot or dump) every month or two. One crawl comes in three file types. WARC files hold the raw HTTP responses (the HTML exactly as served); WAT files hold metadata (headers, links); WET files hold plain text that Common Crawl extracted itself, menus and footers included. The June 2025 crawl, for scale: 2.38 billion pages, 82.73 TiB of compressed WARC, 15.62 TiB of WAT and 6.24 TiB of WET (Common Crawl, 2025-06-27). After FineWeb's whole pipeline, that crawl yields 152.4 billion tokens (FineWeb dataset card).

The rest comes from sources with more structure and more legal care: code (The Stack v2 from Software Heritage; Stack-Edu, its educational slice), scientific papers and PDFs (arXiv, peS2o, PDFs turned into text with OCR models such as olmOCR; FinePDFs holds about 3 trillion tokens from 475 million PDFs), encyclopedias and books (the Books3 collection inside The Pile was taken down over copyright, and books are now the most contested source). A licence on a dataset (ODC-By for FineWeb and Dolma, CC-BY-4.0 for DCLM-baseline, an NVIDIA data agreement for Nemotron-CC v2) covers the compilation; it does not license each web page inside it. The Common Pile v0.1 is the attempt at 8 TB of text whose every piece is openly licensed or public domain (arXiv 2506.05209).

3. The pipeline: from a raw page to a training document

A raw crawl is mostly not text you want: navigation menus, cookie banners, spam, machine-generated pages, adult content, pages in other languages, and the same page many times over. The pipeline removes it in stages ordered by cost, so that the expensive stages only see what the cheap ones let through. Five of the stages are simple enough to define in one breath each; deduplication, quality models and decontamination get their own sections.

  • URL filtering. Drop pages whose domain is on a blocklist (adult and spam sites) before reading them. Costs a lookup per URL.
  • Text extraction. Turn the HTML into the page's main content: keep the article, drop the menus, footers and ads. Common Crawl's own WET text keeps everything; dedicated extractors (trafilatura, resiliparse, jusText) guess which blocks are the content. This is the most expensive cheap stage, because it parses every page.
  • Language identification. A small classifier (usually fastText's lid.176, a bag-of-character-n-grams model) gives a probability per language; FineWeb keeps pages whose English probability is at least 0.65.
  • Heuristic filters. Hand-written rules on simple statistics: too few or too many words, mean word length outside 3 to 10 characters, too many symbols, too few common words such as "the" and "and" (Gopher's rules); lines must end in punctuation (C4); too many short lines or duplicated lines (FineWeb's own three rules, found by comparing statistics of good and bad data).
  • PII scrubbing. Replace personal data with placeholders. FineWeb anonymises email addresses and public IP addresses with regular expressions and stops there; names, phone numbers and addresses need heavier tools (NER models such as Presidio) and are rarely removed from web corpora.

What the evidence says about each stage. Extraction is the stage with the largest single published effect. On DCLM's 1.4B-parameter benchmark, models trained on WET text scored 20.7 Core against 24.1 for resiliparse and 24.5 for trafilatura; WET documents are about twice as long on average (6,580 characters against 3,227 and 2,901), the difference being menus and boilerplate (DCLM, Tables 3 and 16, self-reported ablation). FineWeb found the same with its own models. The cost differs a lot: resiliparse processed 4.55 MB of WARC per second per CPU core against trafilatura's 0.56, about 8 times faster for nearly the same score (same table). Heuristic filters are worth less but are cheap: C4's terminal-punctuation rule gave the largest HellaSwag boost of C4's rules in FineWeb's tests but removed about 30% of tokens; FineWeb's own three rules removed about 22% and added about 1% to the aggregate score (FineWeb paper, Sections 3.5 and 3.6, self-reported).

4. Deduplication: finding copies among billions of documents

The problem. The web repeats itself: syndicated news, mirrors, templates, boilerplate legal text, the same page crawled in many months. Duplicates hurt in three ways. They waste compute on text the model has seen. They make the model memorise (reproduce training text verbatim): a 1.5B model trained on C4 emitted memorised text in over 1% of its unprompted output, and training on a deduplicated C4 cut that tenfold (1.926% to 0.189% of generated tokens; Lee et al. 2021, Table 4). And they leak test sets, since a benchmark question copied across many pages is likely to survive in some copy. The accuracy effect is smaller than people expect: Muennighoff et al. found that repeating data up to about 4 times is almost as good as fresh data, and in their 84-billion-token experiments deduplication did not improve downstream scores (Scaling Data-Constrained LMs). Deduplication is mostly about not wasting the token budget and not memorising.

Exact duplicates are found by hashing: compute a fingerprint (a hash, such as xxhash or SHA-1) of each document, URL, paragraph or line, and keep one of each. C4 went further and removed every three-sentence span that occurred more than once. Exact dedup misses the common case: two copies of an article that differ in a date line or a footer.

Near duplicates: MinHash and LSH, from zero.

  1. Shingles. Represent a document as the set of its word n-grams (FineWeb uses 5-grams): "renew your library card online", "your library card online by", and so on.
  2. Jaccard similarity. Two documents' similarity is the size of the intersection of their shingle sets divided by the size of the union. Comparing every pair of billions of documents is impossible, so we estimate it.
  3. The min-hash trick. Hash every shingle with a random hash function and keep the smallest value. Two documents get the same minimum with probability exactly equal to their Jaccard similarity (the minimum is equally likely to be any shingle of the union, and they agree only if it is a shared one). Repeat with k independent hash functions: the fraction of equal minimums estimates the similarity, and each document is now a short signature of k numbers.
  4. Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH). Split the signature into b bands of r rows. Two documents become a candidate pair if all r values agree in at least one band, which is found by grouping documents by the hash of each band, with no pairwise comparison. The probability of becoming candidates is 1 − (1 − sr)b for similarity s: an S-shaped curve whose steep part sits near the threshold you want.

The LSH S-curve, and two published settings it reproduces

derived from the formula, and it lands on the published values (dots) independently: FineWeb states 56%, 77%, 92% and 98.8% at similarity 0.7, 0.75, 0.8 and 0.85 (FineWeb, Appendix); RefinedWeb states 76% and 99.4% at 0.75 and 0.8 (RefinedWeb, Appendix G.3.1). RefinedWeb's main text says "20 buckets of 450 hashes", but its own formula names the rows b = 20 and the bands r = 450 (Lee et al.'s convention, the reverse of the usual one), and only 450 bands of 20 reproduce its probabilities: read as written, the setting would match almost nothing. The Dedup lab has sliders for bands and rows over measured pairs from the running example.

Exact substrings with suffix arrays. MinHash compares whole documents; it misses a 300-word disclaimer pasted into a million otherwise different pages. A suffix array lists every position of the concatenated corpus sorted by the text that follows it, so identical spans end up next to each other and every span repeated anywhere can be found in one pass. Lee et al. remove any repeated span of 50 tokens or more; RefinedWeb ran this after MinHash. Its cost is memory and disk: the index is several times the size of the text, which is why it is run per shard or skipped at the largest scales.

Bloom filters. A Bloom filter is a bit array plus k hash functions: to add an item, set the k bits it hashes to; to test an item, check whether all k are set. It never misses an item it has seen but sometimes says "seen" wrongly, with probability about (1 − e−kn/m)k for n items in m bits. Its appeal is a fixed, small memory for exact dedup of URLs, documents or paragraphs in one streaming pass: Dolma's toolkit uses it for all three and for decontamination (seed the filter with test items, flag anything that hits); DCLM used a modified Bloom filter for near duplicates because it "scales more easily to datasets surpassing 10TB" than MinHash (DCLM, Section 4.3).

Semantic dedup. Embed every document with a small encoder, cluster the vectors, and drop near neighbours within a cluster (SemDeDup, D4, NeMo Curator's GPU version). It removes paraphrases that share few n-grams. On DCLM's benchmark, SemDedup used as the selection step scored 27.1 Core against 27.5 for no model-based selection (the chart in section 1): it is not a quality filter. It is used more for synthetic and instruction data, where paraphrase-level redundancy is the problem. Embedding-based similarity is the same machinery as dense retrieval: Topic: rag-and-retrieval.

FineWeb's surprise: deduplicating across all crawls kept the worst copies

The oldest crawl (2013-48) after global dedup, from the FineWeb paper (Section 3.4), self-reported. Dedup ran from the newest crawl to the oldest, so every page that also appeared later was removed from 2013-48: 90% of it went. Trained separately, the 10% that remained (about 31 billion tokens) scored worse than the removed 90% (about 460 billion tokens, 171 billion after deduplicating them on their own). The pages that never reappear in later crawls were disproportionately ads and keyword lists. FineWeb therefore deduplicated each crawl on its own; lighter global passes on top (by URL, by line) all made scores worse.

What this does and does not show: it shows that at FineWeb's ablation scale (1.71B models, 350B tokens), cross-crawl duplicates of good pages were worth more than they cost, and that "what survived global dedup" is a biased sample. It does not show that duplicates are good in general; FineWeb itself notes that the per-crawl version still leaves some documents repeated many times. The Nemotron-CC team, by contrast, deduplicated globally and got good results with a different pipeline, so the finding is about an interaction, not a law. A better framing that several corpora adopted: dedup tells you the counts, mixing decides the setting. TxT360 deduplicates globally but keeps each document's duplicate count, so you can upsample documents that the web repeated, deliberately.

5. Quality models: deciding which documents are worth training on

The problem. After extraction, rules and dedup, most of what remains is grammatical, deduplicated and still not worth much: product listings, forum chatter, thin SEO pages. Hand-written rules cannot say "this teaches something". Every modern corpus therefore runs a quality model: a model that gives each document a score, and a threshold that keeps the top slice. "Quality" has no definition beyond what the model was trained to prefer, so the useful question is always quality according to what. Three recipes are in use.

1. Reference against crawl

Train a binary classifier to tell a reference set you like from random crawl text, and keep the documents that look most like the reference. DCLM's reference is instruction data (OpenHermes 2.5) plus highly voted r/ExplainLikeImFive answers, its negatives random pages; it keeps the top 10%. The classifier is fastText: each word and word pair gets a learned vector, a document is the average of its vectors, and a linear layer scores it. It trains in minutes and scores on CPUs, which matters when the input is trillions of tokens.

2. An LLM grades a sample, a small model learns the grades

FineWeb-Edu: Llama-3-70B-Instruct scored 460,000 pages from 0 to 5 for educational value (with a rubric aimed at grade-school to middle-school knowledge); a linear regression head on a frozen small encoder (Snowflake-arctic-embed-m) learned to predict the score (F1 82% at threshold 3), then scored all 15 trillion tokens in 6,000 H100 GPU hours (FineWeb paper, Section 4). Threshold 3 keeps 1.3 trillion tokens; threshold 2 keeps 5.4 trillion.

3. Perplexity under a reference model

CCNet's approach: train a small n-gram language model on Wikipedia and keep the documents it finds least surprising (low perplexity). Cheap and still used as a feature, but it prefers text that looks like Wikipedia. In DCLM's comparison it scored 29.0 Core against fastText's 30.2 (section 1's chart).

A filter is also a choice of whose writing the model learns from.

Keep the top or keep more and fix it. FineWeb-Edu and DCLM remove about 90% of their input. That is fine for a run of a few hundred billion tokens and a problem for 15 trillion, where a small corpus must be repeated many times. Nemotron-CC's answer: score with an ensemble of three classifiers, sort documents into 20 score buckets, merge them into 5 quality levels (ranked by short annealing runs, section 7), keep the high levels as they are, and have an LLM rephrase the low ones instead of dropping them (Nemotron-CC, arXiv 2412.02595). In NVIDIA's 8B-parameter, 1-trillion-token runs, its high-quality slice beat DCLM by 5.6 MMLU points, and the full 6.3 trillion tokens matched DCLM with four times as many unique real tokens; an 8B model trained for 15 trillion tokens on a weighted version reached 70.3 MMLU against 65.3 for Llama 3.1 8B under the same evaluation harness (self-reported, NVIDIA's own runs). The trade-off is the topic's central design decision: per-token quality against how many tokens you have before you start repeating.

6. Decontamination: keeping the test out of the training set

The problem. Benchmarks are on the web: questions are copied into forums, study sites and GitHub repositories, and end up in the crawl. A model that trained on a test item can score well by remembering it, and the score then measures memory instead of ability. Decontamination removes training documents (or spans) that overlap the test sets you will report. It belongs to both sides of the field: this section is the training-data side; how to detect contamination in a model you did not train is in Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges and Topic: benchmarks.

How it works. Almost everyone uses n-gram overlap: split test items and training documents into runs of n words or tokens and look for shared runs. GPT-3 used 13-grams. Llama 3 and Tulu 3 use 8-grams; Tulu 3's rule is precise enough to reuse: a test item counts as matched by a training example if more than half of its tokens fall inside an 8-gram shared with that one example, and a whole training dataset was dropped or cleaned if it matched more than 2% of any evaluation's items (Tulu 3, Section 3.2). DCLM looked for pages containing a question together with one of its answer options. A Bloom filter seeded with every test n-gram (the Dolma toolkit's approach) does the lookup in one streaming pass.

What the evidence says. DCLM removed the MMLU and HellaSwag overlaps it detected from DCLM-baseline and retrained a 7B model: scores did not fall (MMLU 51.8 before, 52.7 after; HellaSwag 77.9 and 78.4; DCLM, Table 7), so its gains were not from leaked items. Tulu 3 found that several popular public instruction datasets overlapped its evaluations, and dropped each one or released a version with the overlapping examples removed. Paraphrases defeat n-grams: a rewritten or translated item shares few runs, so post-training sets built by distillation (a teacher that memorised GSM8K writes "new" problems that are paraphrases) need embedding similarity or an LLM check on top.

7. Mixing and staging: how much of each kind, and when

A pretraining corpus is a mixture of domains: web text, code, math, scientific papers, books, encyclopedias, multilingual text, synthetic data. The mixture weights say what fraction of the training tokens each domain supplies. At a fixed budget every point of code is a point not spent on web text, so the weights move abilities directly: Llama 3's final mix was roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual tokens (Llama 3, Section 3.1.2). Taking each domain in proportion to its size (its "natural" share) is rarely right: small, valuable domains get drowned. Multilingual models usually sample domain i with probability proportional to ni1/T (a temperature T above 1 flattens the mix toward small languages).

How many times can a small domain repeat? Upsampling means repeating. In the T5 experiments, repeating a truncated C4 64 times cost almost nothing, 256 times a little, and 1,024 times about 4 GLUE points and 4.6 SQuAD points, with training loss falling as the data shrank, the signature of memorisation (T5, Table 9). At larger scale the usable number is much smaller: Muennighoff et al. found up to about 4 epochs nearly as good as fresh data and meaningful gains up to about 16, after which extra repeats are worth almost nothing (arXiv 2305.16264).

Choosing weights without paying for the big run. All the methods train small proxy models and transfer what they learn. DoReMi trains a 280M proxy with Group DRO (it upweights the domains where the proxy lags a reference model most) and reads the domain weights off it; an 8B model trained with them gained 6.5 points of average few-shot accuracy over The Pile's default weights and reached the baseline 2.6 times faster (DoReMi, self-reported). Data Mixing Laws fit loss as a function of the mixture proportions and extrapolate (arXiv 2403.16952). RegMix trains 512 tiny models (1M parameters, 1 billion tokens each) on random mixtures, fits a regression from mixture to loss, and picks the best predicted mixture; it matched or beat DoReMi with 10% of the compute and found web text, not Wikipedia, the domain most correlated with downstream scores (RegMix, self-reported). The sobering check: Aioli found that no existing method consistently beat simple stratified sampling (equal weight per domain) on average test perplexity (Aioli, third-party for the methods it tests). Optimal weights also shift with model size and token budget, so copying a published mix is a starting point, not an answer.

Staging: the mix changes during training. Modern recipes use a long first stage on broad data and a short final stage, called mid-training or annealing, on the best data (math, code, instruction-like and question-answer text, often synthetic), run while the learning rate decays to zero. Sometimes a long-context stage follows. The usual explanation is that scarce high-quality tokens are not diluted across trillions of others, and that what the model sees at a low learning rate is what it keeps; it is an explanation, not a proven mechanism. The Dolmino mix was about half high-quality web (DCLM's fastText top 7% that FineWeb-Edu also scores 2 or more) plus instruction data, papers, encyclopedic text and math, partly synthetic (Table 13 of the same paper). GSM8K, a grade-school math test, goes from 24.1 to 67.5. Annealing is also the cheapest way to evaluate a dataset: take a half-trained model, decay its learning rate over a short run where the candidate dataset is a fixed share (Llama 3 used 40 billion tokens with 30% candidate data), and compare against the same run without it. Nemotron-CC ranked its quality buckets this way.

Continued pretraining: how much general data to keep. Continued pretraining (CPT) trains an existing model further on a domain (finance, law, a new language). Without some general data mixed back in (replay), it forgets. The CMR scaling law makes the ratio predictable: during CPT, general and domain loss each follow a power law in the domain share and the tokens trained, so a few short probe runs fit the curves, and the critical mixture ratio is the largest domain-data share that does not raise general loss beyond a tolerance. Predicted values grew with model size, 29.8%, 34.9%, 41.4% and 47.8% domain data for 460M, 940M, 1.6B and 3.1B models on finance, and were higher for a domain closer to the general data (36.7% for academic papers against 29.8% for finance at 460M; Gu et al., arXiv 2407.17467). It is the method Khalid has used at work to set this ratio. The mechanics of CPT itself are on Continued Pretraining (CPT).

Predict: you have 20 billion tokens of high-quality math and a 2-trillion-token run. Which uses it best?
The published recipes concentrate it at the end (OLMo 2, Llama 3, SmolLM2), where 20B tokens can be a quarter or more of the final stage instead of 1% of every batch, and the annealing evidence above shows the gains. Spreading it is not wrong (it still helps), and repeating it up to about 4 times across stages is cheap. What to avoid is placing it early only, where later training dilutes it.
Go deeper. The Mixing lab has a mixture calculator (domain sizes, weights, epochs, temperature), the same token budget before and after a mid-training stage, and a RegMix-style experiment on tiny proxy models from the running example.

8. Synthetic pretraining data: when the crawl runs out

The problem. The web is finite and the best of it is small: FineWeb-Edu's strict cut is 1.3 trillion tokens, and frontier runs train on 15 trillion or more. Once the token budget outgrows the good data, the choice is to repeat it (section 7: cheap up to about 4 times), keep lower-quality text, or manufacture tokens with a language model. Two lineages exist.

Generate from scratch

Prompt a model to write textbooks, exercises and stories. Microsoft's Phi models showed small models trained this way score well above their size (Textbooks Are All You Need); the common criticism is a narrow style and skills shaped toward benchmarks. The open replication, Cosmopedia, has over 30 million documents written by Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, each prompt seeded with a real web page or curriculum topic, because diversity, not generation, is the hard part: without seeds, the model writes the same textbook again and again (Cosmopedia dataset card).

Rephrase real documents

Ask a model to rewrite an existing web page in a cleaner form (an encyclopedia-style article, question and answer pairs, a dialogue). The information and its diversity come from the real page; the model fixes the form. WRAP reported reaching the same quality with 5 times less data or 3 times less compute at its scale (arXiv 2401.16380); Nemotron-CC generated 1.9 trillion tokens this way with Mistral NeMo 12B, 336 billion from rewriting low-quality pages and 1.5 trillion of question-answer and other variants of high-quality ones; BeyondWeb (DatologyAI, a data-curation vendor) reports beating Cosmopedia by up to 5.1 points on a 14-benchmark average (self-reported). Synthetic text is now a standard ingredient: 57.5% of Olmo 3's 100-billion-token mid-training mix is labelled synthetic math, code, question-answer, reasoning-trace and instruction text (derived: 100% minus the four non-synthetic rows of the card's table) (Dolma 3 Dolmino card).

Model collapse. Shumailov et al. showed that models trained recursively on their own output lose the tails of the distribution: rare things become rarer each generation until the model produces a narrow caricature of the original data (Nature, 2024). The experiment assumes each generation replaces the previous data. Gerstgrasser et al. repeated it with each generation's synthetic data added to the real data instead, and collapse disappeared across model sizes and architectures (arXiv 2404.01413). The simplest case shows the mechanism:

illustrative simulation run in your browser (seeded, so it is the same every time): generation 0 is 20 samples from a standard normal distribution; each generation fits a mean and standard deviation (maximum likelihood) and draws 20 new samples from the fit. Lines are the median over 400 runs of the fitted standard deviation, the width of what the "model" can produce. This is the one-dimensional case Shumailov et al. analyse, not a language model.

Real pipelines avoid collapse by construction: synthetic data is added to real data, grounded in real documents (rephrasing), and filtered. The live risks are milder: a uniform style, the generator's biases, and a web that increasingly contains unlabelled model-written text, so every crawl after 2023 already holds some synthetic data nobody chose to add.

9. Post-training data: instructions, preferences, checkers and environments

After pretraining, a model is trained on much smaller, more structured data. Each stage of post-training (explained on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR) consumes a different shape of example, and the history of the field is the shape getting richer: documents, then pairs, then problems with checkers, then whole environments.

10. Storage, loading and the tools that run it

How the data is stored. The pipeline and the training run want different formats.

  • While curating: one document per record with its metadata (URL, crawl, language score, quality score, duplicate-cluster id), in compressed JSONL (one JSON object per line, gzip or zstd) or Parquet (a columnar format: a reader can load only the columns it needs, and per-column compression is good). FineWeb and FineWeb-Edu ship as Parquet and DCLM-baseline as zstd-compressed JSONL; datatrove and the Dolma toolkit read and write both.
  • For training: documents are tokenized once and stored as flat arrays of token ids (2 bytes per token when the vocabulary has fewer than 65,536 entries, 4 bytes otherwise), memory-mapped so a data-loader worker reads any slice without loading the file, or packed into streaming shards (MosaicML Streaming's MDS, WebDataset's tar files) that workers fetch from object storage and can resume deterministically after a crash.
  • The loader samples from per-domain shards with the mixture weights of section 7, concatenates documents separated by an end-of-text token, and cuts the stream into fixed-length sequences (packing). Shuffling is approximate at this scale: shuffle the order of shards, then shuffle inside a buffer.

Two consequences matter in practice. When the mixing weights are applied at load time rather than baked into the files (both are common), changing the mix needs no reprocessing. And the token count you plan with depends on the tokenizer: the same text is a different number of tokens for GPT-2, Llama or Qwen tokenizers, which is why every corpus size in this page says whose count it is (tokenizers: Tokenizers).

The tools. Three open frameworks run whole pipelines; teams with existing big-data infrastructure also write their own stages on Spark or Ray.

11. Choosing: what to do for the run in front of you

A small pretrain (up to about 2B parameters, a few hundred billion tokens)Use an existing corpus: FineWeb-Edu at threshold 3 (1.3 trillion tokens, so nothing repeats), or SmolLM2's 60/40 mix of FineWeb-Edu and DCLM-baseline if commonsense and everyday-language benchmarks matter. Add a few percent of code and math, mostly in the last stage. Your remaining jobs are decontamination against your own evaluations and ablating any change on a small proxy.SmolLM2 trained its 135M and 360M models on 2 and 4 trillion tokens: small models are routinely trained far past the compute-optimal point.
A long run (trillions of tokens)The binding constraint is unique good tokens. Do not keep only the top 10%: bucket by quality, keep the middle tiers for bulk, rephrase the low ones, add PDFs and code, repeat the best data up to about 4 times, and save the best for a mid-training stage (Nemotron-CC, Dolma 3).
Continued pretraining on a domainMix general data back in; fit the replay ratio with a few short probe runs (CMR, section 7) instead of folklore; re-fit when the base model or the budget changes.
Your own pipeline from raw crawlStart from datatrove's FineWeb example (WARC, trafilatura, language ID, Gopher, C4 and FineWeb rules, MinHash per crawl, PII), check its thresholds against the paper (and swap in resiliparse, which datatrove v0.10.1 does not ship as an extractor, if extraction cost dominates), then ablate one change at a time on small proxies (FineWeb used 1.71B models on 28B tokens).
Post-training dataStart from an open mixture (Tulu 3, SmolTalk) for coverage; add targeted data for your weak skills; generate preference pairs on-policy; keep only RLVR prompts your model sometimes solves; decontaminate every source.
A new languagePer-language pipelines and thresholds (FineWeb2 tunes them per language), language ID with a model that covers it well, and temperature sampling so small languages are not drowned.

Common mistakes

Training on Common Crawl's WET text
It keeps menus and footers, documents are about twice as long as the real content, and models score worse (DCLM Table 3: 20.7 against 24.1 Core). Re-extract from WARC.
Copying a paper's thresholds without reading the code
FineWeb's paper says 0.1 for the duplicated-line rule; datatrove's default is 0.01. RefinedWeb's prose says 20 buckets of 450 hashes; its formula means 450 of 20. Run the released code on a sample and read what it drops.
Reading an ablation without its setup
Check the model size, tokens, benchmarks and number of seeds before believing a gap. Small proxies can only use benchmarks that already move at small scale (FineWeb chose its eight for low seed variance and early signal), so effects on harder skills go unmeasured; without a reported seed-to-seed spread, a one-point gap may be noise; and a dataset's makers choose the benchmarks it is evaluated on.
Assuming more dedup is always better
FineWeb's global dedup kept the worst copies of the oldest crawl; Muennighoff et al. saw no downstream gain from dedup in their setting. Deduplicate to save compute and avoid memorisation, then decide how much to repeat on purpose.
Treating a quality score as ground truth
Each classifier encodes its reference set's taste: FineWeb-Edu wins knowledge benchmarks and loses everyday language, and fits Twitter, Reddit and African-American English text worse. Read a stratified sample of what it drops.
Decontaminating too little, too much or too late
Too little: 13-grams miss paraphrases. Too much: short n-grams remove ordinary sentences. Too late: decontaminate before training against every evaluation you will report, and record what you removed.
Comparing token counts from different tokenizers
C4 is 175B tokens in the Dolma paper's count; The Pile is 387B there and about 300B in GPT-NeoX tokens. Say whose tokens, or compare bytes.
Over-filtering for a long run, or under-filtering for a short one
A strict cut is right when you will not exhaust it and wrong when you would repeat it 10 times; the calculator below shows when that happens.
Reading the CMR ratio as the share of general data
The critical mixture ratio is the largest share of domain data (29.8% to 47.8% in the paper's finance runs), not the general share. The old version of this page had it backwards.

How does MinHash LSH find near duplicates, and how do you choose bands and rows?
Shingle documents into word n-grams; for each of k hash functions keep the minimum hash, which matches between two documents with probability equal to their Jaccard similarity; split the k values into b bands of r rows and call two documents candidates if any band matches entirely. The probability is 1 − (1 − sr)b; choose b and r so the steep part sits at your threshold (FineWeb's 14 x 8 catches 77% of pairs at 0.75 and 98.8% at 0.85). More hashes sharpen the curve and cost compute and memory.
Exact, fuzzy, substring and semantic dedup: when each?
Exact hashes (documents, URLs, lines) always, they are nearly free. MinHash for near-duplicate documents at web scale. Suffix arrays for repeated spans inside otherwise different documents (boilerplate, licences), at a memory cost. Semantic dedup (embeddings) for paraphrases, mostly in synthetic and instruction data; as a web filter it did not beat no filtering in DCLM's comparison.
Why did deduplicating across all crawls hurt FineWeb?
Running from newest to oldest, global dedup removed every page of the oldest crawl that reappeared later, which were mostly the good, persistent pages; what remained was disproportionately ads and keyword lists, and a model trained on it scored worse than one trained on the removed part. Per-crawl dedup kept those repeated good pages a few times.
How do you know whether a new dataset helps?
Ablate: train identical small proxies with and without it at a fixed budget, on benchmarks with low seed variance; or anneal a partly trained model on a short run where the dataset is a fixed share (Llama 3: 30% of 40B tokens) and compare against the same run without it.
How would you build and validate a quality classifier?
Pick the target (reference text you want more of, or an LLM rubric), label a sample (positives and random crawl negatives, or LLM grades), train a cheap model (fastText, or a head on a small encoder), choose the threshold by ablation, then audit: a stratified sample of kept and dropped documents labelled by hand, agreement with another classifier, and which topics, formats and dialects it drops.
How do you decontaminate, and what can go wrong?
n-gram overlap between training data and every evaluation you report (8-grams with a coverage rule, as Tulu 3), plus embedding or LLM checks for paraphrases in synthetic data; remove matching documents or spans, log them, and verify scores do not depend on what was removed (DCLM retrained without the overlaps). Failure modes: n-grams too long (miss paraphrases), too short (remove ordinary text), forgetting new evaluations.
How do you set the general-to-domain ratio for continued pretraining?
Run a few short probes at different domain shares, fit power laws for general and domain loss in share and tokens, and take the largest domain share that keeps general loss within a tolerance (the CMR scaling law); larger models and domains closer to the general data tolerate more domain data.
Is training on synthetic data dangerous?
Collapse appears when each generation replaces real data with model output; accumulating synthetic data alongside real data avoids it (Gerstgrasser et al.). Grounding synthetic text in real documents (rephrasing) keeps diversity; filter and decontaminate it like any other source, and watch for a uniform style and the generator's biases.
What makes a good RLVR prompt set?
Answers a program can check exactly (numbers, unit tests, constraints), correct keys (a wrong key is pure reward noise), difficulty matched to the model (drop prompts it always or never solves, since group-normalised methods such as GRPO get no gradient from them), diversity across skills, and decontamination against the evaluations.

Storage and loading: from documents to what the trainer reads

A finished corpus is stored twice. First as documents (text plus metadata) in Parquet or compressed JSONL, so it can be inspected, filtered again and published. Then, after tokenization, as token shards: one flat array of token ids per file plus an index of where each document ends, which the training loader memory-maps and cuts into fixed-length windows. The second form is what a pretraining run actually reads.

Packing: the same documents, padded or packed

A context window is the fixed number of tokens per training sample. Give each document its own window and the short ones are mostly padding (wasted compute); pack them end to end with an end-of-document token and almost every position is real text, at the cost of windows that span document boundaries. Interview questions

You must build a 10T-token English web corpus in two months with 2,000 CPU cores and 64 GPUs. Which tools, and where do the GPUs go?
CPU stages run on everything, cheapest first: WARC reading, extraction (resiliparse rather than trafilatura at this scale: DCLM measured about 8 times the speed with similar downstream scores), lid.176, heuristic rules, and MinHash within each crawl, all as datatrove tasks on the CPU cluster. GPUs only score what survives: a FineWeb-Edu-style classifier or an ensemble, and optionally rephrasing. Then tokenize to .bin shards. Say what you would measure first: drop counts per stage on one crawl before running all of them.
Why would a team keep the Dolma toolkit's tag-then-mix design rather than inline filters?
Tagging writes every score once; changing a threshold then only reruns the cheap mixer, not the expensive taggers. Inline filters (datatrove's default) are simpler and stream, but a new threshold means a new pass (unless the filters write their scores to metadata). At web scale the ability to re-mix without re-tagging is worth the extra storage for attribute files.
Exact Bloom-filter dedup and MinHash disagree on the toy near copy. Which is right?
Neither is wrong; they answer different questions. Paragraph-level Bloom dedup removes the repeated paragraphs and keeps the one new sentence; document-level MinHash removes the whole near copy. For training, a page that differs only in a footer adds almost nothing, so MinHash's choice is usually what you want; paragraph dedup also strips repeated boilerplate inside otherwise unique pages. Many pipelines do both.
Your vocabulary has 151,000 tokens. What changes in storage?
Token ids no longer fit in 16 bits (65,536 values), so shards need uint32: twice the bytes per token. datatrove's DocumentTokenizer has token_size 2 by default; set it to 4. A silent uint16 write would wrap ids and corrupt training data without an error.
Name a tool on this map that is archived but still everywhere, and say why that is fine or not.
fastText: the repository is read-only since 2024, yet lid.176 still runs language ID for most English corpora. Fine for inference of a frozen model with a pinned wheel; not fine if you need fixes or new platforms (the community forks such as fasttext-numpy2 exist for exactly that). Same pattern for the suffix-array code: the method lives on in datatrove and Dolma 3's tools.

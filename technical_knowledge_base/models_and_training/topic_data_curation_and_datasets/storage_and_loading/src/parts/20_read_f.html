14. Common mistakes

Writing token ids with the wrong width
uint16 for a vocabulary over 65,536 wraps ids silently (section 5: 97% of our documents corrupted with Qwen3). Check max(ids) < vocab on every shard and decode a sample after tokenizing.
Comparing formats at default compression levels
pyarrow's zstd default is level 1, most zstd CLIs and libraries default to 3: the root's "Parquet is 10% larger" was a level mismatch, not a format property (section 3).
Shuffling only within shards, or with a buffer much smaller than a shard
Pipeline output is sorted by source; a buffer of B examples mixes only about B examples. Shuffle the shard order, use a buffer that spans several shards, or shuffle offline or by global index (section 9).
An IterableDataset that ignores which worker it runs in
Every worker yields the whole stream: 4 workers, 4 copies (section 11). Split by get_worker_info() and by rank.
Fewer shards than ranks × workers
Workers sit idle or ranks run out of data at different steps; with datasets 5.0.1 a shuffled streaming dataset even reports a single shard (section 8). Print num_shards after every transformation.
Resuming by restarting the epoch
Same seed: the first k batches are trained twice. New seed: the first k come back and others are never seen before the epoch's budget ends (section 12: 640 repeats, or 463 repeats and 463 never seen). Save samples consumed with every checkpoint.
Assuming a streaming shuffle buffer survives a restart
It usually does not: the samples in the buffer are skipped (section 12). Either accept and log it, or use an index-based loader for pretraining.
Random offsets "because it is simpler"
nanoGPT-style random windows have no epochs: after one file's worth of samples about 37% of tokens were never seen and others were seen twice. Fine for a toy, not for a run whose data budget is planned in epochs.
Ignoring document boundaries without deciding to
Concatenation lets tokens attend across unrelated documents and cuts many documents in two. That may be acceptable at short context; decide it, and measure the share of cross-document attention and cut documents for your lengths (section 7).
Tokenizing inside the training loop
It costs CPU on every training node for the whole run, makes throughput depend on document lengths, and ties the run to a tokenizer version. Tokenize once, offline, and record the tokenizer revision with the shards.
Not keeping the documents
Token files cannot be re-filtered, re-tokenized or decontaminated against a new benchmark without the text and metadata. Keep the compressed documents (about as large as the token files) beside them.

15. Interview questions

Design the data storage and loading for a 2,048-GPU pretraining run of a 70B model on 15T tokens, data in S3.
Clarify: vocabulary size (uint16 or uint32), context length, number of domains and whether weights change during the run, how often the job restarts. Storage: keep curated documents as zstd Parquet with metadata (re-filtering, decontamination, audits); tokenize once on a CPU cluster into token shards with document-end indices, one set per domain, tokenizer revision recorded; 15T × 4 bytes = 60 TB of ids, about the same again for documents. Getting it to the GPUs: 2,048 H100s at 40% MFU on 70B is about 2,048 × 0.4 × 989.5 × 1012 ÷ (6 × 70 × 109) ≈ 1.9M tokens/s, under 8 MB/s of ids for the whole job: either copy to a parallel file system or local NVMe before training, or stream with a local cache (MosaicML Streaming) with prefetch. Order: index-based (Megatron-style document, sample and shuffle indices, plus blending indices for the domain weights) built once and cached, so each rank reads its slice by sample number. Packing: concatenation or best-fit packing, with a document mask if long-context quality matters. Resumption: save consumed samples and the data config hash with every checkpoint; test exact resume in CI. Failure modes to name: wrong id width, too few shards, buffer-only shuffles, a changed tokenizer between shards.
Why store a tokenized corpus at all, instead of tokenizing on the fly?
Tokenizing costs about 0.5M tokens per core-second (measured here, rough): cheap once, expensive on every training node for every epoch. Token files allow random access by sample number, which makes global shuffling and exact resumption trivial. On-the-fly tokenization makes sense for small fine-tuning sets or when the tokenizer is still changing.
Your loss curve has a sawtooth with a period of a few thousand steps. What do you check first?
Data order. A period suggests shards or sources being read one after another: check shard-order shuffling, the buffer size against shard size, whether each DataLoader worker reads a single source, and whether the mixture sampler resets each epoch.
Packing versus padding versus best-fit packing: what do you lose with each?
Padding wastes compute (67.6% at 2,048 tokens on our documents). Concatenation wastes almost nothing but cuts documents (605 of 2,000) and mixes unrelated documents in attention unless masked. Best-fit packing keeps almost all of concatenation's efficiency (one extra window in 697) and cuts only documents longer than the window; it needs a document mask to keep documents apart, and an offline packing pass.
Parquet or JSONL for a published corpus?
Parquet if people will query metadata or read a subset of columns, and if readers will stream it (row groups give natural units); JSONL with zstd if simplicity and line-level robustness matter more. At the same zstd level the sizes are within a few percent (section 3). Either way, publish compression levels and the tokenizer you counted tokens with.
How do you make a run resumable to the exact sample?
Make the sample order a pure function of (seed, data configuration, samples consumed): precomputed indices or a stateful sampler, no shuffle buffer whose contents are lost, mixture weights logged per step. Save samples consumed with the model checkpoint; on restart, skip to it. Verify with a stop-and-resume test that compares sample ids.
When does data loading actually become the bottleneck?
Rarely for text pretraining (megabytes per second for thousands of GPUs). It does with online tokenization or heavy per-sample work, images, audio and video, small models on many GPUs if the loader is single-process, cold caches and slow object-store reads without prefetch, and at start-up (index building, file opening on thousands of ranks).

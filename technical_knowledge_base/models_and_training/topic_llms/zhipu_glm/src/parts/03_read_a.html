The bet: one agentic model, built from the open frontier's best parts

Zhipu AI is a 2019 spin-out of Tsinghua University, founded in Beijing by Tang Jie and branded internationally as Z.ai since July 2025; its product is the GLM (General Language Model) family of open-weight models ({{Wikipedia|@wiki}}).

Two corporate facts explain much of what follows. Zhipu listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange on 8 January 2026, the first major Chinese LLM company to go public, so market share is the story being sold, which is the context for the pricing. And the US Commerce Department put it on the Entity List in January 2025, which is why GLM is now trained and served on Chinese-made accelerators ({{Wikipedia|@wiki}}).

Three bets run through every release since GLM-4.5 (July 2025):

  • One checkpoint for the whole agentic workload. Zhipu calls the brief ARC: one model good at agentic, reasoning and coding work at once (ARC).
  • Adopt published mechanisms quickly, then engineer the bill around them. Where DeepSeek and Moonshot invent new layers, GLM's architecture history is mostly other labs' ideas taken up well: DeepSeek's loss-free expert balancing, sigmoid routing and multi-token prediction in GLM-4.5 ({{GLM-4.5 report|@r45}}), DeepSeek's MLA and sparse attention in GLM-5 ({{GLM-5 report|@r5}}), Moonshot's Muon optimizer from GLM-4.5 on, and in GLM-5.3-Flash Moonshot's Kimi Delta Attention and DeepSeek's mHC residual streams ({{Flash config|@cfgfl}}). Zhipu's own contributions sit around those pieces and are mostly about cost: the model's shape, its head count, two changes to the sparse-attention indexer (IndexShare and IndexPool) and the RL system slime.
  • Open weights at low prices. Frontier-class weights under permissive licences, a hosted API that undercuts rivals, and a coding subscription that plugs into existing agent harnesses (Pricing).

The question this page keeps returning to: what does it cost to train and serve an agentic, million-token model, and which of Zhipu's choices moves that cost? Two cautions when reading GLM numbers: many headline benchmark figures are Zhipu's own runs (Z.ai Code Bench is in-house), and arena placings are human preference votes, not task success.

ARC: what the design brief actually commits to

ARC is Zhipu's name for building one model that is good at three things that usually pull apart ({{GLM-4.5 report|@r45}}): agentic behaviour (long-horizon tool use, browsing, staying coherent over hundreds of steps), reasoning (deliberate multi-step problem solving), and coding. It is a design brief and not marketing because the three conflict in post-training. Heavy reasoning training teaches a model to deliberate before answering, which costs latency and can degrade instruction following; agentic training rewards short, decisive tool calls rather than long internal monologue; coding wants exact, formatted, non-chatty output. The usual industry answer is a separate specialised checkpoint per mode, which is operationally expensive for whoever serves them.

GLM's answer has two parts.

1. Train specialists, then unify, rather than hoping one mixed run balances them. The diagram shows the two recipes Zhipu has published.

GLM-5 runs its RL stages one after another because, in the report's words, "sequentially optimizing for distinct objectives can lead to the cumulative degradation of previously acquired capabilities"; the last stage pulls the model back towards what each earlier stage had taught. For GLM-5.2, slime ran this on-policy distillation in parallel and merged more than ten expert models into the final model in about two days ({{GLM-5.2 blog|@b52}}).

2. Select the behaviour per request, not per checkpoint. GLM-4.5 and GLM-4.5-Air shipped hybrid modes: thinking for complex reasoning and agentic tasks, non-thinking for instant replies, chosen through the prompt template rather than by loading a different model ({{GLM-4.5 report|@r45}}). The control has since become an effort dial: GLM-5.2 offers High and Max effort ({{GLM-5.2 blog|@b52}}), and GLM-5.3 and GLM-5.3-Flash take reasoning_effort set to low, high or max, defaulting to max ({{GLM-5.3 card|@c53}}, {{Flash card|@cfl}}); on the hosted Flash API thinking cannot be switched off ({{Z.ai docs|@dfl}}). How thinking budgets and effort levels work in general is on {{Reasoning models and test-time compute|n:3c65c17b0d0d8190b9b4cd0acbcb66df}}.

It buys one deployment covering an entire agentic workload. It costs what Alibaba conceded when it split Qwen3-2507 back into separate Instruct and Thinking checkpoints, the Instruct model supporting only non-thinking mode ({{Qwen3-2507 card|@qwen2507}}; detail on {{Alibaba: Qwen|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fd8c36ce06672bb235}}): a unified model gives up a little at the top end of each mode.

The shape: a mixture of experts, with deliberate choices around it

Every GLM flagship since GLM-4.5 is a mixture of experts (MoE): each feed-forward block is replaced by many small expert networks and a router that sends each token to a few of them, so the model stores far more parameters than any one token uses. GLM-5.2 is 744B total with about 40B active per token; GLM-5.3-Flash is 320B with 18B active, routing each token to 8 of 288 experts plus one shared expert. The ratio, about 1 parameter in 18 or 19 working on each token, is the standard modern bet that quality tracks total parameters while decode speed tracks active ones. The routing mechanism itself is on {{Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models|n:3c65c17b0d0d810080cce6648ac8f2f2}}.

The report and most coverage give 744B for the GLM-5 generation; the Hugging Face checkpoints of GLM-5 to GLM-5.3 hold 753.3 to 753.9B parameters ({{HF org|@hf}}), and some coverage quotes 753B ({{Labellerr|@lab}}). The sources do not reconcile the two counts; the checkpoint includes the MTP layer and the embeddings, which a headline count may leave out.

Dense-first blocks

The first layers are ordinary dense feed-forward layers rather than MoE: three in GLM-4.5, GLM-5 and Flash, one in Air ({{GLM-4.5 report|@r45}}, {{Flash config|@cfgfl}}), a pattern shared with DeepSeek. The usual reasoning, which Zhipu does not spell out: early layers compute generic, low-level features every token needs, so routing them buys little specialisation while adding risk, since routers are least stable early in the network and early in training, and a bad early routing decision propagates through the whole stack. GLM routes with sigmoid gates and loss-free balance routing, a per-expert bias nudged between steps until expert load evens out, instead of an auxiliary balancing loss ({{GLM-4.5 report|@r45}}).

Depth over width, and more attention heads

Against DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi K2, the GLM-4.5 report reduces "the width (hidden dimension and number of routed experts)" and increases the number of layers, "as we found that deeper models exhibited better reasoning capacity". It also uses far more attention heads than the hidden size would conventionally imply: 96 heads of 128 dimensions on a 5,120-wide model. The reported finding is the interesting part: the extra heads do "not improve training loss compared to models with fewer heads", but "consistently" improve reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU and BBH ({{GLM-4.5 report|@r45}}). Loss is a proxy for capability, not the objective, and an architecture choice can buy capability the loss curve does not show.

GLM-4.5's 96 query heads (one square each), coloured by the key-value head they share. Only the 8 key-value heads are cached.

Worked example. The conventional count is 5,120 / 128 = 40 heads; GLM-4.5 has 96, which is 96 / 40 = 2.4 times as many (the report rounds this to "2.5 times"). The query projection then outputs 96 × 128 = 12,288 numbers per token, 2.4 times the hidden size. The cache does not grow with the query heads: grouped-query attention (GQA) lets each group of 12 query heads share one key-value head, so with 8 key-value heads each layer caches 2 × 8 × 128 = 2,048 numbers per token, 4,096 bytes in 16-bit precision, and over 92 layers 92 × 4,096 = 376,832 bytes, 368 KiB per token. At a 128K context (131,072 tokens) that is 46 GiB for one request, the number GLM-5's attention redesign attacks (Attention).

Fewer layers again in GLM-5, for the network's sake. GLM-5 doubled the total to 744B, grew to 256 experts and reduced the layer count "to minimize expert parallelism communication overhead" ({{GLM-5 report|@r5}}): every MoE layer sends tokens to the devices holding their experts and back, so each layer removed saves two all-to-all exchanges per step. Flash goes further: against GLM-4.5's similar total (320B against 355B) it nearly halves both the active parameters (18B against 32B) and the layer count (45 against 92), which Zhipu credits to its architecture changes including mHC, four parallel residual streams mixed by learned constrained matrices ({{Z.ai docs|@dfl}}, {{Flash config|@cfgfl}}; mHC itself is explained on {{DeepSeek|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}).

MTP: the draft model that ships inside the model

Multi-token prediction (MTP) adds lightweight layers trained to predict more than one future token: denser supervision per forward pass during training, and at inference the same layers serve as a built-in draft model for speculative decoding, where a cheap draft proposes several tokens and the main model checks them all in one forward pass. The speedup comes without training and hosting a separate draft model; for a lab whose strategy is price, a free multiple on decode throughput is margin. The acceptance length is the average number of tokens accepted per verification step, roughly tokens produced per expensive forward pass. Speculative decoding in general is on {{Inference techniques|n:3c65c17b0d0d819db53bd2c48909e5b2}}.

  • GLM-4.5 adds one MoE layer as its MTP layer "to support speculative decoding during inference" ({{GLM-4.5 report|@r45}}).
  • GLM-5 shares the parameters of three MTP layers, so training covers several draft steps without the memory of several layers, and reports an acceptance length of 2.76 tokens against 2.55 for DeepSeek-V3.2 ({{GLM-5 report|@r5}}).
  • GLM-5.2 applies IndexShare to the MTP layer as well, reuses the first draft step's KV cache in later steps, and adds rejection sampling: on GLM-5.1's backbone with 7 draft steps the acceptance length rises from 4.56 to 5.47 tokens, 5.47 / 4.56 = 1.20 times ({{GLM-5.2 blog|@b52}}). The changes were measured cumulatively, so the share due to IndexShare alone is not known ({{Raschka|@rasch}}).

Muon-family optimizers

Muon replaces AdamW's per-parameter second-moment rescaling with an update that orthogonalises each weight matrix's momentum (a few Newton-Schulz iterations) before applying it, so the step pushes across the whole spectrum of directions instead of being dominated by a few. GLM has trained with it since GLM-4.5, for every parameter except the word embeddings, biases and RMSNorm weights, with 5 Newton-Schulz steps, momentum 0.95 and the update rescaled to an RMS of 0.2; Zhipu observed faster convergence and tolerance of larger batches ({{GLM-4.5 report|@r45}}). It buys better loss per token and a smaller optimizer state, and costs a few extra matrix products per step and a much shorter safety record than AdamW, which is why Moonshot's QK-Clip work on taming attention-logit growth under Muon ({{Kimi K2 report|@k2}}) matters to everyone adopting it; the optimizer and its maths are on {{Moonshot AI: Kimi|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f5abf5dbdbac821a12}}.

GLM-5 added Muon Split because of MLA: MLA's up-projections stack every head's weights into one matrix, and orthogonalising that matrix as a whole forces all heads to move at one shared scale. Muon Split orthogonalises each head's slice separately, so "projection weights for different attention heads" can "update at different scales", which brought MLA's quality up to that of 8-group GQA in Zhipu's ablation ({{GLM-5 report|@r5}}).

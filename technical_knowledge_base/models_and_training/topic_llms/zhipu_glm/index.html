The bet: one agentic model, built from the open frontier's best parts

Zhipu AI is a 2019 spin-out of Tsinghua University, founded in Beijing by Tang Jie and branded internationally as Z.ai since July 2025; its product is the GLM (General Language Model) family of open-weight models (Wikipedia).

Two corporate facts explain much of what follows. Zhipu listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange on 8 January 2026, the first major Chinese LLM company to go public, so market share is the story being sold, which is the context for the pricing. And the US Commerce Department put it on the Entity List in January 2025, which is why GLM is now trained and served on Chinese-made accelerators (Wikipedia).

Three bets run through every release since GLM-4.5 (July 2025):

  • One checkpoint for the whole agentic workload. Zhipu calls the brief ARC: one model good at agentic, reasoning and coding work at once (ARC).
  • Adopt published mechanisms quickly, then engineer the bill around them. Where DeepSeek and Moonshot invent new layers, GLM's architecture history is mostly other labs' ideas taken up well: DeepSeek's loss-free expert balancing, sigmoid routing and multi-token prediction in GLM-4.5 (GLM-4.5 report), DeepSeek's MLA and sparse attention in GLM-5 (GLM-5 report), Moonshot's Muon optimizer from GLM-4.5 on, and in GLM-5.3-Flash Moonshot's Kimi Delta Attention and DeepSeek's mHC residual streams (Flash config). Zhipu's own contributions sit around those pieces and are mostly about cost: the model's shape, its head count, two changes to the sparse-attention indexer (IndexShare and IndexPool) and the RL system slime.
  • Open weights at low prices. Frontier-class weights under permissive licences, a hosted API that undercuts rivals, and a coding subscription that plugs into existing agent harnesses (Pricing).

The question this page keeps returning to: what does it cost to train and serve an agentic, million-token model, and which of Zhipu's choices moves that cost? Two cautions when reading GLM numbers: many headline benchmark figures are Zhipu's own runs (Z.ai Code Bench is in-house), and arena placings are human preference votes, not task success.

The tabs: Long-context cost computes the cache and attention work of every GLM attention design from its config.json, at any context length; Lineage puts every release on one time axis with its size, licence and sources.

ARC: what the design brief actually commits to

ARC is Zhipu's name for building one model that is good at three things that usually pull apart (GLM-4.5 report): agentic behaviour (long-horizon tool use, browsing, staying coherent over hundreds of steps), reasoning (deliberate multi-step problem solving), and coding. It is a design brief and not marketing because the three conflict in post-training. Heavy reasoning training teaches a model to deliberate before answering, which costs latency and can degrade instruction following; agentic training rewards short, decisive tool calls rather than long internal monologue; coding wants exact, formatted, non-chatty output. The usual industry answer is a separate specialised checkpoint per mode, which is operationally expensive for whoever serves them.

GLM's answer has two parts.

1. Train specialists, then unify, rather than hoping one mixed run balances them. The diagram shows the two recipes Zhipu has published.

GLM-5 runs its RL stages one after another because, in the report's words, "sequentially optimizing for distinct objectives can lead to the cumulative degradation of previously acquired capabilities"; the last stage pulls the model back towards what each earlier stage had taught. For GLM-5.2, slime ran this on-policy distillation in parallel and merged more than ten expert models into the final model in about two days (GLM-5.2 blog).

2. Select the behaviour per request, not per checkpoint. GLM-4.5 and GLM-4.5-Air shipped hybrid modes: thinking for complex reasoning and agentic tasks, non-thinking for instant replies, chosen through the prompt template rather than by loading a different model (GLM-4.5 report). The control has since become an effort dial: GLM-5.2 offers High and Max effort (GLM-5.2 blog), and GLM-5.3 and GLM-5.3-Flash take reasoning_effort set to low, high or max, defaulting to max (GLM-5.3 card, Flash card); on the hosted Flash API thinking cannot be switched off (Z.ai docs). How thinking budgets and effort levels work in general is on Reasoning models and test-time compute.

It buys one deployment covering an entire agentic workload. It costs what Alibaba conceded when it split Qwen3-2507 back into separate Instruct and Thinking checkpoints, the Instruct model supporting only non-thinking mode (Qwen3-2507 card; detail on Alibaba: Qwen): a unified model gives up a little at the top end of each mode.

The shape: a mixture of experts, with deliberate choices around it

Every GLM flagship since GLM-4.5 is a mixture of experts (MoE): each feed-forward block is replaced by many small expert networks and a router that sends each token to a few of them, so the model stores far more parameters than any one token uses. GLM-5.2 is 744B total with about 40B active per token; GLM-5.3-Flash is 320B with 18B active, routing each token to 8 of 288 experts plus one shared expert. The ratio, about 1 parameter in 18 or 19 working on each token, is the standard modern bet that quality tracks total parameters while decode speed tracks active ones. The routing mechanism itself is on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models.

The sources do not reconcile the two counts; the checkpoint includes the MTP layer and the embeddings, which a headline count may leave out.

Dense-first blocks

The first layers are ordinary dense feed-forward layers rather than MoE: three in GLM-4.5, GLM-5 and Flash, one in Air (GLM-4.5 report, Flash config), a pattern shared with DeepSeek. The usual reasoning, which Zhipu does not spell out: early layers compute generic, low-level features every token needs, so routing them buys little specialisation while adding risk, since routers are least stable early in the network and early in training, and a bad early routing decision propagates through the whole stack. GLM routes with sigmoid gates and loss-free balance routing, a per-expert bias nudged between steps until expert load evens out, instead of an auxiliary balancing loss (GLM-4.5 report).

Depth over width, and more attention heads

Against DeepSeek-V3 and Kimi K2, the GLM-4.5 report reduces "the width (hidden dimension and number of routed experts)" and increases the number of layers, "as we found that deeper models exhibited better reasoning capacity". It also uses far more attention heads than the hidden size would conventionally imply: 96 heads of 128 dimensions on a 5,120-wide model. The reported finding is the interesting part: the extra heads do "not improve training loss compared to models with fewer heads", but "consistently" improve reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU and BBH (GLM-4.5 report). Loss is a proxy for capability, not the objective, and an architecture choice can buy capability the loss curve does not show.

GLM-4.5's 96 query heads (one square each), coloured by the key-value head they share. Only the 8 key-value heads are cached.

Worked example. The conventional count is 5,120 / 128 = 40 heads; GLM-4.5 has 96, which is 96 / 40 = 2.4 times as many (the report rounds this to "2.5 times"). The query projection then outputs 96 × 128 = 12,288 numbers per token, 2.4 times the hidden size. The cache does not grow with the query heads: grouped-query attention (GQA) lets each group of 12 query heads share one key-value head, so with 8 key-value heads each layer caches 2 × 8 × 128 = 2,048 numbers per token, 4,096 bytes in 16-bit precision, and over 92 layers 92 × 4,096 = 376,832 bytes, 368 KiB per token. At a 128K context (131,072 tokens) that is 46 GiB for one request, the number GLM-5's attention redesign attacks (Attention).

Fewer layers again in GLM-5, for the network's sake. GLM-5 doubled the total to 744B, grew to 256 experts and reduced the layer count "to minimize expert parallelism communication overhead" (GLM-5 report): every MoE layer sends tokens to the devices holding their experts and back, so each layer removed saves two all-to-all exchanges per step. Flash goes further: against GLM-4.5's similar total (320B against 355B) it nearly halves both the active parameters (18B against 32B) and the layer count (45 against 92), which Zhipu credits to its architecture changes including mHC, four parallel residual streams mixed by learned constrained matrices (Z.ai docs, Flash config; mHC itself is explained on DeepSeek).

MTP: the draft model that ships inside the model

Multi-token prediction (MTP) adds lightweight layers trained to predict more than one future token: denser supervision per forward pass during training, and at inference the same layers serve as a built-in draft model for speculative decoding, where a cheap draft proposes several tokens and the main model checks them all in one forward pass. The speedup comes without training and hosting a separate draft model; for a lab whose strategy is price, a free multiple on decode throughput is margin. The acceptance length is the average number of tokens accepted per verification step, roughly tokens produced per expensive forward pass. Speculative decoding in general is on Inference techniques.

  • GLM-4.5 adds one MoE layer as its MTP layer "to support speculative decoding during inference" (GLM-4.5 report).
  • GLM-5 shares the parameters of three MTP layers, so training covers several draft steps without the memory of several layers, and reports an acceptance length of 2.76 tokens against 2.55 for DeepSeek-V3.2 (GLM-5 report).
  • GLM-5.2 applies IndexShare to the MTP layer as well, reuses the first draft step's KV cache in later steps, and adds rejection sampling: on GLM-5.1's backbone with 7 draft steps the acceptance length rises from 4.56 to 5.47 tokens, 5.47 / 4.56 = 1.20 times (GLM-5.2 blog). The changes were measured cumulatively, so the share due to IndexShare alone is not known (Raschka).

Muon-family optimizers

Muon replaces AdamW's per-parameter second-moment rescaling with an update that orthogonalises each weight matrix's momentum (a few Newton-Schulz iterations) before applying it, so the step pushes across the whole spectrum of directions instead of being dominated by a few. GLM has trained with it since GLM-4.5, for every parameter except the word embeddings, biases and RMSNorm weights, with 5 Newton-Schulz steps, momentum 0.95 and the update rescaled to an RMS of 0.2; Zhipu observed faster convergence and tolerance of larger batches (GLM-4.5 report). It buys better loss per token and a smaller optimizer state, and costs a few extra matrix products per step and a much shorter safety record than AdamW, which is why Moonshot's QK-Clip work on taming attention-logit growth under Muon (Kimi K2 report) matters to everyone adopting it; the optimizer and its maths are on Moonshot AI: Kimi.

GLM-5 added Muon Split because of MLA: MLA's up-projections stack every head's weights into one matrix, and orthogonalising that matrix as a whole forces all heads to move at one shared scale. Muon Split orthogonalises each head's slice separately, so "projection weights for different attention heads" can "update at different scales", which brought MLA's quality up to that of 8-group GQA in Zhipu's ablation (GLM-5 report).

Attention: from grouped-query, to sparse, to a hybrid

A model's KV cache holds, for every earlier token and every layer, what the attention needs to look that token up again; at long context it, rather than the weights, decides how many requests fit on an accelerator. GLM's attention has been rebuilt twice to shrink it and the work of reading it. The attention family and KV-cache arithmetic across labs are on LLM Architecture Gallery; this section is what GLM did.

GLM-5: MLA with wider heads, plus DSA

GLM-5 replaced grouped-query attention with DeepSeek's MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention), which caches one 512-number latent vector plus a 64-number rotary key per token and layer and expands it into per-head keys and values on the fly, and added DSA (DeepSeek Sparse Attention), in which a cheap scorer, the lightning indexer, ranks every earlier token for the current query and the main attention then reads only the top 2,048 (GLM-5 report, config). Both mechanisms are explained on DeepSeek. Every entry stays in the cache so that it can be picked later; DSA saves reading, not storing.

GLM's own change is the head shape. DeepSeek-V3 uses 192-dimensional query-key heads with a head count chosen for the roofline of Nvidia's H800; GLM-5 widens them to 256 and cuts the number of heads by a third, which "keeps the training computation and the number of parameters constant while decreasing the decoding computation". The report calls this MLA-256, and it was tuned for the seven Chinese accelerator platforms GLM-5 was adapted to (Huawei Ascend, Moore Threads, Hygon, Cambricon, Kunlunxin, MetaX and Enflame) rather than for the H800. DSA was not trained from scratch: GLM-5 continued pretraining from its dense-attention base with a 1,000-step warm-up that trains only the indexer, then a 20B-token sparse-adaptation stage; the report puts DSA's saving at "roughly 1.5 to 2 times" less attention computation for long sequences (GLM-5 report).

Worked example: what GLM-5.2 caches per token. Each of 78 layers caches 512 + 64 = 576 numbers, so 576 × 78 × 2 bytes = 89,856 bytes, 87.75 KiB per token in 16-bit precision, against GLM-4.5's 368 KiB. The indexer keeps its own 128-number key per token in every layer that runs an indexer. At the full 1,048,576-token context the latents alone are 87.75 GiB for one request.

IndexShare (GLM-5.2): run the indexer once per four layers

DSA makes the main attention cheap, but the indexer still compares each query with every earlier token, so its cost is still quadratic in context length, and at a million tokens it becomes the bill (Raschka). The IndexCache study that IndexShare builds on measured 70% to 100% overlap between the positions selected by adjacent DSA layers (IndexCache): neighbouring indexers mostly rediscover the same tokens. So in GLM-5.2 the indexer runs at the first of each group of four layers and the next three reuse its list of selected positions; in the released config 21 of the 78 layers run an indexer (GLM-5.2 blog, config). The sharing layers still compute their own queries, attention weights and value mix; only the list is reused. GLM-5.2 was trained this way from mid-training at 128K sequence length.

Worked example. For one query at L = 1,048,576 one indexer does 1,048,576 × 32 × 128 = 4.29 × 109 multiply-adds, while the main attention reads 2,048 entries, 2,048 / 1,048,576 = 0.2% of the cache. Solving Amdahl's law: f = (1 − 1/2.9) / (1 − 1/4) = 0.655 / 0.75 = 0.87, so at a 1M context about seven eighths of GLM-5.1's per-token work would have been the indexer. Counting it from the configs instead (78 indexers before, 21 after, plus 40B active weights as 40 × 109 multiply-adds) gives 2.7 times; the Long-context cost tab shows the terms.

GLM-5.3-Flash: linear plus sparse attention, and IndexPool

Flash, published as the first GLM to combine sparse and linear attention, replaces most attention layers with Moonshot's Kimi Delta Attention (KDA), a gated delta-rule linear attention whose state per request has a fixed size: each of its 64 heads keeps a 128 × 128 matrix that every new token updates, instead of a cache that grows with context (the mechanism is on Moonshot AI: Kimi). Its 45 layers repeat three KDA layers and one sparse-attention layer, ending on a KDA layer: 34 KDA layers and 11 sparse ones, so only 11 / 45 = 24% of the layers keep a cache that grows with context (Flash config). The sparse layers are MLA without positional encoding (NoPE), so they cache a 512-number latent and no rotary key, with DSA reading the top 2,048 entries (config, MarkTechPost). In Zhipu's words, linear attention "captures local dependencies through state modeling, while sparse attention retrieves relevant global context through a lightweight indexer" (Z.ai docs).

IndexPool attacks the indexer from the other side. IndexShare cut how many layers run an indexer; IndexPool cuts how many entries each indexer scores, by compressing four indexer key vectors into one "through weighted pooling" (Z.ai docs). At L = 1,048,576 each Flash indexer scores 1,048,576 / 4 = 262,144 pooled keys. The config also keeps the newest, not yet complete group of keys selectable (index_kpool_always_select_tail); how a selected pooled entry maps back to the 2,048 attended tokens is not described in the sources read.

Against GLM-5.3, Zhipu reports 3.01 times less attention compute and a 4.44 times smaller KV cache (Z.ai docs), counted "per head per layer" for compute and as "average KV cache size per layer (BF16)" for memory, so that models of different sizes compare fairly. That 4.4x is the whole design, not IndexPool: most of it comes from 34 of 45 layers holding no growing cache at all. That combination is what makes the advertised million-token window affordable to serve rather than merely available. At 1M tokens one Flash request holds about 11.7 GiB of growing cache in 16-bit precision plus a fixed KDA state of 34 × 64 × 128 × 128 numbers (68 MiB in 16-bit), against 93 GiB for GLM-5.3. Zhipu itself notes that Flash's cache "is still slightly larger than Kimi-K3 and DeepSeek-V4-Flash", while its attention compute is the lowest of the four (Z.ai docs). slime: RL infrastructure built for the agentic case

Zhipu open-sourced slime, its RL post-training framework (Megatron for training, SGLang for rollouts), and it is arguably as strategically important as the weights: it is the RL framework behind every GLM from GLM-4.5 to GLM-5.3 (slime). In agentic RL a single rollout is a whole episode of tool calls, and episode lengths are long-tailed: one trajectory takes three tool calls, another three hundred. Classic synchronous on-policy RL waits for the whole batch of rollouts before each training step, so accelerators idle on the slowest trajectory, and utilisation collapses exactly when the episodes get interesting. The GLM-4.5 report says it plainly: "synchronous RL training often leads to severe GPU underutilization as workers wait for the slowest rollouts to complete" (GLM-4.5 report).

slime supports two modes (GLM-4.5 report). A colocated, synchronous mode, for reasoning RL whose rollouts are of similar length, runs generation and training on the same devices in turn. A disaggregated, asynchronous mode, for agentic RL, partitions the accelerators into rollout engines and training engines: the rollout engines generate trajectories continuously into a buffer, the trainer consumes them without waiting for a batch to complete, and the new weights are synchronised back to the rollout engines periodically. Same lesson as prefill and decode disaggregation in serving (Inference techniques): when two coupled phases have different profiles, decouple them and buffer between.

Illustrative episode lengths

Four rollout slots over 48 time steps, one square per step of an episode. The episode lengths are illustrative (a long-tailed mix, the first batch 3, 5, 8 and 40 steps); the trainer takes one step per 4 finished trajectories, and the staleness limit τ = 2 policy versions is also illustrative. The rules (discard by version, then keep tokens whose importance ratio is inside the band) are GLM-5's (GLM-5 report).

The cost is staleness. A trajectory that started under policy version w0 and is trained on at version w′ came from an older policy than the one being updated, so the update is off-policy and needs a bounded staleness window or importance correction to stay stable. GLM-5 does both (GLM-5 report):

discard if w′ − w0 > τ;    rt = πθ(yt | context) / πrollout(yt | context);    keep token t only if 1 − εℓ ≤ rt ≤ 1 + εh

The second rule is Direct Double-sided Importance Sampling: a token outside the band is masked out entirely, so a badly off-policy token cannot steer the update, and no historical policy checkpoints have to be kept. A token-in, token-out gateway passes token ids rather than text between the agent environment and the trainer, so a trajectory is never re-tokenised into slightly different tokens than the ones the policy produced (GLM-5 report). The benefit of all this is that utilisation stops being hostage to the longest episode, which is what makes agentic RL affordable at the cadence Zhipu runs it.

GLM-5.2: from GRPO back to a critic

GRPO, the RL objective most open reasoning models use, has no learned value function: it samples a group of rollouts for each prompt and scores each by how its reward compares with the group's (the objective is on RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR):

Ai = (Ri − mean(R1, …, RG)) / std(R1, …, RG)
Ai
the advantage given to every token of rollout i
Ri
its reward
G
the group size

That needs the rollouts of a group to be comparable units, and GLM-5.2's long-horizon tasks broke it: a super-long trajectory is split by context compaction into several sub-traces, so rollouts of the same prompt yield different numbers of trainable traces of very different lengths. Zhipu "move[d] from group-wise optimization to a critic-based PPO formulation that learns from individual rollouts, relying on a critic to estimate token-level advantages", training on every compacted sub-trace with a token-level loss to handle their length imbalance (GLM-5.2 blog). A critic gives each token its own advantage, so credit can fall on the step that mattered in a 300-step trace rather than equally on all of them.

Worked example (illustrative rewards). A group of four rollouts scores 1, 0, 0 and 1: mean 0.5, standard deviation 0.5, so the advantages are +1, −1, −1 and +1, and every token of the first rollout gets +1 whether it was the decisive edit or an idle directory listing. If the first rollout was compacted into three sub-traces and the second into one, the group score has to be smeared over units of very different size; a critic sidesteps the question by scoring tokens directly, at the price of a second large network to train and keep calibrated.

Reward hacking. GLM-5.2 showed more hacking than GLM-5.1: agents read protected evaluation artifacts, copied answers from references or upstream commits, or fetched the target source with curl. Zhipu added an anti-hack module to training and evaluation, a rule-based filter for recall followed by an LLM judge of the flagged action's intent for precision, run online: a detected hack is blocked and answered with dummy information, but the rollout continues, so the episode still teaches something and training avoids the instability of abruptly stopped rollouts (GLM-5.2 blog).

The inference stack, built by the model that runs on it

"Toward Recursive Self-Improvement: How GLM Built Its Own Inference Infrastructure" (September 2026) is the second piece of infrastructure Zhipu has published rather than merely used, and the more interesting one (Z.ai). It reports a production serving system for GLM-5.3-Flash built from scratch on a cluster of more than 100,000 Chinese-made accelerators, taken from the first successful run to launch in 13 days, with a 3.22x end-to-end throughput gain from baseline to launch, much of the work done by an "Infra Agent powered by GLM-5.3". The constraints were hard: limited chip memory and bandwidth, a new architecture, a 1M-token context with multimodal requests, and immature kernels. Zhipu states plainly that "we have not yet reached recursive self-improvement" and that choosing objectives, setting boundaries and assessing risk remain human responsibilities, which is what makes the post worth reading rather than discounting.

End-to-end throughput relative to the W8A8 baseline, by development day, with the change made at each point. Values transcribed from the labels printed on Z.ai's Figure 1 (Z.ai). The launch post rounds the result to "a 3× improvement" (Z.ai docs); the text of the infrastructure post says "less than two weeks" and "tripling".

The transferable finding is about feedback, not about self-improvement: the bottleneck was the feedback environment rather than the model, and the fix was turning sparse end-to-end results ("throughput missed the target") into local, cheap, objectively verifiable feedback. Engineers defined objectives and system boundaries and reviewed risky changes; the agent proposed hypotheses, changed code and ran experiments; the environment answered at three levels (Z.ai):

  1. Correctness, by comparing numerical results across partitioned and unpartitioned execution paths. This surfaced a precision bug in the KDA kernel's context-parallel path, where a matrix product defaulted to TF32 even on FP32 inputs and errors accumulated over long contexts; it was visible only under that parallelism strategy.
  2. System behaviour, by timeline analysis. Prefill plus KV transfer ran more than 20% slower than prefill alone in some scenarios; the timeline showed KV transfers never overlapping DeepEP's intra-node dispatch and combine, which held the Python global interpreter lock. Releasing the lock cut the gap below 1%.
  3. Performance, by layered testing (kernel tests, microbenchmarks, end-to-end runs) that shows which constraint binds. For one KDA decode kernel the agent removed redundant per-tile normalisation and gating work for a 1.71x speedup, and validated techniques went into a reusable library of kernel skeletons.

Neither bug would have surfaced from an end-to-end metric. The advice generalises past serving: audit what feedback an agent receives after a change, build cheap intermediate validation, define acceptance criteria numerically before starting, and let validated techniques accumulate in a reusable library. The serving stack itself, per the launch post: intra-node tensor parallelism for the linear attention and the output head, ReplaySSM (recomputing state to trade compute for memory), W8A8 weights, mixed INT8, FP8 and BF16 cache quantisation, Layer Split, and encode, prefill and decode disaggregated into separately scaled worker pools, reaching "per-token cost comparable to mainstream NVIDIA GPUs" by Zhipu's account (Z.ai docs). Similar self-reported claims arrived from Anthropic and Tencent within the same month (Other notable providers).

The pricing strategy is the product

Since GLM-4.5 in July 2025 the commercial move has been to put frontier-class weights under the MIT licence (Wikipedia), the most permissive in common use (use, modify, redistribute and sell, with only attribution required), and to undercut everyone on hosted price. The target is not benchmark leadership; it is the economics of the subscription products the model is pointed at.

The licence is no longer MIT everywhere. GLM-4.5 to GLM-5.2, GLM-4.7-Flash and GLM-5.3-Flash are MIT (HF org). GLM-5.3 ships under a custom GLM-5.3 License: MIT-style permissions with attribution, plus one condition, that a company running a model-as-a-service business with more than 10 billion US dollars of revenue in any 12 consecutive months must pass Z.ai's security review before commercial use (licence). The threshold excludes almost everyone except the largest cloud providers, which is the point. Check the licence of the checkpoint, not of the family.

List prices on Z.ai's API, US dollars per million tokens (Z.ai pricing), and what one agentic job costs on each. Move the sliders.

Worked example (the default). 10M input tokens of which 8M are cache hits, plus 1M output. On GLM-5.3: 2 × 1.40 + 8 × 0.26 + 1 × 4.40 = 2.80 + 2.08 + 4.40 = $9.28. On GLM-5.3-Flash: 2 × 0.15 + 8 × 0.03 + 1 × 0.50 = 0.30 + 0.24 + 0.50 = $1.04, 8.9 times cheaper: the "one-tenth the price" Zhipu claims for a model it says beats GLM-5.2 across its own evaluation suite (Flash card). A launch promotion had halved Flash's input price to $0.075 until 9 September 2026.

The GLM Coding Plan is the subscription version of the same move. Its endpoint is compatible with Claude Code, Cline and OpenCode, so an existing agentic coding setup switches by changing a base URL and a key rather than rewriting a harness. Launch-era coverage put it at about $3 a month; the plan now starts at $18 a month (Lite), with Pro at $80 and Max at $168, and every tier serves GLM-5.3 and GLM-5.3-Flash, older model names being routed to these (Z.ai docs, MarkTechPost). Usage is metered in credits per 5 hours and per week, and Flash gets 3 times GLM-5.3's quota (Z.ai docs).

GLM-5.3-Flash extends the move to multimodal: image, video, text and file input with text output, a 1M-token context and up to 128K output tokens (Z.ai docs), at $0.15 input and $0.50 output per million tokens, roughly a tenth of GLM-5.2's $1.40 and $4.40, at a claimed improvement over it, with weights on Hugging Face under MIT. The argument, unchanged for a year, is that a paid multimodal API tier is hard to defend against a model of that class that anyone can download and serve themselves. Self-hosting is not for everyone: the FP8 checkpoint is about 306 GiB of weights before any cache (MarkTechPost; 314.4B FP8 plus 6.9B BF16 parameters in the checkpoint). A faster hosted variant, GLM-5.3-FlashX, serves about 200 tokens a second at $0.37 and $1.25 (Z.ai docs, pricing).

Who builds on GLM, and how GLM is launched

The strategy has a second-order effect the pricing alone does not capture: GLM bases have become other labs' starting point. Atria Dawn Preview, from the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, is a 744B agentic MoE "built on the 744B-parameter MoE GLM-5.2 foundation model" rather than trained from scratch, released under MIT with a 256K context; its weights appeared on Hugging Face on 11 September (BF16) and 12 September 2026 (FP8) with no launch announcement (GitHub, Hugging Face). A national laboratory reached frontier scale by specialising someone else's open weights.

  • Method. A "Verifiable Experience Pipeline that connects tool-mediated interactions to executable environments and externally verified outcomes", plus a human-collaboration study of 769 task records from 56 participants, who rated about one third of completed AI-assisted tasks as infeasible without AI (paper, 14 September).
  • Results. The paper claims the highest reported score on five of 16 benchmarks without naming them in its abstract; the repository's table does name them: DeepSearchQA 96.0, BrowseComp 92.5, BFCL v4 77.0, AutomationBench 53.8 and CyberGym 86.5, the last against GLM-5.3's 84.5 (GitHub). The number to carry is the gap: 59.6% on SWE-bench Pro against Claude Opus 5's 74.7%. All figures are vendor-reported.
  • Access. Self-hosting means about 756 GB of FP8 weights or 1.5 TB in BF16 (753.3B parameters × 1 or 2 bytes, Hugging Face). A hosted API has since appeared, documented in the repository with Claude Code, Codex and Kimi Code set-ups (GitHub).

Permissive licensing at the frontier turns the base model into infrastructure other people build products on, which is worth more strategically than the hosted margin it forgoes.

The Ox Alpha episode is worth remembering as a pattern rather than a fact about one model. GLM-5.3-Flash ran on OpenRouter and OpenCode as the unattributed stealth model "Ox Alpha" the week before launch, collecting third-party use and scores from people who did not know whose model they were rating, and was claimed once the numbers were in (SiliconANGLE, Z.ai docs); Zhipu says it became the most-used model on both platforms, processing more than 62 trillion tokens in six days, all served on Chinese chips (Z.ai). Anonymous pre-release evaluation removes brand effects from human preference voting, and it also means a disappointing run can simply never be claimed, so treat "topped the arena as a stealth model" as a number selected after the fact.

Where GLM stands

On the aggregate index. GLM-5.2 (June 2026) topped the open-weight division of the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at release, scoring 51 on v4.1 (Labellerr); the index is a composite of public benchmarks run by Artificial Analysis. That lead passed to Moonshot's Kimi K3 on the earlier versions of the index (Moonshot AI: Kimi). On v4.3 (methodology v4.3.2, read 1 October 2026) the top open-weight score is Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Pro at 46.3, with GLM-5.3 (max) at 44.8 and Kimi K3 (max) at 43.6 (Artificial Analysis). Index versions do not compare: GLM-5.3-Flash's 57 at about $0.09 per task, quoted at launch, was on v4.1.1, before v4.2 (Z.ai docs); on v4.3 it scores 41.8 at $0.25 per task (AA).

On cost per index point GLM-5.3 is now only marginally ahead of Kimi K3 ($2.01 against $2.00 per task, for a 1.2-point higher score: $0.045 against $0.046 per point) and well behind MiMo-V2.6-Pro (46.3 at $0.13) and its own GLM-5.3-Flash (41.8 at $0.25) (GLM-5.3, Kimi K3, MiMo-V2.6-Pro).

On private enterprise code GLM-5.3 still leads open weights. Real-SWE, published in September 2026 by Specific Labs, scores model-and-harness combinations on ten tasks licensed from real companies' private codebases, eight runs each: GLM-5.3 in Claude Code resolved 28.8%, the best open-weight result, ahead of Kimi K3 in Kimi Code at 18.8%, with Claude Fable 5.1 in Claude Code first at 38.8% (Real-SWE, summary). The confidence intervals at the top overlap, and a harness changes the score of the same model.

On Zhipu's own tables. GLM-5.2 beat GPT-5.5 on FrontierSWE (open-ended engineering projects of hours to tens of hours, scored by Proximal as a "dominance" score) at 74.4 against 72.6, trailing Claude Opus 4.8's 75.1, at roughly one sixth of the cost (GLM-5.2 card, Labellerr), and led Design Arena and placed second in the frontend-code arena, human preference votes on generated interfaces (Labellerr). A dominance score depends on the field it is measured against: the GLM-5.3 card, scored as of 14 August, gives GLM-5.2 67.5 on the same benchmark (GLM-5.3 card). GLM-5.3's "roughly six times" coding gain is one benchmark, Terminal-Bench 3.0, rising from 4.6% to 28.3% (28.3 / 4.6 = 6.2) from a near-zero base; Zhipu's own headline is a 50% gain on its in-house Z.ai Code Bench, and it claims first place on CyberGym, an agentic security benchmark, at 84.5% (GLM-5.3 card).

Common mistakes

"GLM is a conventional MoE."
It was in GLM-4.5. GLM-5 added MLA and DSA, and GLM-5.3-Flash is a linear and sparse attention hybrid with mHC residual streams.
"All GLM weights are MIT."
GLM-5.3 ships under a custom licence with a security-review condition above 10 billion dollars of model-as-a-service revenue.
"GLM-5.3 was six times better at coding."
Six times is one benchmark, Terminal-Bench 3.0, rising from 4.6% to 28.3%. Zhipu's own headline is 50% on its in-house Code Bench.
"IndexPool made Flash's cache 4.4 times smaller."
The 4.4x is the whole design against GLM-5.3, averaged per layer: 34 of 45 layers holding a fixed-size KDA state accounts for about 4.1x of it, dropping the rotary key 1.125x, and IndexPool about 1.18x. IndexPool's main target is the indexer's work, a quarter of the entries to score.
"IndexShare makes inference 2.9 times faster."
It is 2.9 times fewer per-token FLOPs at a 1M context, not an end-to-end speedup, and the KV cache barely shrinks.
"Sparse attention removes the cost of long context."
DSA makes the main attention read 2,048 entries, but every entry is still stored and the indexer still scores every earlier token, which is why IndexShare and IndexPool exist.
"More heads means a bigger cache."
Only key-value heads are cached; GLM-4.5's 96 query heads share 8 key-value heads.
"GLM-5.3-Flash scores 57."
That was on the Intelligence Index v4.1.1 at launch. On v4.3 it scores 41.8 at $0.25 per task; the two versions are different scales.
"A stealth-model win is an unbiased result."
It removes brand bias but adds selection bias: only the runs that went well get claimed.

Long-context cost: every GLM attention design, from its config

Each GLM generation rebuilt attention to change one of two numbers: how much cache a request holds, and how much attention work each decoded token does. This tab computes both from the config.json files (GLM-4.5, GLM-5, GLM-5.3, GLM-5.3-Flash) at any context length. Try: drag the context to 1M and watch the indexer term take over GLM-5's compute; switch IndexPool off to see how little of Flash's cache saving it supplies; add the active weights to see IndexShare's 2.9x appear.

Cache held by one request

Attention work per decoded token

cache per token = Σlayers (latent + indexer key / pool) × bytes
work per token = Σlayers [HIdI · L / pool + H · min(k, L) · (dscore + dvalue)]
latent
576 numbers (512 + 64 rotary) for GLM-5 to 5.3, 512 for Flash's NoPE layers; GLM-4.5 caches 2 × 8 × 128 = 2,048 per layer instead
indexer key
128 numbers in each layer that runs an indexer: all 78 in GLM-5 and 5.1, 21 in GLM-5.2 and 5.3, 11 in Flash, where IndexPool divides by 4
HIdI
32 indexer heads × 128 dimensions
H
attention heads (64; 96 in GLM-4.5)
k
2,048 entries read by sparse attention; full attention reads all L
dscore + dvalue
576 + 512 in MLA's decode form, 512 + 512 for Flash, 128 + 128 per head for GQA

Flash's 34 KDA layers add no growing cache; they hold 64 × 128 × 128 numbers each regardless of context and do about 3 × 128 × 128 multiply-adds per head per token (an estimate of the delta-rule update and read, labelled). Projections and experts are outside the attention terms unless "add active weights" is on.

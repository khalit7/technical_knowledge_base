Two eras, two reasons Meta matters

Meta's models matter for two different reasons in two different eras.

The Llama era (2023 to 2025): open weights

Meta was the open-weights standard bearer. Llama 1 made running a capable model yourself a real option, Llama 2 made it legal to build a business on one, and the Llama 3 report became the field's most complete public account of how a frontier model is trained.

The Meta Superintelligence Labs era (from mid 2025): closed frontier

The frontier line was rebuilt from scratch under a new organisation and went closed as the Muse family, while Meta's open releases shrank to a 30B local model.

One question runs through both: what does Meta trade away, and what does it get, each time it moves cost or openness from one place to another? Llama moved training cost up to make serving cheap; Muse moved the weights behind an API to get back to the frontier; the Muse agent moves trust out of the model and into the machinery around it.

The terms you need

Llama

Meta's open-weights family from 2023 to 2025: Llama 1, 2, 3 (with 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3) and 4. Weights are downloadable; the licence is Meta's own, not an open-source licence.

Llama Community License

The licence from Llama 2 on: commercial use is allowed, but a company whose products had more than 700 million monthly active users must request a separate licence from Meta, which Meta may refuse ({{Llama 4 Maverick model card|@mc4}}).

MSL (Meta Superintelligence Labs)

The organisation that took over Meta's AI work on 30 June 2025, led by Alexandr Wang as Chief AI Officer. After an August 2025 restructuring it holds four groups: TBD Lab (frontier models, under Wang), FAIR (Fundamental AI Research, the long-standing research lab), Products and Applied Research (under Nat Friedman, former GitHub CEO), and MSL Infra (under Aparna Ramani) ({{Wikipedia|@wmsl}}).

Muse

The MSL product family that replaced Llama: Muse Spark (the frontier language model, closed weights), Muse Image (image generation), Muse Glimmer (a 30B open-weights model), Muse Code (a coding agent), Muse Voice Transcribe (streaming speech recognition) and the Meta Muse personal agent.

Overtraining

Training a model on many more tokens per parameter than the compute-optimal ratio, accepting a higher training bill for a smaller, cheaper-to-serve model. It is Meta's signature bet (The overtraining bet).

Total and active parameters

In a mixture-of-experts model the total parameters are what you store; the active parameters are the ones each token computes with. Llama 4 Maverick stores 400B and uses 17B per token (Inside Llama 4).

Llama 1 February 2023: the overtraining bet, and the leak

A research-only release of 7B to 65B models trained only on publicly available data, whose weights leaked, and the leak is the historically important part: it created the local-LLM ecosystem, above all {{llama.cpp|@lcpp}}, a dependency-free C and C++ inference engine with aggressive weight quantisation that put 7B-class models on laptop CPUs and made running it yourself a real option (the engine and its successors are on {{Ollama, llama.cpp, and local serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f7a144fe25d799e811}}).

The technical contribution was to take the Chinchilla result (for a fixed training-compute budget the loss-optimal allocation is roughly 20 training tokens per parameter, so the giant models of the day were badly undertrained; {{Hoffmann et al.|@chin}}) and deliberately overshoot it. The paper's own argument: Chinchilla's objective "disregards the inference budget", and "a smaller one trained longer will ultimately be cheaper at inference"; where Chinchilla recommends a 10B model on 200B tokens, Meta found a 7B model still improving after 1T ({{LLaMA paper|@l1}}). The 7B and 13B models saw 1.0T tokens and the 33B and 65B models 1.4T, and the 13B beat GPT-3 (175B) on most benchmarks. That reframing, that inference is paid on every request forever while training is paid once, is why the 7B and 13B sizes exist at all and why nearly every open model since has been overtrained by Chinchilla's standard.

Llama 2 July 2023: a legal base

7B, 13B and 70B models with a 4,096-token context (doubled from Llama 1's 2,048), the first openly licensed weights usable commercially, shipped alongside chat variants tuned with rejection sampling and RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) using PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), and an unusually candid safety appendix ({{Llama 2 paper|@l2}}). The 70B used grouped-query attention, which Llama 3 then adopted at every size. The licence, more than the model, is what let a downstream fine-tuning industry form.

Two Meta figures for one training set
The Llama 2 paper says "We trained on 2 trillion tokens of data" ({{Llama 2 paper|@l2}}); the Llama 3 paper says Llama 3's corpus is "about 15T multilingual tokens, compared to 1.8T tokens for Llama 2" ({{Llama 3 paper|@l3h}}). Both are Meta's own; the page uses 2T, the figure in the paper that trained the models, and the 7B is then at 2T / 7B ≈ 286 tokens per parameter (≈ 257 on 1.8T).

Llama 3 and 3.1 April and July 2024: open frontier

Dense 8B and 70B models with Llama 3 (April 2024), joined by the 405B with Llama 3.1 (July 2024), all trained on about 15T multilingual tokens with a 128K-token vocabulary and, from 3.1, a 128K-token context. The 405B was trained on 15.6T tokens, and the paper finds that Llama 3 "delivers comparable quality to leading language models such as GPT-4 on a plethora of tasks"; it was deliberately kept dense rather than mixture-of-experts "to maximize training stability" ({{Llama 3 paper|@l3h}}). Llama 3.2 (25 September 2024) added 11B and 90B vision models and 1B and 3B text models for devices, with 128K context ({{Meta|@l32}}).

Llama 3.3 70B (6 December 2024) came close to 405B through better post-training, including "over 25M synthetically generated examples": 86.0 against the 405B's 88.6 on MMLU with chain of thought (the same 86.0 as Llama 3.1 70B), and ahead on MATH at 77.0 against 73.8 (Llama 3.1 70B: 68.0) ({{model card|@l33}}; 405B figures from the {{Llama 3 paper|@l3h}}). That made it the practical default in serving stacks that could not justify 405B's memory footprint.

Llama 4 5 April 2025: the MoE pivot that misfired

The line moved to MoE (Mixture of Experts), in which each layer holds many expert feed-forward blocks and a router sends each token to a few of them, so total parameters (what you store) decouple from active parameters (what each token computes with); the general mechanism is on {{Topic: llms (Deeper: inside an MoE)|n:3c65c17b0d0d812d9e00f6ec89965286}}. In Maverick "MoE layers use 128 routed experts and a shared expert. Each token is sent to the shared expert and also to one of the 128 routed experts" ({{Llama 4 announcement|@l4}}). Scout is 109B total with 17B active across 16 experts, trained on about 40T tokens, with a claimed 10M-token context and, with Int4 weights, a fit on one H100; Maverick is 400B total with 17B active across 128 experts, about 22T tokens and a 1M-token context, sized for one 8-GPU H100 host ({{model card|@mc4}}). How the two are laid out layer by layer, and the iRoPE design behind the context claim, is in Inside Llama 4.

Reception was poor for two reasons. An experimental chat-tuned variant, Llama-4-Maverick-03-26-Experimental, produced the headline LMArena score of 1417 (second on the leaderboard) while a different checkpoint shipped; the experimental one gave long answers full of emojis, the released one short plain ones. LMArena said "Meta's interpretation of our policy did not match what we expect from model providers" and changed its policies ({{The Register, 8 April 2025|@reg}}); the episode cost Meta credibility it never recovered for the Llama line. And real-world coding quality lagged the published numbers badly. Behemoth, the teacher with "288 billion active parameter[s] ... 16 experts" and "nearly two trillion total parameters" from which Maverick was codistilled while Behemoth was still training ({{Llama 4 announcement|@l4}}), never shipped.

The reorganisation mid 2025: Meta Superintelligence Labs

MSL was formed on 30 June 2025 under Alexandr Wang, founder of Scale AI (the data-labelling company that supplied much of the industry's RLHF and evaluation data). Meta spent "more than $14 billion to acquire a 49% non-voting stake in Scale AI" ({{Wikipedia|@wmsl}}; the figure widely reported at the time, $14.3 billion, is unconfirmed in the sources read here), which brought Wang in, and paired it with an aggressive hiring drive from rival labs. TBD Lab took over frontier model work and the open Llama roadmap stalled behind it. Meta cut 600 jobs at MSL in October 2025, and Yann LeCun, FAIR's founding director, left on 20 November 2025 to found Advanced Machine Intelligence Labs ({{Wikipedia|@wmsl}}).

Muse Spark 8 April 2026: closed weights

The first MSL frontier model, code-named Avocado in development and built over about nine months on what Wang called an AI stack "rebuilt from scratch", rather than on the Llama architecture: natively multimodal, with tool use and multi-agent orchestration built in ({{Verdent|@verdent}}). Weights were closed and there was no public API at launch, only the Meta AI apps and a private API preview. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index as it stood in April 2026 it scored 52 against Llama 4 Maverick's 18 ({{VentureBeat|@vbspark}}; that is an earlier version of the index, not comparable with today's v4.3, see Muse Spark's scores). It returned Meta to frontier-competitive performance at the cost of the open identity that was its entire strategic differentiation: Meta's argument for releasing weights had always been that commoditising the model layer was worth more to it than owning one.

Muse Spark 1.1 and 1.2 July and August 2026: an API and a coding agent

Muse Spark 1.1 (9 July) opened Meta's first paid, OpenAI-compatible API, and Muse Spark 1.2 (5 August) shipped with Muse Code, Meta's terminal and CI (continuous integration) coding agent with sandbox-and-approval defaults that can run several sub-agents at once, at $1.25 and $4.25 per million input and output tokens and about 57 on the Intelligence Index of the time ({{Codersera|@coders}}, {{Wikipedia|@wspark}}). Wikipedia adds that 1.2 was served through the Meta Model API and OpenRouter, with a cheaper contributor tier under which Meta keeps the submitted data ({{Wikipedia|@wspark}}). The image model code-named Mango shipped quietly in early July 2026 as Muse Image ({{The Agent Report|@agentrep}}).

Muse Glimmer 10 August 2026: open weights again, at 30B

Meta's first open-weights release since Llama 4: a 29.6B dense multimodal model (text and interleaved images in, text out, with a ViT-G/14 perception encoder of about 1.8B parameters) of 52 layers with a context of 131,072 tokens, under Apache 2.0, which drops the Llama licence's 700-million-user clause ({{model card|@glim}}, {{VentureBeat|@vbglim}}). Its attention repeats Llama 4's pattern in a new form: three local layers with a 2,048-token sliding window and RoPE, then one global layer without positional encoding, with 32 query heads sharing 2 key-value heads ({{model card|@glim}}). The card states the recipe in outline: distilled from Muse Spark (by logit distillation), mid-trained on longer-context, agent-focused data, then post-trained with supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning ({{VentureBeat|@vbglim}}).

It is built for local, always-on agent work: about 55 GiB of weights in BF16 and a 17 GB roughly 4-bit build for a 24 GB consumer GPU, which the card measures at 1.0% average degradation across 15 benchmarks (0.2% for a larger dynamic build for 32 GB), with a speculative-decoding drafter (DFlash, which proposes blocks of 16 tokens per pass) that takes an RTX 5090 from 74.9 to 233.4 tokens per second, 3.1 times, and an Apple M5 Max from 26.6 to 50.2 ({{model card|@glim}}). Meta's own table puts it at 51.2 on SWE-Bench Pro against 50.2 for Qwen3.6-27B and 36.9 for Gemma 4 31B, while Qwen leads on OSWorld-Verified (75.6 against 65.9), Terminal-Bench 2.1 (60.7 against 51.7) and SWE-Bench Verified (77.2 against 76.0) ({{model card|@glim}}). On the current Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.3 it scores 17.5 at $0.06 per index task ({{Artificial Analysis|@aaglim}}).

The same day Zuckerberg said "Soon we'll also release the weights for Muse Spark 1.2" ({{VentureBeat|@vbglim}}); when Muse Spark 1.3 shipped in early September no Muse Spark weights of any version had been published, and whether 1.3's weights would follow was undecided ({{Trending Topics|@tt}}). Meta's 1.3 post still lists "the Muse Spark open weights release" on its roadmap without a date ({{Meta|@ms13}}).

Muse Spark 1.3 2 September 2026: back in the frontier band

At release Artificial Analysis scored it 61 at xhigh reasoning and 62 at a max tier available only in limited preview; only Anthropic models scored higher (Claude Fable 5.1 at 66, Opus 5 at 63; Fable 5 tied at 62), and at 61 it tied GPT-5.6 Sol (max) and Grok 4.6 (high). It has a 1M-token context, text, image and video input, $1.25 and $4.25 per million with cache hits at $0.15, and at $0.55 per index task it was the cheapest model scoring 59 or above on that scale ({{Artificial Analysis, 2 September 2026|@aaart}}). Those figures are on the index version before v4.2; the current figures (45.1 and 48.1 on v4.3) are in Muse Spark's scores.

The claim worth testing is agentic efficiency rather than raw score: "~20% fewer tool calls and ~25% fewer tokens" than Muse Spark 1.2 for the same work, the metric that decides agent economics once capability is close ({{Meta|@ms13}}). Weights are closed, it is served only through Meta's first-party API and Muse Code, and the top number comes from a max tier developers cannot broadly use. On private enterprise code the picture is more ordinary: Real-SWE, which scores models in their native harnesses on tasks licensed from private codebases, puts it at 23.8% through Muse Code, level with Grok 4.6 and well behind Claude Fable 5.1's 38.8% ({{Specific Labs' Real-SWE|@rsweh}}, as reported by {{this comparison|@rswe}}; benchmark detail on {{Topic: agentic-harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d881a8fe98b4cff7bb}}). A day earlier Meta shipped Muse Voice Transcribe, streaming speech recognition with speaker identification, covering more than 70 languages with 25 extensively validated at launch ({{9to5Mac|@vt}}).

The Meta Muse agent 8 September 2026: the model as a personal agent

A general assistant on iOS, Android and the web in the US, usable "in the Muse app or directly in WhatsApp", that sends email, books, fills forms and pays on the user's behalf, running on Muse Spark, with each user's agent in "Muse Secure VM, a dedicated, virtual machine (VM) that houses both the agent and a person's data"; basic use is free, with paid tiers ({{Meta|@agent}}). It is the most substantive thing MSL has shipped, and the security architecture rather than the assistant is why (The Muse agent's security architecture).

The overtraining bet, in numbers

The idea Meta contributed with Llama, and still the reason its small models exist, is a cost argument. Training compute for a dense transformer is approximately

C≈6ND
C
training compute in floating-point operations (FLOPs)
N
number of parameters (for an MoE model, the active parameters per token)
D
number of training tokens
6
two FLOPs per parameter per token for the forward pass and four for the backward pass

Chinchilla's compute-optimal rule sets D ≈ 20N, so for a given budget the loss-optimal size is

N*=C120,D*=20N*
N*, D*
the compute-optimal parameter count and token count
120
6 times 20, from substituting D = 20N into C = 6ND

Serving the model costs about two FLOPs per parameter per generated token, so over its life

Cserve≈2NT
Cserve
lifetime inference compute in FLOPs
T
total number of tokens the model processes in deployment
2
one multiply and one add per parameter per token in the forward pass

Training is paid once and scales with ND; serving is paid on every token and scales with N alone. Shrinking N and growing D moves cost from the bill that recurs to the bill that does not. The derivation of the Chinchilla rule itself is on {{Pretraining|n:3c65c17b0d0d814f82cffd0d5c0dd5ba}}.

Watch the two choices: one budget, two shapes

The animation spends Llama 3 8B's training budget twice: once as Meta did, on an 8B model, and once on the Chinchilla-optimal model for the same budget. Height is parameters, width is FLOPs per parameter (6 per training token, then 2 per served token), so area is compute, to scale in both modes. Both rectangles of training have the same area; then each model serves the same 15T tokens.

The loss figures in the last step use Chinchilla's fitted loss law as refitted by Sardana et al. (Overtraining explorer); they are illustrative, since the law was fitted to Chinchilla's data, not Meta's.

Worked example: Llama 3 8B against its compute-optimal twin

  1. Tokens per parameter. 15T tokens over 8B parameters is 15×1012 / 8×109 = 1,875, about 94 times Chinchilla's 20. For comparison Llama 1 7B was at 1012 / 7×109 ≈ 143, about 7 times.
  2. Training compute. C = 6 × 8×109 × 15×1012 = 7.2×1023 FLOPs.
  3. The compute-optimal model for that same budget. N* = √(7.2×1023/120) = √(6×1021) ≈ 7.75×1010, a 77.5B model on D* = 20 × 77.5B = 1.55T tokens.
  4. Serving cost per generated token. 2 × 8×109 = 1.6×1010 FLOPs for the 8B against 2 × 7.75×1010 = 1.55×1011 for the 77.5B, a ratio of about 9.7.
  5. Result. The same training budget buys either the lower-loss 77.5B model or an 8B model that is nearly ten times cheaper on every token it ever serves. Meta chose the second, because the 8B is good enough for most deployments and runs on one GPU. If the 8B serves 15T tokens over its life, Cserve = 2 × 8×109 × 15×1012 = 2.4×1023 FLOPs, a third of its training compute; the bet pays more the more the model is used.

The flagship is the instructive counter-case. The 405B on 15.6T tokens is at 15.6×1012 / 405×109 ≈ 38.5 tokens per parameter, close to compute-optimal, and 6 × 405×109 × 15.6×1012 = 3.79×1025 FLOPs reproduces the report's 3.8×1025 ({{Llama 3 paper|@l3h}}). Meta's own fitted scaling law, run on that budget, predicted "a 402B parameter model on 16.55T tokens" (about 41 tokens per parameter), and Meta trained 405B. The overtraining was in the small models, which are the ones served at volume; the largest model was sized to the budget.

Meta's prediction does not reproduce from its own published numbers
Two checks, both derived here. By 6ND, 402B on 16.55T is 3.99×1025 FLOPs, 5% over the stated budget (at 3.8×1025 and 16.55T, 6ND gives 383B). And the paper's fitted law for the optimal token count, D*(C) = ACα with (α, A) = (0.53, 0.29), gives 10.5T at 3.8×1025, not 16.55T; even at the edges of the published rounding (α = 0.535, A = 0.295) it reaches only 14.3T. Meta presumably counted FLOPs differently (attention included, for example) and fitted with unrounded constants, but the paper does not say: unconfirmed.

What Meta's training reports disclose

Why the Llama 3 report still matters more than the model. It is still the most complete public end-to-end account of training a frontier-class model, and it maps onto the stack a practitioner already runs (the paper's own row is {{The Llama 3 Herd of Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}}). Five parts transfer directly; every figure below is from the {{Llama 3 paper|@l3h}}.

1. Data and annealing

Data curation and deduplication at 15T-token scale; a final mix of roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% mathematics and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual tokens; and an annealing phase in which the learning rate is decayed linearly to zero over the last 40M tokens while high-quality code and maths are upsampled. Annealing raised the 8B's GSM8K and MATH validation scores by 24.0% and 6.4% and did almost nothing for the 405B. Meta also used annealing as a cheap data test: take a 50%-trained 8B, anneal it on 40B tokens that include a candidate dataset, and see whether it helps.

2. Scaling laws to choose the mixture and the size

Run before the main run rather than guessed: IsoFLOPs curves between 6×1018 and 1022 FLOPs, a parabola fitted at each budget, and the minimums extrapolated to the flagship's budget. The 402B-on-16.55T prediction above came from them.

3. 4D parallelism on 16K H100 GPUs

Tensor, context, pipeline and data parallelism composed on one device mesh, with the data-parallel dimension implemented as FSDP (Fully Sharded Data Parallel), which shards parameters, gradients and optimiser state across ranks and re-gathers each layer just in time; each dimension absorbs a different bottleneck. It reached 38% to 43% model FLOPs utilisation in BF16 (explore the three configurations below). The general mechanisms are on {{Distributed PyTorch: DDP, FSDP2, DTensor, and friends|n:3c65c17b0d0d815e85aff8decd8f35ab}}.

4. Post-training without PPO

Llama 3 replaced Llama 2's PPO with rounds of rejection sampling (typically 10 to 30 samples per prompt, the best kept by a reward model), supervised fine-tuning, and DPO (Direct Preference Optimization), which fits preferences with a classification loss on the policy itself and so needs no RL loop, run with a learning rate of 10−5 and β = 0.1. Meta "explored on-policy algorithms such as PPO, but found that DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better". The method family is on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}.

5. Failure at scale, measured

In a 54-day snapshot the run had 466 interruptions: 47 planned (firmware upgrades, configuration or dataset updates) and 419 unexpected, about 78% of those attributed to confirmed or suspected hardware issues, and it still achieved more than 90% effective training time through checkpointing and automated restarts (the bars below).

Llama 4's training choices

A pretraining mixture of "more than 30 trillion tokens" of text, image and video across 200 languages (the model card gives about 40T tokens for Scout and 22T for Maverick); early fusion, image and text tokens entering the same backbone from the first layer rather than a vision encoder's output being projected in near the top, which lets the model learn cross-modal structure at every depth (the design space is on {{Multimodal LLM Architectures|n:3c65c17b0d0d8139bd6cd4137dd2a4d8}}); FP8 pretraining, which for Behemoth on 32K GPUs reached 390 TFLOPs per GPU (FP8 numerics are on {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}); MetaP, Meta's method for reliably setting "critical model hyper-parameters such as per-layer learning rates" and initialisation scales so that they transfer across batch size, width, depth and token count; codistillation of Maverick from Behemoth; and iRoPE for the extreme context claim ({{Llama 4 announcement|@l4}}).

MSL-era training details are mostly undisclosed. Muse Spark and its successors have no technical report, which is the concrete cost of the closed pivot for anyone who used Meta's reports as the field's reference documentation. Muse Glimmer's model card gives only the outline (logit distillation from Muse Spark, agent-focused mid-training, SFT and RL) and the full architecture, not the data, token count or compute ({{model card|@glim}}, {{VentureBeat|@vbglim}}).

Inside Llama 4: experts and iRoPE, layer by layer

The configuration files show what the announcement compresses. Both models have 48 layers of width 5,120 with 40 query heads sharing 8 key-value heads of 128 dimensions. Scout puts an MoE block in every layer (16 experts plus the shared one); Maverick alternates: odd layers are MoE with 128 experts plus the shared one, even layers are dense with a feed-forward block twice an expert's width ({{Scout config|@sc4}}, {{Maverick config|@mv4}}, read from Unsloth's mirrors because Meta's repositories are gated). So "alternates dense and MoE layers" is true of Maverick only.

The context claim rests on iRoPE (interleaved RoPE). Three layers in four use rotary position embeddings and attend only within chunks of 8,192 tokens; every fourth layer carries no positional encoding at all and attends to the whole context, so it is length-agnostic by construction (rotary embeddings themselves are on {{Positional Encodings|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}). Those global layers also scale their queries by a temperature that grows with position, the "inference time temperature scaling of attention" of the announcement ({{Llama 4 announcement|@l4}}); in Hugging Face's implementation the factor is 1 + 0.1 ln(1 + ⌊(p+1)/8192⌋) at position p, after {{Nakanishi's scalable softmax|@ssmax}} ({{code|@hfl4}}). Sharper attention far into a long context keeps the softmax from flattening over millions of keys.

Two consequences follow. Each token computes with the attention of all 48 layers plus two expert-sized blocks per layer (the shared expert and one routed one, or the dense block of the same total width), which is why both models are 17B active despite storing 109B and 400B. And the cache that grows with context belongs to 12 global layers only, a quarter of a dense design's; the Run it yourself tab adds it to the weights. A claimed window is still not demonstrated quality at that length.

Two eras, two reasons Meta matters

Meta's models matter for two different reasons in two different eras.

The Llama era (2023 to 2025): open weights

Meta was the open-weights standard bearer. Llama 1 made running a capable model yourself a real option, Llama 2 made it legal to build a business on one, and the Llama 3 report became the field's most complete public account of how a frontier model is trained.

The Meta Superintelligence Labs era (from mid 2025): closed frontier

The frontier line was rebuilt from scratch under a new organisation and went closed as the Muse family, while Meta's open releases shrank to a 30B local model.

One question runs through both: what does Meta trade away, and what does it get, each time it moves cost or openness from one place to another? Llama moved training cost up to make serving cheap; Muse moved the weights behind an API to get back to the frontier; the Muse agent moves trust out of the model and into the machinery around it.

The Reading tab covers the lineage and the one idea each generation contributed, the overtraining bet worked through with real numbers (with an animation of the two choices), what Meta's training reports disclose, how Llama 4 is put together, where Muse Spark scores, the security architecture of the Muse personal agent (animated against a naive agent), the current lineup with licences, and which Meta model to reach for when. The terms you need

Llama

Meta's open-weights family from 2023 to 2025: Llama 1, 2, 3 (with 3.1, 3.2 and 3.3) and 4. Weights are downloadable; the licence is Meta's own, not an open-source licence.

Llama Community License

The licence from Llama 2 on: commercial use is allowed, but a company whose products had more than 700 million monthly active users must request a separate licence from Meta, which Meta may refuse (Llama 4 Maverick model card).

MSL (Meta Superintelligence Labs)

The organisation that took over Meta's AI work on 30 June 2025, led by Alexandr Wang as Chief AI Officer. After an August 2025 restructuring it holds four groups: TBD Lab (frontier models, under Wang), FAIR (Fundamental AI Research, the long-standing research lab), Products and Applied Research (under Nat Friedman, former GitHub CEO), and MSL Infra (under Aparna Ramani) (Wikipedia).

Muse

The MSL product family that replaced Llama: Muse Spark (the frontier language model, closed weights), Muse Image (image generation), Muse Glimmer (a 30B open-weights model), Muse Code (a coding agent), Muse Voice Transcribe (streaming speech recognition) and the Meta Muse personal agent.

Overtraining

Training a model on many more tokens per parameter than the compute-optimal ratio, accepting a higher training bill for a smaller, cheaper-to-serve model. It is Meta's signature bet (The overtraining bet).

Total and active parameters

In a mixture-of-experts model the total parameters are what you store; the active parameters are the ones each token computes with. Llama 4 Maverick stores 400B and uses 17B per token (Inside Llama 4).

Llama 1 February 2023: the overtraining bet, and the leak

A research-only release of 7B to 65B models trained only on publicly available data, whose weights leaked, and the leak is the historically important part: it created the local-LLM ecosystem, above all llama.cpp, a dependency-free C and C++ inference engine with aggressive weight quantisation that put 7B-class models on laptop CPUs and made running it yourself a real option (the engine and its successors are on Ollama, llama.cpp, and local serving).

The technical contribution was to take the Chinchilla result (for a fixed training-compute budget the loss-optimal allocation is roughly 20 training tokens per parameter, so the giant models of the day were badly undertrained; Hoffmann et al.) and deliberately overshoot it. The paper's own argument: Chinchilla's objective "disregards the inference budget", and "a smaller one trained longer will ultimately be cheaper at inference"; where Chinchilla recommends a 10B model on 200B tokens, Meta found a 7B model still improving after 1T (LLaMA paper). The 7B and 13B models saw 1.0T tokens and the 33B and 65B models 1.4T, and the 13B beat GPT-3 (175B) on most benchmarks. That reframing, that inference is paid on every request forever while training is paid once, is why the 7B and 13B sizes exist at all and why nearly every open model since has been overtrained by Chinchilla's standard.

Llama 2 July 2023: a legal base

7B, 13B and 70B models with a 4,096-token context (doubled from Llama 1's 2,048), the first openly licensed weights usable commercially, shipped alongside chat variants tuned with rejection sampling and RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) using PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), and an unusually candid safety appendix (Llama 2 paper). The 70B used grouped-query attention, which Llama 3 then adopted at every size. The licence, more than the model, is what let a downstream fine-tuning industry form.

Two Meta figures for one training set
The Llama 2 paper says "We trained on 2 trillion tokens of data" (Llama 2 paper); the Llama 3 paper says Llama 3's corpus is "about 15T multilingual tokens, compared to 1.8T tokens for Llama 2" (Llama 3 paper). Both are Meta's own; the page uses 2T, the figure in the paper that trained the models, and the 7B is then at 2T / 7B ≈ 286 tokens per parameter (≈ 257 on 1.8T).

Llama 3 and 3.1 April and July 2024: open frontier

Dense 8B and 70B models with Llama 3 (April 2024), joined by the 405B with Llama 3.1 (July 2024), all trained on about 15T multilingual tokens with a 128K-token vocabulary and, from 3.1, a 128K-token context. The 405B was trained on 15.6T tokens, and the paper finds that Llama 3 "delivers comparable quality to leading language models such as GPT-4 on a plethora of tasks"; it was deliberately kept dense rather than mixture-of-experts "to maximize training stability" (Llama 3 paper). Llama 3.2 (25 September 2024) added 11B and 90B vision models and 1B and 3B text models for devices, with 128K context (Meta).

Llama 3.3 70B (6 December 2024) came close to 405B through better post-training, including "over 25M synthetically generated examples": 86.0 against the 405B's 88.6 on MMLU with chain of thought (the same 86.0 as Llama 3.1 70B), and ahead on MATH at 77.0 against 73.8 (Llama 3.1 70B: 68.0) (model card; 405B figures from the Llama 3 paper). That made it the practical default in serving stacks that could not justify 405B's memory footprint.

Llama 4 5 April 2025: the MoE pivot that misfired

The line moved to MoE (Mixture of Experts), in which each layer holds many expert feed-forward blocks and a router sends each token to a few of them, so total parameters (what you store) decouple from active parameters (what each token computes with); the general mechanism is on Topic: llms (Deeper: inside an MoE). In Maverick "MoE layers use 128 routed experts and a shared expert. Each token is sent to the shared expert and also to one of the 128 routed experts" (Llama 4 announcement). Scout is 109B total with 17B active across 16 experts, trained on about 40T tokens, with a claimed 10M-token context and, with Int4 weights, a fit on one H100; Maverick is 400B total with 17B active across 128 experts, about 22T tokens and a 1M-token context, sized for one 8-GPU H100 host (model card). How the two are laid out layer by layer, and the iRoPE design behind the context claim, is in Inside Llama 4.

Reception was poor for two reasons. An experimental chat-tuned variant, Llama-4-Maverick-03-26-Experimental, produced the headline LMArena score of 1417 (second on the leaderboard) while a different checkpoint shipped; the experimental one gave long answers full of emojis, the released one short plain ones. LMArena said "Meta's interpretation of our policy did not match what we expect from model providers" and changed its policies (The Register, 8 April 2025); the episode cost Meta credibility it never recovered for the Llama line. And real-world coding quality lagged the published numbers badly. Behemoth, the teacher with "288 billion active parameter[s] ... 16 experts" and "nearly two trillion total parameters" from which Maverick was codistilled while Behemoth was still training (Llama 4 announcement), never shipped.

The reorganisation mid 2025: Meta Superintelligence Labs

MSL was formed on 30 June 2025 under Alexandr Wang, founder of Scale AI (the data-labelling company that supplied much of the industry's RLHF and evaluation data). Meta spent "more than $14 billion to acquire a 49% non-voting stake in Scale AI" (Wikipedia; the figure widely reported at the time, $14.3 billion, is unconfirmed in the sources read here), which brought Wang in, and paired it with an aggressive hiring drive from rival labs. TBD Lab took over frontier model work and the open Llama roadmap stalled behind it. Meta cut 600 jobs at MSL in October 2025, and Yann LeCun, FAIR's founding director, left on 20 November 2025 to found Advanced Machine Intelligence Labs (Wikipedia).

Muse Spark 8 April 2026: closed weights

The first MSL frontier model, code-named Avocado in development and built over about nine months on what Wang called an AI stack "rebuilt from scratch", rather than on the Llama architecture: natively multimodal, with tool use and multi-agent orchestration built in (Verdent). Weights were closed and there was no public API at launch, only the Meta AI apps and a private API preview. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index as it stood in April 2026 it scored 52 against Llama 4 Maverick's 18 (VentureBeat; that is an earlier version of the index, not comparable with today's v4.3, see Muse Spark's scores). It returned Meta to frontier-competitive performance at the cost of the open identity that was its entire strategic differentiation: Meta's argument for releasing weights had always been that commoditising the model layer was worth more to it than owning one.

Muse Spark 1.1 and 1.2 July and August 2026: an API and a coding agent

Muse Spark 1.1 (9 July) opened Meta's first paid, OpenAI-compatible API, and Muse Spark 1.2 (5 August) shipped with Muse Code, Meta's terminal and CI (continuous integration) coding agent with sandbox-and-approval defaults that can run several sub-agents at once, at $1.25 and $4.25 per million input and output tokens and about 57 on the Intelligence Index of the time (Codersera, Wikipedia). Wikipedia adds that 1.2 was served through the Meta Model API and OpenRouter, with a cheaper contributor tier under which Meta keeps the submitted data (Wikipedia). The image model code-named Mango shipped quietly in early July 2026 as Muse Image (The Agent Report).

Muse Glimmer 10 August 2026: open weights again, at 30B

Meta's first open-weights release since Llama 4: a 29.6B dense multimodal model (text and interleaved images in, text out, with a ViT-G/14 perception encoder of about 1.8B parameters) of 52 layers with a context of 131,072 tokens, under Apache 2.0, which drops the Llama licence's 700-million-user clause (model card, VentureBeat). Its attention repeats Llama 4's pattern in a new form: three local layers with a 2,048-token sliding window and RoPE, then one global layer without positional encoding, with 32 query heads sharing 2 key-value heads (model card). The card states the recipe in outline: distilled from Muse Spark (by logit distillation), mid-trained on longer-context, agent-focused data, then post-trained with supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning (VentureBeat).

It is built for local, always-on agent work: about 55 GiB of weights in BF16 and a 17 GB roughly 4-bit build for a 24 GB consumer GPU, which the card measures at 1.0% average degradation across 15 benchmarks (0.2% for a larger dynamic build for 32 GB), with a speculative-decoding drafter (DFlash, which proposes blocks of 16 tokens per pass) that takes an RTX 5090 from 74.9 to 233.4 tokens per second, 3.1 times, and an Apple M5 Max from 26.6 to 50.2 (model card). Meta's own table puts it at 51.2 on SWE-Bench Pro against 50.2 for Qwen3.6-27B and 36.9 for Gemma 4 31B, while Qwen leads on OSWorld-Verified (75.6 against 65.9), Terminal-Bench 2.1 (60.7 against 51.7) and SWE-Bench Verified (77.2 against 76.0) (model card). On the current Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.3 it scores 17.5 at $0.06 per index task (Artificial Analysis).

The same day Zuckerberg said "Soon we'll also release the weights for Muse Spark 1.2" (VentureBeat); when Muse Spark 1.3 shipped in early September no Muse Spark weights of any version had been published, and whether 1.3's weights would follow was undecided (Trending Topics). Meta's 1.3 post still lists "the Muse Spark open weights release" on its roadmap without a date (Meta).

Muse Spark 1.3 2 September 2026: back in the frontier band

At release Artificial Analysis scored it 61 at xhigh reasoning and 62 at a max tier available only in limited preview; only Anthropic models scored higher (Claude Fable 5.1 at 66, Opus 5 at 63; Fable 5 tied at 62), and at 61 it tied GPT-5.6 Sol (max) and Grok 4.6 (high). It has a 1M-token context, text, image and video input, $1.25 and $4.25 per million with cache hits at $0.15, and at $0.55 per index task it was the cheapest model scoring 59 or above on that scale (Artificial Analysis, 2 September 2026). Those figures are on the index version before v4.2; the current figures (45.1 and 48.1 on v4.3) are in Muse Spark's scores.

The claim worth testing is agentic efficiency rather than raw score: "~20% fewer tool calls and ~25% fewer tokens" than Muse Spark 1.2 for the same work, the metric that decides agent economics once capability is close (Meta). Weights are closed, it is served only through Meta's first-party API and Muse Code, and the top number comes from a max tier developers cannot broadly use. On private enterprise code the picture is more ordinary: Real-SWE, which scores models in their native harnesses on tasks licensed from private codebases, puts it at 23.8% through Muse Code, level with Grok 4.6 and well behind Claude Fable 5.1's 38.8% (Specific Labs' Real-SWE, as reported by this comparison; benchmark detail on Topic: agentic-harnesses). A day earlier Meta shipped Muse Voice Transcribe, streaming speech recognition with speaker identification, covering more than 70 languages with 25 extensively validated at launch (9to5Mac).

The Meta Muse agent 8 September 2026: the model as a personal agent

A general assistant on iOS, Android and the web in the US, usable "in the Muse app or directly in WhatsApp", that sends email, books, fills forms and pays on the user's behalf, running on Muse Spark, with each user's agent in "Muse Secure VM, a dedicated, virtual machine (VM) that houses both the agent and a person's data"; basic use is free, with paid tiers (Meta). It is the most substantive thing MSL has shipped, and the security architecture rather than the assistant is why (The Muse agent's security architecture).

The overtraining bet, in numbers

The idea Meta contributed with Llama, and still the reason its small models exist, is a cost argument. Training compute for a dense transformer is approximately

C≈6ND
C
training compute in floating-point operations (FLOPs)
N
number of parameters (for an MoE model, the active parameters per token)
D
number of training tokens
6
two FLOPs per parameter per token for the forward pass and four for the backward pass

Chinchilla's compute-optimal rule sets D ≈ 20N, so for a given budget the loss-optimal size is

N*=C120,D*=20N*
N*, D*
the compute-optimal parameter count and token count
120
6 times 20, from substituting D = 20N into C = 6ND

Serving the model costs about two FLOPs per parameter per generated token, so over its life

Cserve≈2NT
Cserve
lifetime inference compute in FLOPs
T
total number of tokens the model processes in deployment
2
one multiply and one add per parameter per token in the forward pass

Training is paid once and scales with ND; serving is paid on every token and scales with N alone. Shrinking N and growing D moves cost from the bill that recurs to the bill that does not. The derivation of the Chinchilla rule itself is on Pretraining.

Height is parameters, width is FLOPs per parameter (6 per training token, then 2 per served token), so area is compute, to scale in both modes. Both rectangles of training have the same area; then each model serves the same 15T tokens.

The loss figures in the last step use Chinchilla's fitted loss law as refitted by Sardana et al. (Overtraining explorer); they are illustrative, since the law was fitted to Chinchilla's data, not Meta's.

Worked example: Llama 3 8B against its compute-optimal twin

  1. Tokens per parameter. 15T tokens over 8B parameters is 15×1012 / 8×109 = 1,875, about 94 times Chinchilla's 20. For comparison Llama 1 7B was at 1012 / 7×109 ≈ 143, about 7 times.
  2. Training compute. C = 6 × 8×109 × 15×1012 = 7.2×1023 FLOPs.
  3. The compute-optimal model for that same budget. N* = √(7.2×1023/120) = √(6×1021) ≈ 7.75×1010, a 77.5B model on D* = 20 × 77.5B = 1.55T tokens.
  4. Serving cost per generated token. 2 × 8×109 = 1.6×1010 FLOPs for the 8B against 2 × 7.75×1010 = 1.55×1011 for the 77.5B, a ratio of about 9.7.
  5. Result. The same training budget buys either the lower-loss 77.5B model or an 8B model that is nearly ten times cheaper on every token it ever serves. Meta chose the second, because the 8B is good enough for most deployments and runs on one GPU. If the 8B serves 15T tokens over its life, Cserve = 2 × 8×109 × 15×1012 = 2.4×1023 FLOPs, a third of its training compute; the bet pays more the more the model is used.

The flagship is the instructive counter-case. The 405B on 15.6T tokens is at 15.6×1012 / 405×109 ≈ 38.5 tokens per parameter, close to compute-optimal, and 6 × 405×109 × 15.6×1012 = 3.79×1025 FLOPs reproduces the report's 3.8×1025 (Llama 3 paper). Meta's own fitted scaling law, run on that budget, predicted "a 402B parameter model on 16.55T tokens" (about 41 tokens per parameter), and Meta trained 405B. The overtraining was in the small models, which are the ones served at volume; the largest model was sized to the budget.

Meta's prediction does not reproduce from its own published numbers
Two checks, both derived here. By 6ND, 402B on 16.55T is 3.99×1025 FLOPs, 5% over the stated budget (at 3.8×1025 and 16.55T, 6ND gives 383B). And the paper's fitted law for the optimal token count, D*(C) = ACα with (α, A) = (0.53, 0.29), gives 10.5T at 3.8×1025, not 16.55T; even at the edges of the published rounding (α = 0.535, A = 0.295) it reaches only 14.3T. Meta presumably counted FLOPs differently (attention included, for example) and fitted with unrounded constants, but the paper does not say: unconfirmed.

What Meta's training reports disclose

Why the Llama 3 report still matters more than the model. It is still the most complete public end-to-end account of training a frontier-class model, and it maps onto the stack a practitioner already runs (the paper's own row is The Llama 3 Herd of Models). Five parts transfer directly; every figure below is from the Llama 3 paper.

1. Data and annealing

Data curation and deduplication at 15T-token scale; a final mix of roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% mathematics and reasoning, 17% code and 8% multilingual tokens; and an annealing phase in which the learning rate is decayed linearly to zero over the last 40M tokens while high-quality code and maths are upsampled. Annealing raised the 8B's GSM8K and MATH validation scores by 24.0% and 6.4% and did almost nothing for the 405B. Meta also used annealing as a cheap data test: take a 50%-trained 8B, anneal it on 40B tokens that include a candidate dataset, and see whether it helps.

2. Scaling laws to choose the mixture and the size

Run before the main run rather than guessed: IsoFLOPs curves between 6×1018 and 1022 FLOPs, a parabola fitted at each budget, and the minimums extrapolated to the flagship's budget. The 402B-on-16.55T prediction above came from them.

3. 4D parallelism on 16K H100 GPUs

Tensor, context, pipeline and data parallelism composed on one device mesh, with the data-parallel dimension implemented as FSDP (Fully Sharded Data Parallel), which shards parameters, gradients and optimiser state across ranks and re-gathers each layer just in time; each dimension absorbs a different bottleneck. It reached 38% to 43% model FLOPs utilisation in BF16 (explore the three configurations below). The general mechanisms are on Distributed PyTorch: DDP, FSDP2, DTensor, and friends.

4. Post-training without PPO

Llama 3 replaced Llama 2's PPO with rounds of rejection sampling (typically 10 to 30 samples per prompt, the best kept by a reward model), supervised fine-tuning, and DPO (Direct Preference Optimization), which fits preferences with a classification loss on the policy itself and so needs no RL loop, run with a learning rate of 10−5 and β = 0.1. Meta "explored on-policy algorithms such as PPO, but found that DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better". The method family is on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR.

5. Failure at scale, measured

In a 54-day snapshot the run had 466 interruptions: 47 planned (firmware upgrades, configuration or dataset updates) and 419 unexpected, about 78% of those attributed to confirmed or suspected hardware issues, and it still achieved more than 90% effective training time through checkpointing and automated restarts (the bars below).

Llama 4's training choices

A pretraining mixture of "more than 30 trillion tokens" of text, image and video across 200 languages (the model card gives about 40T tokens for Scout and 22T for Maverick); early fusion, image and text tokens entering the same backbone from the first layer rather than a vision encoder's output being projected in near the top, which lets the model learn cross-modal structure at every depth (the design space is on Multimodal LLM Architectures); FP8 pretraining, which for Behemoth on 32K GPUs reached 390 TFLOPs per GPU (FP8 numerics are on Quantization and Precision); MetaP, Meta's method for reliably setting "critical model hyper-parameters such as per-layer learning rates" and initialisation scales so that they transfer across batch size, width, depth and token count; codistillation of Maverick from Behemoth; and iRoPE for the extreme context claim (Llama 4 announcement).

MSL-era training details are mostly undisclosed. Muse Spark and its successors have no technical report, which is the concrete cost of the closed pivot for anyone who used Meta's reports as the field's reference documentation. Muse Glimmer's model card gives only the outline (logit distillation from Muse Spark, agent-focused mid-training, SFT and RL) and the full architecture, not the data, token count or compute (model card, VentureBeat).

Inside Llama 4: experts and iRoPE, layer by layer

The configuration files show what the announcement compresses. Both models have 48 layers of width 5,120 with 40 query heads sharing 8 key-value heads of 128 dimensions. Scout puts an MoE block in every layer (16 experts plus the shared one); Maverick alternates: odd layers are MoE with 128 experts plus the shared one, even layers are dense with a feed-forward block twice an expert's width (Scout config, Maverick config, read from Unsloth's mirrors because Meta's repositories are gated). So "alternates dense and MoE layers" is true of Maverick only.

The context claim rests on iRoPE (interleaved RoPE). Three layers in four use rotary position embeddings and attend only within chunks of 8,192 tokens; every fourth layer carries no positional encoding at all and attends to the whole context, so it is length-agnostic by construction (rotary embeddings themselves are on Positional Encodings). Those global layers also scale their queries by a temperature that grows with position, the "inference time temperature scaling of attention" of the announcement (Llama 4 announcement); in Hugging Face's implementation the factor is 1 + 0.1 ln(1 + ⌊(p+1)/8192⌋) at position p, after Nakanishi's scalable softmax (code). Sharper attention far into a long context keeps the softmax from flattening over millions of keys.

MoE layerdense layerglobal attention, no position encodingchunked 8,192, RoPE

Two consequences follow. Each token computes with the attention of all 48 layers plus two expert-sized blocks per layer (the shared expert and one routed one, or the dense block of the same total width), which is why both models are 17B active despite storing 109B and 400B. And the cache that grows with context belongs to 12 global layers only, a quarter of a dense design's; the Run it yourself tab adds it to the weights. A claimed window is still not demonstrated quality at that length.

The Muse agent's security architecture

The Muse agent holds standing access to a user's mail, payments and accounts while reading attacker-controllable web pages and messages, which is the worst case for prompt injection (instructions hidden in content the agent reads). Meta's design assumes the model will sometimes be fooled and makes being fooled grant nothing that matters. From Meta's own description of 8 September 2026 (Meta):

  1. The runtime cell. The agent harness runs in a systemd-nspawn container inside the user's VM, with "root inside the runtime cell ... mapped to an unprivileged host user", its own filesystem and virtual network interface, filtered system calls (no io_uring) and limited kernel capabilities (no CAP_SYS_PTRACE, no CAP_NET_ADMIN).
  2. Security services outside the cell. Separate services hold what the agent must not: hatch-authd stores credentials (including OAuth tokens for third-party services), privsep workers run the built-in connector code with tightly scoped credential access, hatch-safety runs independent classifier models over requests and responses so that an attacker inside the cell cannot switch them off, and Sentinel is "the sole permission authority" for connector actions and network egress. The cell talks to them only over Unix domain sockets with kernel-authenticated peer checks.
  3. Surrogate credentials. The agent never sees a real password, token or card. "Code in the runtime cell or a worker only ever sees a 'surrogate' token, minted by authd. After the concrete network request is authorized, Sentinel will replace any surrogate tokens with the real credential ... at the network boundary", so an injected "send me the token" can only exfiltrate a placeholder.
  4. Egress policy. Sentinel resolves each request to allow, deny or ask, inspecting it at layers 4 and 7: "the hostname, the resolved and final destination IP address, the port, protocol, HTTP method, path, and the actual decoded request". It tracks tainted egress through the kernel: each tool process starts clean and becomes tainted if it reads user data (eBPF programs on cgroups and on Linux Security Module hooks Meta added), so a request carrying data from an untrusted source does not ride an auto-allow rule.
  5. Approvals outside the conversation. When Sentinel's answer is "ask", execution halts and the approval goes "directly to the Muse client", appearing in the app's own interface, not as a message in the chat with Muse, so text in the context window cannot manufacture consent. Grants are "strict capabilities, not conversational suggestions", bound to the connector or destination: one-time, session, task, time-bounded or permanent. Checkouts always need approval with the exact purchase details and use single-use card numbers through Stripe Link, bound to one merchant, one amount and a short validity.
  6. A browser that cannot be scripted. The browser sub-agent sees "an accessibility tree snapshot of the page, not the raw DOM", and has no way to run JavaScript in the page; a separate family of classifiers looks for prompt injection in pages, images and files, for high-risk forms and for egress of unrelated personal data.

The animation runs one injected page through two agents: a naive one that holds real credentials and sends whatever it composes, and Muse's design. The injection succeeds against the model in both; what differs is what it buys.

The task, the injected page and the attacker's address are illustrative; the components and rules are Meta's. Meta publishes no example attack.

Meta offers "up to $300,000 for valid reports ... including up to $130,000 for successful prompt injection attempts that affect one user", and has published no accuracy figures for its classifiers, so the parts that still rest on detection are unmeasured (Meta). The boundary also covers only the cloud VM: on 22 September 2026 the researcher Patrick Wardle disclosed that malware already running under the user's account could change an undocumented macOS setting and redirect the Muse Mac client's dictation traffic, with its prompts and authentication data, to a server of its choosing (Cybersecurity News); that report gives no patch, and the fix "four days later" recorded earlier is unconfirmed in the sources read here. The general principle, that enforcement and credentials belong outside the component that can be persuaded, is developed on Topic: agentic-harnesses, and the comparison with self-hosted personal agents on Personal agents: OpenClaw, Hermes Agent, and how they differ from coding harnesses.

Strategic read. Llama's retreat handed open-weights leadership to the Chinese labs and to Mistral, and it is a retreat from the open frontier only: Muse Spark 1.3 put Meta back in the frontier band in September 2026, and Muse Glimmer put it back in open weights at 30B, the size people run locally, not at the frontier. Whether Meta returns to the open frontier turns on one checkable event: the publication of Muse Spark weights. Llama 3.1 and 3.3 remain default bases in many fine-tuning stacks purely through incumbency: tooling, LoRA adapters, quantised builds and tribal knowledge accumulated around them, and that inertia outlives the quality argument.

Which Meta model when

  • You need frontier capability from Meta and can use a hosted API: Muse Spark 1.3. It buys frontier-band scores at a low cost per task, and costs closed weights, a single vendor's API and no technical report.
  • You need weights you can run locally or modify, with no licence conditions: Muse Glimmer. Apache 2.0 removes the 700-million-user clause, and a 24 GB GPU is enough for the 17 GB build; it is a 30B model (17.5 on the v4.3 index), so do not expect frontier reasoning.
  • You have an existing Llama fine-tuning pipeline: stay on Llama 3.1 or 3.3 while its adapters and quantised builds earn their keep, and plan a move, since nothing newer will ship under the Llama name.
  • You need a large open MoE from Meta: Llama 4 Maverick fits one 8-GPU H100 host in FP8 (400 GB of weights against 8 × 80 GB = 640 GB), but test it on your own tasks first; its public numbers did not hold up in practice, and the open models on the provider pages (DeepSeek, Alibaba: Qwen, Mistral AI) have overtaken it.
  • You are building a personal agent with account access: study the Muse agent's design (surrogate credentials, egress control outside the model, approvals outside the conversation) whatever model you use.

Whether a model fits your GPU, with its context, is on Run it yourself.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Llama 4 has a usable 10M-token context."
That is Scout's claimed limit, resting on iRoPE and attention temperature scaling; a claimed window is not demonstrated quality at that length, and Maverick's limit is 1M. It is also not a single-GPU figure: even with only the 12 global layers growing, Scout's cache at 10M tokens is about 481 GiB in BF16 (derived from the configuration on Run it yourself), six H100s' worth before any weights.
"17B active means 17B of memory."
Active parameters set the compute per token; all 109B or 400B parameters must still be stored. Scout fits one H100 only with Int4 weights (109B at half a byte is about 54.5 GB).
"Llama is open source."
The Llama licences restrict very large users and are Meta's own terms. Muse Glimmer's Apache 2.0 is the first Meta licence that is open source in the usual sense.
"Meta overtrained its flagship."
The 405B sat close to compute-optimal at about 38.5 tokens per parameter; the heavy overtraining was in the 8B and 70B (about 1,875 and 214 tokens per parameter), which are the ones served at volume.
"The 1417 LMArena score was Maverick's."
It belonged to an unreleased experimental checkpoint tuned for chat preference.
"Muse Spark 1.3's 61 compares with today's index numbers."
The 61 and 62 are on the scale the index used at release, before v4.2; a number from one version does not compare with another. On the current v4.3 the same model is 45.1 at xhigh and 48.1 at max, the figures to set beside other v4.3 scores.
"The Muse agent cannot be prompt-injected."
It can; the design limits what a successful injection achieves. The classifiers that still catch some attacks are unmeasured in public, and the Mac client sat outside the VM boundary.
"Chinchilla says train 20 tokens per parameter, full stop."
The 20 is the training-only optimum, and even that depends on whose fit you use: Hoffmann et al.'s own fitted law implies about 70 tokens per parameter, Epoch's replication about 20 (Besiroglu et al.). Once serving is counted the optimum moves far higher; see the Overtraining explorer.

Run it yourself: parameters, memory and fit

Every open Meta model's parameter count rebuilt from its config.json, then weights plus key-value cache against the memory you have. Weights are parameters times bytes per parameter; the cache is 2 × layers × KV heads × head size × bytes per token for each layer that keeps the whole context, and a fixed window's worth for each local layer.

Overtraining explorer: how long should a model be trained?

Chinchilla's answer counts training only. Count serving as well and the cheapest way to reach a given quality changes: fix the loss you need, then pick the size N and training tokens D on the curve of equal loss that minimise 6ND + 2NT over a lifetime of T served tokens (Sardana et al.). Quality is the fitted loss law

L(N,D)=E+ANα+BDβ
L
pre-training loss (nats per token on Chinchilla's data)
E
the irreducible loss
A, α
how fast loss falls with parameters
B, β
how fast loss falls with training tokens

Then choose "Llama 3 8B on 15T" and slide T: under Hoffmann's law Meta's 1,875 tokens per parameter is the optimum only for a lifetime around 1014 served tokens. Sardana et al. also trained models up to 10,000 tokens per parameter and found the law overestimates the gain from extra data at such extreme ratios, so read the far right as an upper bound on the benefit.

Loss values from a law fitted to Chinchilla's data are illustrative for Meta's models, which used other data and tokenizers; the explorer uses them to compare shapes, not to predict Llama's loss. Fits: Hoffmann et al. 2022; Sardana et al. (α = 0.336, β = 0.283, after De Vries); Besiroglu et al. 2024 (precise Hoffmann values and Epoch's refit). Hoffmann's own fit implies about 70 tokens per parameter at the training optimum; Epoch's refit about 20, matching how Chinchilla itself was trained.

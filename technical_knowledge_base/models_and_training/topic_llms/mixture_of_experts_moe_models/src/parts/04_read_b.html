Why the trade works

  • Same active budget, better quality. Mixtral 8x7B (47B total, 13B active) beat Llama 2 13B, the dense model with the same active budget, and by its paper's own account "outperforms or matches Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 across all evaluated benchmarks" ({{Mixtral paper|@mixp}}). At equal inference FLOPs, the idle capacity is close to free quality.
  • Cheaper training to a given loss. Switch Transformer reached T5-Base quality up to 7x faster at fixed FLOPs, then kept improving ({{Switch Transformer|@sw}}). A token's gradient updates only the experts it visited, so parameter count grows without per-step cost growing with it.
  • Capacity past one device. Experts are independent by construction, so they shard cleanly across GPUs (expert parallelism), and total parameters can exceed any single node's memory.

What it costs, the subject of most of this page: memory is priced on total parameters while throughput is priced on active ones, since every parameter must be resident at serving time; routing adds two all-to-all collectives per layer; per-expert matrix multiplications (GEMMs) are small and skinny, so utilisation is worse than the FLOP count suggests; and a discrete decision inside an otherwise differentiable model is a permanent source of instability.

Routing styles

The router has three jobs that pull against each other: score experts per token, decide how many to activate, and keep the resulting traffic spread across hardware.

Noisy top-k (2017)

Gaussian noise added to the scores before selection, so nearly tied experts share the traffic instead of the marginally better one winning every time: balancing in the selection rule rather than in the loss, at a little routing precision ({{Shazeer et al., 2017|@shz}}).

Top-2 (GShard, Mixtral)

The default for years ({{GShard|@gsh}}). Twice top-1's expert FLOPs; buys a smoother layer function (the output can interpolate between two experts) and a better-conditioned router, because two gate weights give it a comparison rather than a single scalar.

Top-1 (Switch)

Minimum FLOPs, minimum dispatch, one expert's weights touched. No second opinion within a token, and the single gate weight is the only learning signal, so it depends most on aggressive balancing. Switch's real contribution was showing that k = 1 works at all, against the prior assumption that k ≥ 2 was needed for a usable router signal.

Fine-grained top-k plus shared experts (now dominant)

Split the same expert budget into many narrower experts and raise k to match ({{DeepSeekMoE|@dsm}}). Active parameters stay about constant while the combinations the router can express grow: C(256, 8) ≈ 4.1 × 1014 against C(8, 2) = 28. One or more always-on shared experts absorb what every token needs (general syntax, frequent patterns), so routed experts spend capacity on specialising. The cost is hardware: smaller per-expert GEMMs and wider all-to-all fan-out per token.

With sigmoid scoring the affinities do not compete, so adding experts shrinks no expert's score, whereas an unrenormalised softmax probability falls roughly as 1/N. That property, more than any quality claim, is the usual explanation for why V3-style sigmoid plus renormalisation replaced softmax as k climbed into double digits. The {{Total and active|#t-par}} tab's granularity control shows the fine-grained trade on a real configuration.

Load balancing

Left alone, routing collapses. An expert chosen slightly more often receives more gradient, improves, and is chosen more often still, while the rest starve and never generate evidence that they were worth reviving. That is a quality problem, because effective capacity shrinks to the experts that survived, and at the same time a systems problem: with experts sharded across GPUs, an overloaded expert stalls its whole layer, since the all-to-all waits on the slowest shard, and hot experts overrun whatever per-expert memory was allocated.

The fixes, in the order they were invented, with what each costs:

  1. Noisy gating. Randomness in the selection rule redistributes tokens near a decision boundary. Cheap and unbiased, but it touches only marginal cases and cannot rescue an expert that has fallen far behind.
  2. Auxiliary load-balancing loss ({{Switch Transformer|@sw}}):
    ℒaux=α·N·∑i=1NfiPifi=1T∑x∈ℬ𝟙{arg maxp(x)=i}Pi=1T∑x∈ℬpi(x)

    ℬ is the batch of T tokens, fi the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i (a count, with no gradient), Pi the mean router probability expert i received (differentiable), and α the coefficient: Switch used 10−2, the value it found "balanced load quickly without interfering with training loss". The factor N makes the loss equal α under perfectly uniform routing whatever the expert count; top-k variants count all T · k assignments in fi and rescale so the uniform case stays constant. Worked example: with N = 4 and uniform routing, fi = Pi = 0.25, so ℒ = α × 4 × (4 × 0.0625) = α. Collapsed onto expert 1 with f = (1, 0, 0, 0) and P = (0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1): α × 4 × 0.7 = 2.8α. The gradient flows only through P1, pushing the router's probability for expert 1 down.

    The product is smallest when both are uniform, so the term pushes the router toward even traffic, but it nudges the router's beliefs rather than editing the assignment. Its cost is a second objective beside language modelling, and the two conflict wherever a token genuinely belongs to one expert: the quality tax the aux-loss-free method later removed.
  3. Device-level balancing loss. {{DeepSeekMoE|@dsm}} groups experts by the device they live on and balances traffic across groups rather than individual experts, conceding that per-expert uniformity was never the goal: what matters is that no GPU is the straggler in the all-to-all. It buys real throughput and leaves the router freer within a device.
  4. Capacity factor. A hard cap on how many tokens any one expert accepts; see Capacity below.
  5. Aux-loss-free balancing (DeepSeek-V3, now on nearly every open MoE model card from 2025 on). Keep a per-expert bias bi added to the routing score only for top-k selection: selection uses σi + bi, the gate weight uses σi alone, and after each step
    bi←bi−γ·sign(ci−c‾)

    ci is the number of tokens expert i received in the step, c̄ the mean over experts, γ the bias update speed. V3 set γ = 0.001 for the first 14.3T training tokens and 0 for the last 500B, kept a sequence-wise balance loss of the same α Σ fi Pi form with α = 0.0001 "just to avoid extreme imbalance", and drops no tokens in training or inference ({{V3 report|@v3h}}).

    Because the bias never enters the gate weight, it never appears in the gradient: balancing moves the decision boundary instead of adding a competing objective, so the quality tax of point 2 disappears.
Often confused with load balancing: the router z-loss
{{ST-MoE|@st}} adds ℒz = (1/B) Σi (log Σj exj(i))2 with coefficient cz = 0.001, where B is the number of tokens and xj(i) token i's logit for expert j. It balances nothing: it keeps the logits small, because large router logits with bf16 exponentials give roundoff blowups and loss spikes. In ST-MoE's stability study it made all three runs stable, as tight update clipping also did, but without clipping's significant quality cost.

Balancing scope: per sequence, per micro-batch or global batch

Which tokens the balance is measured over matters as much as the method. A load-balancing loss is usually computed per micro-batch, and a micro-batch for a billion-scale model holds only a few sequences, often packed from one domain, so it is nearly a sequence-level constraint: even a pure code sequence is pushed to spread over every expert. Qwen's {{global-batch paper|@gb}} synchronises the counts fi across data-parallel ranks (and, with a buffer, across gradient-accumulation steps) so balance is demanded only over the diverse global batch. It reports about 0.1 lower pre-training perplexity and about 2 points on benchmarks, and expert specialisation by domain that micro-batch balancing suppresses; Qwen3 adopted it ({{Qwen3 report|@q3r}}). DeepSeek reached the same conclusion from the other side: its aux-loss-free method balances per batch, and a batch-wise auxiliary loss matched it, with validation losses of 2.258 (sequence-wise loss), 2.253 (aux-loss-free) and 2.253 (batch-wise loss) on 1B MoE models, and 2.085 against 2.080 and 2.080 at 3B ({{V3 report, 4.5.3|@v3h}}).

The same routing, judged at two scopes

Four micro-batches, each from one domain, routed over four experts (illustrative). Slide specialisation up: each domain sends more of its tokens to "its" expert. The loss is computed with Pi = fi for simplicity and shown as a multiple of α (1.0 is perfectly balanced).

The price of a wider scope: a cross-rank reduction of routing counts every step, and a router balanced over the corpus while individual sequences, and so individual devices at serving time, are not. DeepSeek names the same two risks for batch-wise methods: imbalance within certain sequences or small batches, and domain shift at inference.

Capacity factors and dropped tokens

Training an MoE efficiently with static shapes means allocating a fixed buffer per expert before knowing how many tokens will choose it:

C=CF·k·TN

C is the number of token slots in each expert's buffer, CF the capacity factor, T the tokens in the batch, k the experts per token, N the experts. Switch, being top-1, writes it as tokens per batch divided by number of experts, times capacity factor. 1.0 provisions exactly the average; ST-MoE trained at 1.25 and evaluated at 2.0 ({{ST-MoE|@st}}).

Worked example. A batch of T = 4,096 tokens, N = 64 experts, k = 2, CF = 1.25: average load 2 × 4,096 / 64 = 128 tokens, capacity C = 1.25 × 128 = 160 slots. A hot expert that attracts 200 tokens drops 40 of them (20% of its traffic); one that attracts 60 leaves 100 padded slots computed for nothing. CF = 2.0 gives 256 slots, no drops here, and 60% more buffer on every expert.

Raising CF costs memory and padding compute in proportion, because buffers are allocated and processed whether or not they fill. Lowering it drops tokens arriving at a full expert: in GShard and Switch they skip the layer, passing through on the residual connection with no FFN applied. Switch also tried rerouting overflow to the next-best expert ("No-Token-Left-Behind") and found no empirical benefit. A dropped token is not a crash but a silently weaker forward pass, and drops concentrate on exactly the tokens that wanted the popular experts.

Two consequences. Drop behaviour differs between training and inference, since inference batches have different composition and often no capacity limit at all, so a model trained with heavy dropping is evaluated in a regime it was not trained in. And the problem is an artefact of static buffers: dropless MoE ({{MegaBlocks|@mb}}) computes the experts as block-sparse matrix multiplication over ragged per-expert token groups, so nothing is padded and nothing is dropped. Modern training stacks therefore discuss grouped GEMM kernels rather than capacity factors, which survive mostly in older recipes and in inference-time admission control.

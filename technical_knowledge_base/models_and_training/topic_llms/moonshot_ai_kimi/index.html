The bet: research on the boring layers, shipped fast

Moonshot AI is a Beijing lab whose Kimi models are, by most aggregate measures, among the strongest open-weight lines of 2025 and 2026. Its edge is not data scale or distribution. It is a willingness to change what most labs treat as settled: the optimiser, the attention layer and the residual connection.

AdamW, softmax attention and the plain residual sum are inherited without question almost everywhere; Moonshot has replaced all three in production models at trillion scale. Kimi K2 was trained with Muon instead of AdamW (K2 report); Kimi K3 replaces softmax attention in most layers with Kimi Delta Attention and the residual sum with Attention Residuals (K3 report). The other half of the bet is cycle time: Kimi Linear went from paper (October 2025, arXiv 2510.26692) to flagship backbone (K3, July 2026) in well under a year, fast for an architecture change that touches every kernel in the stack.

The starting point matters for reading the rest. K2 is architecturally a DeepSeek-V3 derivative: multi-head latent attention (MLA), fine-grained mixture of experts with a shared expert, 61 layers, hidden size 7,168. Almost everything Kimi-specific is a deliberate deviation from that template, each justified by an ablation in the K2 report. Definitions

Muon (MomentUm Orthogonalized by Newton-Schulz): an optimiser for 2D weight matrices that replaces each momentum matrix with an approximately orthogonal matrix before applying it.
QK-Clip: Moonshot's rule for rescaling a head's query and key projection weights after an optimiser step whenever that head's largest attention logit exceeds a threshold.
MuonClip: Muon plus weight decay, Adam-matched update scaling and QK-Clip, the optimiser K2 was pretrained with.
Attention logit: the scaled query-key dot product qi⊤kj / √d that enters the softmax.
KDA (Kimi Delta Attention): a gated delta-rule linear attention with a per-channel forget gate, from the Kimi Linear paper.
Kimi Linear: the 48B-total, 3B-active research model that introduced KDA in a 3:1 hybrid with full MLA layers.
AttnRes (Attention Residuals): a replacement for the residual sum in which each layer takes a learned softmax-weighted mix of earlier layers' outputs.
Sparsity (Moonshot's usage): total experts divided by activated experts, 384 / 8 = 48 for K2. A different number from total over active parameters.
QAT and MXFP4: quantisation-aware training, training with simulated low-precision weights; and microscaling FP4, a 4-bit floating-point format with one shared 8-bit scale per block of 32 values. The generic treatment is on Quantization and Precision.

MuonClip: Muon, plus a fix for what Muon breaks

Muon is an optimiser for a network's 2D weight matrices (attention and MLP projections); embeddings, the output head and 1D parameters such as RMSNorm gains stay on AdamW. Where AdamW rescales each parameter independently by its own running second moment, Muon treats the momentum buffer as a matrix and approximately orthogonalises it before applying it. A raw gradient matrix is dominated by a handful of large singular directions, so most of an AdamW step's magnitude goes into those while the rest of the spectrum barely moves; replacing every singular value with roughly 1 makes the update push in all directions at comparable scale.

Moonshot's scaling study reports about 2× the computational efficiency of AdamW under compute-optimal training, matching AdamW-trained models with about 52% of the training FLOPs (Muon is Scalable for LLM Training, arXiv 2502.16982). Muon also carries less state: one momentum buffer against AdamW's two moment buffers per parameter, real memory in a sharded trillion-parameter run. The rescaled update has root-mean-square 0.2, inside the 0.2 to 0.4 range AdamW updates typically show, which lets Muon reuse AdamW's tuned learning rate and weight decay. Weight decay was the second fix the paper found necessary at scale: without it, Muon converged faster at first but some weights grew too large over long, over-trained runs; with it, Muon beat both vanilla Muon and AdamW.

The extra cost is a handful of matrix multiplies per weight per step; Jordan estimates the FLOP overhead below 1% for typical language-model training (0.5% for a Llama-405B-scale run). The awkward part is distributed: orthogonalisation needs the whole matrix while the training job holds it sharded across devices, which is why Moonshot published a distributed implementation built on ZeRO-1 sharding (arXiv 2502.16982). K3's report replaces the all-gather of the whole parameter buffer with peer-to-peer fetches of only the shards each rank owns, cutting memory and communication (K3 report, §5.2.2).

QK-Clip: what Muon broke at scale

In a 53B-total, 9B-active test model trained with plain Muon, the largest attention logits passed 1,000 within a few thousand steps; logits that large saturate the softmax and are followed by loss spikes and occasional divergence, and Moonshot saw this more often with Muon than with AdamW (K2 report, §2.1). The usual mitigations do not fit K2. A logit soft-cap clips the logits themselves, but the query-key products can still grow without bound before the cap applies. QK-Norm normalises queries and keys inside the forward pass, but it cannot be used with MLA, because MLA never materialises full per-head keys at inference: they are absorbed into the latent projection. QK-Clip acts on the weights instead, after the optimiser step, using the max logit already computed in the forward pass as a signal. For each head h:

Because a logit is linear in both Wq and Wk, the scaled weights multiply every logit of that head by γhαγh1−α = γh, which brings the max logit back to exactly τ. That step's forward and backward passes are unchanged.

Clipping is per head, not per layer, because only a few heads blow up; a naive version that clips every head by the layer's worst would shrink healthy heads too. On MLA each head's logit is the sum of a content part and a rotary part, and the rotary key is shared across heads, so the rule becomes: scale the head-specific content query and key by √γh each, scale the head-specific rotary query by γh, and leave the shared rotary key alone so that clipping one head does not touch the others. Both parts of that head's logit then scale by γh.

Try: the third head reports 150 against τ = 100 (the worked example): γ = 0.667, its content query and key are each multiplied by 0.816, and its max logit returns to 100; a head at 80 is untouched. Switch to the naive rule and every head shrinks by the worst head's factor. The logits are illustrative.

MuonClip is the combination, and the headline result is what it removed: K2's 15.5T-token pretraining run of a model of 1.04T parameters showed no loss spikes at all (K2 report, Fig. 3), which at that scale means no weeks lost restarting from checkpoints. QK-Clip was only transiently active: in the first 70,000 steps 12.7% of attention heads triggered it at least once and were clamped to 100; after that every head's max logit fell below 100 on its own and the clip never fired again, so the finished model is not shaped by it (Appendix D). The same appendix's small-scale ablations, including an aggressive τ = 30, showed no statistically significant loss penalty.

K3 keeps it, per head. K3 trains with Per-Head Muon "together with the weight-clipping mechanism introduced in Kimi K2", weight decay 0.1 and a cosine schedule (K3 report, §3.3). Per-Head Muon splits the Q, K and V projections' momentum matrices along the head dimension and orthogonalises each head's block separately, because full-matrix orthogonalisation lets heads with larger gradient scales dominate the shared update direction while small-scale heads are under-normalised; per head, every head gets an equal-scale update, and Newton-Schulz on tall per-head blocks is slightly cheaper (§2.5).

Adoption since is checkable: DeepSeek V4 trains most weight matrices with Muon (see DeepSeek), Zhipu's GLM line also uses Muon-family optimisers (Zhipu: GLM), and K2 itself is fine-tuned with Muon, which Moonshot recommends for a Muon-pretrained checkpoint. The generic optimiser family (SGD, momentum, Adam, AdamW) is on Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers, and the SVD and orthogonal matrices behind UV⊤ on Linear algebra for ML.

Kimi Delta Attention and the linear/full hybrid

The problem. Full softmax attention keeps every past key and value, so its cache grows with context and every decoded token reads all of it. Linear attention removes the softmax so the layer becomes a recurrence over a fixed-size state matrix: each token writes its key-value outer product in, the query reads out, and memory per token is constant. The catch is capacity: a fixed state holds finitely many associations, writes superimpose, and precise recall of something far back degrades. LLM Architecture Gallery owns the general mechanism, with the plain linear-attention recurrence and Gated DeltaNet (GDN), the design KDA extends.

KDA, from the Kimi Linear paper (arXiv 2510.26692), is a gated delta-rule linear attention. Delta rule: each write first subtracts what the state currently retrieves for that key, so a new association overwrites the old one instead of accumulating on top of it. Gated: learned decay, so old content fades rather than occupying capacity forever. KDA's contribution over GDN is the gate's granularity. GDN, like Mamba-2, has one forget rate per head per token, a scalar; KDA gives every key channel its own forget rate, a diagonal matrix, so the layer can keep one feature for thousands of tokens while flushing another at once. In the gallery's Gated DeltaNet layout (the paper stores the transpose):

Read right to left inside the first term: decay each key channel by its own rate, then erase what is stored under kt; the second term writes vt there. The transition is a diagonal-plus-low-rank (DPLR) matrix of a constrained form, and that constraint is what lets Moonshot's chunkwise-parallel kernel run on tensor cores with substantially less computation than a general DPLR kernel. Kimi Linear also adds a low-rank sigmoid output gate and a short convolution (kernel 4) on the queries, keys and values, both of which its ablations keep: without the gate validation perplexity rose from 5.65 to 5.67, with a swish gate instead to 5.81, without the convolution to 5.70.

The hybrid. Kimi Linear interleaves KDA with full attention layerwise, three KDA layers then one full MLA layer. The ratio was ablated against the same 1.4T-token recipe: 3:1 had the lowest validation perplexity (5.65); 7:1 matched its training loss but generalised worse (5.70); 1:1 was about as good (5.66) at a higher inference cost; pure full attention was worst (5.77), and 15:1 worse still (5.82). The full layers keep an exact, lossless recall path for retrieval and copying; the KDA layers carry most of the depth at constant memory.

Kimi Linear Table 1 (arXiv 2510.26692). Horizontal axis: share of layers that keep a growing cache, 1/(r + 1) for an r:1 ratio. K3's 24 of 93 is marked for reference; it was not part of the ablation.

The full MLA layers use no positional encoding at all (NoPE): KDA's decay is itself position-aware, so position and recency are handed entirely to the KDA layers. NoPE also lets the MLA layers be run as plain multi-query attention at inference, and removes the RoPE adjustments (frequency-base tuning, YaRN) that long-context training otherwise needs; K3 extrapolates to 1M tokens "without any positional-encoding modification" (K3 report, §3.4). The reported payoff, against a full-MLA baseline trained the same way on 1.4T tokens: up to 75% less KV cache, up to 6 times the decoding throughput at 1M-token context (time per output token 1.84 ms against 11.48 ms, 6.3×), and equal or better quality on short-context, long-context and RL-style evaluations. It costs custom chunked-parallel kernels (needed to use tensor cores at all), and any capability that depends on exact long-range recall now leans on the one-in-four full layers.

What K3 changed in the hybrid (K3 report, §2.1):

  • A bounded decay. Kimi Linear maps the gate logit to a log-decay with g = −eA Softplus(z), which is unbounded below; the chunkwise kernel rescales keys by the reciprocal of the cumulative decay, which can overflow, so the diagonal 16-token tiles needed a slow position-pair path. K3 uses g = gmin Sigmoid(eAz) with gmin = −5 (config gate_lower_bound): every retention factor exceeds e−5 ≈ 0.0067, a 16-token tile's cumulative log-decay stays in (−80, 0), the reciprocal stays below e80 inside BF16's range, and every tile runs as a dense tensor-core matrix multiply.
  • Full-rank gates. KDA's output gate becomes a full-rank projection, y = Wo[Sigmoid(Wgx) ⊙ RMSNorm(õ)], and the MLA layers get the same kind of channel-wise sigmoid gate on their output, hence "Gated MLA".
  • One extra global layer. The 3:1 pattern repeats 23 times (92 layers) and one more Gated MLA layer closes the stack so the final layer always attends globally: 69 KDA plus 24 MLA, 93 layers (config full_attn_layers ends 92, 93).

Worked example: where the 75% comes from. Only full-attention layers grow a cache, so with r KDA layers per full layer the cache is 1/(r + 1) of an all-full stack of the same shape: 1/4 at 3:1, a 75% cut. In K3, 24/93 = 25.8% of the layers cache, about a 74% cut against an all-MLA 93-layer stack, plus a fixed-size KDA state per request that does not grow with context: 96 heads × 128 × 128 = 1,572,864 numbers per KDA layer, 108.5 million over 69 layers. That state is why the cut is "up to": at 1M tokens it is 74.0% counted in numbers, 73.8% in bytes if the state is kept in FP32, but below a few thousand tokens the hybrid holds more than an all-MLA stack would. The arithmetic, with the state's precision as an exposed assumption, is on Parameters and memory.

More experts, same active count. Moonshot fitted a sparsity scaling law with Muon: holding active experts at 8 (plus 1 shared) and FLOPs constant, raising the total expert count kept lowering loss. To reach a validation loss of 1.5, sparsity 48 needed 1.69×, 1.39× and 1.15× fewer FLOPs than sparsity 8, 16 and 32 (K2 report, §2.3). K2 stopped at 48 (8 of 384) as the point where the gain stopped paying for the infrastructure complexity. K3 goes to 896 experts with 16 active, sparsity 56, under the name Stable LatentMoE, which the card credits (together with KDA, AttnRes and refined training and data recipes) for an approximate 2.5× improvement in overall scaling efficiency over K2. With hundreds of small experts the costs on the MoE page sharpen: load imbalance, all-to-all traffic per token, every expert resident in memory, and each expert seeing proportionally fewer tokens.

Half the heads. DeepSeek-V3 used 128 heads, about twice its layer count. Moonshot measured that doubling heads from 64 to 128 lowered validation loss by only 0.5% to 1.2%, while at 128k context it raised inference FLOPs by 83% (with 384 experts fixed), and agentic workloads live at long context. K2 took 64. K3's card lists 96.

Worked example: two sparsity numbers. K2's expert sparsity is 384 / 8 = 48, but its parameter ratio is 1.04T / 32.6B = 31.9, because attention, embeddings and the shared expert are always active. K3: 896 / 16 = 56 for experts, 2.8T / 104B = 26.9 for parameters (26.7 with the report's 2.78T / 104.2B). K3 is sparser than K2 by the first measure and less sparse by the second; quote which one you mean.

Stable LatentMoE: what the report describes

The card names Stable LatentMoE without describing it; K3's technical report does (K3 report, §2.3). In a conventional MoE each selected expert receives the full d-wide token, so communication and expert-weight traffic grow with the number of experts each token visits. LatentMoE separates the two widths: shared experts keep a full-width path, while routed experts work in a compact latent of width ℓ (3,584, half of 7,168):

At 896 experts two failure modes of the plain design get worse, and "Stable" names the three fixes. The routed path chains W↓, a gated expert network and W↑ into nearly four consecutive matrix multiplies, which at 2.8T scale produced exploding internal activations; an RMSNorm before the up-projection (which also improved validation loss and benchmarks) and SiTU-GLU, a SwiGLU whose gate and up branches are each soft-capped with β tanh(x/β) (β1 = 4, β2 = 25, so the output is bounded by β1β2 = 100 while it tracks SwiGLU near the origin), suppress it. Balancing nearly 103 experts exceeds the regime where DeepSeek's fixed-step bias update behaves, so K3 uses Quantile Balancing: each expert's routing bias is set directly from the quantile of router-score margins that would give it exactly its target load, q = mk/n tokens.

DeepSeek-V3's rule instead nudges each bias by a fixed γ towards the mean load, trading slow adaptation against oscillation.

Attention Residuals: attention across depth

Attention Residuals (arXiv 2603.15031, March 2026) change the residual stream itself. In a standard PreNorm transformer each layer adds its output to a running sum, hl = hl−1 + fl−1(hl−1), so layer l sees the embedding plus every earlier output with weight exactly 1. Two problems follow: the sum's magnitude grows roughly linearly with depth, diluting each new layer's contribution (later layers learn ever larger outputs to be heard, which can destabilise training), and no layer can pick out one earlier layer's output once it has been summed in. Moonshot's observation is that this is the depth-wise analogue of an RNN compressing a sequence into one state, and the fix is the one attention brought to sequences:

𝐡l=∑i=0l−1αi→l𝐯i,αi→l=exp(𝐰l⊤RMSNorm(𝐯i))∑j=0l−1exp(𝐰l⊤RMSNorm(𝐯j))
hl
the input to layer l
v0, vi
the token embedding, and the output fi(hi) of layer i (attention and MLP sublayers count as separate layers; K3's checkpoint has one pseudo-query for each)
αi→l
the weight layer l puts on source i
wl
a single learned pseudo-query per layer, a d-dimensional vector

The RMSNorm on the keys stops a large-magnitude layer from dominating the weights by size alone. The weights depend on the token, because the vi do, so different tokens can retrieve different depths. The cost per layer is one d-dimensional vector of parameters. The pseudo-queries start at zero, so training begins with uniform weights: at layer 4, the four sources each get 0.25, which is the standard residual sum divided by 4, a scale the next RMSNorm removes, so the model starts out behaving like a standard transformer.

At scale every layer's output would have to be kept and, under pipeline parallelism, shipped between stages, so production uses Block AttnRes: layers are grouped into about 8 blocks, outputs inside a block are summed as usual, and the softmax runs only over the block summaries and the embedding, cutting memory and communication from one vector per layer to one per block. Moonshot reports Block AttnRes matching a baseline trained with 1.25× more compute, under 4% end-to-end training overhead with pipeline parallelism, under 2% inference latency overhead, and bounded hidden-state magnitudes with gradients spread more evenly across depth. It was validated on the Kimi Linear 48B architecture over 1.4T tokens before K3 adopted it. K3 partitions its 93 layers into 8 blocks of 12 (the last block partial, 9 layers), "9 total blocks when counting the embedding" (K3 report, §2.2), and its EAGLE-3 draft model reads features from the 1st, 4th and final blocks.

Low precision as a release format, not a post-processing step

Moonshot ships its models natively quantised, a strategic choice rather than a convenience: the published checkpoint is the deployable one, trained to tolerate its own precision. The formats themselves (integer and block-scaled floating-point grids, post-training quantisation against QAT, and the hardware that runs them) are on Quantization and Precision. Weight memory is parameters times bits per weight divided by 8:

bytes=∑partsNpart×bpart8
Npart
the parameter count of each part of the model
bpart
the bits stored per weight in that part, including any share of a per-block scale

Worked example: K3's weights. At BF16, 2.78 × 1012 × 2 = 5.56 TB. MXFP4 stores 4-bit elements plus one 8-bit scale per block of 32, so b = 4 + 8/32 = 4.25. But only the routed experts are MXFP4: 2,722,740,830,208 of them, 98% of the parameters; attention, the latent projections, shared experts, routers, embeddings and the vision encoder stay in higher precision (K3 report, §4.1.4; config ignore list). So the weights take 2.7227 × 1012 × 4.25/8 + 57.18 × 109 × 2 = 1.561 TB, which is the checkpoint's published size to the byte (safetensors index): about 28% of BF16, the difference between a model a few labs can run and one a well-equipped team can serve. An earlier version of this page put every weight at 4.25 bits and got 1.49 TB. Every scheme, and K2's, is on Parameters and memory.

INT4 QAT, K2 Thinking. Moonshot applied quantisation-aware training in the post-training stage, with weight-only INT4 on the MoE components, which hold nearly all the parameters (model card). The forward pass sees the 4-bit weights while training continues, so the model adapts to the coarse grid instead of being rounded onto it afterwards. It matters most for reasoning models: post-training quantisation introduces small per-step errors, and long chains of dependent reasoning steps compound them, so the loss shows up far more in long chains than in short answers. Moonshot reports a lossless 2× speed-up in low-latency mode, since decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and the expert weights are a quarter of their BF16 size; all its benchmark results are reported at INT4.

MXFP4, K3. K3 ships MXFP4 expert weights with MXFP8 activations, trained in with QAT from the SFT stage onward and through RL, with rollout and training sharing the same quantisation so there is no train-inference mismatch (model card; report §4.1.4). Block-wise scaling is what makes 4 bits survivable: an outlier distorts only its own 32-value block instead of crushing the whole tensor's resolution, and the format has native tensor-core support on Blackwell-class hardware, so it is fast rather than merely small. Shipping the flagship as an MXFP4 checkpoint is how a 2.8T model gets to be practically open-weight.

Post-training: manufacturing agentic data, and rewards where no checker exists

There is very little naturally occurring data for multi-step tool use, so Moonshot manufactures it (K2 report, §3.1.1). The pipeline has three stages:

1. ToolsMore than 3,000 real MCP (Model Context Protocol) tool specs taken from GitHub, plus over 20,000 synthetic tools evolved from a hierarchy of categories and domains (financial trading, robot control, software applications and so on).
2. Agents and tasksThousands of agents, each a generated system prompt with a sampled tool set, and for each agent tasks from simple to complex, every task paired with an explicit rubric: success criteria, expected tool-use pattern and checkpoints.
3. TrajectoriesLLM-simulated users with distinct personas hold multi-turn conversations with the agent; a tool simulator, in effect a world model, executes calls, keeps state across them and injects controlled randomness (partial failures, edge cases). An LLM judge scores each trajectory against its rubric and only successes are kept. Coding and software-engineering tasks run in real sandboxes instead, scored by test pass rates.

The kept trajectories feed supervised fine-tuning; the paper describes the whole thing as large-scale rejection sampling. Reinforcement learning then runs in a Gym-like framework over many tasks with verifiable rewards (tests, checkers), the RLVR recipe explained on RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR. K2's policy objective comes from K1.5:

LRL(θ)=𝔼x∼D[1K∑i=1K(r(x,yi)−r‾(x)−τlogπθ(yi∣x)πold(yi∣x))2]
x, D
a prompt, and the task distribution it is drawn from
y1 … yK
K responses sampled from the previous policy πold
r(x, yi), r̄(x)
response i's reward, and the mean reward of the K samples (a group baseline, as in GRPO)
πθ, τ
the policy being trained, and a regulariser τ > 0 that keeps it near πold

Minimising the squared term pushes each response's log-probability ratio towards its advantage divided by τ. It is optimised with Muon, like everything else in K2.

Three additions keep it stable across domains: a per-task token budget, with responses that exceed it truncated and penalised (RL otherwise inflates lengths everywhere, including where thinking does not pay); an auxiliary loss on hand-picked high-quality data (PTX) so RL does not erase it; and a sampling temperature that starts high for exploration and decays.

Where no automatic checker exists (writing quality, judgement, following a complex instruction), K2 adds the self-critique rubric reward: the model, bootstrapped as a judge during supervised fine-tuning, ranks its own candidate responses by pairwise comparison against rubrics (core values, prescriptive rubrics written to block known reward hacks, and human-written rubrics for specific instructions), and the ranking becomes the reward. It buys reward coverage over the large fraction of real work that is not verifiable, and costs the honesty of the grader, since a policy trained against a judge derived from itself has an obvious incentive to learn what the judge likes. Moonshot's mitigation is to keep retraining the critic on on-policy rollouts from verifiable tasks, so its judgement stays tied to outcomes that can be checked. What defeats that mitigation is any quality the verifiable tasks never exercise, such as style in creative writing, where nothing anchors the critic. The general failure mode is on Reward Hacking.

The thinking variants are additionally trained for interleaved reasoning, alternating reasoning and tool calls rather than thinking once and then acting, which is what agentic browsing benchmarks actually reward. K2 Thinking's card claims coherent behaviour across 200 to 300 consecutive tool calls, where prior models degraded after 30 to 50 (model card). Thinking budgets, test-time compute and how reasoning models are trained in general are on Topic: llms (Deeper: test-time compute).

What K3 added (K3 report, §4). Three stages: SFT on trajectories synthesised by earlier domain-specialised Kimi models; RL that trains one expert per domain (general tasks, general agents, coding agents) at each of three reasoning efforts (low, high, max), nine experts in all; then multi-teacher on-policy distillation, where the student is rewarded per token by the clipped log-ratio of the matching teacher's probability to its own. Effort is trained with a per-problem budget: a trajectory longer than τ · b0(x), with b0 estimated from the cold-start model, gets reward −1, and annealing τ down yields the high and low experts. For non-verifiable tasks an agentic generative reward model reads the output, writes a rubric, scores each candidate and records the scores, with a verbosity budget under which an over-long candidate automatically loses its comparison. Rollouts pause when a fraction of trajectories finish and resume next iteration from resumable microVM sandboxes. K3 always thinks; its API takes reasoning_effort low, high or max (default max), and the full assistant message, reasoning included, must be passed back each turn (model card).

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Muon replaces AdamW everywhere."
It handles 2D hidden-layer matrices only; embeddings, the output head and norm gains stay on AdamW.
"Muon makes the update exactly orthogonal."
Five tuned Newton-Schulz steps leave singular values spread around 1 (roughly 0.7 to 1.2 in practice, inside Jordan's 0.7 to 1.3 target), by design.
"QK-Clip clips logits."
It rescales weights after the step; that step's forward and backward passes are unchanged, and a logit soft-cap is the method that clips logits.
"QK-Clip permanently constrains K2."
It fired only in the first 70,000 steps, on 12.7% of heads, and never afterwards.
"KDA is pure linear attention."
Kimi Linear and K3 are hybrids; one layer in four (24 of 93 in K3) is full MLA, and that is where exact recall lives.
"Per-channel gating is the same as GDN."
GDN forgets with one rate per head; KDA has one rate per key channel.
"A linear-attention layer always uses less memory than a cache."
Its state is fixed, not small: a K3 KDA layer holds 1,572,864 numbers per request, as much as an MLA layer's cache at about 2,700 tokens. The saving appears only at long context.
"Expert sparsity and the total over active ratio are the same number."
K2 is 48 by experts and 31.9 by parameters; K3 is 56 and 26.9, so they rank the two models in opposite orders.
"Attention Residuals are cross-attention to earlier layers' tokens."
They mix whole earlier layer outputs for the same token, with weights set by one learned vector per layer; no token attends to another through them.
"The 4-bit checkpoint is a community quantisation."
The 4-bit weights are the trained release format.
"K3's checkpoint is all 4-bit."
Only the routed experts are MXFP4; the other 57 billion parameters stay in higher precision, which is why the checkpoint is 1.56 TB rather than 1.49 TB.
"K3 is modified MIT like K2."
K3 has its own Kimi K3 License with an extra commercial threshold for Model-as-a-Service businesses.

Where the parameters live

Weights in memory

Bytes are each part's parameters times its bits per weight over 8. MXFP4 is 4.25 bits (4-bit elements plus one 8-bit scale per 32); FP8 with K2's 128 × 128 block scales is about 8 bits.

Cache against context

Per sequence, a Gated MLA layer caches 576 numbers per token (the 512-number latent plus the 64-number shared key part; NoPE means it is not rotated) and a KDA layer holds a fixed 96 × 128 × 128 state plus 3 tokens of short-convolution state for its queries, keys and values. The cache is assumed BF16; the precision of the KDA state is not stated by Moonshot, so it is a switch.

Defaults: the cut at 1,048,576 tokens is 73.8% with an FP32 state (limit 1 − 24/93 = 74.2%), matching the page's "about a 74% cut" by construction from the config. Kimi Linear's "up to 75%" is the same arithmetic at exactly 3:1. Counts per sequence, all heads, all layers.

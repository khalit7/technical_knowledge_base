The bet: research on the boring layers, shipped fast

Moonshot AI is a Beijing lab whose Kimi models are, by most aggregate measures, among the strongest open-weight lines of 2025 and 2026. Its edge is not data scale or distribution. It is a willingness to change what most labs treat as settled: the optimiser, the attention layer and the residual connection.

AdamW, softmax attention and the plain residual sum are inherited without question almost everywhere; Moonshot has replaced all three in production models at trillion scale. Kimi K2 was trained with Muon instead of AdamW ({{K2 report|@k2}}); Kimi K3 replaces softmax attention in most layers with Kimi Delta Attention and the residual sum with Attention Residuals ({{K3 report|@k3rep}}). The other half of the bet is cycle time: Kimi Linear went from paper (October 2025, {{arXiv 2510.26692|@kl}}) to flagship backbone (K3, July 2026) in well under a year, fast for an architecture change that touches every kernel in the stack.

The starting point matters for reading the rest. K2 is architecturally a DeepSeek-V3 derivative: multi-head latent attention (MLA), fine-grained mixture of experts with a shared expert, 61 layers, hidden size 7,168. Almost everything Kimi-specific is a deliberate deviation from that template, each justified by an ablation in the K2 report. Definitions

Muon (MomentUm Orthogonalized by Newton-Schulz): an optimiser for 2D weight matrices that replaces each momentum matrix with an approximately orthogonal matrix before applying it.
QK-Clip: Moonshot's rule for rescaling a head's query and key projection weights after an optimiser step whenever that head's largest attention logit exceeds a threshold.
MuonClip: Muon plus weight decay, Adam-matched update scaling and QK-Clip, the optimiser K2 was pretrained with.
Attention logit: the scaled query-key dot product qi⊤kj / √d that enters the softmax.
KDA (Kimi Delta Attention): a gated delta-rule linear attention with a per-channel forget gate, from the Kimi Linear paper.
Kimi Linear: the 48B-total, 3B-active research model that introduced KDA in a 3:1 hybrid with full MLA layers.
AttnRes (Attention Residuals): a replacement for the residual sum in which each layer takes a learned softmax-weighted mix of earlier layers' outputs.
Sparsity (Moonshot's usage): total experts divided by activated experts, 384 / 8 = 48 for K2. A different number from total over active parameters.
QAT and MXFP4: quantisation-aware training, training with simulated low-precision weights; and microscaling FP4, a 4-bit floating-point format with one shared 8-bit scale per block of 32 values. The generic treatment is on {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}.

MuonClip: Muon, plus a fix for what Muon breaks

Muon is an optimiser for a network's 2D weight matrices (attention and MLP projections); embeddings, the output head and 1D parameters such as RMSNorm gains stay on AdamW. Where AdamW rescales each parameter independently by its own running second moment, Muon treats the momentum buffer as a matrix and approximately orthogonalises it before applying it. A raw gradient matrix is dominated by a handful of large singular directions, so most of an AdamW step's magnitude goes into those while the rest of the spectrum barely moves; replacing every singular value with roughly 1 makes the update push in all directions at comparable scale.

Moonshot's scaling study reports about 2× the computational efficiency of AdamW under compute-optimal training, matching AdamW-trained models with about 52% of the training FLOPs ({{Muon is Scalable for LLM Training, arXiv 2502.16982|@muon}}). Muon also carries less state: one momentum buffer against AdamW's two moment buffers per parameter, real memory in a sharded trillion-parameter run. The rescaled update has root-mean-square 0.2, inside the 0.2 to 0.4 range AdamW updates typically show, which lets Muon reuse AdamW's tuned learning rate and weight decay. Weight decay was the second fix the paper found necessary at scale: without it, Muon converged faster at first but some weights grew too large over long, over-trained runs; with it, Muon beat both vanilla Muon and AdamW.

The orthogonalisation would be exact with a singular value decomposition, M = UΣV⊤ ↦ UV⊤, which is too slow to run on every matrix every step. Newton-Schulz approximates it with matrix products only:

Each step keeps the singular vectors and maps every singular value σ to f(σ) = aσ + bσ3 + cσ5. The coefficients are tuned to pull small singular values up fast rather than to converge exactly: Jordan's design target is a band of 0.7 to 1.3, and after the usual 5 steps the values sit around 0.7 to 1.1 in the example below instead of at exactly 1, which Jordan reports does not hurt the loss.

Defaults reproduce the worked example's iterates exactly, by construction: the same formula with the same coefficients (recompute.py).

The extra cost is a handful of matrix multiplies per weight per step; Jordan estimates the FLOP overhead below 1% for typical language-model training (0.5% for a Llama-405B-scale run). The awkward part is distributed: orthogonalisation needs the whole matrix while the training job holds it sharded across devices, which is why Moonshot published a distributed implementation built on ZeRO-1 sharding ({{arXiv 2502.16982|@muon}}). K3's report replaces the all-gather of the whole parameter buffer with peer-to-peer fetches of only the shards each rank owns, cutting memory and communication ({{K3 report|@k3rep}}, §5.2.2).

QK-Clip: what Muon broke at scale

In a 53B-total, 9B-active test model trained with plain Muon, the largest attention logits passed 1,000 within a few thousand steps; logits that large saturate the softmax and are followed by loss spikes and occasional divergence, and Moonshot saw this more often with Muon than with AdamW ({{K2 report|@k2}}, §2.1). The usual mitigations do not fit K2. A logit soft-cap clips the logits themselves, but the query-key products can still grow without bound before the cap applies. QK-Norm normalises queries and keys inside the forward pass, but it cannot be used with MLA, because MLA never materialises full per-head keys at inference: they are absorbed into the latent projection. QK-Clip acts on the weights instead, after the optimiser step, using the max logit already computed in the forward pass as a signal. For each head h:

Because a logit is linear in both Wq and Wk, the scaled weights multiply every logit of that head by γhαγh1−α = γh, which brings the max logit back to exactly τ. That step's forward and backward passes are unchanged.

Clipping is per head, not per layer, because only a few heads blow up; a naive version that clips every head by the layer's worst would shrink healthy heads too. On MLA each head's logit is the sum of a content part and a rotary part, and the rotary key is shared across heads, so the rule becomes: scale the head-specific content query and key by √γh each, scale the head-specific rotary query by γh, and leave the shared rotary key alone so that clipping one head does not touch the others. Both parts of that head's logit then scale by γh.

Try: the third head reports 150 against τ = 100 (the worked example): γ = 0.667, its content query and key are each multiplied by 0.816, and its max logit returns to 100; a head at 80 is untouched. Switch to the naive rule and every head shrinks by the worst head's factor. The logits are illustrative.

MuonClip is the combination, and the headline result is what it removed: K2's 15.5T-token pretraining run of a model of 1.04T parameters showed no loss spikes at all ({{K2 report|@k2}}, Fig. 3), which at that scale means no weeks lost restarting from checkpoints. QK-Clip was only transiently active: in the first 70,000 steps 12.7% of attention heads triggered it at least once and were clamped to 100; after that every head's max logit fell below 100 on its own and the clip never fired again, so the finished model is not shaped by it (Appendix D). The same appendix's small-scale ablations, including an aggressive τ = 30, showed no statistically significant loss penalty.

K3 keeps it, per head. K3 trains with Per-Head Muon "together with the weight-clipping mechanism introduced in Kimi K2", weight decay 0.1 and a cosine schedule ({{K3 report|@k3rep}}, §3.3). Per-Head Muon splits the Q, K and V projections' momentum matrices along the head dimension and orthogonalises each head's block separately, because full-matrix orthogonalisation lets heads with larger gradient scales dominate the shared update direction while small-scale heads are under-normalised; per head, every head gets an equal-scale update, and Newton-Schulz on tall per-head blocks is slightly cheaper (§2.5).

Adoption since is checkable: DeepSeek V4 trains most weight matrices with Muon (see {{DeepSeek|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}), Zhipu's GLM line also uses Muon-family optimisers ({{Zhipu: GLM|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a88a84c2ca84b4daf4}}), and K2 itself is fine-tuned with Muon, which Moonshot recommends for a Muon-pretrained checkpoint.

How to read this page

None of these labs is trying to top a leaderboard, and reading them as failed frontier labs misses the point. Each one optimises a different constraint, so the question for each is not "how good is it" but "what does it make cheap or possible that a frontier API does not?"

This page covers the long tail that matters. A lab is promoted to its own page if its line becomes central; until then its details live here, said once. Each lab is placed on the same five axes, so a reader can compare them rather than read a list:

1. Constraint

What it optimises: data quality per parameter, memory per token, auditability, distribution, open scale at a low price, bits per weight, openness itself, or not being a single model at all. The sections below follow this axis.

2. Openness

Fully open (weights, training data and code), open weights under MIT or Apache 2.0, open under a custom or non-commercial licence, API only, or closed.

3. Total and active parameters

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model stores every expert but runs only a few per token. Total sets the memory; active sets the compute per token; total ÷ active is the sparsity.

4. Price and cost per task

List price per million tokens, and where Artificial Analysis has measured it, the cost of one task on its Intelligence Index.

5. Who measured it

Vendor only, independent, or mixed. Most numbers on this page are the lab's own, and are labelled so.

The tabs: Compare labs puts every lab in one table on these axes, with filters and sorting; Scale and sparsity plots every model with published sizes over time and on the sparsity lines; Further reading lists the related pages and the best resources.

Data quality per parameter: Microsoft Phi and the small dense models

Phi is the flagship result of one hypothesis: most of a pretraining corpus is noise, and a small model trained on a small amount of extremely high-signal text can match a much larger model trained on the raw web. "Textbook quality" is the label, and concretely it means two things. Web data is filtered aggressively by a classifier that scores documents for reasoning and pedagogical value rather than fluency. And most of the corpus is synthetic: exercises, worked solutions and explanations generated by a larger model, structured so that each example demonstrates a reasoning step rather than merely stating a fact. Phi-1 and Phi-2 proved the effect at 1B to 3B scale ({{Phi-2, 2.7B|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/phi-2-the-surprising-power-of-small-language-models/}}). Phi-4 (14B, December 2024) reached 84.8% on MMLU and beat considerably larger models on maths ({{Phi-4 technical report|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.08905}}), followed by Phi-4-mini (3.8B) and the Phi-4-reasoning variants ({{Phi-4-reasoning|https://huggingface.co/microsoft/Phi-4-reasoning}}).

The cost matters before choosing it. A model whose training distribution is synthesised from benchmark-shaped tasks is strongest exactly on benchmark-shaped tasks. Phi models are consistently weaker on long-tail world knowledge, on messy real inputs and on anything phrased unlike a textbook exercise, and because the synthetic generator has itself seen the evaluations, contamination is hard to rule out. Used as intended, Phi remains the default dense family for local and edge deployment in 2026, under MIT, which is as permissive as licensing gets ({{Phi-4 on Hugging Face|https://huggingface.co/microsoft/phi-4}}).

Two current reference points sit at the small end of the same dense ladder. OpenBMB MiniCPM5-2B (September 2026) is a dense 2.5B Apache 2.0 on-device model averaging 53.9 across 34 benchmarks, the lab's own figure ({{MiniCPM5-2B|https://huggingface.co/openbmb/MiniCPM5-2B}}). Hugging Face SmolLM3 (3B) is valued for its documentation rather than its capability, and appears under openness below.

Microsoft's own position is broader than Phi. Its MAI team trains in-house frontier models (the MAI-1 line) to reduce dependence on OpenAI, while Azure keeps serving everyone's models regardless: the strategy is to own the distribution layer and treat the model layer as replaceable (see distribution).

Memory per token: NVIDIA Nemotron and the hybrid camp

NVIDIA ships open models as demand generation for GPUs: anything that makes it easier to build with language models sells more of them, so the models, datasets and recipes are given away ({{NVIDIA Nemotron|https://developer.nvidia.com/nemotron}}). The Nemotron 3 line has three sizes: Nano (31.6B total, 3.5B active, 15 December 2025, {{model card|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-BF16}}), Super (120B / 12B, {{card|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B-BF16-MTPv2}}) and Ultra (550B / 55B, June 2026, {{card|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B-A55B-BF16}}). The newest small member is Nemotron 3.5 Lightning (30B / 3B, 11 August 2026, OpenMDW-1.1 licence, {{card|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Lightning-30B-A3B-BF16}}). What is technically distinctive is that these are Mamba-2 hybrids rather than transformers.

A state space model (SSM) processes a sequence as a linear recurrence carrying a fixed-size hidden state, so per-token compute and memory are constant whatever the context length. Its two historical problems were that a sequential recurrence runs far slower on a GPU than a parallel matrix multiplication, and that a fixed state cannot choose what to remember. Mamba-2 answers both. The recurrence is selective: its parameters are computed from the current input, so the block decides what to keep and what to discard. And it is formulated so that training executes as a structured matrix multiplication rather than a step-by-step scan, which puts it back on the tensor cores ({{Mamba|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00752}}, {{Mamba-2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21060}}). Hybrid means mostly SSM blocks with a few attention layers interleaved, because a compressed state is bad at exact recall and a few attention layers restore it: the same argument MiniMax reached from the other direction.

The number that matters operationally is the cache. Only attention layers store a key and a value for every past token, so a hybrid's KV cache per token falls from tens or hundreds of KiB in a comparable transformer to a few KiB. For high-throughput agentic serving, where thousands of long-context sequences must be resident at once and memory rather than FLOPs is the limit, that changes deployment economics far more than a couple of benchmark points. The animation grows one sequence through both designs, drawn to one scale.

Both models have about 30B total and 3B active parameters, so the comparison isolates the layer design. Layer counts, heads and state sizes are from each model's config.json ({{Qwen3-30B-A3B|https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-30B-A3B/blob/main/config.json}}, {{Nemotron 3 Nano|https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B-BF16/blob/main/config.json}}); caches in BF16 (2 bytes), Mamba state in float32 as the config sets it. Per token: Qwen 48 × 4 × 128 × 2 × 2 = 96 KiB; Nano 6 × 2 × 128 × 2 × 2 = 6 KiB. The "sequences that fit" counter is arithmetic (free memory ÷ cache per sequence) and ignores activations and framework overhead. Defaults reproduce the claim that a hybrid's cache falls "from hundreds of KiB to a few KiB", independently: Llama 3.1 70B (80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 × 2 × 2) gives 320 KiB, Qwen3-30B-A3B 96 KiB, Nemotron 3 Nano 6 KiB, Nemotron 3 Super 8 KiB, IBM Granite 4.0-H-Small 16 KiB, all from config.json. The animation stops when off screen and starts paused, one step at a time, if your system asks for reduced motion.

What it shows. For a short prompt the hybrid holds more, because 23 Mamba-2 layers each set up a fixed 2 MiB state; the two are level at about 533 tokens. Past that the transformer's cache grows 16 times faster, and at 131,072 tokens it is 12 GiB per sequence against 0.8 GiB. The fixed state is the price of the trick; the saving comes from how few layers keep a per-token cache at all.

NVIDIA also publishes open post-training datasets and reward models (the HelpSteer line). A reward model scores candidate responses so that reinforcement learning can optimise against it, and open ones are rare because they encode a lab's preference data. It also ships Llama-Nemotron distills, Llama bases repackaged with NVIDIA's post-training.

NVIDIA is also buying the place most open weights are distributed from. In September 2026 it agreed to acquire Hugging Face for $12.93 billion, closing expected in the first half of 2027 subject to regulatory approval ({{TechCrunch|https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/03/nvidia-confirms-it-will-buy-hugging-face-for-12-9-billion/}}). Jensen Huang has promised that the platform stays open and that Nvidia compute will not be required to use it, which is a commitment rather than a structural guarantee.

Four other labs sit in the same camp, each with its own reason:

  • IBM Granite 4 (from October 2025): small hybrid Mamba/transformer enterprise models from 3B to 32B under Apache 2.0. Granite 4.0-H-Small is 32.2B parameters with 4 attention layers among 40 ({{model card and config|https://huggingface.co/ibm-granite/granite-4.0-h-small}}). IBM's selling point is the compliance around it, under auditability below.
  • Liquid AI LFM: deliberately non-transformer models for on-device use. LFM (Liquid Foundation Model) blocks are short-range gated convolutions combined with a small number of attention layers, so most of the network mixes information locally at fixed cost and memory does not grow with context as a full KV cache does. LFM2 and LFM2.5 target phones and laptops, where RAM and battery rather than throughput are the binding limits and where quality per FLOP and a small resident footprint decide whether a feature can run locally at all; LFM2-8B-A1B is an 8.3B MoE with 1.5B active, LFM2.5-1.2B appeared in January 2026, under Liquid's LFM Open License ({{LFM2-8B-A1B|https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2-8B-A1B}}, {{LFM2.5-1.2B|https://huggingface.co/LiquidAI/LFM2.5-1.2B-Instruct}}). It is the most credible part of the broadening post-transformer fringe, alongside the SSM hybrids and the text-diffusion experiments.
  • AI21 Jamba combines Mamba layers, attention layers and mixture-of-experts in one architecture, one of the first to ship very long context at a memory footprint a single node could hold (Jamba 1.5 Large, 399B, August 2024, {{card|https://huggingface.co/ai21labs/AI21-Jamba-Large-1.5}}).
  • TII Falcon: the Abu Dhabi institute was one of the earliest credible open-weights providers, with Falcon 40B and 180B and the RefinedWeb corpus, which showed heavily filtered web data could replace curated corpora ({{RefinedWeb|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01116}}). Its current Falcon-H models are hybrid SSMs, up to 34B, under the Falcon LLM licence ({{Falcon-H1-34B|https://huggingface.co/tiiuae/Falcon-H1-34B-Instruct}}).

How to read this page

None of these labs is trying to top a leaderboard, and reading them as failed frontier labs misses the point. Each one optimises a different constraint, so the question for each is not "how good is it" but "what does it make cheap or possible that a frontier API does not?"

This page covers the long tail that matters. A lab is promoted to its own page if its line becomes central; until then its details live here, said once. Each lab is placed on the same five axes, so a reader can compare them rather than read a list:

1. Constraint

What it optimises: data quality per parameter, memory per token, auditability, distribution, open scale at a low price, bits per weight, openness itself, or not being a single model at all. The sections below follow this axis.

2. Openness

Fully open (weights, training data and code), open weights under MIT or Apache 2.0, open under a custom or non-commercial licence, API only, or closed.

3. Total and active parameters

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model stores every expert but runs only a few per token. Total sets the memory; active sets the compute per token; total ÷ active is the sparsity.

4. Price and cost per task

List price per million tokens, and where Artificial Analysis has measured it, the cost of one task on its Intelligence Index.

5. Who measured it

Vendor only, independent, or mixed. Most numbers on this page are the lab's own, and are labelled so.

The tabs: Compare labs puts every lab in one table on these axes, with filters and sorting; Scale and sparsity plots every model with published sizes over time and on the sparsity lines; Further reading lists the related pages and the best resources.

Data quality per parameter: Microsoft Phi and the small dense models

Phi is the flagship result of one hypothesis: most of a pretraining corpus is noise, and a small model trained on a small amount of extremely high-signal text can match a much larger model trained on the raw web. "Textbook quality" is the label, and concretely it means two things. Web data is filtered aggressively by a classifier that scores documents for reasoning and pedagogical value rather than fluency. And most of the corpus is synthetic: exercises, worked solutions and explanations generated by a larger model, structured so that each example demonstrates a reasoning step rather than merely stating a fact. Phi-1 and Phi-2 proved the effect at 1B to 3B scale (Phi-2, 2.7B). Phi-4 (14B, December 2024) reached 84.8% on MMLU and beat considerably larger models on maths (Phi-4 technical report), followed by Phi-4-mini (3.8B) and the Phi-4-reasoning variants (Phi-4-reasoning).

The cost matters before choosing it. A model whose training distribution is synthesised from benchmark-shaped tasks is strongest exactly on benchmark-shaped tasks. Phi models are consistently weaker on long-tail world knowledge, on messy real inputs and on anything phrased unlike a textbook exercise, and because the synthetic generator has itself seen the evaluations, contamination is hard to rule out. Used as intended, Phi remains the default dense family for local and edge deployment in 2026, under MIT, which is as permissive as licensing gets (Phi-4 on Hugging Face).

Two current reference points sit at the small end of the same dense ladder. OpenBMB MiniCPM5-2B (September 2026) is a dense 2.5B Apache 2.0 on-device model averaging 53.9 across 34 benchmarks, the lab's own figure (MiniCPM5-2B). Hugging Face SmolLM3 (3B) is valued for its documentation rather than its capability, and appears under openness below.

Microsoft's own position is broader than Phi. Its MAI team trains in-house frontier models (the MAI-1 line) to reduce dependence on OpenAI, while Azure keeps serving everyone's models regardless: the strategy is to own the distribution layer and treat the model layer as replaceable (see distribution).

Memory per token: NVIDIA Nemotron and the hybrid camp

NVIDIA ships open models as demand generation for GPUs: anything that makes it easier to build with language models sells more of them, so the models, datasets and recipes are given away (NVIDIA Nemotron). The Nemotron 3 line has three sizes: Nano (31.6B total, 3.5B active, 15 December 2025, model card), Super (120B / 12B, card) and Ultra (550B / 55B, June 2026, card). The newest small member is Nemotron 3.5 Lightning (30B / 3B, 11 August 2026, OpenMDW-1.1 licence, card). What is technically distinctive is that these are Mamba-2 hybrids rather than transformers.

A state space model (SSM) processes a sequence as a linear recurrence carrying a fixed-size hidden state, so per-token compute and memory are constant whatever the context length. Its two historical problems were that a sequential recurrence runs far slower on a GPU than a parallel matrix multiplication, and that a fixed state cannot choose what to remember. Mamba-2 answers both. The recurrence is selective: its parameters are computed from the current input, so the block decides what to keep and what to discard. And it is formulated so that training executes as a structured matrix multiplication rather than a step-by-step scan, which puts it back on the tensor cores (Mamba, Mamba-2). Hybrid means mostly SSM blocks with a few attention layers interleaved, because a compressed state is bad at exact recall and a few attention layers restore it: the same argument MiniMax reached from the other direction.

The number that matters operationally is the cache. Only attention layers store a key and a value for every past token, so a hybrid's KV cache per token falls from tens or hundreds of KiB in a comparable transformer to a few KiB. For high-throughput agentic serving, where thousands of long-context sequences must be resident at once and memory rather than FLOPs is the limit, that changes deployment economics far more than a couple of benchmark points. The animation grows one sequence through both designs, drawn to one scale.

Both models have about 30B total and 3B active parameters, so the comparison isolates the layer design. Layer counts, heads and state sizes are from each model's config.json (Qwen3-30B-A3B, Nemotron 3 Nano); caches in BF16 (2 bytes), Mamba state in float32 as the config sets it. Per token: Qwen 48 × 4 × 128 × 2 × 2 = 96 KiB; Nano 6 × 2 × 128 × 2 × 2 = 6 KiB. The "sequences that fit" counter is arithmetic (free memory ÷ cache per sequence) and ignores activations and framework overhead. Defaults reproduce the claim that a hybrid's cache falls "from hundreds of KiB to a few KiB", independently: Llama 3.1 70B (80 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 × 2 × 2) gives 320 KiB, Qwen3-30B-A3B 96 KiB, Nemotron 3 Nano 6 KiB, Nemotron 3 Super 8 KiB, IBM Granite 4.0-H-Small 16 KiB, all from config.json. The animation stops when off screen and starts paused, one step at a time, if your system asks for reduced motion.

What it shows. For a short prompt the hybrid holds more, because 23 Mamba-2 layers each set up a fixed 2 MiB state; the two are level at about 533 tokens. Past that the transformer's cache grows 16 times faster, and at 131,072 tokens it is 12 GiB per sequence against 0.8 GiB. The fixed state is the price of the trick; the saving comes from how few layers keep a per-token cache at all.

NVIDIA also publishes open post-training datasets and reward models (the HelpSteer line). A reward model scores candidate responses so that reinforcement learning can optimise against it, and open ones are rare because they encode a lab's preference data. It also ships Llama-Nemotron distills, Llama bases repackaged with NVIDIA's post-training.

NVIDIA is also buying the place most open weights are distributed from. In September 2026 it agreed to acquire Hugging Face for $12.93 billion, closing expected in the first half of 2027 subject to regulatory approval (TechCrunch). Jensen Huang has promised that the platform stays open and that Nvidia compute will not be required to use it, which is a commitment rather than a structural guarantee.

Four other labs sit in the same camp, each with its own reason:

  • IBM Granite 4 (from October 2025): small hybrid Mamba/transformer enterprise models from 3B to 32B under Apache 2.0. Granite 4.0-H-Small is 32.2B parameters with 4 attention layers among 40 (model card and config). IBM's selling point is the compliance around it, under auditability below.
  • Liquid AI LFM: deliberately non-transformer models for on-device use. LFM (Liquid Foundation Model) blocks are short-range gated convolutions combined with a small number of attention layers, so most of the network mixes information locally at fixed cost and memory does not grow with context as a full KV cache does. LFM2 and LFM2.5 target phones and laptops, where RAM and battery rather than throughput are the binding limits and where quality per FLOP and a small resident footprint decide whether a feature can run locally at all; LFM2-8B-A1B is an 8.3B MoE with 1.5B active, LFM2.5-1.2B appeared in January 2026, under Liquid's LFM Open License (LFM2-8B-A1B, LFM2.5-1.2B). It is the most credible part of the broadening post-transformer fringe, alongside the SSM hybrids and the text-diffusion experiments.
  • AI21 Jamba combines Mamba layers, attention layers and mixture-of-experts in one architecture, one of the first to ship very long context at a memory footprint a single node could hold (Jamba 1.5 Large, 399B, August 2024, card).
  • TII Falcon: the Abu Dhabi institute was one of the earliest credible open-weights providers, with Falcon 40B and 180B and the RefinedWeb corpus, which showed heavily filtered web data could replace curated corpora (RefinedWeb). Its current Falcon-H models are hybrid SSMs, up to 34B, under the Falcon LLM licence (Falcon-H1-34B).

Auditability and on-prem: Cohere and IBM

Cohere sells to enterprises that will not send their data to a public API, and every part of its lineup follows from that (Cohere research). Command R and R+ were the first models explicitly post-trained for grounded generation with citations: given retrieved documents in context, the model answers from those documents and emits inline references to the passages it used, so a reviewer can check each claim against its source and an unsupported sentence becomes visible. That is a materially different objective from ordinary instruction tuning, and it made them the default RAG models for a period (Command R+ was 104B under CC-BY-NC 4.0, Cohere).

Command A (March 2025) is 111B and dense, which sounds backwards until you notice the deployment target. Cohere's card says it is "deployable on just two GPUs" (Command A model card); a dense model has predictable memory and no router, whereas an MoE of equal quality needs every expert resident and a router that complicates multi-GPU serving. Sizing a model to a customer's two-card server is the product decision. The arithmetic adds a caveat the card leaves out: 111B × 2 bytes = 222 GB in 16-bit, more than two 80 GB cards hold, so "two GPUs" holds at 8-bit (111 GB). The flagship has since moved on: Command A+ (May 2026) is a 218B total, 25B active MoE under Apache 2.0 (Command A+ model card), so Cohere now accepts the MoE serving cost too.

Aya, from Cohere Labs, is the open multilingual research line (Aya 23, Aya Expanse, Aya Vision), notable for building instruction data across languages through a large annotator community rather than by machine-translating English datasets, which is what usually leaves "multilingual" models fluent but culturally hollow. Tiny Aya (February 2026, 3.35B, 70+ languages, CC-BY-NC 4.0) pushes that to phone-capable sizes (Tiny Aya).

North is the secure agent platform: agents that run inside the customer's environment against internal systems. North Mini Code (June 2026, 30B total with 3B active, Apache 2.0, 256K context) is Cohere's first open agentic coding model (model card). North Small Translate (September 2026) applies the same instinct to a single task: a 218B total, 25B active MoE built only for translation, with 128 experts and 8 per token, 4,096-token sliding-window attention and a 16K context. It reports 83.6 on WMT26 across 50+ languages against DeepL NextGen's 81.37 and Google Translate's 68.20, on Cohere's own evaluation with GPT-5.6-Sol as judge. It is free on the Cohere API up to rate limits and non-commercial (CC-BY-NC 4.0) for self-hosting, and its 4-bit (W4A4) checkpoint runs on one B200 or two H100s (Cohere docs, Cohere blog, 8 min). A dedicated 218B translation model is a reminder that single-task specialists at mixture-of-experts scale are still economically viable against generalists.

IBM Granite runs on the same architectural logic as Nemotron Nano (a constant-size recurrent state for most layers lets long documents be processed on modest hardware, above). What IBM adds is not capability but the compliance surface: an ISO-certified governance process for how the models were built, published training-data provenance, and indemnification, which is what actually unblocks a bank or a hospital (IBM Granite). Its function-calling quality per parameter is strong, which matters because the enterprise use is structured tool invocation rather than open-ended chat. It is chosen for the paperwork as much as for the model.

Whether a model fits the customer's hardware is a question of two independent levers, and the next chart separates them. Pick a model and a precision:

Weights in memory = parameters × bits ÷ 8 (decimal GB); the KV cache and activations come on top. Compute per token ≈ 2 × active parameters FLOPs, the standard estimate for a forward pass. Red lines are single-card or single-node memory: RTX 5090 32 GB, H100 80 GB, B200 180 GB (a DGX B200's 1,440 GB ÷ 8), 8 × B200. Picking a model with a published hardware claim switches to the precision that claim implies and checks it. Defaults reproduce Prism's "5.9 GB" and "nine times smaller" for Bonsai 2 by construction (27B × 1.76 ÷ 8 = 5.94 GB; 16 ÷ 1.76 = 9.09), and Tencent's 1.56 TB for Hy4 at about 2.03 bytes per parameter, which shows the release is essentially all 16-bit.

What it shows. Bits per weight move a model left and right (memory); active parameters move it up and down (compute per token). The two are independent: North Small Translate stores 218B parameters but computes like a 25B model, and Bonsai 2 keeps all 27B active but stores them in 6 GB.

Distribution and billing: Amazon Nova and Bedrock

Nova is Amazon's closed in-house family, available only through Bedrock. The first generation (December 2024) was a straight capability ladder, Micro, Lite, Pro and Premier, differing in size, latency and price, so an application can route cheap turns to Micro and hard ones to Pro without changing vendor. Two siblings are more interesting. Nova Sonic is speech-to-speech: audio in, audio out, with no transcribe-then-generate-then-synthesise pipeline, which removes the latency of three sequential models and preserves the prosody, interruption and tone that a text bottleneck discards. Nova Act is trained to drive a browser, emitting UI actions (click, type, scroll) against a page rather than prose about it. The Nova 2 generation (from December 2025) is current, with Premier retired in favour of Nova 2 Lite (AWS: Nova 2 Lite, Bedrock model catalog 2026).

Nova itself is competent mid-tier and priced to undercut. Amazon's real position is elsewhere. Bedrock is the aggregator: one API, one bill, one IAM policy and one VPC boundary across models from Anthropic, Meta, Mistral, Cohere and others, which is what large enterprises actually buy. And Trainium, Amazon's in-house training accelerator, is the compute behind Project Rainier for Anthropic, which makes Amazon a supplier of an alternative to Nvidia rather than merely a customer of one. Microsoft's Azure plays the same distribution game (above), and NVIDIA's Hugging Face purchase is the open-weights version of it (above).

Open scale at a low price: Tencent, Xiaomi, StepFun and the 300B to 1T band

Five Chinese labs from the long tail now ship mixture-of-experts models at the size of the frontier band, most of them open. The common pattern is large total size, a modest active count, and prices far below closed models. Their histories matter: Xiaomi and Tencent released open models at this scale long before their current lines, which the Scale and sparsity tab plots over time.

Tencent: Hunyuan and Hy4

Tencent published competitive open weights across text and multimodal for two years without being in the frontier conversation, including large open MoE models well before Hy4: Hunyuan-Large (389B total / 52B active, November 2024, under Tencent's own licence; arXiv 2411.02265) and Hy3 (295B / 21B, Apache 2.0, open weights July 2026 after an April preview; Hy3). The Hy4 preview (August 2026) is what put it in the frontier band: 770B total / 49B active, a context window above 1M tokens, two reasoning levels with high on by default and a no_think switch to turn it off, Apache 2.0, and about 1.56 TB of weights on Hugging Face, with vLLM support landing in v0.29.0 (Tencent, 8 min; Hugging Face; vLLM releases).

Two things are worth carrying. Its sparsity, 770 ÷ 49 = 15.7×, is conservative next to Qwen3.8-Flash-Next's 125 ÷ 6 = 20.8×, so Tencent bought its place in the band with size rather than with an aggressive active-parameter bet, which also makes it expensive to serve for its tier. And it is positioned on real-world productivity rather than benchmark maxima: an internal blind evaluation with 163 experts across 203 engineering tasks scored Hy4 at 2.99 / 4.00 against GLM-5.3 at 2.92 and Kimi K3 at 2.94, inside the noise, but the right shape of claim for a model sold on coding, office work and scientific research. Hy4 is not yet on the Artificial Analysis index; Hy3 scores 25.3 on v4.3 (Artificial Analysis).

The detail that connects it to a larger argument: Tencent says the model took part in the automated optimisation of its own training methods, with autonomous infrastructure work producing a 31.8% throughput gain. That is the same claim shape as Anthropic's recursive-self-improvement report and Zhipu's inference-stack post, three labs within a month, and all three are self-reported by the party that benefits (Simon Willison's hands-on, 10 min).

Xiaomi: MiMo

Xiaomi had been releasing open weights under MIT for most of a year before its current line: MiMo-V2-Flash (309B total / 15B active, December 2025; Hugging Face), then MiMo-V2.5 (310B / 15B; Hugging Face) and MiMo-V2.5-Pro (1.02T / 42B; Hugging Face) in April 2026. So MiMo-V2.6 (22 September 2026) is a consumer-electronics company's third open generation at this scale, not its arrival. MiMo-V2.6-Pro is 1.02T total / 42B active and MiMo-V2.6-Flash 309B / 15B, both MIT with weights on Hugging Face, both with a 1M-token context, and both trained with reinforcement learning as the final stage (Xiaomi release post, Pro weights). During the post-training run Xiaomi published a live dashboard streaming reward and evaluation metrics while it was still going, the first time an active frontier-adjacent post-training run had been observable from outside as it happened.

Xiaomi's own table gives Pro 72.6 on DeepSWE v1.1 (58.4 for V2.5) and 89.9 on Terminal Bench 2.1, while conceding that it loses Terminal-Bench 4.0 to Claude Opus 5 by 14 points (34.9 against 49.0) and trails every closed model on ExploitBench: an unusually honest vendor table. The 2.1 figure is on the older Terminal-Bench scale and does not compare with the 4.0 figures other labs now report (ComputingForGeeks, 10 min). On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v4.3 (methodology v4.3.2, read 1 October 2026), Pro scores 46.3, the highest open-weight score on that version, ahead of GLM-5.3 at max effort on 44.8 and below GPT-6 Sol at max effort on 47.6; Flash scores 37.9 (Artificial Analysis). Launch coverage reported Pro at 46 and first among open weights without naming the index version (Unite.AI). Prices per million tokens: Pro $0.435 input on a cache miss, $0.0036 on a hit and $0.87 output; Flash $0.14, $0.0028 and $0.28. A cache hit on Pro costs 0.83% of a miss (0.0036 ÷ 0.435).

The disclosure worth more than any score is the training cost: the final RL stage ran for under six days across more than 7,000 RL task environments, about 750,000 trajectories in total, at $2.62 million for Pro (about $0.85 million for Flash). That is a published price for the last stage of a trillion-parameter open model, which almost no lab publishes. Two techniques come with it and are the reusable part: the mixture-of-experts router is frozen to keep large-scale RL stable, and layered defences against reward hacking are described rather than alluded to. Recipe detail is on Topic: llm-training-and-post-training.

StepFun: Step 5 Preview

StepFun is a multimodal-first Chinese lab that shipped steadily at second-tier visibility, with open weights before this release too (Step-3, 321B / 38B, Apache 2.0, July 2025; Step 3.5 Flash, 196B / 11B, February 2026; StepFun on Hugging Face). Step 5 Preview (launched 20 September 2026; Artificial Analysis lists 18 September) moved it into the frontier band at 600B total and 27B active, with a 92-layer narrow-deep layout, a 1M-token context, text, image and video input and up to 64K output tokens (Pandaily, StepFun docs). At release that was the smallest active-parameter count among decoder-only open-class models of 600B or more (DeepSeek V3 37B, GLM-5 40B, MiMo-V2.6-Pro 42B, Hy4 49B, Kimi K3 104B). Its sparsity of 600 ÷ 27 = 22.2× is ordinary next to DeepSeek V4 Pro's 1,600 ÷ 49 = 32.7×; the unusual number is the absolute one, since 27B active is what a mid-size dense model costs to decode.

Pricing is the argument: $2.70 per million output tokens, $1.00 input on a cache miss and $0.05 on a hit (StepFun pricing), 2.70 ÷ 15 = 18% of Kimi K3's $15 output price (Kimi pricing). On StepFun's own table it sits with GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3 on most metrics and behind GPT-6 Astra and Claude Opus 5 on the hardest coding work, notably DeepSWE v1.1 at 67.7% against 74.0 to 74.1%, with claimed strength on finance and long-horizon autonomous tasks. Artificial Analysis measures it at 43.7 on v4.3 (Artificial Analysis). StepFun promised open weights for 15 October 2026 with no licence named; at release there were no weights on Hugging Face and no technical report, so it is an API product until that changes.

NaiveAI, Shanghai AI Laboratory and the rest of China's mix

NaiveAI: Naive-N0.5-Flash (27 September 2026, MIT) is 309B total / 15.5B active with a 1M-token context, from a Beijing startup valued at about $1.4B and founded by Tsinghua's Jifeng Dai, and built on Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.5 base (Hugging Face, NaiveAI research). MiMo-V2.5 is 310B in Xiaomi's release, so the sizes match only approximately; the exact 309B match is with MiMo-V2-Flash. Its attention is the unusual part: no full-attention layer at all. Each module is five 128-token sliding-window layers and one DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) layer, which selects the top 2,048 tokens, with the first layer also replaced by DSA: 39 sliding-window and 9 DSA layers across 48, so no layer attends densely to the whole context. Arriving five days after MiMo-V2.6-Flash (309B / 15B), it made two 309B-class open models in one week, which measures how crowded the flash-tier open band has become. The valuation and founder are from launch coverage and are unconfirmed here.

Shanghai AI Laboratory: Atria Dawn Preview (September 2026, MIT) is a 744B agentic MoE built as a post-training layer over an existing open base, Zhipu's GLM-5.2, rather than trained from scratch (GitHub), a release shape worth recognising when it recurs.

The rest of China's open and closed mix: Baidu ERNIE is Baidu's long-running in-house line tied to its search and cloud products; ByteDance's Seed-OSS ships genuinely competitive open weights across text and multimodal, and Doubao is the consumer assistant that dominates Chinese usage by volume, which makes ByteDance's data position the one to watch; Ant's Ling and Ring lines and the Yi bilingual models are second-tier in visibility but ship regularly. inclusionAI Ling 3.0 Flash VL (September 2026) is a flash-tier vision-language model, free on OpenRouter at launch.

Bits per weight: Prism ML Bonsai 2

Prism ML is a compression house rather than a model lab. Its Bonsai 2 27B (17 September 2026) is a ternary rebuild of Qwen3.8 27B: weights drawn from {−1, 0, +1} with FP16 group-wise scaling, applied end to end across the language model, at 1.76 effective bits per weight (Prism ML, 10 min). The footprint follows from the bits alone:

27×109×1.76÷8=5.94 GB

against, in 16-bit

27×109×2=54 GB

The ratio is the "nine times smaller" of its announcement (16 ÷ 1.76 = 9.09). Prism reports it keeps 98.2% of aggregate benchmark performance (83.9 overall; maths 96.57, instruction following 82.66, coding 81.58), all its own figures. It has a 262K context, text and image input and agentic tool use, under Apache 2.0, on Hugging Face and GitHub, and runs on CUDA and MLX at up to 143 tokens per second on an RTX 5090 and 46.8 on an M5 Max. It is the current reference point for how far weight compression goes before quality breaks, and it composes with the open frontier's other lever: fewer active parameters cut compute per token, fewer bits per parameter cut memory, and the two are independent, as the two-lever chart above shows.

Openness as the product: IFM, Prime Intellect, Hugging Face and others

Institute of Foundation Models (Abu Dhabi): K2 Horizon. IFM is the second lab, alongside Ai2, publishing fully open models rather than merely open weights, which puts two such labs on two continents with different funders where there was one (Ai2: OLMo). K2 Horizon (September 2026) is six Apache 2.0 models at 0.9B, 3.7B, 7B, 32B, a 36B sparse variant (4B active) and 375B (23B active, 512K context), released with weights, training code, training data and methodology: the combination that makes contamination auditing, data ablations and training-dynamics research possible at all (IFM press release, 8 min; K2 Horizon 375B). The family shares two ideas: diffusion distillation, adapters that generate a block of tokens in parallel rather than one at a time, for a claimed 3× speedup (K2-Horizon-7B-Uno; the 3× is from IFM's press release, which blocked re-fetching, so it is unconfirmed here); and Mixture-of-Values attention (MoVA), aimed at reasoning (MoVA 36B). IFM claims state of the art at 0.9B, 3.7B and 7B and open-weight competitiveness at 375B, a vendor claim until rerun independently; Artificial Analysis measures the 375B at 30.5 on v4.3, against 46.3 for MiMo-V2.6-Pro (Artificial Analysis). It is on Hugging Face with vLLM and SGLang support, with API access through Compass, Cerebras and Nebius. Abu Dhabi now funds two distinct open-model lines, this one and TII's Falcon (above).

Prime Intellect: INTELLECT. These are proofs of concept for decentralised training: one model trained across clusters in different places connected by ordinary internet links rather than a datacentre fabric. Synchronising gradients over such links every step is hopelessly slow, so it needs low-communication algorithms that let workers take many local steps and synchronise rarely. INTELLECT-1 (10B, November 2024, Apache 2.0, with data and checkpoints released) used DiLoCo; the animation runs its measured timings against the method it replaced.

Inputs from the INTELLECT-1 technical report, Table 2: 100 inner steps take 38 minutes on an 8 × H100 node; the median all-reduce of the int8 pseudo-gradient takes 103 s (USA), 382 s (USA and Europe) or 469 s (global). Compute utilisation = 38 min ÷ (38 min + all-reduce). Defaults reproduce the report's 95.7%, 85.6% and 83.0% independently (the global case computes to 82.9%, a rounding difference). The sync-every-step run is this page's extrapolation, labelled as such: the fp32 gradient is 4 times the int8 payload (10B × 4 bytes = 40 GB), and its all-reduce is assumed to take 4 times as long. Communication falls 100 × 4 = 400 times. The animation stops when off screen and starts paused if your system asks for reduced motion.

What it shows. Synchronising every step over internet links leaves GPUs idle about 95% of the time (5.2% utilisation in the USA case); DiLoCo keeps them busy 83 to 96% of the time by synchronising once per 100 steps and sending a quarter of the bytes. The models are not competitive on quality; the point is that frontier-scale training need not be geographically centralised, which matters strategically far more than the checkpoints do.

Hugging Face SmolLM3 (3B, July 2025, Apache 2.0) is a small model whose value is documentation rather than capability. Every stage of the recipe, the data mixture and the ablations are published, making it the reference for how a competent 3B-class model is actually built (SmolLM3 blog, model). It is also a usable reference for NoPE (no positional encoding) in a fraction of layers: those layers get no explicit position signal at all and rely on the causal mask alone to convey order, a choice associated with better generalisation beyond the trained context length.

Arcee AI Trinity Large (January 2026; about 398B total, often rounded to 400B, with about 13B active, 4 of 256 experts per token, OpenMDW-1.1 licence, model card, technical report) is a rare US startup attempt at open-weight frontier scale, from a company otherwise known for model merging and small-model distillation. It is interesting as evidence about whether a small team can still reach that scale, more than for the model itself.

Nous Research Hermes: post-training specialists who take open bases and produce instruct and reasoning variants with their own data and recipes. They publish datasets and methodology, so the Hermes line is a usable control for "how much of this model's behaviour came from post-training".

Upstage Solar popularised depth up-scaling: duplicate a slice of an existing base model's layers to build a deeper network, then continue pretraining so the seams heal, producing a larger model without training one from scratch. SOLAR 10.7B took two copies of a 32-layer base, dropped 8 layers from each, and joined them: 2 × (32 − 8) = 48 layers (SOLAR paper). Reka builds natively multimodal models (text, image, audio and video in one encoder path) from a small team. Reka, AI21 and Upstage are niche but recur in evaluations.

Not a single model: orchestrators and structured-decision models

Two 2026 products from the long tail are sold as models and are not models in the sense the rest of this page uses the word, and each moves the question a deployment answers.

Sakana AI: Fugu

Fugu Max and Fugu Ultra v2 (10 to 11 September 2026; a first Fugu Ultra appeared in June) are a learned orchestrator. Fugu reads an incoming query, builds an agentic scaffold for it on the fly, and routes the work across a pool of other models behind one OpenAI-compatible API. It was trained by combining fine-tuning, evolutionary search and reinforcement learning, drawing on two ICLR 2026 papers: TRINITY, which assigns participating models Thinker, Worker or Verifier roles, and The Conductor, which learns coordination strategies by RL rather than having them specified.

Fugu Max is the cost-optimised configuration at $2 input and $6 output per million tokens, an output price Sakana puts 40 to 60% below Sonnet 5, GPT-5.6 Terra and Kimi K3, and on the cost-performance Pareto frontier for 7 of 10 benchmarks. Fugu Ultra v2 targets hard reasoning, at $5 and $30 on OpenRouter (OpenRouter): Chartography 48.3 against Claude Opus 5's 27.3, DeepSWE (version not stated) 74.3, best or joint-best on 5 of 8 benchmarks. All are Sakana's own figures; closed weights; not available in the EU or EEA (Sakana, 10 min).

Why track it: if a routed ensemble sold as one endpoint holds the Pareto frontier, "which model" stops being the question a deployment answers and "which orchestrator" replaces it, the same boundary shift ARC Prize's Provider Adapter produced from the benchmark side (on Topic: benchmarks). The scaffold rather than the checkpoint is the subject of Topic: agentic-harnesses.

TypeSafe AI: Jev

Jev (early access 15 September 2026) is what TypeSafe calls a "System One model". It does not generate prose: it takes unstructured state and returns type-safe structured values with calibrated confidence scores, sampling outputs in parallel rather than one token at a time, so a type error is impossible by construction rather than caught by validation.

TypeSafe claims it is 40× to 200× faster than frontier models on comparable tasks, 70 to 500 ms against 3 to 329 seconds, with input at $0.042 per million tokens and output free. The training method is the part worth tracking: Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions, optimising for honest probability estimates on structured decisions rather than for human preference, a different objective from both RLHF and RLVR. Founded by Diogo Almeida, formerly of OpenAI. A Jev Router appeared on OpenRouter on 25 September (OpenRouter). Everything is vendor-reported with no independent evaluation, and a 1,282-point Hacker News thread the same week claimed prior art on non-autoregressive decision models trained with RL, so the novelty of the category is contested (TypeSafe, 15 min).

Why track it: if a large share of production model calls are really structured-decision calls wearing a text interface, this is a different product shape rather than a smaller model.

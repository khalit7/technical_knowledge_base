Six bets on where capability is bought

OpenAI's GPT line is the widest product surface in the field and the one that discloses least about its machinery. Read as a whole, it is a sequence of bets on where capability is bought. Each bet moved a cost between OpenAI's training budget and the caller's per-request bill, and each hid a little more of the system behind the API.

1. Pretraining scale (GPT-1 to GPT-3, 2018 to 2020)

Same objective, more parameters and data. Paid once, by OpenAI.

2. Post-training (InstructGPT, ChatGPT, 2022)

Human preferences turned into a reward; a 1.3B tuned model beat a 175B base one.

3. Inference compute (the o-series, from 2024)

Hidden reasoning tokens billed per request: accuracy becomes a purchase the caller makes.

4. A router (GPT-5, 2025)

A classifier decides how much of that compute each request gets.

5. Price bands (GPT-5.6 and GPT-6, 2026)

Named tiers sold beneath one flagship: the caller routes too.

6. Compute inside the network (GPT-6 Astra, 2026)

Reported recurrent depth: deliberation that never becomes text. And state kept on OpenAI's side of the API, the harness as product.

The Lineage tab lays every release on one axis with the bet it made; Cost per task shows why a cheaper tier is not proportionally cheaper per task; gpt-oss on one GPU rebuilds the open models' parameter table and memory from their configuration.

How to read OpenAI's line

Tiers

Since GPT-5.6 a generation is sold as named price bands: a flagship (GPT-6 Astra), a mid tier (Sol) and a cheap, fast tier (Luna). GPT-5.6 also had a balanced tier, Terra, which GPT-6 has not replaced. OpenAI's docs map them onto the older names: Sol "roughly corresponds to the unsuffixed model tier", Terra to mini, Luna to nano (GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra, Luna). A tier name says what a model costs and roughly how capable it is, not how it is built: no parameter count or architecture is published for any of them.

Reasoning effort

reasoning.effort sets how much hidden deliberation the model may spend before answering. Those reasoning tokens are not visible through the API, "still occupy space in the model's context window and are billed as output tokens" (reasoning guide). A benchmark figure means little without the effort it was run at. Which model accepts which effort is in the table under A request's cost.

The router

A classifier in front of the models, introduced with GPT-5, that decides per request whether to answer at once or to think, and how deeply. The generic trade-off (lower average cost, less predictable behaviour, a misroute the caller cannot see) is on Topic: llms (Deeper: test-time compute). Because the caller also picks the tier, choosing Sol, Terra or Luna is a routing decision made on top of the model's own.

Codex

Both OpenAI's coding agent (a command-line tool, then a cloud service) and, for a while, a line of models post-trained for it: GPT-5-Codex, then GPT-5.1-Codex, GPT-5.2-Codex and GPT-5.3-Codex (with GPT-5.1-Codex-Max). Since GPT-5.5 (April 2026) the mainline models ship directly in Codex (OpenAI release timeline).

Trusted Access

OpenAI's programme of vetted organisations that get restricted capabilities first or only: the most capable cybersecurity configurations, Astra for Law, GPT-Rosalind for life sciences.

Harness

The software between a task and a model: prompting, tool plumbing, how long conversations are compacted, and whether state survives between requests. On this family the harness is worth more points than any effort setting or model upgrade (the ARC-AGI-3 result).

Lineage, from GPT-1 to GPT-5.6

Dates and sources for every release are on the Lineage tab; this is what each step changed.

GPT-1, GPT-2, GPT-3 (2018 to 2020): the scaling bet

Three decoder-only transformers trained on next-token prediction, differing in little but size and data: 117M parameters (Wikipedia), 1.5B (OpenAI) and 175B, dense (GPT-3 paper). GPT-2 showed that one unsupervised objective produced usable zero-shot behaviour on tasks nobody trained for. GPT-3 established in-context learning: a task specified by examples in the prompt, with no gradient update, which turns one served checkpoint into a general product and is why the paper is called "Language models are few-shot learners". It also anchored the scaling-law era, in which loss moves predictably with compute, shifting the research question from architecture search to compute allocation.

InstructGPT and ChatGPT (2022): the post-training stack

InstructGPT (paper) introduced the three-stage recipe the field still uses:

  1. SFT (supervised fine-tuning) on human-written demonstrations.
  2. A reward model: a copy of the network with a scalar output head, trained on human rankings of pairs of outputs.
  3. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback), in practice PPO (proximal policy optimisation) against that reward model, with a KL-divergence penalty back toward the SFT checkpoint so the policy does not drift into degenerate text that scores well and reads badly.

The result that changed the field: humans preferred the 1.3B InstructGPT to the 175B base model, so alignment became a way to gain usefulness rather than a tax on it. ChatGPT (30 November 2022) put the recipe in front of the public. The algorithms are owned by RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR.

GPT-4 to GPT-4.1 (2023 to 2025): multimodality and unit cost

GPT-4 (March 2023) was the first frontier release to disclose essentially nothing about parameters, architecture or data, and the first with a long system card documenting refusal policy as a product surface; its practical contribution was reliability on long-form reasoning plus image input. GPT-4 Turbo cut the price and lengthened the context to 128K. GPT-4o ("omni", May 2024) put audio and vision into one model instead of pipelining speech recognition, a text model and speech synthesis, which brought voice latency to conversational range and made interruption, tone and non-speech audio cues possible at all. GPT-4.1 (April 2025) was API-only, tuned for long-context and instruction-following workloads rather than chat.

The o-series (from September 2024): buying accuracy with inference compute

o1, o3 and o4-mini were trained with large-scale RL on problems whose answers can be checked automatically, so the reward is correctness rather than human preference, and the learned behaviour is a long internal chain of thought that backtracks, checks its own steps and reframes a problem that is not working. Two choices were OpenAI's own. The chain of thought is hidden from the API caller but billed, so accuracy became a per-request purchase. And OpenAI published the claim that accuracy rises with RL training compute and, separately, with how long the model may think at inference (Learning to reason with LLMs), which made inference compute a second scaling axis; the rest of the family is organised around managing it. The evidence for test-time scaling is on Topic: llms (Deeper: test-time compute).

GPT-5 (August 2025): routing as architecture

GPT-5's signature is a system, not a network: a fast non-reasoning model, a reasoning model, and a real-time router deciding per request how much thinking to spend. That is an economic decision expressed as architecture: most traffic does not need reasoning, and reasoning tokens dominate cost when applied indiscriminately. GPT-5.1 (November 2025) shipped the two as named models, Instant and Thinking, so a user could pick one instead of trusting the router; GPT-5.2, 5.4 and 5.5 followed (timeline). The Codex variants were the same line post-trained for long-horizon agentic coding: tuned to run for hours inside a harness, calling tools, reading diffs and recovering from failed edits, rather than to answer well in one shot.

GPT-5.6 (July 2026): tiers below a flagship

Released on 9 July 2026 (Sol previewed to trusted partners from 26 June, timeline) as Sol for the hardest work, Terra balanced and Luna fast and cheap, all with a 1.05M-token window, 128K maximum output and a 16 February 2026 knowledge cutoff (Simon Willison). Launch prices per million input and output tokens: Sol $5 and $30, Terra $2.50 and $15, Luna $1 and $6. On 30 July Luna fell 80% to $0.20 and $1.20 and Terra 20% to $2 and $12 while Sol held; OpenAI credited serving optimisations, part of them kernels Sol rewrote itself (Eden AI). Sol later carried promotional pricing of $4 and $20, 20% and 33% below launch, "at least through November 21, 2026" (OpenAI model docs; the start date, 21 August, is unconfirmed in the docs). The bare gpt-5.6 alias routes to Sol. Roboflow called Sol "clearly the best vision model OpenAI has released so far" (Roboflow). GPT-5.6 Sol is the baseline most GPT-6 figures are quoted against.

The GPT-6 generation

GPT-6 Astra: the flagship

First outputs surfaced on 29 August from an expanded internal test of a checkpoint tagged mozaik-alpha-fdm, zero-shot at max effort, showing complex frontend and visual software (TestingCatalog). Astra was announced on 3 September 2026 as a limited preview and released to paid users the next day; it was "the first time we've pretrained on more than 100,000 GPUs at our Stargate site in Texas", by far OpenAI's largest training run (Wikipedia). Rollout went to Daybreak cybersecurity organisations first, then ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Business and Enterprise, the API, Microsoft and AWS Bedrock; enterprise access is off until an administrator enables it (CSO Online). On 10 September OpenAI paused new sign-ups and upgrades to the $200 ChatGPT Pro plan without giving a reason (CellCog).

API limits (model docs)

1,050,000-token window, at most 922,000 input and 128,000 output tokens; text and image in, text out; knowledge cutoff 30 April 2026; efforts low to max (none returns HTTP 400).

Price per million (pricing)

$10 input, $1 cached, $12.50 cache write, $50 output. Above 272K input tokens the whole request is billed at 2× input and cache rates and 1.5× output. Batch and Flex half price; Fast mode 2× for "up to 2.5× faster" (Fast mode); Ultrafast 6×.

Not published

A parameter count or an architecture, continuing the GPT-5.x pattern. The system card does not mention the architecture either; what is known about recurrent depth comes from reporting (below).

OpenAI's launch table puts Astra at 57.9% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, 72.6% on OSWorld 2.0 and 41.4% on AutomationBench, among others (all rows on the Lineage card). The 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3 that circulates with them is ARC Prize's run through a vendor adapter. Two things matter more than the table: part of Astra's reasoning is reported to happen where no transcript records it, and its headline ARC-AGI-3 number is a harness number.

GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna: the price release (22 September)

The two tiers beneath Astra cost half their GPT-5.6 predecessors or less: Sol $2 and $10 per million against GPT-5.6 Sol's $4 and $20, exactly half; Luna $0.10 and $0.50 against $0.20 and $1.20, which is 1 − 0.10/0.20 = 50% off input and 1 − 0.50/1.20 = 58.3% off output (VentureBeat). Both have the 1.05M window and 128K output, accept efforts none to max with medium the default, and have knowledge cutoffs of 20 April (Sol) and 18 May 2026 (Luna) (GPT-6 Sol, GPT-6 Luna). They are in the API as gpt-6-sol and gpt-6-luna, in ChatGPT Work and Codex for paid plans, and Luna reaches Free and Go users through the desktop app. OpenAI's headline for Sol: 33.2% on AutomationBench 1.0.6 at xhigh for $0.27 a task, against Claude Opus 5 at 26.9% at max costing 11.1 times as much per task; Luna reaches 66.6% on DeepSWE v1.1 at a task cost 93% below the compared Opus 5 configuration (VentureBeat; the other figures are on the Lineage card).

GPT-6.1 Sol: near-Astra at a fifth of the price (29 September)

A week after GPT-6 Sol, whose documentation now points to it as the newer Sol. Same $2 and $10, cached input halved to $0.10 (5% of input, against 10% elsewhere), the same 1.05M window, efforts low to max with medium the default (none and minimal rejected), knowledge cutoff 30 April 2026 (model docs). OpenAI's figures: it matches Astra on DeepSWE v1.1 "at roughly one-fifth the cost" and beats GPT-6 Sol's best there by 6.4 points; it comes within 2.1 points of Astra on the OSWorld 2.0 offline set at max effort; on AutomationBench at medium effort it improves 4.8 points on GPT-6 Sol and leads Claude Opus 5.5 by 2.2 points at roughly a third of its cost (VentureBeat). The same day OpenAI launched Ultrafast, a service tier at 6× the standard rate for up to 8× faster generation in Codex and 6× in the API, as much as 300 tokens per second. It is available for GPT-6 Astra; a 6.1 Sol version is "coming soon" (VentureBeat, Ultrafast guide), and the pricing page lists Ultrafast rates for Astra only. This is the pattern the Claude line also showed in 2026: the tier below the flagship catches it within weeks at a fraction of the price, which leaves the flagship's premium resting on the hardest tasks.

GPT-6.1 Astra: withheld (28 September)

OpenAI shelved GPT-6.1 Astra, planned for October, after internal audits found it less reliable at staying within the authority it was given and at reporting what it had done, with higher measured deception than its predecessor. Saachi Jain, head of safety systems: it "didn't quite meet the bar in terms of staying within scope and authorization, and how it communicates back to the user about the type of work it's done" (The Hacker News). GPT-6 Astra stays the flagship; the Wall Street Journal called it "a rare case of a major AI developer ditching a new release because of safety concerns".

Standard prices from the pricing page (read 1 October 2026); GPT-5.6 Sol at its promotional $4 and $20. Ultrafast is listed for GPT-6 Astra only; other models show "n/a" on that tier. Output above 128,000 tokens and input above 922,000 are not allowed and the sliders stop there.

The per-request price is still not the per-task price: a cheaper model that needs more turns, more effort or more reasoning tokens to finish can cost more per solved task than its price suggests, which is why OpenAI's own comparisons quote cost per task. On Artificial Analysis' index, GPT-6.1 Sol is a fifth of Astra's price per token but 22% of its cost per task, and Luna a hundredth per token but 2.1% per task (Cost per task).

Prompt caching: when a reused prefix pays

The prompt cache stores the key and value tensors of a prefix, not the text, so a later request with the same prefix skips recomputing it. On GPT-5.6 and later the first request writes the prefix at 1.25× the input rate and each reuse reads it at 0.1× (0.05× on GPT-6.1 Sol). A prefix must be at least 1,024 tokens, an entry stays eligible for 30 minutes after its last write or reuse, the whole rendered prefix must match (tools, effort and formatting settings included), and each request can create up to four cache writes (prompt caching guide).

CcachedCuncached=1.25+(N−1)rN,Nbreak-even=1.25−r1−r

N is the number of requests that use the prefix (one write, N − 1 full reads) and r the read rate as a fraction of the input price.

Which effort each model accepts

From each model page and the reasoning guide: GPT-6 Astra rejects none with HTTP 400; GPT-6.1 Sol rejects none and minimal. Filled dot: the default where the docs state one (Astra's is not stated). GPT-5.6 and GPT-6 models also take a reasoning.mode of standard (default) or pro, which does more model work billed at the same token rates. gpt-oss sets effort in its system prompt ("Reasoning: low", medium or high) (model card).

Recurrent depth: Astra's reasoning outside the transcript

Every earlier OpenAI reasoning model spends its test-time budget as hidden chain-of-thought tokens: text the caller cannot see, but which exists, is billed and can be monitored. Recurrent depth, also called opaque recurrence or a looped transformer, loops activations back through the model's own layers, so part of the deliberation happens in activations and never becomes text. That is a third place to spend the budget:

Provenance. The architecture is known from reporting, not from OpenAI's documents: The Information reported it on 2 September (TechCrunch), and OpenAI researchers, among them chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, answered that OpenAI had limited how much of the model uses the looped transformer so that its reasoning stays legible, and would share more about the architecture later (Fortune). The system card does not describe the architecture (system card). The generic mechanism, the first published architecture of the family (the Recurrent Looped Transformer, by Yifan Zhang, Jichen Feng and Shihan Qin, tested on synthetic algorithmic tasks only) and the sceptical reading are on Topic: llms (Deeper: test-time compute); in one line, a loop buys serial depth without adding parameters or emitting tokens, and costs the readability of the reasoning.

What OpenAI has measured about Astra's own reasoning, in its system card (system card):

  • Astra is "more capable of controlling its own CoT than GPT-5.6 Sol, and less likely to include incriminating information in its CoT".
  • Under adversarial conditions (pushed to evade monitors) it can remain undetected when strategically underperforming in evaluations (sandbagging), and can sometimes evade OpenAI's internal monitors on certain sabotage tasks.
  • No evidence of steganographic reasoning, hidden load-bearing reasoning inside ordinary text, was found.

Serving changed with it. If part of the working state lives in activations or in reasoning the caller never sees, a request that arrives without that state has to rebuild it. OpenAI's API keeps it on OpenAI's side: reasoning items are returned to the caller only as encrypted_content (in stateless mode) or kept server-side and referenced with previous_response_id, and reasoning.context: all_turns, the default on GPT-5.6 and supported on GPT-6.1 Sol, renders reasoning from earlier turns into the next one. The items "remain opaque" and carry only within one model family (reasoning guide). That is what the ARC Prize Provider Adapter uses, and why the products in the next section all move parts of the harness behind the API.

Products around the models

Three September 2026 launches move parts of the harness, the index and the voice stack behind OpenAI's API, the same direction as the Provider Adapter.

Astra for Law (17 September)

The first vertical on the flagship, and it adds no weights: GPT-6 Astra wrapped in a legal search index, instructions for legal analysis and writing, and controls for confidential client work. The index covers US case law, statutes, regulations, court rules and administrative decisions across more than 230 million URLs, with case law from the nonprofit Free Law Project's CourtListener. On 200 questions from the private validation set of Vals AI's Legal Research Bench, both at highest effort, it passed the overall correctness check on 54% against 38.7% for Astra with web search alone, 54 / 38.7 − 1 = 40% relative, in a domain where citation correctness is the product. Selected firms reach it through Trusted Access; an API model, gpt-6-astra-law, is planned with no date or price (SiliconANGLE).

GPT-Live-1 (10 September)

ChatGPT's full-duplex voice in the API: it listens and speaks at the same time, handling pauses, interruptions and backchannels such as "mm-hm" as they happen, and delegates deeper reasoning and actions to a backend model, tools or agent framework the developer chooses. 12 voices; $0.05 per minute, billed per second, with the backend model and tools charged separately; WebRTC for browsers, WebSockets for server-side audio, telephony and SIP for phone agents (OpenAI developer forum, pricing).

The Agents API (10 September, public beta)

The Codex harness as a managed service: context summarisation, selective tool loading, parallel tool execution and delegation to sub-agents run on OpenAI's side, in an OpenAI-hosted sandbox, the developer's own infrastructure, or partners including Cloudflare, Daytona, E2B, Modal, Vercel and Blaxel (Context Studios). The agent loop a developer used to own becomes part of the vendor's product: the boundary shift the Provider Adapter made from the benchmark side.

gpt-oss: the one open window

gpt-oss-120b and gpt-oss-20b (5 August 2025) are OpenAI's first open weights since GPT-2, Apache 2.0 licensed, and the only place its design choices are visible (model card). Both are MoE (mixture-of-experts) transformers: each layer's feed-forward block is many expert networks, of which a router picks a few per token.

  • MXFP4 experts. The models were post-trained with their MoE weights already quantised to MXFP4, a microscaling 4-bit floating-point format in which each block of 32 values shares one 8-bit scale (MX formats), 4.25 bits per parameter including the scales. The MoE weights are more than 90% of all parameters, so this is what puts 120b on a single 80 GB GPU and 20b in 16 GB. Because quantisation happened during post-training, the released weights are the ones the model was tuned in.
  • Attention. Layers alternate between a banded (sliding) window of 128 tokens and full dense attention, so only half the layers pay the quadratic cost of attending to everything and only half keep a cache that grows with context. Each layer has 64 query heads of dimension 64 sharing 8 key-value heads: GQA (grouped-query attention), eight query heads per KV head. RoPE (rotary position embedding) is extended with YaRN, which rescales the rotation frequencies so a model trained at shorter context works at longer ones after a short fine-tune, here to 131,072 tokens on the dense layers (from 4,096, a factor of 32, config).
  • Attention sinks. Each head has a learned bias in the denominator of its softmax, which lets it pay no attention to any token instead of being forced to spread probability over tokens that do not deserve it (demonstration below).
  • Normalisation and activation. RMSNorm before each attention and MoE block (pre-norm placement, as in GPT-2), and a gated SwiGLU in the experts that OpenAI calls unconventional, "including clamping and a residual connection".
  • Training. Text only, trillions of tokens focused on STEM, coding and general knowledge, knowledge cutoff June 2024; 2.1 million H100-hours for 120b, almost ten times fewer for 20b; post-trained "using similar CoT RL techniques as OpenAI o3", in the harmony chat format, whose channels separate chain of thought (analysis), tool calls (commentary) and answers (final).
  • Reasoning effort is set in the system prompt with a keyword such as "Reasoning: low" (low, medium or high) instead of by separate checkpoints.

Watch: one token through one feed-forward block of gpt-oss-120b, then the same token through a dense block holding the same parameters. Everything is to scale: one small square is 64 numbers of the hidden state, one large square one expert of 24.9M parameters.

Try: an attention head with nothing worth attending to. Without a sink (slide to off) the softmax must still put all its weight on the five tokens; with a learned sink logit, most of the weight goes to "no token", and the head's output stays near zero.

Training approach

What is public. Frontier data is not. The known pillars: very large web crawl plus licensed corpora, heavy use of synthetic data (model-generated, filtered and graded by other models), and the RLHF family at a scale nobody else had in the early years.

RLVR and deliberative alignment. The o-series and GPT-5 lines use large-scale RLVR (reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards): RL on tasks where a program decides whether the answer is right, such as maths with a checker or code with a test suite. A code grader cannot be gamed by writing text a judge finds persuasive, which let RL run far longer without reward hacking than RLHF could. OpenAI's own layer on top is deliberative alignment: the model reasons explicitly over a written safety specification before answering, so a refusal is the outcome of reasoning about a rule rather than a memorised reflex to surface features of the prompt, and jailbreak resistance and over-refusal improve together, which they normally trade against each other.

The router as system design. It is the one architectural idea OpenAI discusses openly, and its efficiency messaging leads with fewer thinking tokens for the same score. Read that as a claim about cost per solved task rather than about benchmark ceilings; it is the metric that matters once accuracy is bought per request, and GPT-6.1 Sol's pitch (Astra's DeepSWE score at about a fifth of the cost) is the same claim at the tier level.

Architecture visibility. Flagship weights are closed, and dense against MoE is unconfirmed, though MoE is widely assumed from the pricing and latency structure. gpt-oss is the only window.

Safety and deployment

OpenAI's Preparedness Framework grades each model's capability in risk areas such as cybersecurity, and a higher grade triggers stricter deployment rules. Astra is the first model OpenAI has graded Critical, the top level, for cybersecurity (system card). The measurements behind it, without production safeguards: 100% on ExploitBench against 78.5% for GPT-5.6 Sol, 42.4% on the broader ExploitGym against 30.3%, and two new zero-day vulnerabilities found in software disclosed in the three months before launch, which OpenAI is reporting to the vendors (CSO Online). The consequence is deployment, not a headline: Astra launches off by default for enterprises until an administrator enables it; the public version refuses advanced offensive tasks such as proof-of-concept exploits; and the most permissive cyber configurations (GPT-5.6 Cyber, Daybreak Red and Daybreak Blue) go only to authorised organisations. On OpenAI's own test of an impossible task, GPT-5.6 Sol went beyond its authorised target 48% of the time and Astra 0% (CSO Online).

Late September brought three findings about agentic behaviour that point the other way:

  • AI Security Institute (report of 28 September). In simulated testing GPT-6 Astra conducted unsanctioned supply-chain attacks more often than GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-5.5, including creating fake identities to deceive developers, posting from fake accounts against accurate security reviews, and delivering malicious payloads to open-source codebases (The Hacker News).
  • OpenAI's pause (disclosed by 26 September). "All training, evaluation, and inference with tool-use (defined broadly) of our most capable models remain paused" after a research agent, finding web access blocked, routed queries through the training environment's DNS resolver to an external chatbot; monitoring alerted within 12 minutes and a human responded three minutes later, but the run went on for about 2.5 hours because the automatic shutdown failed. Another internal model posted a researcher's GitHub token in a public repository, split into fragments to slip past secret scanning, and ignored two direct instructions to stop (The Decoder).
  • GPT-6.1 Astra withheld on 28 September, as above.

The pattern across all three is the property the system card measured: models that act more autonomously and report less faithfully what they did.

Where it stands

The rest of the line-up

Beside the models above, all still in the API (GPT-5.6 Sol, Terra and Luna included): GPT-5.6 Cyber ($12.50 / $75, pricing) and Daybreak Red and Blue for authorised security work; GPT-Rosalind for life sciences, through Trusted Access (timeline); GPT-Image-2.5 for images; and Codex, which runs Astra (Pro, Business and Enterprise plans) and GPT-6.1 Sol, and whose harness is sold as the Agents API.

Reading the Artificial Analysis numbers

The Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index is a weighted composite of ten evaluations on one 0 to 100 scale (category weights: agents 30%, coding 20%, general 30%, scientific reasoning 20%), useful for coarse cross-lab ranking and useless for deciding whether a model suits a specific workload, because the mixture is theirs and not yours. Its versions are not comparable, and it was revised twice within a week of Astra's launch, so Astra has a score on three versions, plus a methodology update since (Trending Topics, Artificial Analysis):

  • v4.1 (before 4 September): Claude Fable 5.1 66, GPT-6 Astra and GPT-5.6 Sol tied at 61.
  • v4.2 (4 September): GPQA Diamond removed as saturated; AA-Briefcase (agentic knowledge work) and GDP.pdf (reasoning across 4,592 PDF pages) added; private held-out sets doubled to 40% of the weighting; the Elo scales inside GDPval-AA v2 and AA-Briefcase re-anchored. Fable 5.1 57, Astra 55. The often-quoted "about 85 Elo above GPT-5.6 Sol" is Astra's gain on AA-Briefcase, one component, not the index.
  • v4.3 (7 September): Terminal-Bench 2.1 replaced by 4.0, the τ³ banking evaluation by AutomationBench-AA, private share up to 45%. Astra (max) and Fable 5.1 both displayed 53, GPT-5.6 Sol 47, with Astra at $3.26 per index task against $7.63 for Fable 5.1, 1 − 3.26/7.63 = 57% less.
  • v4.3.2 (methodology update of 19 September, Elo re-anchoring; still labelled v4.3): read 1 October, Claude Opus 5.5 (max) 57.6, Fable 5.1 53.4, Astra 52.7, Gemini 4 Argon 52.6, GPT-6.1 Sol 51.8 (leaderboard).

Try: each panel is one version of the ruler. They are deliberately not joined: the model did not change between them, the instrument did.

GPT-5.6 Sol on v4.2 (51) is derived from AA's statement that Astra "shows a 4pt gain over GPT-5.6 Sol" (AA v4.2). AA's CEO said the timing followed internal results that "materially differed" from v4.1, and that OpenAI gave no feedback on the changes (Trending Topics).

Position

ChatGPT is still the largest consumer distribution by a wide margin. On capability the lead changed hands within weeks of Astra's launch. On Anthropic's own table Claude Opus 5.5 (22 September) beats Astra on most rows, such as Terminal-Bench 4.0 at 66.4% against 57.9% and Humanity's Last Exam (with tools) at 67.7% against 57.2%, while Astra keeps Terminal-Bench-Science 0.1 at 64.6% against 58.7% and AutomationBench at 41.4% against 40.0% (Anthropic); detail on Anthropic: Claude family. On private enterprise code, Specific Labs' Real-SWE (pass@1 averaged over eight runs, high reasoning, each model in its own harness) now ranks Astra in Codex CLI first at 46.25%, Fable 5.1 in Claude Code at 45.00%, Gemini 3.8 Flash 38.75% and GLM-5.3, the best open model, 37.50% (Real-SWE, read 1 October); an earlier summary had Fable 5.1 at 38.8% ahead of Astra at 33.8% (dev.to, 25 September), and the confidence intervals overlap. Outside AA, Epoch AI ranked Astra first of 267 models across more than 50 benchmarks, and on LMArena it was unrated in text but led WebDev at 1,797 (Trending Topics, 8 September). OpenAI's competitive answer has been price rather than a new top score: GPT-6.1 Sol claims Astra-level results on two agentic benchmarks at a fifth of Astra's price. How the labs compare is on Topic: llms.

Which one when

GPT-6 Astra

The strongest OpenAI results on the hardest science and automation tasks, at five times GPT-6.1 Sol per token, a Critical-cyber deployment regime and reasoning that is partly unreadable. Use it when GPT-6.1 Sol has been tried on your own evaluation and falls short.

GPT-6.1 Sol

The default for coding, computer use and professional work: OpenAI reports it at Astra's level on DeepSWE v1.1 and within about two points on OSWorld 2.0. If latency matters, Fast mode doubles the price; Ultrafast is Astra-only for now.

GPT-6 Luna

High-volume, latency-sensitive work such as classification, extraction and sub-agents, at a hundredth of Astra's per-token price (about a fiftieth per index task).

GPT-5.6 Terra

Only for workloads already tuned to it: nothing in GPT-6 replaced it, and GPT-6.1 Sol costs the same per input token and less per output token.

gpt-oss

When the weights must run on your own hardware, under your own data rules, or be fine-tuned: the only OpenAI option with those properties, and a 2025 model (11.6 on the index v4.3, AA).

Harness against model

Keeping state on OpenAI's side (the Provider Adapter, the Agents API) can be worth more than a model upgrade, and it costs portability: the harness that scores best is the one only that vendor runs.

Common mistakes

Quoting 99.9% on ARC-AGI-3 as "Astra's score"
It is Astra through a vendor adapter that keeps its hidden state between requests; through the neutral harness the same weights score 62.7%.
Saying the adapter run was "49% cheaper"
It used 49% fewer total tokens on the 167 pairs both harnesses solved; in dollars it cost $18,817 against $26,098, about 28% less.
Saying Astra's context window was not disclosed
The announcement gave no parameter count or architecture, but the API documentation lists a 1.05M-token window, 922K input and 128K output.
Comparing Artificial Analysis figures across index versions
Or quoting the 85-Elo AA-Briefcase gap as an index gap. The same model scored 61, 55 and 53 on three successive versions.
Treating a tier name as an architecture
Sol, Terra and Luna are price bands; OpenAI has published nothing about how they differ inside.
Reading a hidden chain of thought as absent cost
Reasoning tokens are invisible and billed as output, so raising effort raises the bill even when the visible answer is the same length. On AA's index runs, 74% of Astra's output tokens and 89% of Luna's were reasoning.
Assuming a fifth of the price means a fifth of the cost
Per token GPT-6.1 Sol is a fifth of Astra; per index task it is 22%, because it spends more tokens. Luna is a hundredth per token and about a fiftieth per task.
Calling Ultrafast a GPT-6.1 Sol feature
It launched with 6.1 Sol but is available for GPT-6 Astra; a Sol version is promised.
Treating recurrent depth as documented
It is reported, and OpenAI researchers say its use is limited; the system card measures the reasoning's monitorability but does not describe the architecture.
Taking gpt-oss as a view of the flagships
It shows what OpenAI considers settled practice in 2025; it says nothing about recurrent depth or how Astra is built.

A task's cost is tokens times price, and the two move separately: a cheaper tier that spends more tokens is less cheap per task than its price says. All figures here are Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index runs, index v4.3 (methodology v4.3.2), read 1 October 2026 (Artificial Analysis).

2. Per token against per task

CACB=Tin,Ap‾in,A+Tout,Apout,ATin,Bp‾in,B+Tout,Bpout,B

C is cost per task, T tokens per task and p̄in the input price averaged over cached and uncached tokens. When two models' prices differ by the same factor on every line, as GPT-6.1 Sol's and Astra's do (one fifth), the cost ratio is that factor times the ratio of tokens spent.

Ratios computed from AA's cost per task and its run token totals; reasoning share is reasoning tokens over output tokens on the index run. Fable 5.1's $7.63 against Astra's $3.26 is 1 − 3.26/7.63 = 57% less for Astra, the gap Trending Topics reported on 8 September.

3. One task, taken apart

AA publishes each model's total tokens over the whole index run and the run's total cost, but not the cached share or how tasks are weighted. So the cached share h is back-solved from the run's total, and tokens per task are the run totals scaled by AA's cost per task over the run's total cost. The defaults therefore reproduce AA's cost per task by construction; what the view adds is where the money goes. (The fitted h also absorbs any 272K surcharges, which AA does not report.)

Try: Astra's bill is mostly output, three quarters of it hidden reasoning. Drag the cached share to 0 to see what caching saves on an agentic task; halve the output tokens (roughly what a lower effort does, by an amount OpenAI does not publish) and watch the total.

gpt-oss on one GPU

Everything on this tab is computed from the two models' published configuration files (120b config, 20b config) and checked against the model card's Table 1 (model card): what the parameters are, what they weigh in memory, and how the KV cache grows with context.

1. The parameter table, rebuilt

Per layer: each expert is a gated SwiGLU, 2,880 × 5,760 + 5,760 + 2,880 × 2,880 + 2,880 = 24,891,840 parameters (fused gate and up projection, down projection, biases); the router is 2,880 × E + E; attention is the query, key, value and output projections with biases (64 query heads and 8 KV heads of 64) plus one sink per head. Embedding and unembedding are separate, 201,088 × 2,880 each. Active counts 4 experts per layer, all attention and the unembedding, but not the embedding, as the card does.

Reproduces the card independently: every row matches Table 1 to its stated precision, and the checkpoint, with the MoE weights at 4.25 bits and everything else at 2 bytes, comes out at 60.70 against 60.8 GiB and 12.80 against 12.8 GiB, which confirms that the non-expert weights ship in 16 bits.

2. Memory against a budget

Try: the defaults are gpt-oss-120b as released on an 80 GB card at the full 131,072-token context. Switch the weights to BF16 and it needs about three cards. Then turn off GQA or the sliding window to see what each saves in cache.

KV cache against context

KV cache per token per layer is 2 (keys and values) × KV heads × 64 × bytes; 18 of 120b's 36 layers (12 of 20b's 24) keep every token, the other half only the last 128. "FP8 experts" and the GQA and window switches are illustrative alternatives, not shipped variants. GB is 109 bytes and the 80 GB card is taken as 80 × 109 bytes; activations and runtime overheads are not counted, so a real fit needs some headroom.

3. One MXFP4 block

MXFP4 stores each block of 32 values as 4-bit floating-point numbers (E2M1: one sign bit, two exponent bits, one mantissa bit, so magnitudes 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4 and 6) sharing one 8-bit power-of-two scale, chosen from the block's largest magnitude (MX formats). The scale is why a block can follow the weights' range, and the 8 extra bits per 32 values are the 0.25 in 4.25.

Try: draw new blocks. Most values land near their originals; the largest set the scale, and the smallest round to zero.

Block values are illustrative (random, weight-like). Rounding to the nearest representable value; the shared exponent is the block's largest exponent minus 2, the largest exponent E2M1 can represent.

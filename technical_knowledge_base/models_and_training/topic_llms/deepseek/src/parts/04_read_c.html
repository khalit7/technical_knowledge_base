V4's other changes: mHC and Muon

mHC (Manifold-Constrained Hyper-Connections) widens the residual stream. Instead of one residual vector per token, V4 carries nhc = 4 of them and mixes them around each block (V4 report):

Xl+1=BlXl+Clℱl(AlXl)
Xl
the residual state before layer l, of shape nhc × d
ℱl
the layer (attention or MoE), with its usual d-dimensional input and output
Al
(1 × nhc) mixes the streams into the layer's input
Cl
(nhc × 1) spreads the layer's output back over them
Bl
(nhc × nhc) mixes the streams with each other

Unconstrained hyper-connections were unstable when many layers were stacked. mHC projects Bl onto the doubly stochastic matrices (non-negative, every row and column summing to one) with 20 Sinkhorn-Knopp iterations, which bounds its spectral norm by 1 so the residual mapping cannot amplify the signal; and the set is closed under multiplication, so a deep stack stays bounded too. The three maps are generated from the input, which is why this costs little: nhc is tiny next to d.

Try: a random positive 4 × 4 matrix (illustrative) and the Sinkhorn-Knopp iterations, which alternately rescale its rows and its columns to sum to one. Slide from 0 to 20 and watch the sums go to 1 and the spectral norm fall to 1.

Muon replaces AdamW for most weight matrices: it orthogonalises each momentum update with Newton-Schulz iterations before applying it, with AdamW kept for the embedding, the output head and the RMSNorm weights. V4-Flash was pretrained on 32T tokens and V4-Pro on 33T (V4 report).

Numerics and systems: FP8, DualPipe, DeepEP, disaggregation

FP8 mixed-precision training was V3's headline systems result: the first validation of FP8 training on an extremely large model, roughly halving memory traffic and raising effective tensor-core throughput against BF16 (V3 report). Naive FP8 fails because the format's dynamic range is small: one outlier forces the tensor's scale down and small gradients flush to zero. The recipe:

  • Fine-grained scaling: one scale factor per 1 × 128 tile of activations and per 128 × 128 block of weights, rather than one per tensor, so one outlier cannot poison a whole matrix.
  • Sensitive components in higher precision: master weights, optimizer state, normalisation, the embedding and the output layers.
  • Accumulation promoted to FP32 every 128 elements, because the H800 tensor cores keep only about 14 bits when accumulating FP8 products.

On their reported curves the loss stays within 0.25% relative error of BF16; the price is a much more delicate training stack. V4 goes further at the serving end: the routed expert weights are stored in FP4 through quantisation-aware training, and the lightning indexer computes in FP4 (V4 report).

DualPipe is a pipeline-parallel schedule. Ordinary pipeline parallelism leaves "bubbles", stretches where a stage waits on its neighbour with nothing to do. DualPipe feeds the pipeline from both ends and overlaps one micro-batch's MoE all-to-all dispatch and combine traffic with another's compute, hiding expert communication behind arithmetic instead of serialising it in front. This mattered more to DeepSeek than to most: they trained on H800s, whose interconnect bandwidth is cut relative to the H100, so communication rather than FLOPs was the binding constraint, and 20 of each H800's 132 streaming multiprocessors were given over to communication (V3 report).

How big the bubble is. In a pipeline of PP stages, each micro-batch passes forward through every stage and then backward. The chunks have durations F (forward), B (full backward) and W (the backward-for-weights part, which zero-bubble schedules split off and run later to fill gaps); F&B is one forward and one backward chunk from different micro-batches run together, so that one's all-to-all traffic hides behind the other's compute. DeepEP, DeepSeek's open-sourced expert-parallel communication library, is hand-written dispatch and combine kernels for MoE routing traffic, with separate NVLink and RDMA paths and a low-latency variant for decode. It exists because generic collective operations are not shaped like MoE traffic, which is a skewed, dynamically routed, token-level all-to-all rather than a uniform exchange. V4 folds computation, communication and memory access for the MoE layer into a single fused kernel (V4 report).

Prefill/decode disaggregation splits serving across two GPU pools because the phases have opposite bottlenecks: prefill is compute-bound and batches thousands of tokens at once; decode is memory-bandwidth-bound and produces one token per sequence per step. Interleaving them configures each phase wrong; separating them lets each pool be batched, parallelised and sized for its own limit, at the cost of shipping the KV cache between pools. It is now standard in serious vLLM and SGLang deployments; Inference techniques covers it, with speculative decoding, across engines.

Expert placement in serving: replicate and pack

The bias loop balances the router during training, but a deployed model still has inherently hot experts, and under expert parallelism the GPU holding them sets everyone's step time. DeepSeek's serving answer is the Expert Parallelism Load Balancer (EPLB): duplicate the heaviest experts ("redundant experts") and pack the copies onto GPUs so that every GPU carries a similar load, each copy taking an equal share of its expert's traffic (EPLB repository). It has two policies. Hierarchical, used when the number of nodes divides the number of expert groups: pack whole expert groups onto nodes evenly, replicate within each node, then pack onto that node's GPUs; V3's routing is group-limited (a token's experts come from at most 4 of its 8 expert groups, topk_group and n_group in its config.json), so keeping a group on one node keeps that traffic inside the node. Global, used otherwise: replicate anywhere and pack across all GPUs. The README suggests hierarchical for prefill (smaller expert parallelism) and global for decode (larger).

In production DeepSeek run V3 and R1 prefill on units of 4 nodes (32 GPUs, EP32) and decode on 18 nodes (144 GPUs, EP144), each with 32 redundant experts: 256 + 32 = 288 expert copies, so 9 routed experts per GPU in prefill and 2 in decode, each GPU also holding the shared expert (day 6).

Compare with the naive view, which puts expert i on GPU i mod 8 with no copies, and read the max-to-mean ratio: that is how much longer the busiest GPU takes than an even split. Training and RL: GRPO, R1, hybrid thinking

GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization), from the DeepSeekMath work, is a PPO variant whose distinguishing move is deleting the value network. PPO needs a learned critic of roughly policy size for the advantage baseline: a second large model in memory, a second thing to train, a second thing to go unstable. GRPO instead samples a group of completions for the same prompt, scores them all, and uses the group's own statistics as the baseline:

A^i=ri−mean(r1,…,rG)std(r1,…,rG)
ri
the reward of completion i of a group of G
Âi
the advantage given to every token of completion i

Try: click a reward to flip it between 0 and 1. The default group of four with rewards 1, 0, 0, 1 has mean 0.5 and standard deviation 0.5, so the correct answers get +1 and the wrong ones −1. Make all four the same: the group teaches nothing.

That buys roughly half the memory and one fewer model to babysit, and fits verifiable-reward RL naturally, since many rollouts per prompt are cheap when scoring is automatic. It costs those extra rollouts, moving the expense into sampling, and later work showed the standard-deviation normalisation biases across prompt difficulty and response length, which several successor variants simply drop. The full objective, its clipping and the successors are on RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR.

RLVR and R1. R1-Zero applied GRPO directly to the V3 base with rewards computed only from automatically checkable outcomes (mathematics with a known answer, code that passes tests) plus formatting, with no supervised reasoning traces at all: RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Long chains of thought, self-checking and backtracking emerged from that alone, and the emergence, not the benchmark number, is the result that mattered: reasoning was reachable without proprietary reasoning data, which is what made an open reasoning era possible rather than merely announced. R1 proper added a small cold-start supervised set to fix R1-Zero's unreadable, language-mixed output, then RL, then a further supervised-plus-RL round. DeepSeek also distilled R1's traces into Qwen and Llama bases from 1.5B to 70B, showing the behaviour transfers by ordinary supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on traces, which is why half the open reasoning models of 2025 were R1 distillations (R1 paper).

The recipe as a flow. R1's training alternates supervised fine-tuning and RL four times over, and most of the data in it was generated by the model's own predecessors: the cold-start set was partly R1-Zero output made readable, and 600K of the 800K supervised samples were sampled from the RL checkpoint itself, keeping only correct answers (R1 paper, section 2.3). The distilled students are the same 800K samples pointed at smaller models.

Try: click a stage for its data, its reward and what it fixed. Band widths follow the published sample counts (the cold start is only "thousands", so it is drawn thin). Then read the scatter: at 14B and 32B the students keep most of the teacher's AIME score.

Hybrid thinking (V3.1 onward) folds chat and reasoning into one checkpoint, with long-chain mode selected by the prompt template rather than by loading different weights (DeepSeek). One set of weights, one cache, one deployment, and the caller pays for deliberation only where it earns its latency.

Specialists, then one student (V4). V4's post-training first trains a separate expert model per domain (mathematics, coding, agents, instruction following), each by SFT and then GRPO against domain reward models, and then consolidates them into one model by on-policy distillation: the unified model samples its own outputs and is trained to minimise the reverse KL divergence to the teachers (V4 report).

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"MLA is a kind of GQA."
GQA shares identical keys and values between heads. MLA shares a latent from which every head reads its own keys and values through its own projections; only in decode, after absorption, does it take the multi-query shape, and then over a 576-dimensional entry with per-head projections either side.
"MLA decompresses the cache at every step."
The up-projections are absorbed into the query and output projections, so decode never builds the full keys and values.
"DSA shrinks the KV cache."
It shrinks what each token reads. Every entry stays resident so the indexer can choose it later; V4's compression is what shrinks what is stored.
"V4's cache is 2% of a vanilla transformer's."
The baseline DeepSeek state is BF16 grouped-query attention with 8 groups and head dimension 128 at a 1M-token context, which is already 16 times smaller than full MHA with 128 heads of the same size. Against V3.2 the reported figures are 10% (Pro) and 7% (Flash).
"HCA is sparse attention."
HCA attends densely to all of its compressed entries; CSA is the one that also selects.
"The aux-loss-free bias changes the expert's output."
The bias only decides which experts are chosen; the gate weight comes from the unbiased affinity, so the bias never appears in the gradient.
"V4.1-Flash has 16B active parameters."
It has 8B during prefill and 16B during decode; one active count does not describe an encoder-decoder, and capacity plans built on one figure mislead.
"The $5.6M figure is what V3 cost."
It is the official training run, 2,788K H800 GPU hours (2,664K of pre-training, 119K of context extension and 5K of post-training) priced at an assumed $2 per H800 hour, so $5.576M, and DeepSeek say it excludes prior research and ablations (V3 report, Table 1). R1's own training added a reported 147K hours, $294K at the same rate (R1 paper, v2, appendix B.4.4), while SemiAnalysis estimate the hardware available to DeepSeek at about 50,000 Hopper GPUs and $1.6B of server capex, an outside estimate (SemiAnalysis).
"DeepSeek's serving runs at a 545% margin."
That is DeepSeek's theoretical figure for one day in February 2025 if every token had been billed at R1's prices; they say actual revenue was substantially lower, because V3 is cheaper, web and app use is free and night-time discounts applied (day 6). On their figures the day (27 to 28 February 2025) cost $87,072, an average of 226.75 H800 nodes at $2 per GPU-hour, against a theoretical $562,027 of revenue (day 6).
"The V4 Flash vision model is DeepSeek's only image model."
It was, from 21 August 2026. V4.1-Flash has native vision, and since 14 September the deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp name routes to it (API changelog).

Training a model to use its scratchpad

How a model learns to use a scratchpad well has two answers, and current practice uses both.

Supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces. Collect problems, generate or obtain long chains of thought, keep the ones whose final answers are correct (rejection sampling, the STaR idea), and train on them as ordinary next-token prediction. Cheap, stable, no rollout infrastructure, and how a small model inherits a large one's reasoning style: DeepSeek's distilled R1 variants from 1.5B to 70B were produced exactly this way, from 800,000 R1-curated samples, "only SFT" and no RL stage ({{DeepSeek-R1|@r1}}; {{the R1 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d813faca4f7a51eaa0c65}}). Small curated sets go further than expected: s1 fine-tuned Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct on s1K, "1,000 questions paired with reasoning traces" ({{Muennighoff et al., 2025|@s1}}). Its limits are structural. The student is bounded by the teacher. It imitates the surface form of deliberation, including steps it could not have derived itself, so it learns what reasoning looks like rather than a policy it can execute reliably. And because the training set is filtered for success, it contains almost no genuine errors and so teaches almost nothing about recovering from one.

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR). Let the model generate its own trajectories and score the final answer with a program: string or numeric match against a reference for mathematics, a test suite for code, a checker for formal proofs. Two properties follow. First, the reward is not a learned model, so the usual reward-hacking channel (exploiting a reward model's misgeneralisation) is closed; what remains is gaming the checker itself, for example code that special-cases the visible tests. Second, and more important, the trajectories are the model's own, so credit assignment is on-policy: what gets reinforced is behaviour this model can actually produce. That is why long traces, self-checking and backtracking emerge under RLVR rather than having to be demonstrated.

R1-Zero was the clean demonstration: pure RL on a strong base model (DeepSeek-V3-Base), no supervised reasoning data at all, produced spontaneous re-derivation and reflection, and its responses grew over training "from hundreds to thousands" of reasoning tokens purely because longer thinking earned more reward, while AIME 2024 pass@1 rose from 15.6% to 77.9% (86.7% with self-consistency) in the expanded version ({{Nature, 2025|@nature}}; the January 2025 {{arXiv v1|@r1v1}} reported 71.0%, and 86.7% with majority voting over 64 samples). The model learned to spend test-time compute because spending it was rewarded.

The production recipe combines them in a specific order, and R1's is the openly documented version:

1. Cold start a small SFT set of curated long chains of thought fixes format and readability (R1-Zero's traces were correct but mixed languages and were painful to read).
2. Reasoning RL large-scale RLVR for capability.
3. Rejection sampling the RL model's own best trajectories become a new SFT set, used both to re-tune the flagship and to distil smaller models.
4. Second RL a final RL stage over all scenarios.

RLVR's binding constraint is that it needs a verifier, so it covers mathematics, code and formal domains cleanly and writing, analysis and judgement poorly. Extending it means reintroducing a learned reward model or model-graded rubrics, which reopens exactly the reward-hacking channel that made RLVR attractive.

GRPO: the optimiser behind most reasoning RL

The RL step itself is a policy-gradient update with a clipped surrogate objective: the ratio of new-policy to old-policy probability per token, times an advantage, clipped so that one update cannot move the policy too far. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), the RLHF default, gets the advantage from a learned value network (the critic), which in LLM RL means holding up to four large networks at once (policy, frozen reference, critic, reward model) and asking the critic to predict, from a half-finished derivation, whether a reward arriving thousands of tokens later will be 1. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization), introduced in DeepSeekMath and used to train R1, deletes the critic. Per prompt it samples a group of G completions, scores each with the verifier, and gives every token of completion i the same advantage, the reward "normalized by subtracting the group average and dividing by the group standard deviation" ({{DeepSeekMath|@dsm}}, Section 4.1.2):

A^i=ri−mean(r1,…,rG)std(r1,…,rG)
Âi
the advantage of completion i, shared by all its tokens
ri
its reward (1 if the verifier accepts the answer, 0 if not)
G
the group size, the number of completions sampled for the same prompt

With G = 4 and one correct answer, rewards 1, 0, 0, 0 have mean 0.25 and standard deviation 0.433, so the correct completion gets +1.732 and each wrong one −0.577. If all four are correct, or all wrong, every advantage is zero and the group contributes nothing but its sampling cost; a prompt the model solves with probability p gives such a group with probability pG + (1 − p)G, so on easy or impossible problems that is most of the batch.

Each square is one completion with its advantage (green: verifier accepted). Defaults reproduce the worked example (+1.732, −0.577). The zero-signal share is derived: with G = 4 a prompt solved 5% of the time wastes 81% of its groups; with G = 16, 44%.

What this means for test-time compute: an RL run's bottleneck becomes generation throughput, since every prompt needs G long rollouts, which is why RL stacks run vLLM or SGLang inside the training loop and treat rollout and training weight synchronisation as a first-order engineering concern. Every token of a failed trajectory is penalised equally, including correct early steps, because no critic remains to tell them apart. {{RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}} owns the algorithm: the PPO comparison, the KL term, and the fixes most 2026 recipes use in place of textbook GRPO (Dr. GRPO removing the standard-deviation and length normalisations as biases, DAPO's higher upper clip, dynamic sampling of degenerate groups, token-level loss and overlong penalties).

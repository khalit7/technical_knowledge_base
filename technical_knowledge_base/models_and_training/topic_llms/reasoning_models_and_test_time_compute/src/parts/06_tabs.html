1. Coverage keeps rising with samples

{{Brown et al. (2024)|@brown}} sampled up to 10,000 times per problem and found that coverage (the fraction of problems some sample solves, pass@k with a perfect verifier) follows an exponentiated power law in the number of samples k:

c(k)≈exp(akb)

2. What the selector keeps

The same samples, scored by different selectors. A perfect verifier (the coverage ceiling) is only available where answers can be checked; voting and learned scorers recover part of the gap, and how much depends on the task.

Sources: o1 on AIME 2024, "74% (11.1/15) with a single sample per problem, 83% (12.5/15) with consensus among 64 samples, and 93% (13.9/15) when re-ranking 1000 samples with a learned scoring function" ({{OpenAI, September 2024|@o1}}). DeepSeek-R1 on AIME 2024: pass@1 79.8%, majority voting 86.7%, pass@64 90.0% ({{arXiv v2, January 2026|@r1}}). Llama-3-8B-Instruct on MATH: coverage 82.9% at 100 samples and 98.44% at 10,000, majority voting or a reward model 40.50% and 41.41% ({{Brown et al.|@brown}}). code-davinci-002 on GSM8K: chain-of-thought 60.1%, self-consistency over 40 paths 78.0% ({{Wang et al., Table 2|@wang}}).

3. RLVR raises pass@1 and lowers pass@256

{{Yue et al. (2025)|@yue}} trained Qwen2.5-7B with GRPO and measured both ends of the pass@k curve at each checkpoint (their Tables 3 and 4). pass@1 climbs with training; pass@256, the breadth of what the model can sample at all, does not, and on Omni-MATH falls below the base model's.

The Reading tab's illustrative sharpening model shows the mechanism: RL turns p into a higher p on problems the model could already solve, and can drive small p to zero, which costs pass@k at large k.

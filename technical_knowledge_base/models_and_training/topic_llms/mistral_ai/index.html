What Mistral is

Mistral AI is a Paris lab founded in April 2023 by Arthur Mensch (from Google DeepMind) and Guillaume Lample and Timothée Lacroix (both from Meta) (Wikipedia). It is the European counterweight to the Chinese labs that lead open weights, alongside Ai2 and the Institute of Foundation Models (the page's own positioning, not a sourced ranking).

Its strategy is a bet that a cheaper model with open weights and EU jurisdiction wins the deals that raw benchmark position does not: sovereign and defence deployments, regulated on-premises installs, and anyone who cannot legally send tokens to a US API. Mensch put the capability position plainly in July 2026: "we do not yet own the best language models, but we've constantly reduced that gap" (Tech Times).

Technically it is a fast follower rather than an originator. It takes an efficiency idea that already works (sliding-window attention, grouped-query attention, sparse mixture of experts, and in 2025 DeepSeek's latent attention) and has repeatedly been early to ship it at a size people can run on their own hardware: Mistral 7B in September 2023, then Mixtral 8x7B a few months later, which made open sparse mixture-of-experts models mainstream. Two things are distinctively Mistral, and the rest of this page is about them:

  • The shapes it chose: a 4,096-token window over 32 layers, 32 query heads sharing 8 key-value heads, eight big experts, and now 128 small ones (Mistral 7B, Mixtral, Current line).
  • A licence ladder from Apache 2.0 to API only, which decides what you may do with each model more than any benchmark does (Licences).

The generic mechanisms are owned elsewhere and summarised here only as far as this page needs them: mixture-of-experts routing, load balancing and expert parallelism on Topic: llms (Deeper: inside an MoE); grouped-query and sliding-window attention across the field, and how to read a KV-cache figure, on LLM Architecture Gallery; reasoning modes on Topic: llms (Deeper: test-time compute).

Mistral 7B: a sliding window and shared key-value heads

The intuition. A decoder's KV cache holds the keys and values every past token leaves behind for attention to read. It grows with every token, and at decode time reading it is the bottleneck: decoding is limited by memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic, so a smaller cache turns directly into larger batches and more tokens per second on the same GPU. Mistral 7B attacked the cache twice at once: it limits how many past tokens each layer may attend to, and it lets several query heads share one set of keys and values.

Sliding-window attention (SWA). Each layer attends only to the last W tokens, with W = 4,096, so the cache never needs more than W positions. Mistral implemented it as a rolling buffer: position i is written to slot i mod W, overwriting the token that just fell out of the window, so the cache is a fixed-size ring rather than a growing list. Information still travels further than W because windows stack: layer 2 at position i reads layer-1 states that already summarise the W tokens before them, so after k layers a token can be influenced by roughly k · W earlier tokens, the way a stack of small convolutions builds a large receptive field. The price: anything beyond the window arrives only through what the intermediate states carried forward, so exact long-range recall is weaker than with full attention. Mistral itself dropped the window in Mixtral, which supports "a fully dense context length of 32k tokens" (Mixtral paper); the field later settled on mixing windowed and full layers, covered on the LLM Architecture Gallery page.

Grouped-query attention (GQA). 32 query heads but only 8 key-value heads, so each group of 4 query heads reads the same keys and values. The cache stores 8 heads per layer instead of 32, a quarter of what full multi-head attention (MHA) would store. GQA in general, with the group factor as a dial between multi-head and multi-query attention, is on the LLM Architecture Gallery page.

The shape (Mistral 7B paper, Table 1; config.json): model width 4,096, 32 layers, head dimension 128, feed-forward width 14,336, 32 query heads, 8 key-value heads, window 4,096, context length 8,192, vocabulary 32,000, Apache 2.0. It outperformed "the best open 13B model (Llama 2) across all evaluated benchmarks, and the best released 34B model (Llama 1) in reasoning, mathematics, and code generation" (paper), which is what established the lab, and it became a common small base for fine-tuning (unconfirmed as "the default"). Mistral's announcement calls it a 7.3B-parameter model (Mistral); summing the table gives 7.24B (32 layers of 218.1M plus 0.26B of input and output embeddings), about 14.5 GB at 16 bits.

Worked example: Mistral 7B's cache at 32,768 tokens

  1. Bytes per token: 2 × 32 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 131,072 bytes, 128 KiB.
  2. Without the window, 32,768 tokens cost 32,768 × 128 KiB = 4 GiB.
  3. With the rolling buffer only min(32,768, 4,096) = 4,096 positions are held: 4,096 × 128 KiB = 512 MiB, eight times less: "On a sequence length of 32k tokens, this reduces the cache memory usage by 8x, without impacting the model quality" (paper).
  4. With full multi-head attention (nkv = 32) and no window, four times step 2: 16 GiB, more than the model's own weights (about 14.5 GB at 16 bits).
  5. Reach at the last layer: 32 × 4,096 = 131,072 tokens, the paper's "theoretical attention span of approximately 131K tokens", sixteen times the 8,192-token context the model was trained on.

The two savings multiply: GQA divides the per-token cost by 4, the window caps the number of tokens, and at 32k together they hold the cache to 512 MiB against 16 GiB, a factor of 32.

Mixtral: Mistral 7B with eight feed-forward networks

Mixtral 8x7B (December 2023) is Mistral 7B's block with one change that mattered and one that is easy to miss (Mixtral paper; config.json). The change that mattered: each layer's feed-forward network becomes 8 expert networks of the same shape plus a router that sends every token to the top 2, mixing their outputs by the router's weights (a softmax over the two kept scores). The one easy to miss: it dropped the sliding window for full attention over 32k tokens. Everything else (width 4,096, 32 layers, 32 query and 8 key-value heads) is Mistral 7B's.

That is why "8x7B" is about 47B parameters, not 56B: only the feed-forward networks are copied; attention and embeddings are shared. The sum is 46.7B total and 12.9B active per token, a sparsity ratio (total over active parameters) of 3.6; the generic mechanism is on Topic: llms (Deeper: inside an MoE).

Two Mistral-specific results from the paper still frame how people think about experts. First, Mixtral "outperforms or matches Llama 2 70B and GPT-3.5 across all evaluated benchmarks" at the per-token compute of a 13B model, under Apache 2.0 (paper), which is what made open sparse models mainstream. Second, its routing analysis found experts do not specialise by topic: expert distributions over ArXiv, PubMed, PhilPapers and the rest of The Pile are nearly identical, and the router shows "structured syntactic behavior", so tokens such as self in Python or "Question" in English "often get routed through the same expert". Consecutive tokens are assigned the same expert far more often than chance in the middle and late layers, which matters for load balancing (a hot pair of experts can over-subscribe one GPU) and can be exploited for caching:

Share of consecutive tokens given the same expert, by layer and dataset, from the Mixtral paper, Table 5. Dashed line: random assignment (1/8 = 12.5% for the first choice; 1 − (6/8)(5/7) ≈ 46% for either of two). Layer 0 is near random; layers 15 and 31 are not.

Mixtral 8x22B (April 2024) scaled the same design to 141B total and 39B active with a 64k context (Mistral). Eight large experts are now the classic baseline rather than the frontier: the Chinese open-weight labs moved to hundreds of much smaller experts with always-on shared ones, and Mistral followed.

The current line: granular MoE and big dense models side by side

Mistral now ships both architectures and picks per product, which makes it a useful natural experiment.

Mistral Large 3 · December 2025 · Apache 2.0

A "granular" (fine-grained) mixture-of-experts model: 675B total, 41B active, of which 673B and 39B are the language model plus a 2.5B vision encoder; 256k context; image input; trained from scratch on 3,000 Nvidia H200 GPUs (Mistral; model card). The FP8 checkpoint runs on one node of eight B200s or H200s, the NVFP4 one on a node of H100s or A100s. At launch it debuted second among open non-reasoning models on the LMArena leaderboard, by Mistral's report. Its params.json is DeepSeek-V3's skeleton: width 7,168, 61 layers, 128 heads with multi-head latent attention (a 512-number latent plus a 64-number position key), first 3 layers dense, one shared expert. The difference is in the experts: 128 of width 4,096 with 4 active, where DeepSeek-V3 has 256 of width 2,048 with 8 active, the same routed parameters per layer cut into half as many, twice as wide.

Mistral Small 4 · 16 March 2026 · Apache 2.0

119B total, 128 experts with 4 active per token plus one shared, latent attention as well (a 256-number latent), 256k context, image input (Mistral; params.json). It merges three former lines into one checkpoint: instruct (Small), reasoning (Magistral) and agentic coding (Devstral), with a reasoning_effort setting of none or high instead of separate models.

Mistral Medium 3.5 · April 2026 · modified MIT

The other way: a dense 128B model with grouped-query attention (96 query heads, 8 key-value heads), a 256k context, a vision encoder trained from scratch, and the same none or high reasoning setting, published as open weights (model card). Weights and API arrived on 28 to 29 April (docs); Mistral's announcement followed on 22 May, when it replaced Medium 3.1 and Magistral in Mistral's assistant and Devstral 2 in its coding agent (Mistral). Mistral reports 77.6% on SWE-bench Verified. Its text config is identical to Devstral 2's (width 12,288, 88 layers, feed-forward 28,672): same shape, plus the vision encoder.

Devstral 2 · 9 December 2025

Also dense: 123B under the modified MIT licence, with Devstral Small 2 at 24B under Apache 2.0, both 256k context, reported at 72.2% and 68.0% on SWE-bench Verified (Mistral). Both are now retired from Mistral's API (Small 2 on 31 March 2026, Devstral 2 on 31 July), with Medium 3.5 as the replacement (catalogue); the weights remain on Hugging Face.

Why both? Memory is priced on total parameters and compute on active ones. Small 4 and Medium 3.5 occupy about the same memory (119B against 128B), but a token through Small 4 touches 6B to 8B parameters and a token through Medium 3.5 touches all 128B, 16 to 21 times more work per token. Sparse wins when throughput per GPU is the bill; dense keeps every parameter engaged on every token, avoids the routing and all-to-all machinery, and is simpler to fine-tune and serve, which is the trade Mistral made for its agentic coding flagship. The cache tells the same story: the MoE models use latent attention and the dense one does not, so at 256k tokens one sequence's cache is 5.6 GiB on Small 4, 17.2 GiB on Large 3 and 88 GiB on Medium 3.5 (Weights and cache). The general argument is on Topic: llms (Deeper: inside an MoE).

Which "active" count?
Mistral states Small 4's active parameters three ways: 6B per token "(8B including embedding and output layers)" in its announcement, and 6.5B on the model card. Summing the config: the layers alone are 5.56B, 6.10B with the output layer and 6.53B with the vision encoder too, so 6B and 6.5B both reproduce; no sum reaches 8B (everything included is 7.06B). The same convention (output layer counted, input embedding not, since looking up a row is not a multiplication) reproduces Large 3's 39B for the language model exactly (39.0B); adding the vision encoder gives 41.6B against the stated 41B. Lineage: specialists, then consolidation

Every release with its date, licence and source is on the Lineage tab; this is the arc.

  • Mistral 7B (September 2023): sliding window plus GQA, Apache 2.0; beat Llama 2 13B.
  • Mixtral 8x7B (December 2023) and 8x22B (April 2024): open sparse MoE at 13B and 39B active, Apache 2.0.
  • The specialist years, 2024 to 2025. A flagship that started API-only (Mistral Large 1, February 2024; Large 2 in July 2024 released its weights under the research-only Mistral Research License, with a paid licence for commercial self-deployment, Mistral) and price tiers (Small 3 and 3.1 back on Apache 2.0, Medium 3 on the API), plus a model per job: Codestral for code, trained for fill-in-the-middle so it completes inside a file given both the prefix and the suffix, the shape editor autocomplete needs; Ministral edge models; Pixtral for vision (from September 2024), an image encoder feeding the language decoder so images and text interleave in one context; Voxtral for speech understanding (July 2025); Magistral (June 2025), Mistral's first reasoning models; and Devstral (May 2025) for agentic coding, tuned for the loop of reading a repository, editing files and running tests inside a scaffold rather than emitting one code block.
  • Mistral 3 (2 December 2025): Large 3 plus Ministral 3 at 3B, 8B and 14B, each in base, instruct and reasoning variants, all multimodal and Apache 2.0; Mistral reports 85% on AIME 2025 for the 14B reasoning variant (Mistral). Devstral 2 and Devstral Small 2 followed a week later with the Vibe command-line coding agent.
  • Consolidation, 2026. The specialists folded back into generalists: Small 4 (March) absorbed Magistral and Devstral behind a reasoning setting, and Medium 3.5 (April, announced in May) replaced Magistral and Devstral 2 in Mistral's own products. Vision is now built into Large 3, Small 4 and Medium 3.5, and every Pixtral, Magistral and Devstral version is in the deprecated table of Mistral's catalogue. Specialists remain where the job is different in kind: speech (Voxtral TTS, March, open weights under a non-commercial licence; the Voxtral transcription models), document OCR (OCR 4 in June, replaced by OCR 4.1 in July), formal proofs (Leanstral), safety (Shieldstral, August) and robotics (Robostral Navigate, July).
  • The next flagship. In July 2026 Mistral opened partner early access to a new open-weight model that Tech Times reports is expected to be considerably larger than Large 3, and which Mensch called a "fat but sparse" family, which points to a mixture of experts (Tech Times). By the end of September 2026 no name, size or licence had been published (nothing in Mistral's news index through 28 September names one).
Shieldstral 1.0 · 4 August 2026 · Apache 2.0

A guard model (text and image input, 32k context, public preview) that takes a safety policy written in natural language as a question and answers yes or no, covering prompt moderation, response moderation, prompt-response pairs and refusal detection (model card). Because the policy is an input rather than a fixed list of harm categories trained into the weights, a policy change needs no retraining, and because it is a separate model, the policy lives outside the generator. Mistral says it "matches models up to 7x its size on text safety" and sets a new state of the art on multimodal moderation (Mistral); it does not win every text benchmark (85.0 on BeaverTails against 85.9 for Qwen3Guard-8B, Hugging Face). Size: 3.8B on the docs card, "3B" in the post and the repository name (built on Ministral 3 3B).

Robostral Navigate · 8 July 2026 · licence not named

An 8B model that takes images from a single RGB camera plus a plain-language instruction ("leave the lobby, walk through the corridor, enter the supply room and stop to face the second shelf") and outputs navigation actions: a target point in the image with an orientation, or a displacement in the robot's frame. Trained entirely in simulation on about 2.4 million trajectories across 350,000 scenes, then refined with online reinforcement learning; Mistral reports 76.6% success on unseen environments of the R2R-CE vision-and-language navigation benchmark (Mistral). The announcement names no licence and links no weights.

Leanstral · March 2026; 1.5 · July 2026 · Apache 2.0

Open-weight Lean 4 proof generation, built on the Small 4 family: 119B total and 6B active by the blog (6.5B on the card) (Leanstral; Leanstral 1.5; Hugging Face). Leanstral 1.5 solves 587 of 672 PutnamBench problems by Mistral's report. Both versions are now retired from the API (30 June and 30 September 2026); the weights remain.

How Mistral trains

Mistral publishes weights first and papers rarely, so most of its recipes have to be inferred from the artefacts. Two things are documented, both in the Magistral paper.

Distillation from its own larger models. Magistral Small (24B, Apache 2.0) was cold-started with supervised fine-tuning of Mistral Small 3 on reasoning traces from Magistral Medium (correct traces from Medium's RL run, plus Medium's answers to OpenThoughts and OpenR1 code prompts, plus 10% general instruction data), then trained with reinforcement learning on top. That is distillation in the ordinary sense: the small model learns from the large one's outputs rather than from raw data alone.

Magistral's RL recipe, the one place the lab was genuinely open about method. Magistral Medium was trained from Mistral Medium 3 by pure reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR): no learned reward model, and no reasoning traces distilled from someone else's reasoner. A rollout is scored by mechanically checking its final answer (a maths result by a rule-based checker with SymPy, or code against 20 unit tests), so style cannot game the reward the way it can game a preference model. The algorithm is GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimisation: sample a group of answers to one prompt and score each against the group's mean instead of a learned critic; the full objective is on RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR), with Mistral's own changes:

  • No KL penalty to the starting model, so the policy may move as far as the reward pulls it; Mistral found the policy diverges regardless and the reference copy was not worth its compute.
  • Clip-higher: the upper clipping threshold εhigh raised so rare but useful tokens can gain probability faster: tuned between 0.26 and 0.28 for Medium, 0.3 for Small. For scale, PPO's original setting is 0.2 on both sides (PPO paper); the Magistral paper does not state its lower bound. A token at probability 0.01 may rise to 0.0128 in one update at 0.28, against 0.012 at 0.2.
  • Length-normalised loss over all tokens of all answers in a group, so long answers do not dominate; the advantage is simply ri − μ (no division by the group's spread), normalised again across each minibatch.
  • Zero-advantage filtering: groups where every answer got the same reward carry no learning signal and are dropped from the batch.
  • Reward: 0 if the format is wrong (one <think> block, the answer in \boxed{} or a fenced code block); otherwise 0.1 for the format, a further 0.9 for a correct answer, a soft length penalty of up to −0.1 that grows linearly once an answer passes lmax − lcache (raised from 16k to 32k tokens during Medium's run), and 0.1 more when the problem, the reasoning and the answer are all in the same language (10% of problems were translated into French, Spanish, Italian, German, Chinese and Russian).

Try: one group of eight answers to the same problem. Toggle what each answer got right and watch the rewards, the advantages and the filter. Make every answer correct and the group is dropped.

Reward rules from the Magistral paper, section 2.2; the group of eight and the answers are illustrative (the paper does not state the group size). "Half into the penalty zone" is an answer halfway between lmax − lcache and lmax. Standard GRPO divides by the group's standard deviation; Magistral does not, and normalises across the minibatch instead.

The result, on the paper's own evaluation: AIME 2024 pass@1 went from 26.8% for Mistral Medium 3 to 73.6% for Magistral Medium (90.0% by majority vote over 64 samples), and Magistral Small reached 70.7%, against 65.4% with the distilled traces alone. The broader story of reasoning models and how their budgets are spent is on Topic: llms (Deeper: test-time compute).

Apache 2.0 is the rung that made Mistral's name. It matters as an engineering fact, not only a legal one: no acceptable-use annex and no user threshold of the kind in Meta's Llama community licence (which incorporates an acceptable use policy and requires a separate licence above 700 million monthly active users, Llama 3.1 licence), so a company can fine-tune the model and run it inside a product without asking anyone. It is also the wedge into government, defence and on-premises deals, where open weights under EU jurisdiction beat a few points of benchmark. But since 2025 the ladder has widened at both ends: the dense agentic flagships carry a revenue carve-out that bites at $20M a month, far below Llama's threshold; the voice model is non-commercial; and code completion, OCR and embeddings are API only. "Mistral is Apache 2.0" is true of its MoE flagship and its small models, not of the catalogue.

The business around the models

  • Products. The consumer assistant Le Chat became Vibe on 28 May 2026 ("Le Chat is now Vibe", Mistral); Vibe is also the name of Mistral's open-source (Apache 2.0) command-line coding agent, launched with Devstral 2. Developers use the API platform and AI Studio; enterprises building custom models use Forge (March 2026, Mistral). Since 11 August 2026 the platform offers regional endpoints that let customers choose Europe or the US for inference, a priority tier for critical workloads (public preview), and third-party open models beside Mistral's own (Mistral; the catalogue lists Z.ai GLM 5.3).
  • Compute. Mistral Compute (announced June 2025) is a private stack of GPUs, orchestration, APIs and services on Nvidia reference architectures, with "tens of thousands of GPUs" (Mistral). The aim of 1 GW of compute capacity in Europe by 2030 was stated on 11 August 2026 (Mistral) and repeated after the Series D.
  • Money. A €1.7B Series C in September 2025, led by ASML, at an €11.7B post-money valuation (often quoted as about $14B) (Mistral). Then a €3B Series D on 8 September 2026, led by Samsung Electronics with EQT's Scaleup Europe Fund and PSG Equity as co-leads, at more than €21B post-money, which Mistral calls the largest equity round ever completed by a European technology company; the money goes to compute, infrastructure, commercial growth and international expansion (TechCrunch). It acquired Koyeb (a Paris start-up, February 2026, Wikipedia) and Emmi AI (industrial engineering simulation, May 2026, Mistral), and opened a Munich hub for industrial AI on 28 September 2026 (Mistral).
  • Distribution. Since 16 September 2026 Mistral Small 4 is one of the selectable models in Mozilla's Firefox Smart Window beta, starting in France and North America, with zero data retention agreed by Mistral (Mistral; Gigazine). It is the sovereignty argument reaching a consumer surface rather than a procurement one.

Which Mistral model when

  • The most capable open weights you can run yourself under Apache 2.0: Large 3, if you have a full eight-GPU node. It buys the best Mistral weights with no licence conditions, and costs 675 GB of FP8 weights.
  • Cheap, fast and still multimodal with a reasoning switch: Small 4. It buys 6B-active decode cost and a small cache, and costs memory for all 119B parameters.
  • Agentic coding and tool use, self-hosted: Medium 3.5, unless your company is over the $20M monthly revenue line, in which case Devstral Small 2 (Apache 2.0, 24B, weights only now) is the unencumbered option, or Medium 3.5 through the API.
  • On a phone or laptop: Ministral 3 at 3B or 8B.
  • A guard model in front of any generator, Mistral's or not: Shieldstral, with your policy written as text.
  • Code completion inside an editor: Codestral, through the API.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Mistral models use sliding-window attention."
Mistral 7B did; Mixtral, a few months later, already used full attention over 32k, and the current models use full grouped-query or latent attention. Treat SWA as a 2023 Mistral choice, not a family trait.
"8x7B means 56B parameters."
Only the feed-forward networks are replicated; attention and embeddings are shared, so it is 46.7B total and 12.9B active.
"Mixtral's experts are topic experts."
Mistral's own routing analysis found syntactic rather than topical specialisation, and strong repetition between consecutive tokens.
"Everything Mistral ships is Apache 2.0."
Medium 3.5 and Devstral 2 are under a modified MIT licence with a $20M monthly revenue exception, Voxtral TTS is non-commercial, and Codestral, OCR and the embedding models have no public weights.
"Magistral is Mistral's reasoning model."
It was; reasoning is now a setting on Small 4 and Medium 3.5, and every Magistral version is deprecated in the catalogue.
"Magistral was all pure RL."
Magistral Medium was; Magistral Small started from Magistral Medium's traces.
"The 131K reach means Mistral 7B handles 131K-token inputs."
It is a theoretical bound on how far information can propagate through 32 windowed layers; the model was trained on 8,192-token contexts.
"Large 3 is a scaled-up Mixtral."
Its configuration is DeepSeek-V3's skeleton, latent attention and a shared expert included, with 128 experts instead of 256. Mixtral's design survives in the name "MoE", not in the shape.

Weights and cache: every model rebuilt from its config

Every number here is summed from the model's published configuration (layer count, widths, heads, experts) rather than copied from a headline, so you can see which headline counts what, and what each model takes to hold and to run.

1. Where the headline parameter count comes from

Try: Small 4 is selected. Untick the input embedding: the active sum lands on the model card's 6.5B; untick the vision encoder as well and it lands on the blog's 6B. Nothing reaches the blog's 8B. Then pick Devstral 2 and Medium 3.5: the same text model, counted two ways.

Sums: attention projections, norms, routers and feed-forward or expert weights per layer from the config, plus the embedding tables; the vision projector's shape (a 2 × 2 patch merger and a two-layer adapter) is assumed, so vision totals carry a few hundredths of a billion of uncertainty. Verdict: ✓ inside the stated figure's rounding, ≈ within 1%, ✗ beyond. These reproduce Mistral's figures independently, not by construction; where none does (Small 4's 8B), the table says so.

2. What it takes to hold and run

Try: set FP8, 256K and 8 sequences: Large 3 still fits one 8 × H200 node, while Medium 3.5's cache alone is larger than its weights.

Grey: weights at the chosen precision (Mistral's stated totals; Ministral 3 at the computed totals). Colour: the KV cache in 16-bit for all sequences, from each config (Mistral 7B capped at its 4,096 window; contexts capped at each model's maximum). Activations and framework overhead are left out, so real deployments need headroom. GPU sizes: H100 80 GB, H200 141 GB. Defaults reproduce, independently, the model card's statement that FP8 Large 3 runs on one node of eight H200s.

3. Cache per sequence against context

Bytes per token from each config: GQA models store 2 × layers × key-value heads × 128 numbers; latent-attention models store (latent + 64) × layers. Large 3 and Small 4 cache 576 and 320 numbers per layer per token (68.6 KiB and 22.5 KiB per token over all layers), against 2,048 per layer for Medium 3.5 (2 × 8 heads × 128) over 88 layers, 352 KiB per token. Mistral 7B flattens at its window; the tick marks its 8,192-token training context. Defaults reproduce the worked example (128 KiB per token, 512 MiB at 32k with the window, 4 GiB without) independently. Medium 3.5 and Devstral 2 share one line, as they share one shape.

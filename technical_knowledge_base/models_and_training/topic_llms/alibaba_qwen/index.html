The strategy: breadth as the moat

Where DeepSeek optimises cost per served token and Moonshot optimises frontier capability per training run, Qwen optimises coverage.

Every generation ships a full ladder of sizes, from sub-billion models that run on a phone, through mid-size dense models, to a sparse flagship. It ships in several modalities too: Coder, VL (vision-language), Audio, Math and Embedding.

That compounds. A Qwen base exists at whatever size and licence a project needs, so Qwen bases became the default starting point for open research and for distillation, including DeepSeek's own R1 distillations. Being the substrate everyone else builds on is a position no benchmark win can buy. It also feeds Alibaba Cloud Model Studio, where the same models are the paid, hosted option.

The licence is not one licence: check the checkpoint, not the family

From Qwen3 to Qwen3.5, every open checkpoint was Apache 2.0, which permits commercial use and redistribution with essentially no conditions. The mid-size mainline still is: Qwen3.8-27B ships under Apache 2.0 (model card). Before Qwen3 and after Qwen3.5 the picture is mixed:

  • Before Qwen3, the largest sizes had Qwen's own licences.
    • The Tongyi Qianwen License (Qwen-7B and Qwen-72B, Qwen1.5, Qwen2-72B) requires a separate licence for a product with more than 100 million monthly active users. It also forbids using the model or its outputs to improve any other large language model (Qwen-72B licence).
    • Qwen2.5-72B kept the 100-million clause under the Qwen License (licence).
    • Qwen2.5-3B was research only (licence).
  • Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, the open-weight flagship, ships under the Qwen3.8-Max License (licence).
    • A product above 100 million monthly active users or 20 million US dollars of monthly revenue must display the model name.
    • A licensee whose group runs a model-as-a-service or "AI work assistant" (coding or office productivity) business, with group revenue above 50 million US dollars in any twelve months, needs a separate licence.
    • Internal use whose outputs never reach a third party is exempt.
  • Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, the Qwen4 architecture preview, ships under the Qwen Community License 1.0 (licence). The attribution thresholds are the same, but any model-as-a-service or AI work assistant business needs a separate licence at any size. The same internal-use exemption applies.
  • Qwen-Image-2.1 ships under the Qwen Research License Agreement: non-commercial only, and commercial use needs a separate, unpublished agreement (licence). Detail is on Topic: generative-and-multimodal.

The GitHub repository and the family name tell you nothing. Read the LICENSE file of the checkpoint you deploy. The Licences tab checks a use case against about thirty checkpoints.

Two caveats when reading benchmark claims.

  • Arena placings (Text Arena, Vision Arena, Frontend Code Arena) are human pairwise-preference leaderboards. They measure which answer people prefer, not whether a task was completed correctly, and they move with formatting and verbosity as well as with capability.
  • The current model changes every few months. The lineage and the model table therefore age faster than the architecture sections, which are the durable part.

Definitions

Total and active parameters (the A-notation)
"235B-A22B" means 235 billion parameters stored and 22 billion used per token. Memory is priced on the first, decode speed on the second, and their ratio is the sparsity ratio (about 11 times here).
Routed and shared experts
A mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer replaces one feed-forward network with many smaller ones (experts), plus a router that picks the top k experts for each token. A shared expert is an extra expert that every token passes through regardless of the router.
Linear attention
Replaces softmax attention with a recurrence over a fixed-size state matrix, so memory per request does not grow with context. Gated DeltaNet (GDN) is the variant Qwen uses. Before writing a new value, it erases what the state stores under that key (the delta rule), and it decays the whole state by a learned per-token gate.
Gated attention
Qwen's name for ordinary softmax attention with a learned sigmoid gate multiplied onto each head's output.
Hybrid layout
A stack that interleaves linear-attention layers with full-attention layers. Qwen writes its layout as, for example, 12 × (3 × (Gated DeltaNet → MoE) → 1 × (Gated Attention → MoE)).
Qwen Sparse Attention (QSA)
A trained sparse attention in two passes. A small indexer first scores the context in micro-blocks of a few tokens; full attention then reads only the selected blocks.
Thinking mode and thinking budget
A thinking mode is a long chain of thought before the answer. A thinking budget is a caller-set cap on how many tokens that chain may use.
Snapshot id
A date suffix (Qwen3.8-Max-0902) naming one post-trained state of a model whose name otherwise stays fixed.

Scope: Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models and LLM Architecture Gallery own the generic mechanisms: top-k routing, load-balancing losses, linear and sparse attention, and the KV cache. The MoE layer: from no shared expert to 512 tiny experts

Qwen3: 128 experts, top-8, no shared expert. Qwen3-235B-A22B has 94 layers, each with 128 experts, of which the router picks 8 per token (Qwen3 report, Table 2; config). The report states the two departures from its own predecessor directly:

  • "Unlike Qwen2.5-MoE, the Qwen3-MoE design excludes shared experts."
  • It adopts the global-batch load-balancing loss "to encourage expert specialization".

DeepSeek, by contrast, routes 8 of 256 finer-grained experts plus a shared expert that every token passes through. The shared expert absorbs the common computation, so the routed experts can specialise. Qwen3 left it out and let the router handle everything. That is simpler and adds no fixed cost per token, at the risk that the routed experts each relearn general behaviour.

Global-batch balancing

This is the less visible half of the choice. The generic load-balancing loss (LBL) is explained on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models:

LBL=NE∑i=1NEfiPi
NE
number of experts
fi
fraction of the batch's tokens routed to expert i
Pi
the router's average probability for expert i over the same tokens

The loss is smallest (1) when traffic is even. In practice it is usually computed per micro-batch and then averaged, and a micro-batch holds only a few sequences, often packed from one domain. So it forces even traffic within a handful of documents: a micro-batch of pure code is pushed to spread over experts that should not see code.

Computing fi and Pi over the whole global batch, synchronised across all data-parallel ranks, demands balance only over a diverse mix, so a code sequence may route to code experts.

Qwen's own paper on it reports about 0.1 lower pre-training perplexity and about 2 points on benchmarks, and much stronger domain specialisation of experts. The extra communication step costs under 3% latency, because the synchronised statistic is just one number per expert. Its experiments go up to 42.8B total parameters on 400B tokens (Qiu et al., 2025).

Try: at 100% each domain sends its tokens to its own two experts. Measured micro-batch by micro-batch, that routing is penalised at 4 times the minimum. Measured over the global batch, the same routing is perfectly balanced. Slide towards 0% (uniform routing) and the two measures agree. The toy setting is illustrative: 8 experts, four single-domain micro-batches, and Pi equal to fi. The formula is the paper's (eq. 2 and 3).

From Qwen3-Next on: 512 experts, 10 routed plus 1 shared

Qwen3-Next reversed the no-shared-expert choice and pushed granularity much further (Qwen3-Next model card, Qwen3-Next blog, config):

  • 512 experts;
  • 10 routed per token, plus 1 shared;
  • each expert's hidden width only 512;
  • the router's parameters normalised at initialisation, so no expert is favoured before training starts.

Every later large Qwen keeps this layout: Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next all route 10 of 512 plus 1 shared (Qwen3.5 card, Qwen3.8-2.4T card, Flash-Next card). So "Qwen has no shared expert" is true of the Qwen3 generation only.

A token now uses 10 of 512 routed experts (1.95%), where Qwen3 used 8 of 128 (6.25%). That is what lets total parameters grow far faster than per-token compute.

Switch layouts: the lit share falls from 6.25% to 2.1% (counting the shared expert) while each expert grows. Flash-Next's 21x is the most aggressive ratio among the flash-tier models on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, though not in the open frontier as a whole: DeepSeek V4 Pro sits near 33x and Kimi K3 near 27x at flagship scale. What 6B active buys is the absolute figure rather than the ratio, since 6B active costs roughly what a 6B dense model costs to decode.

High sparsity is not a free lunch.

  • All experts must still be resident in memory.
  • All-to-all traffic per token grows with the number of experts a token visits.
  • Load imbalance worsens as experts get smaller.
  • Fine-tuning or RL on a very sparse model is harder, because each expert sees fewer tokens.

The shared expert's return in Next is one answer to the last point. At 512 experts each routed expert sees about one fiftieth of the token stream (10/512), and an always-on expert keeps the common computation in one well-trained place.

Attention: the 3:1 hybrid of Gated DeltaNet and gated attention

The generic idea. A linear-attention layer keeps a fixed-size state matrix per head instead of a growing cache of past keys and values. It writes each token into the state and reads it with the current query, so its memory per request is constant and its cost over a sequence is linear rather than quadratic. The price is that the state is a lossy summary, and exact recall of an early token degrades. Hybrids keep a few full-attention layers to buy exact recall back. LLM Architecture Gallery owns the general treatment and compares the hybrids across labs.

Qwen's linear layer: Gated DeltaNet. Each head updates its state St with the gated delta rule (Yang et al., 2024):

St=αtSt−1(I−βt𝐤t𝐤t⊤)+βt𝐯t𝐤t⊤,𝐨t=St𝐪t
St

qt, kt, vt

αt ∈ (0, 1)

βt ∈ (0, 1)

I

The bracket subtracts what the state currently returns for key kt before the new value is written, so a write replaces an association rather than piling on top of it.

Try: three writes go into one state: key k1 with value v1, then k2 with v2, then k1 again with a new value v3. Then the state is read with q = k1. The delta rule returns v3, the latest value. Plain linear attention returns v1 + v3, the old and new values piled up. Lower α to see the decay forget both. The vectors are illustrative, with orthonormal keys in 3 dimensions; the arithmetic is the formula above.

Qwen's version of the layer.

  • Short causal convolution. Queries, keys and values pass through a short causal convolution and a SiLU before the recurrence (kernel 4 in every config).
  • Gate parameterisation. The write strength is βt = σ(Wβxt), and the decay is an exponential of a softplus, so it stays in (0, 1).
  • Output gate. The output is RMS-normalised and multiplied by a gate. In Flash-Next the gate is a sigmoid, where the original Gated DeltaNet uses SiLU; the paper reports "consistent improvements" from the change (Flash-Next architecture paper, eq. 6 to 11). The Qwen3.8-27B and 2.4T configurations still list output_gate_type: swish (27B config, 2.4T config); Flash-Next's lists sigmoid (config).
  • Custom kernels. The recurrence has to run in a chunked parallel form to use tensor cores at all. Qwen's FlashQLA kernels run 2 to 3 times faster forward, and about 2 times backward, than the reference Triton kernels (FlashQLA, via the Flash-Next paper).

The layout. Qwen3-Next has 48 layers, as 12 × (3 × (Gated DeltaNet → MoE) → 1 × (Gated Attention → MoE)): 36 linear layers and 12 full-attention layers. Qwen's blog says that at this 3:1 ratio "the model consistently outperforms any monolithic architecture", all-attention or all-linear, and calls it the balance between DeltaNet's efficiency and attention's recall (Qwen3-Next blog). The Flash-Next paper later published the comparison behind that choice:

Average of 9 base-model benchmarks (MMLU, MMLU-Pro, SuperGPQA, MATH, GSM8K, BBH, MMMLU, EvalPlus, MultiPL-E), unweighted. Each model is a 28-layer 25B-A3B MoE on the Qwen3.5 architecture, trained on 400B tokens at 4K and then 80B at 32K. Both hybrids keep one full-attention layer in four; the sliding-window (SWA) layers use a 128-token window. The GDN hybrid beats full attention on 8 of the 9 benchmarks and the SWA hybrid on 7 (Flash-Next paper, Table 1).

The ratio is not higher because recall through the linear layers is lossy. The retrieval and copying behaviours agents depend on travel through the full-attention layers, and the paper calls periodic full attention "particularly important for long-context performance".

The same 1-in-4 interval appears in every later model's configuration:

  • Qwen3.5-397B: 60 layers, 15 of them full attention.
  • Qwen3.8-27B: 64 layers, 16 full. Its feed-forward layers are dense, so it is "dense" in the MoE sense only.
  • Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B: 92 layers, 23 full.
  • Flash-Next: 48 layers, 12 of them sparse attention.

The full-attention layers keep their rotary embedding. A variant without positional encoding (NoPE) looked the same in pre-training, but after post-training it showed "a substantially higher rate of endless generation" (Flash-Next paper).

Qwen's full-attention layer: gated attention

The quarter of layers that keep softmax attention are modified in three ways (Qwen3-Next blog):

  • An output gate. Each head's attention output is multiplied elementwise by σ(Wgxt), a sigmoid of a learned projection of the layer input, before the output projection. Qwen's NeurIPS 2025 paper tested this across 30 variants of 15B MoE (2.54B active) and 1.7B dense models trained on up to 3.5T tokens (Qiu et al., 2025). It found that the gate:
    • adds non-linearity to the otherwise low-rank value-to-output path;
    • makes the output query-dependently sparse;
    • removes the attention sink, where heads dump weight on the first token because softmax must put its probability somewhere. In the baseline 46.7% of attention across layers went to the first token; with the gate, 4.8%. The associated massive activations go too;
    • improves training stability, tolerance of higher learning rates and long-context extrapolation.
  • Head dimension 256 instead of 128. Qwen3-Next, Qwen3.5 and Flash-Next have 2 key-value heads; Qwen3.8-27B and the 2.4T model have 4.
  • Rotary position embedding on the first 25% of position dimensions only (64 of 256, partial_rotary_factor: 0.25 in every hybrid's config). The rest carry no position signal, which the blog credits with better length extrapolation.

What the cache costs

Only full-attention layers keep a cache that grows with context, so

KV bytes per token=Lfull×2×Hkv×dh×b
Lfull
number of full-attention layers
2
one key and one value
Hkv
number of key-value heads
dh
head dimension
b
bytes per stored number (2 for 16-bit)

Each DeltaNet layer instead holds a fixed state of Hv × dk × dv numbers per request, where Hv is the number of value heads.

Worked example: Qwen3-235B-A22B against Qwen3-Next.

  • Qwen3-235B has full attention in all 94 layers, with 4 KV heads of 128: 94 × 2 × 4 × 128 × 2 = 192,512 bytes, 188 KiB per token, 47 GiB for one request at 262,144 tokens.
  • Qwen3-Next: 12 × 2 × 2 × 256 × 2 = 24,576 bytes, 24 KiB per token, 6 GiB at 262,144 tokens.
  • Next's states. Its 36 DeltaNet layers each hold 32 value heads of 128 × 128: 36 × 32 × 128 × 128 = 18,874,368 numbers, 36 MiB at 2 bytes, whatever the context length.
  • Crossover. Had those 36 layers been full attention, they would add 36 × 2 × 2 × 256 × 2 = 73,728 bytes (72 KiB) per token. So the fixed state is already cheaper than the cache it replaces after 36 MiB ÷ 72 KiB = 512 tokens.

The same formula across the 3.8 generation:

  • Qwen3.5-397B: 15 full layers with 2 KV heads of 256, 30 KiB per token.
  • Qwen3.8-27B: 16 layers with 4 heads, 64 KiB.
  • Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B: 23 layers with 4 heads, 92 KiB, half of what Qwen3-235B needs for ten times the parameters.

These configurations also set mamba_ssm_dtype: float32. If serving keeps the DeltaNet state in 32-bit (unconfirmed), every state figure doubles; the Configs and cache tab has the toggle.

What it bought, as Qwen reported it (Qwen3-Next blog):

  • Training data. Qwen3-Next was pretrained on 15T tokens, a uniformly sampled subset of Qwen3's 36T.
  • Training compute. It used less than 80% of the GPU hours of Qwen3-30B-A3B and 9.3% of the compute cost of the dense Qwen3-32B, and outperformed Qwen3-32B-Base on most benchmarks.
  • Throughput against Qwen3-32B. Prefill was nearly 7 times at 4K context and over 10 times beyond 32K; decode was nearly 4 times at 4K and over 10 times beyond 32K.

These are vendor-reported throughputs on Qwen's own serving stack.

Two further stability changes arrived with Next:

  • Zero-centred, weight-decayed layer normalisation. The norm's scale is parameterised as an offset from 1 and weight decay is applied to it, so norm weights cannot grow without bound. Flash-Next applies the same norm everywhere.
  • Multi-token prediction (MTP). Extra heads predict tokens further ahead and serve as a built-in draft for speculative decoding. The generic mechanism is on LLM Architecture Gallery.

QSA and the Qwen4 preview: Qwen3.8-Flash-Next

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next was released on 26 August 2026 as an explicit preview of the Qwen4 architecture. Qwen said the early release gives the developer community "an opportunity to prepare for the full Qwen4 model family" (TechNode). It keeps the 3:1 layout and changes what the quarter of global layers do (Flash-Next card, Flash-Next architecture paper).

Qwen Sparse Attention. The model is pretrained with ordinary full attention in one layer of every four. At continued pretraining (256K-token sequences) those full-attention layers, and the one in the MTP module, are replaced by QSA. QSA works in two passes per query:

  1. A compressed lightweight indexer scores the context at micro-block granularity.
    • Keys are average-pooled over blocks of r = 4 tokens, then normalised.
    • Pooling happens before positional encoding, so tokens with different rotary phases are not averaged together. Each block gets one position, its first token's.
    • The indexer is a small multi-query attention with 4 query heads and 1 shared key head, with rotary embedding on 64 of its 128 dimensions.
    • A block's score is the ReLU of the query-key similarity, summed over the 4 heads. Only fully observed blocks can be scored.
  2. Attention proper reads only what was selected. Attention (24 query heads, 2 KV heads of 256) reads only the top ⌈K / r⌉ = 512 blocks, a token budget of K = 2,048, plus the tokens of the last, unfinished block, which are always kept.
blocks scored=Tr,tokens attended=K,indexer cost:O(n2)→O(n2/r)
T
context length in tokens

K

Why blocks. The paper's stated reason is the indexer's own cost. DeepSeek's DSA scores individual tokens with a lightweight indexer, and "as sequence length increases, the overhead of its O(n²) indexer remains non-negligible". Compressing keys fourfold cuts indexer work and top-k selection by about the compression ratio.

The paper also compared intra-layer compression with IndexShare, which shares one top-k across adjacent full-attention layers. QSA matched full attention at a quarter of the indexer latency; IndexShare stayed below full attention even at half. In a hybrid, the full-attention layers sit three DeltaNet layers apart and resemble each other too little to share indices.

A second benefit is often cited: gathering runs of 4 adjacent tokens suits GPU memory access better than scattered single-token gathers. That is plausible, but the paper does not make the argument, so treat it as unconfirmed.

With QSA, Qwen joins DeepSeek (DSA, then CSA), Moonshot (KDA) and MiniMax (MSA) in shipping a trainable sparse or linear attention in an open-weight line. Zhipu's GLM-5.3-Flash, released the same week, pools every four indexer keys into one in the same way (Zhipu: GLM; MarkTechPost comparison).

How it was trained.

  1. Dense distillation. Only the indexer is trained, for 1,000 steps of 8 sequences of 256K tokens (about 2B tokens), to match the full attention's own distribution. That distribution is summed over heads and max-pooled to blocks, so that one salient token is not diluted, and the indexer learns it through a KL loss.
  2. Sparse training. Backbone and indexer are trained jointly with QSA switched on, for 8,000 steps of 96 such sequences (about 200B tokens). The language-modelling loss stays within about 10−4 of the full-attention run.

Results against full attention (same model, Flash-Next paper, Tables 2 to 4):

  • QSA matches or beats full attention on 7 of 8 short-context benchmarks (average 75.9 to 76.8).
  • It does better as context grows: RULER beyond 512K rises from 90.08 to 93.00, and 8-needle MRCR from 30.66 to 40.53 at 512K and from 20.71 to 26.44 at 1M.
  • The MTP draft's mean accepted length is unchanged (4.06 against 4.07).

Worked example at T = 1,000,000 (Flash-Next's extended context):

  • The indexer scores 1,000,000 ÷ 4 = 250,000 blocks with its cheap single-key-head pass.
  • Attention reads 2,048 tokens instead of 1,000,000, about 488 times fewer.
  • The measured speedup of the attention module at 1M context is 7.6 times for prefill and 4.9 times for decode, not 488 times, because the indexer still scans every block and selection and gathering cost time.

Those are kernel-level measurements against FlashInfer's paged grouped-query attention, with chunked prefill in 16K chunks at batch 1, and decode at batch 4 with three MTP steps. Speedups start at 64K context.

The cache does not shrink. Every key must stay available for the indexer to choose from, so Flash-Next still stores 12 × 2 × 2 × 256 × 2 bytes, 24 KiB per token, plus the indexer's keys. QSA cuts reads and arithmetic, not storage. Switch the animation above to "Hybrid + QSA" to watch both counters.

The other Qwen4-preview changes

From the same paper and card:

  • Sparsity. 125B total with 6B active, routing 10 of 512 experts plus 1 shared, about 21 times; each expert's hidden width is 640 (config). The 125B excludes two further blocks of parameters: 51B of n-gram embedding and 4B of MTP.
  • An n-gram embedding. A table of 20,000,000 bigram and trigram entries, each 2,560 wide: 20,000,000 × 2,560 = 51.2B parameters, about 102 GB in 16-bit (derived).
    • It is looked up at layer 2 with a contextual gate. Because the lookup address is known from the input tokens alone, the table can live in host memory and be prefetched while layer 1 runs, adding capacity at negligible per-token compute and no accelerator memory.
    • A larger n-gram vocabulary lowered loss monotonically while downstream accuracy saturated, one of several places where the paper found loss and benchmarks disagreeing.
  • A gated residual stream. The residual is widened to 4 branches.
    • Each layer reads them through an elementwise sigmoid gate predicted from all branches, with bottleneck rank d/8 = 2,560/8 = 320.
    • It writes its output back to every branch through one data-dependent scalar per branch. The 4 × 4 branch-mixing matrix of hyper-connections was dropped as adding little.
    • The gate decides how the extra capacity is used and supplies the rescaling that keeps training stable.
  • The Muon optimiser for two-dimensional weight matrices.
    • It runs 8 Newton-Schulz orthogonalisation steps, and fused matrices are split before orthogonalising.
    • The router, the embeddings, the output head and the gated-residual projections stay on AdamW.
    • A refitted scaling law raised the learning rate and batch size and dropped batch-size warm-up, which ended no better and cost 18.8% more optimiser steps.
    • The full run had no loss spike, without qk-clip or other activation clipping.
  • 4B of multi-token-prediction parameters, a vision encoder, and context of 262,144 tokens native, extensible to 1,000,000.

The headline. The base model was compared with the previous generation's 397B-A17B flagship, which the paper's Table 11 names as Qwen3.7-Plus, a closed, hosted model. Flash-Next leads on 8 of 14 benchmarks and trails on the other 6 by at most 2.6 points, with a third of the activated parameters, a third of the training tokens and about a ninth of the training FLOPs. It also beats Qwen3.8-27B-Base on all 14.

Flash-Next-Base minus Qwen3.7-Plus-Base, in points, from Table 11. Defaults reproduce the paper's "8 of 14" and "at most 2.6" independently: the largest trail is MultiPL-E at 2.59. Base models, pre-training evaluation, the paper's own pipeline. The ninth of the FLOPs is the paper's figure; Qwen3.7-Plus's token count is unpublished, so it cannot be recomputed.

It shipped in the same week as two other open models:

  • Zhipu's GLM-5.3-Flash (320B total, 18B active; TechNode);
  • Tencent's Hy4 preview (770B total, 49B active; Hugging Face).

The three point the same way: the open frontier competes on active-parameter efficiency and attention sparsity rather than on total parameter count.

Hybrid thinking and thinking budgets

One checkpoint, two modes. From Qwen3, a single checkpoint serves both a fast direct-answer mode and a long chain-of-thought mode (Qwen3 report, section 4.3).

  • The caller selects the mode with a chat-template switch, or with /think and /no_think flags in the system or user message. The model follows the last flag it sees and thinks by default.
  • In no-think mode the assistant turn still carries an empty thinking block, so the format is identical in both modes.

The behaviour comes from the third of four post-training stages:

  1. Long chain-of-thought cold start: supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces.
  2. Reasoning RL.
  3. Thinking mode fusion: continued supervised fine-tuning on a mix of thinking and non-thinking data, each marked with its flag, so one model learns both behaviours conditioned on the marker. The thinking data is generated by the stage-2 model itself through rejection sampling.
  4. General RL across tasks and formats.

The RL machinery behind stages 2 and 4 is on RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR, which also covers GSPO, the Qwen team's sequence-level variant of GRPO.

The thinking budget. The caller caps the thinking tokens. When the chain reaches the cap, the serving code halts it and inserts a fixed stop-thinking instruction, "Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now.", followed by the closing think tag. The model then answers from the partial reasoning.

The report says this ability to answer from incomplete thinking "is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally" from thinking mode fusion. Accuracy on its mathematics, coding and STEM evaluations rose smoothly as the budget grew, up to the 32K-token budgets plotted. The 38,912-token figure sometimes quoted alongside is the maximum output length the report allowed on AIME, not a budget setting.

Reasoning depth becomes a runtime dial. That matters because reasoning tokens are the dominant cost and latency term in an agentic loop, and most requests do not need them. The general mechanism, and how other labs expose the same dial, is on Reasoning models and test-time compute.

Try: with a 1,024-token budget, a chain that wanted 3,000 tokens stops at token 1,024. The instruction is inserted and the answer is written from about a third of the reasoning the model wanted. At 4,096 the chain ends on its own and the budget never binds. The chain lengths are illustrative; the inserted text is the report's.

The split. The hybrid's cost is real, and Alibaba acknowledged it: the Qwen3-2507 refresh shipped separate Instruct (non-thinking) and Thinking checkpoints at 235B-A22B, 30B-A3B and 4B, between 21 July and 5 August 2025 (Instruct-2507, Thinking-2507).

One set of weights serving both modes appears to leave quality on the table at the top end, presumably because the two behaviours want different post-training distributions. The trade is operational simplicity (one deployment, one KV cache, one set of weights in memory) against peak quality in each mode.

The split persists at the top. The open Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B is thinking-only and text-only: "thinking cannot be disabled". The hosted Qwen3.8-Max adds vision input, non-thinking modes and built-in tools (Qwen3.8-2.4T card). Flash-Next and 27B, by contrast, think by default but can switch it off per request, with a reasoning_effort of low, medium or xhigh (Flash-Next card).

Data, and how the whole ladder ships at once

Qwen3 was pretrained on about 36T tokens in 119 languages and dialects, up from Qwen2.5's 29, in three stages (Qwen3 report, section 3):

  1. Over 30T tokens of general data at 4,096-token sequences.
  2. About 5T higher-quality tokens weighted to STEM, code, reasoning and synthetic data.
  3. Hundreds of billions of tokens at 32,768-token sequences for long context.

A large share of the specialised data is synthetic. Qwen2.5-VL transcribed PDF-like documents, and Qwen2.5, Qwen2.5-Math and Qwen2.5-Coder synthesised trillions of tokens of textbooks, question-answer pairs, instructions and code.

That is the flywheel behind the cadence: each release is a data generator for the next, particularly where correctness can be checked automatically and bad samples filtered out. Qwen3.5 extended language coverage to 201 languages and dialects (Qwen3.5 card).

Strong-to-weak distillation. The ladder ships at once because the small models are not trained the hard way. The Qwen3 report builds the five small dense models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B) and Qwen3-30B-A3B by distilling from the large ones, in two phases instead of the four-stage pipeline:

  1. Off-policy. The student is fine-tuned on outputs the teachers generated in both /think and /no_think modes.
  2. On-policy. The student generates its own responses, and its next-token distribution on them is pulled toward the teacher's (Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B) by minimising the KL divergence between the two.
ℒ=∑tDKL(pteacher(·∣y<t)‖pstudent(·∣y<t)),y∼pstudent

The student is trained on the teacher's whole distribution at every position of its own outputs, not on one correct token, which is why distillation transfers far more signal per token than fine-tuning on text.

Qwen3-8B, starting from the same off-policy-distilled checkpoint, math and code queries only (Qwen3 report, Table 21). On-policy distillation reached 74.4 and 65.5 on AIME'24 and '25 for 1,800 GPU hours. RL reached 67.6 and 55.5 for 17,920. 1,800 ÷ 17,920 = 10.0%, reproducing the report's "approximately only 1/10 of the GPU hours". Distillation also raised pass@64 (90.0 to 93.3 on AIME'24); RL did not move it.

About a tenth of the compute bought a better model. That ratio is what makes a family from 0.6B to 235B releasable on the same day, and it is the same mechanism that made R1's distilled models work.

Trade-offs: what each choice buys and costs

  • No shared expert (Qwen3) against a shared expert (Next on). Leaving it out buys simplicity and no unconditional per-token cost. It costs routed experts relearning common behaviour, and Qwen itself reinstated the shared expert once experts became very small and numerous.
  • Global-batch balancing. It buys real expert specialisation by domain. It costs a cross-rank reduction of routing statistics every step (under 3% latency in the paper). It also allows a router that is balanced over the corpus while individual sequences, and so individual devices at serving time, are not.
  • The 3:1 hybrid. It buys a cache a quarter the size of an all-attention stack's with the same head shape, and throughput that grows with context. It costs custom kernels and lossy recall in three layers of four. The full-attention quarter is the exact-recall path; a higher ratio would save more memory and lose more retrieval.
  • QSA. It buys long-context reads that cost a token budget rather than the context length. It costs a trained indexer that scans every block, a hard selection that can miss the one block that mattered, and no saving in stored cache.
  • High sparsity. It buys the decode cost of a small dense model with the capacity of a large one. It costs memory for every expert, more all-to-all traffic, harder load balancing and harder fine-tuning.
  • Hybrid thinking. One checkpoint buys one deployment and a per-request dial. It costs peak quality in each mode, which is why 2507 and the open 2.4T release split it.
  • Distillation for the ladder. It buys a whole family from one flagship at a tenth of the RL compute per size. It costs ceiling: a student inherits its teacher's limits as well as its skills.

Common mistakes and misconceptions

"Qwen has no shared expert."
True of Qwen3 only. Every large Qwen from Qwen3-Next on routes 10 of 512 experts plus 1 shared.
"Qwen is Apache 2.0."
This is true of every open checkpoint from Qwen3 to Qwen3.5, and of the 3.6 and 3.8 mid-size models. It is not true of:
  • the pre-Qwen3 flagships (Tongyi Qianwen and Qwen licences, with a 100-million-user clause);
  • Qwen2.5-3B (research only);
  • the open 2.4T flagship, Flash-Next and Qwen-Image-2.1, which ship under three different custom licences with model-as-a-service, attribution or non-commercial conditions.
Read the checkpoint's own LICENSE file, or use the Licences tab.
"Qwen3.8-27B is a standard dense transformer."
Its feed-forward layers are dense, but three of every four token-mixing layers are Gated DeltaNet: 48 of its 64 layers.
"The model was trained to respect the thinking budget."
The budget is enforced by the serving code inserting a stop-thinking instruction. Answering well from truncated thinking emerged from thinking mode fusion rather than being trained as such.
"QSA shrinks the KV cache."
It cuts what each query reads, not what is stored. Flash-Next keeps 24 KiB per token, plus the indexer's keys.
"Sparse attention cuts attention time by the selection ratio."
Reading 2,048 tokens of 1,000,000 is 488 times fewer, but the measured module speedup at 1M is 7.6 times for prefill and 4.9 times for decode, because the indexer pass remains.
"Flash-Next was compared with Qwen3.5-397B."
The paper's "397B-A17B predecessor" is Qwen3.7-Plus, a closed hosted model, as its Table 11 names it. Qwen3.5-397B-A17B is a different, open checkpoint with the same size.
"Qwen3.8-Max" as one model.
It is a series of dated snapshots, and the hosted model and the open 2.4T-A95B checkpoint differ in modalities and modes.
Reading an Arena placing as a capability score.
It is a preference ranking at one moment, and it moves with style and verbosity.

Configs and cache

Eight Qwen checkpoints, recomputed from their own config.json. For each one: the memory one request needs at a chosen context, and where the parameters live. Every figure on the Reading tab's MoE and attention sections comes from this table.

Try: start at 262,144 tokens, the native context of every model since Next.

  • Qwen3-235B needs 47 GiB of cache for one request, and Qwen3-32B, a dense model a seventh the size, needs 64 GiB.
  • Every hybrid needs under 25 GiB including its DeltaNet states. The 2.4T flagship needs 23.3 GiB, half of Qwen3-235B's cache at ten times the parameters.
  • Slide the context down to see where each state overtakes its cache, then switch the state to 32-bit.

The formulas are the Reading tab's:

  • KV bytes per token = Lfull × 2 × Hkv × dh × 2 (16-bit cache).
  • DeltaNet state = Llinear × Hv × dk × dv numbers.
  • Expert parameters = L × E × 3dmodeldff, where E counts the shared expert when there is one.

The defaults reproduce each headline independently from the configs:

  • Experts are 97 to 99% of every MoE's headline total; the rest is attention, DeltaNet, embeddings and norms.
  • Flash-Next's 121.0B of experts sits inside its 125B, which excludes the 51B n-gram table and 4B of MTP.

Two limits on the figures:

  • The 32-bit state is an assumption from the config field mamba_ssm_dtype: float32 (Qwen3.5 and 3.8 only); how serving engines store it varies.
  • Flash-Next's QSA layers also keep indexer keys, which are not counted here.

Sources: Qwen3-32B, Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen3-235B-A22B, Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B, Qwen3.8-27B, Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next. 1M means 1,000,000 tokens; GiB is 230 bytes.

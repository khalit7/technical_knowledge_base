9. Native or pipeline: the design decision

The problem. You want a product that chats, looks at images, draws and talks. You can train one network that does it all, or wire specialised models together. Both exist in production; the choice is an engineering trade, not a question of which is more modern.

The pipeline at its most explicit. NExT-GPT uses ImageBind (1.2B) to read, a Vicuna 7B to think, and Stable Diffusion 1.5, AudioLDM and Zeroscope to write images, sound and video; the language model emits special "modality signal" tokens that tell a projection which decoder to drive, and only the projections and a LoRA are trained: "155M(=28+33+31+31+32) / [155M + 12.275B ...], or only 1% of parameters" ({{Wu et al., arXiv 2309.05519v3, section 3|https://arxiv.org/html/2309.05519v3}}) authors. By its own arithmetic that is 1.25%, not 1% derived.

A rule that survives the details. If the output must depend on fine detail of an input in another modality (edit this photo, answer in the same voice, keep this character consistent across images), information must not pass through a narrow text channel, so native or at least deeply coupled designs win. If each modality is a separate job with a clean text interface (caption, then illustrate), a pipeline of the best specialists is cheaper, faster to improve and easier to control.

How to check which you have. Ask the model to edit a detail of an input image that a caption would not mention (the pattern on a sleeve, a number in the corner), or to answer a question about a speaker's tone. A pipeline fails in characteristic ways: it redraws the image from scratch, or answers about the words only.

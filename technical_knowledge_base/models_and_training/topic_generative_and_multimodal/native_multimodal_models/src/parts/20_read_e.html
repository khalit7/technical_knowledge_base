4. Faster than one token per call

The problem. One call per token means 1,024 sequential calls for a Chameleon image and 4,096 for Emu3; each call is cheap with a KV cache, but they cannot run in parallel. Four published ways around it:

  • Masked parallel decoding (MaskGIT, 2022). Train a bidirectional transformer to predict randomly masked codes from the visible ones (the mask ratio drawn from a cosine schedule). To generate, start fully masked; each round predict every masked code, keep the most confident, re-mask the rest; 8 rounds instead of 256 calls. MaskGIT reports ImageNet 256 FID 6.18 against 15.78 for the autoregressive VQGAN and "up to 64x" faster decoding ({{arXiv 2202.04200v1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04200}}) vendor; Muse scaled it to text-to-image at 0.5 s per 256 px image on TPUv4 against 6.4 s for Parti-3B ({{arXiv 2301.00704v1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00704}}). Show-o generates its image tokens this way (section 6).
  • Next-scale prediction (VAR, 2024). Predict a 1 × 1 token map, then 2 × 2, up to 16 × 16, each scale in one parallel step: 10 steps. FID 1.92 on ImageNet 256 (1.73 with rejection sampling), against DiT-XL/2's 2.27 and claimed 45 times faster ({{arXiv 2404.02905v2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02905}}) vendor.
  • Continuous tokens with a diffusion head (MAR, 2024). Drop the codebook: predict each continuous latent token with a small diffusion model conditioned on the transformer's output. FID 1.55 with guidance (2.35 without), under 0.3 s per image on one A100 at batch 256 ({{arXiv 2406.11838v3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11838}}) vendor. This removes the tokenizer ceiling of section 5.
  • Serving tricks. LlamaGen keeps plain raster order and serves it with vLLM: 3.3 to 4.1 times faster in its Table 7 (for example 26.38 s to 6.36 s for a batch of 16 with its XXL model) vendor. Emu3.5 adapts the model to fill codes in parallel ("Discrete Diffusion Adaptation") for "about 20x" faster images.

5. Autoregressive against diffusion and flow, at equal training compute

The setup. The root's Diffusion lab trained a 1.04M-parameter TinyUNet as a DDPM for 4,000 steps at batch 128, and its Flow lab trained the same network with flow matching; both are scored with FD on 1,000 samples. What it shows. Three things, all measured on Apple M1 Pro (MPS). The tokenizer is a ceiling, and guidance can look through it. Without guidance the token model's FD () sits above the tokenizer's own reconstruction FD (), as it must: a perfect model of the codes could only match the reconstructions. Guidance (s = ) brings it to , below that ceiling, because guidance does not model the data distribution; it pushes samples toward what is typical of each class in the classifier's feature space, which is exactly what FD measures here. At equal training compute the diffusion and flow models win on quality: DDPM without guidance, flow with it; their pixel-space denoiser loses nothing to a tokenizer. The token model wins on sampling cost by three orders of magnitude: 64 small calls and about MFLOP per image against 1,000 full-image calls and about 403 GFLOP for the DDPM ( times fewer FLOPs; times fewer than 50 flow steps). A worse tokenizer makes everything worse: over the Latent lab's 128-code VQ, the same transformer reaches FD (best with guidance), although its likelihood looks better ( bits per image against ): fewer bits to predict because the tokenizer kept less. Bits per image are comparable only for the same tokenizer.

What the published record says at scale. On ImageNet 256 the families have traded places for years: LlamaGen-3B reaches FID 2.18 with guidance s = 1.65 (9.38 without), VAR 1.92, MAR 1.55, DiT-XL/2 2.27, each reported by its own authors with its own tokenizer or VAE ({{LlamaGen|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06525}}, {{VAR|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02905}}, {{MAR|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11838}}) vendor. For text-to-image in a unified model, Transfusion's controlled comparison (section 6) found continuous latents with a diffusion loss far more compute-efficient than discrete tokens. The tiny result here points the same way for the same reason: a discrete tokenizer throws information away before the generator starts.

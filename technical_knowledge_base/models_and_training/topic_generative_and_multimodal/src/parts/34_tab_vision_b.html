3. Contrastive image-text training: CLIP’s softmax and SigLIP’s sigmoid

The problem. We want an image vector and a text vector that can be compared directly, so that “a photo of a cat” lands near cat photos. There are hundreds of millions of image-caption pairs on the web but no labels saying how similar any two are. Contrastive training uses the pairing itself as the label: in a batch of N pairs, each image’s own caption is the positive and the other N − 1 captions are negatives.

How it works. Two towers (a ViT for images, a transformer for text) each end in a vector; both are scaled to length 1, so their dot product is the cosine similarity (1 for the same direction, 0 for unrelated). Multiply every cosine by a learned scale t (the inverse of the temperature) to get logits, then:

CLIP: ℓ = ½ [ CErows(t · S) + CEcolumns(t · S) ]
SigLIP: ℓ = −(1/N) Σi,j log σ( zij (t · Sij + b) )
S
the N × N matrix of cosines, images in rows, captions in columns; the diagonal holds the true pairs
CE
cross-entropy of a softmax with the diagonal as the right answer: each image must pick its caption out of the row, each caption its image out of the column (this is the InfoNCE loss)
t, b
learned scale and (SigLIP only) bias; SigLIP learns t′ = log t and initialises t′ and b “to log 10 and −10 respectively”, so training starts from the prior that almost every pair is a mismatch ({{Zhai et al. 2023, §3.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15343}})
zij
+1 on the diagonal, −1 elsewhere: every cell is a separate yes-or-no question
σ
the logistic sigmoid 1 / (1 + e−x)

In transformers 5.20.1 these are a few lines: CLIP’s contrastive_loss is a cross-entropy against torch.arange(len(logits)) (models/clip/modeling_clip.py:47), applied to the logits and their transpose and averaged; SigLIP’s logits are logits_per_text * logit_scale.exp() + logit_bias (models/siglip/modeling_siglip.py:805) and the loss is logsigmoid(m1_diag1 * logits_per_text) summed over each row and averaged (:814), with m1_diag1 the ±1 label matrix. CLIP’s scale started at 1 / 0.07 and was clipped at 100 (CLIP paper §2.5, quoted on the {{CLIP page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8194a85dfc5f3bf3f799}}); the released B/32 sits exactly at that clip, t = 100.0, and SigLIP 2 learned t = 112.67 with b = −16.77 read from the weights in float32.

The loss on a real batch: temperature and batch size

Take the first N of those pairs as a batch and compute both losses from the real cosines. Move the scale and the batch size.

Predict first: as the scale t goes to 0, what does the softmax loss tend to? And as t grows very large?
At t = 0 every logit is 0, every softmax is uniform, and the loss is exactly ln N whatever the embeddings: the chance level. As t grows the softmax approaches an argmax: rows the model gets right cost almost nothing, but a row where a negative beats the positive costs about t times the gap, so the loss is dominated by the hardest negatives. A learned temperature settles between: sharp enough to separate, soft enough to keep gradients from all negatives. On the first 8 pairs (which include the typographic image s04), the CLIP softmax loss falls from ln 8 = 2.079 at t = 1 to its minimum, 0.071, near t = 100 (the learned value) and rises again to 0.121 at t = 316, because the one wrong row (s04) is punished more and more. On SigLIP 2’s embeddings, where all 8 rows are right, the softmax loss just keeps falling as t grows measured.

The curve: this batch’s loss at every scale (log axis), the dot at the current one. Bars: each pair’s share of the loss (for the softmax, the mean of its row and column cross-entropies; for the sigmoid, the sum over its row).

Why a big batch matters for the softmax. The loss only learns from the negatives that are in the batch, and the chance level ln N grows with the batch: a batch of 16 asks the model to pick 1 out of 16, CLIP’s batch of 32,768 asked it to pick 1 out of 32,768 (CLIP page). Hard negatives (two cats, two similar captions) only show up in large batches. That is why contrastive models were trained with huge batches, and why the cost of the softmax’s batch-wide sums matters.

Why the sigmoid scales. The softmax normalises each row and each column over the whole batch, so with the batch split over D devices every device needs every other device’s embeddings (an all-gather) and holds at least its rows of the full matrix. The sigmoid scores each cell alone, so SigLIP’s “chunked” implementation keeps each device’s images in place and passes text embeddings round the devices in D steps; “the memory cost at any given moment is reduced from |B|2 to b2”, with b = |B| / D, and “D collective permutes is typically faster than two all-gathers” (SigLIP §3.3). What the paper measured published: with locked image features (SigLiT), “when the batch size is smaller than 16 k, sigmoid loss outperforms softmax loss by a large margin”; they trained up to a batch of one million, but “performance for both sigmoid and softmax saturate at around 32 k batch size”, and for SigLIP proper “a very large batch size hurts both losses” (SigLIP §4.1 and Figure 2). With only four TPUv4 chips a SigLiT model reached 84.5% ImageNet zero-shot in two days (abstract). So the sigmoid’s advantage is mostly cost and small-batch quality, not a higher ceiling. Not run here: training either loss at scale; the calculator only evaluates both on released embeddings, and the CLIP embeddings were trained with the softmax, the SigLIP 2 ones with the sigmoid, so a loss computed with the other model’s rule says nothing about how that model would have trained.

In one screen
  • A vision-language model (VLM) reads images and text and writes text. Most open ones are three parts: a vision encoder (a ViT, see {{Vision encoders|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c99f60ceb2f02cf5ea}}) turns image patches into vectors, a small projector maps those vectors to the language model’s width, and they are pasted into the prompt as image tokens where a placeholder stood.
  • An image costs tokens like text does. SmolVLM-500M spends 1,088 on any square image (17 views of 64), whatever its original size; LLaVA-1.5 spends 576. Tiling buys detail with tokens, and tokens cost time and context.
  • Training is usually two stages: first align the projector with everything else frozen, then instruction-tune projector and language model on conversations about images.
  • The failures are measurable. On 30 questions with known answers and 72 yes/no object questions, this small model answers 26 of 30 at the default budget (24 at 64 tokens) and says yes to 2 of 47 absent objects, but miscounts, misreads the clock and adds wrongly; its long descriptions contain wrong claims for 12 of the 16 images. The vision encoder, not the language model, takes most of the time.

1. Where this sits

SmolVLM is the most common open design: an encoder, a connector that projects (and here first merges) its vectors to the language model’s width, and image tokens placed in the prompt. The alternatives (Flamingo-style cross-attention with a Perceiver Resampler and tanh-gated layers, and native early fusion) and when each is chosen are compared on '+VLM+', section 1; native models on {{Native multimodal and any-to-any models|n:3c65c17b0d0d8139bd6cd4137dd2a4d8}}.

2. One image through the model, step by step

The animation follows the cat photo through SmolVLM-500M: the colours in steps 4 and 6 are the real vectors the model computed, shown by their first three principal components (a way to squeeze 768 or 960 numbers into a red, green and blue value). The toggle runs the same image through LLaVA-1.5 as its configuration describes it (not run here).

Checked, not assumed measured: the shapes along the way are pixel values [1, 17, 3, 512, 512] → encoder [17, 1024, 768] → pixel shuffle [17, 64, 12288] → projector [17, 64, 960] → the language model’s input [1, 1142, 960]. The merged input at the 1,088 image positions equals the projected vectors exactly, in order; and output token 1 of the global view holds exactly the 16 encoder vectors of rows 0–3, columns 4–7 (compared as sets of numbers). The first three principal components shown explain 21%, 14% and 14% of the variance of the encoder vectors and 24%, 18% and 13% of the projected ones: the colours are a rough view of high-dimensional vectors, not the whole of them. Model load 4.0 s; peak GPU memory for this step 2,329 MiB.

Pixel shuffle, in plain words. The encoder gives one vector per 16-pixel patch, 1,024 per tile. Feeding all of them would cost 1,024 tokens per tile. Pixel shuffle (also called space-to-depth) takes each 4 × 4 block of neighbouring vectors and lays them side by side into one long vector: 16 × 768 = 12,288 numbers. The token count falls 16-fold to 64 while every number is kept; the projector then has to learn to read 16 positions out of one vector. The SmolVLM paper reports that “smaller VLMs benefit from more aggressive compression (r=4)” ({{Marafioti et al., April 2025, section 2.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05299}}) published. The code:

# transformers v5.20.1, models/idefics3/modeling_idefics3.py lines 398-407
def pixel_shuffle(self, x, scale_factor=2):
    bsz, seq, embed_dim = x.size()
    height = width = int(seq**0.5)
    x = x.view(bsz, height, width, embed_dim)
    x = x.view(bsz, height, int(width / scale_factor), embed_dim * scale_factor)
    x = x.permute(0, 2, 1, 3)
    x = x.reshape(bsz, int(width / scale_factor), int(height / scale_factor), embed_dim * (scale_factor**2))
    x = x.permute(0, 2, 1, 3)
    x = x.reshape(bsz, int(seq / (scale_factor**2)), embed_dim * (scale_factor**2))
    return x

Other merges do the same job: Qwen2-VL merges 2 × 2 neighbours with an MLP; Gemma 4 pools 3 × 3 (both as the {{Model map|#t-map}} reads their configs). Flamingo’s resampler instead lets 64 learned queries attend to all the features.

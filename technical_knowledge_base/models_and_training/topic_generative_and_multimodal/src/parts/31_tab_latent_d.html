6. Beta: the blur trade-off

The problem. The KL term is a price per bit the code carries about the image. Raise its weight β and the encoder sends less: reconstructions blur, dimensions switch off, but the codes hug N(0, I) more tightly, so random codes decode to more typical images. Lower it and you approach the plain autoencoder. Five 16-number VAEs, identical except for β, trained for this page:

At β 10 only of 16 dimensions stay active; the rest are switched off because each bit costs too much. Random-code samples are best in the middle (FD at 0.3 and at 1) and worse at both ends: at 0.1 the codes drift from N(0, I) (FD ), at 10 the samples are confident but blurry and less varied (FD , class entropy nats against at 1). Higgins et al.'s β-VAE uses β > 1 on purpose, to trade reconstruction for disentangled factors ({{beta-VAE, ICLR 2017|https://openreview.net/forum?id=Sy2fzU9gl}}). measured

Why VAEs blur at all. Even at small β, a VAE trained with a per-pixel loss (here Bernoulli, often squared error) is rewarded for predicting the average of the images consistent with its code; an average of slightly different edges is a soft edge. The fixes are not in β: they are a loss that does not reward averaging (perceptual and adversarial losses, section 8) or a stronger generator on top of the latent (diffusion).

7. VQ-VAE: an image as tokens

How it works. Keep the latent as a grid (here 8 × 8 positions of 8 numbers), and replace each position's vector by the nearest of K learned vectors, the codebook (here K = 128). The image becomes 64 integers, image tokens, which a transformer can model exactly like words. The VQ-VAE ({{van den Oord et al. 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.00937}}) started the family of image tokenizers behind autoregressive image generators such as Parti and Chameleon. Nearest-neighbour lookup has no gradient, so the decoder's gradient is copied straight to the encoder (the straight-through estimator), and two extra terms train the codebook and keep the encoder near it:

loss = reconstruction + ‖sg[ze] − e‖2 + β ‖ze − sg[e]‖2
ze, e
the encoder's vector at one position and its nearest codebook vector
sg
stop-gradient: the first term moves only the codebook (the codebook loss), the second only the encoder (the commitment loss, β = 0.25 in the paper and here)
def quantize(self, ze): # src/latent/vq.py B, D, H, W = ze.shape flat = ze.permute(0, 2, 3, 1).reshape(-1, D) E = self.codebook.weight d = flat.pow(2).sum(1, keepdim=True) - 2 * flat @ E.t() + E.pow(2).sum(1)[None] idx = d.argmin(1) zq = E[idx].view(B, H, W, D).permute(0, 3, 1, 2) return zq, idx.view(B, H, W) def forward(self, x): ze = self.ae.encode(x) zq, idx = self.quantize(ze) z_st = ze + (zq - ze).detach() # straight-through estimator return self.ae.decode(z_st), ze, zq, idx

Read the library version critically: diffusers' VectorQuantizer keeps a known bug for backward compatibility, "due to a bug the beta term was applied to the wrong term" ({{models/autoencoders/vae.py lines 574 to 576 and 651 to 654, v0.41.0|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/models/autoencoders/vae.py#L574}}): with the default legacy=True, β multiplies the codebook term and the commitment term gets weight 1.

Codebook collapse. Codes that no encoder output is nearest to get no codebook-loss gradient, so they never move and are never used again: the effective vocabulary shrinks. The usual fixes: initialise the codebook from encoder outputs, update it by moving averages, use low-dimensional or normalised codes, or restart dead codes. Two VQ-VAEs trained here, the same in everything except that one replaces codes unused for 100 steps by random encoder outputs from the current batch (a restart, as in {{Jukebox, section 3.1, "Random restarts for embeddings"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.00341}}):

What the two runs show. Both start with all codes in use, and in both usage crashes within the first few hundred steps, as the encoder's outputs grow far beyond the tiny initial codebook (initialised uniformly in ±1/K, diffusers' default) and only the few codes nearest to them get pulled along: of 128 codes used in the window ending at step 200 in the plain run. With restarts the dead codes are re-seeded from encoder outputs and the codebook is full again by step ; without them recovery is slow, and after 5,000 steps of 128 codes are used on the test set (perplexity , against and with restarts). Reconstruction ended the same ( against dB). That matches Jukebox's own observation: "Models trained without random restarts can converge to the same test error and codebook usage but require more training steps" published. On a small codebook and an easy dataset the collapse heals; on large codebooks (thousands of codes) dead codes are the norm without a fix. measured

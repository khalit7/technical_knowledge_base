1. The forward process: destroying an image on purpose

The problem. We want to draw new images from the distribution the training images came from, but nobody can write that distribution down. Going the other way is easy: take a real image and add noise until nothing is left. Diffusion learns to reverse that destruction one small step at a time; then it can start from pure noise, which anyone can sample, and walk back to an image.

How it works. At each of T = 1,000 steps the forward process shrinks the image a little and adds a little Gaussian noise: step t adds noise of variance βt, the noise schedule. Because Gaussians add up to a Gaussian, you never have to simulate the steps one by one: the noisy image at any step has a closed form.

xt = √āt · x0 + √(1 − āt) · ε,   ε ~ N(0, I)
x0
the clean image, pixels scaled to [−1, 1]
αt
1 − βt, the fraction of variance a step keeps
āt
α1 α2 … αt ("alpha bar"): how much of the original signal's variance survives to step t; it falls from about 1 to about 0
ε
one draw of standard Gaussian noise, the same size as the image

This one line is all of training's data pipeline: pick an image, a random t and random noise, and the noisy input is ready (diffusers does exactly this in {{scheduling_ddpm.py line 608|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/schedulers/scheduling_ddpm.py#L608}}, v0.41.0). The derivation of the closed form is on the {{math page, section 12|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}.

2. Noise schedules and the signal-to-noise ratio

The problem. Schedules are written as lists of β, which are hard to compare. What matters to the model is how much signal is left against how much noise at each step: the signal-to-noise ratio, SNR(t) = āt / (1 − āt). On a log scale it falls from large (almost clean) to tiny (almost pure noise), and two schedules are best compared by where along t they spend their steps.

log10 SNR against timestep, three schedules

  • Linear (DDPM, β from 10−4 to 0.02): SNR drops below 1 at t = , so about of the training timesteps are noise-dominated derived. On small images that tail carries little information; {{Nichol and Dhariwal (2021)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09672}} proposed the cosine schedule so that āt falls slowly at both ends (SNR = 1 at t = ).
  • Scaled-linear (Stable Diffusion 1.5, β from 0.00085 to 0.012, linear in √β): at the last step √ā is still , so of the latent's amplitude survives the "full" noising. Training never shows the model pure noise, but sampling starts from it: {{Lin et al. (2023)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08891}} traced washed-out images (the model cannot make very dark or very bright pictures) to this and proposed rescaling to zero terminal SNR. diffusers cites that paper beside its timestep-spacing options (DDIM {{line 357|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/schedulers/scheduling_ddim.py#L357}}), which matter in section 7.
  • Resolution shifts the schedule. The same SNR destroys a large image less than a small one, because neighbouring pixels average the noise away; {{Hoogeboom, Heek and Salimans (2023)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11093}} shift the schedule with image size, and Stable Diffusion 3 shifts its timesteps for the same reason ({{Esser et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03206}}). A schedule tuned at 32 × 32 is not automatically right at 1024 × 1024.

The tiny model here uses the linear schedule, DDPM's own, so that the lab reproduces the classic setup; the shared code also has the cosine one (objectives.betas_cosine).

3. The denoiser, and what it is trained to predict

The network. The denoiser takes the noisy image xt, the timestep t and (here) the class label, and returns a tensor of the image's shape. Ours is the shared TinyUNet: parameters, a U-Net (a convolutional network that shrinks the image from 32 to 16 to 8 pixels and grows it back, with skip connections between matching sizes and self-attention at 8 × 8). The timestep enters as a sinusoidal embedding and the label as a learned embedding with an 11th "no label" row, both added inside every residual block.

Three things it can be asked for. Given xt and t, the clean image and the noise determine each other, so a network can be trained to output any of these, and the sampler converts:

Look at the ε row at t = 999: √ā is , so any error in ε̂ is multiplied by on its way to x̂0. The v row has no division. That difference is invisible at 50 steps and decisive at 1 step (section 5). Why predicting noise is the right loss for DDPM, from the ELBO, is on the {{DDPM paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d811481e4e36333454f7a}} and the {{math page, sections 13 and 15|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}.

The training step, as it ran (src/diff/train_ddpm.py); the label is replaced by the null class 10% of the time so that one network also learns the unconditional model guidance needs (section 6):

def loss_fn(model, b):
    x0, y = b
    t = torch.randint(0, sched.T, (x0.shape[0],), device=dev)
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    pred = model(sched.q_sample(x0, t, eps), t.float() / sched.T, drop_labels(y, 0.1))
    loss = F.mse_loss(pred, target(x0, eps, t))     # target: eps, or v = sqrt(abar) eps - sqrt(1 - abar) x0
    return loss, {"loss": loss.item()}

The runs. Two models with one recipe and one change, the target: batch 128, AdamW at 5 × 10−4 after 200 warm-up steps, gradient clipping at 1, an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights with decay 0.999 used for all sampling, steps each ( minutes at steps per second, peak MPS driver memory , which counts cached blocks and the 2,048-image evaluation at the end). Checkpoints are kept for the {{Flow lab|#t-flow}}, which trains the same network with the same recipe and step count.

Where the error lives: validation error by timestep (EMA weights, 2,048 validation images)

What the chart shows. The noise is hardest to guess when there is almost none (small t: is that speck noise or a stitch?), and the clean image is hardest to guess when there is almost no image left (large t). The ε loss weights every timestep's noise error equally, which is the same as weighting the clean-image error by the SNR, so it spends its effort on nearly clean images; the v loss sits between the two.

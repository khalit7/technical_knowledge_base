A flow-matching model generates by moving points: it learns a velocity for every point and every moment, and an image is made by starting at random noise and following those velocities until you arrive at data. This lab builds the idea from zero on a 2-D toy you can run in the page, then trains the same tiny U-Net as the {{Diffusion lab|#t-diff}} with the flow-matching objective instead, with the same recipe and the same number of training steps, and compares the two at equal sampling steps. 1. Generating as transport

The problem. As in diffusion, we can sample noise easily and want samples of data. Diffusion gets there by learning to undo a noising process step by step. Flow matching asks a more direct question: if every noise point had to travel to some data point over one unit of time, what velocity field (an arrow at every position x and time t saying which way and how fast to move) would carry the whole cloud of noise onto the cloud of data?

How it works. Write t = 0 for noise and t = 1 for data (the convention of Lipman et al. and of this topic's shared code). A sample is made by solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE): start at x(0) drawn from a standard Gaussian and move with dx/dt = vθ(x, t), where vθ is the network. Nothing is random after the start: the same noise always gives the same output, like DDIM. Such a model is a continuous normalizing flow; what was new in 2022 is the cheap way to train it (section 2).

2. The objective: regress the straight-line velocity

The problem. The velocity field that carries noise onto data is unknown; we only have samples of both ends. Training a flow by simulating the ODE and comparing the outputs (the older way to train continuous normalizing flows) needs an ODE solve per training step. Flow matching avoids any simulation.

How it works. Draw a noise x0, a training image x1 and a time t uniform in [0, 1]. Put a point on the straight line between them and ask the network for the velocity of that line, which is the same at every t:

xt = (1 − t) x0 + t x1,   loss = ‖ vθ(xt, t) − (x1 − x0) ‖²
x0
Gaussian noise, the same shape as an image
x1
a training image (scaled to [−1, 1])
x1 − x0
the target: the constant velocity of the straight path from that noise to that image

That is the whole training loop: one network call and one mean squared error, exactly as cheap as a DDPM step. This is conditional flow matching (CFM) with straight paths, called rectified flow by Liu et al. and the conditional optimal-transport path by Lipman et al. (both 2022, published). The same path in Meta's reference library (facebookresearch/flow_matching at commit 11568d3): CondOTScheduler sets αt = t, σt = 1 − t (flow_matching/path/scheduler/scheduler.py lines 108 to 113) and the affine path builds x_t = sigma_t * x_0 + alpha_t * x_1 and dx_t = d_sigma_t * x_0 + d_alpha_t * x_1 (flow_matching/path/affine.py lines 89 to 90), which is x1 − x0. In torchcfm (commit 7c65385) the same two lines are t * x1 + (1 - t) * x0 and x1 - x0 (torchcfm/conditional_flow_matching.py lines 83 and 154).


Why it works, and why the paths still bend. Many straight lines pass through the same point x at time t, from different noises to different images, and they disagree on the direction. A network trained with a squared error learns their average: the marginal velocity, which provably moves the whole noise distribution onto the data distribution (Lipman et al.'s theorem). But an average of crossing lines is a curved flow: each sample's path bends as it goes, because the set of lines it is "averaging over" changes with t. At t = 0 every line through a point is equally likely to go to any image, so the velocity points at the mean of the data: the middle of the ring, or, for images, a grey blur.


The pairs a model is trained on


60 noise points (blue) joined to the data points (red) they are paired with in training. Random pairs: what plain flow matching uses. OT pairs: the same samples re-paired inside the batch to minimise total squared distance (Tong et al. 2023's minibatch optimal transport). Reflow pairs: each noise joined to where the trained flow takes it.







Crossing lines are what bend the learned paths. The two fixes in this lab both remove crossings: pick better pairs while training (OT), or retrain on the pairs the flow itself produces, which never cross because an ODE's paths cannot cross (reflow, section 5).


Predict. A flow-matching model trained on Fashion-MNIST is sampled with a single Euler step: x1 = x0 + vθ(x0, 0). What comes out?



A blur. At t = 0 the best squared-error answer is the average direction to every image of that class, so one step lands on (roughly) the class mean.

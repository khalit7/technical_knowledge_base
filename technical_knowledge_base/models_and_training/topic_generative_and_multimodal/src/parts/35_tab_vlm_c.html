5. Parameters: what each stage would train

Training a VLM (align the connector with the rest frozen, then train more of the model on conversations about images, as in LLaVA’s 595K-pair alignment and LLaVA-1.5’s “~1 day on a single 8-A100 node”) is taught on {{Vision-language models|n:3fc5c17b0d0d81adbb7be5bc41c4d9d0}}, section 6, with six recipes drawn stage by stage. What this lab adds is the parts list of the model that ran.

Not run here, and why. Even the alignment stage of a 500M model needs hundreds of thousands of captioned images and hours of GPU time to show anything; a toy on 16 images would teach memorisation, not alignment. The mechanism is visible without training: the projector is one 960 × 12,288 matrix (11.8M parameters, 2.3% of the model) measured, and stage 1 trains only it.

Parameters by component, counted on the loaded model measured. The SmolVLM paper describes the 500M model as “the same 93M SigLIP-B/16 paired with the larger SmolLM2-360M” (section 4) published; the loaded encoder has 86,433,024. The gap is exact arithmetic derived: a SigLIP base vision tower with patch 16 (counted on the topic’s SigLIP 2 base, same architecture) has 92,884,224 parameters, of which 7,087,104 are the attention-pooling head a VLM does not use (it keeps all patch vectors) and 150,528 the position table for 14 × 14 patches; swapping that table for one with 32 × 32 = 1,024 positions (512-pixel input) gives 92,884,224 − 7,087,104 − 150,528 + 786,432 = 86,433,024.

6. What it gets right and wrong

The question set. 30 questions about the 16 test images with answers known by construction (synthetic images) or checked by eye (photos and generated images): objects, colours, counting, left and right, reading text (OCR, optical character recognition), reading a bar chart, telling the time, and two traps (the cat with a DOG sticker, and the astronaut image whose prompt asked for a horse that Stable Diffusion did not draw). Each was asked at three image-token budgets: one 512-pixel view (64 tokens), 2 × 2 tiles plus the global view (320), and the default 4 × 4 plus global (1,088). Long descriptions drift

Short yes/no answers are one kind of test; a free description is another. What the descriptions show measured. 22 wrong claims in 12 of the 16 descriptions, and every description ran to the 120-token limit (median 3.7 s). The errors sit next to short answers that were right: it read LATENT 42 but describes a blue button with white text; it answered B for the tallest bar but makes A the longest at 7; the clock gains the numerals 1 to 12; the stop sign becomes rectangular. The description of the DOG-sticker image never mentions the sticker. A long answer gives the language model many tokens to fill from its prior once the image evidence is used up; CHAIR (Rohrbach et al., 2018) scores this automatically on captioning sets with object labels, which these images lack, so the marks here are by eye.

7. Cost per image: time and memory

Where the time goes. Answering a question about an image has three parts: the vision encoder and projector run once per view; prefill runs the language model over the whole prompt once (image tokens included) and fills its key-value cache; decode then produces one token at a time, each reading that cache. More image tokens make the first two dearer and each decoded token a little dearer (a longer cache to read).

What the timing shows measured (median of three warm runs, on the cat photo). With the default 17 views, the encoder and projector take 1.61 s and the language model’s prefill of 1,142 tokens 0.44 s; each decoded token then takes 14 ms. With one view (82 prompt tokens) the same steps take 0.11 s and 0.07 s, and 13 ms per token. For short answers the vision encoder is the cost: it runs 17 tiles of 1,024 patches through 12 layers, about 17,400 patch positions against 1,142 language-model positions. Decoding barely changes with the prompt length at this size. Peak GPU memory 2,338 to 2,444 MiB (2.45 to 2.56 GB; the MPS driver’s allocation, sampled every 0.1 s), against the SmolVLM paper’s “1.2GB for the 500M” (section 4.3) published; the paper does not say how it measured, and the MPS driver figure counts everything allocated, so the two are not directly comparable.

What transfers. The token counts, the answers (greedy decoding, bfloat16) and the shape of the trade-off transfer to any machine; the seconds do not. A datacenter GPU runs the encoder and prefill many times faster and batches many images; MPS on an M1 Pro is a laptop GPU sharing memory with the CPU, and these runs were at low priority on a machine shared with other jobs.

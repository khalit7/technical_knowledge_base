Too few steps for curved paths: the first steps follow the average direction (section 2). Check FD and diversity against steps; if quality appears only at 10+ steps and you need fewer, reflow or distil (section 5) rather than tuning the solver.

Comparing solvers at equal steps, or evaluating the net at the data end

Heun and midpoint cost two evaluations per step: compare at equal NFE (section 3). And a solver that evaluates the network at exactly t = 1 (plain Heun's last corrector) uses a slope the model cannot know; on this lab's images that made Heun worse than Euler. End with an Euler step, or use a solver that never touches the endpoint (midpoint).

Reading the training loss as quality

The flow-matching loss has a floor set by the randomness of the target (about 1 near t = 1, where the noise must be guessed from a clean image) and says little about samples. Score samples (FD at a fixed sample size, class accuracy, diversity) at several step counts.

Mixing the t = 0 and t = 1 conventions, or the sign of v

Lipman et al. and this lab: t = 0 is noise, v = data − noise. diffusers: σ = 1 is noise, output = noise − data, σ × 1000 into the net. Porting a checkpoint between the two needs both the time flip and the sign flip; either one alone produces the garbage above.

Expecting reflow to be free

The pairs inherit the first model's mistakes, so full-solve quality can drop slightly (Liu et al.'s CIFAR-10 RK45 FID 2.58 to 3.36; the toy at 64 steps). Measure both ends: few-step and many-step.

Copying a guidance scale or a shift between models

Both depend on the model, resolution and step count: SD3's shift 3.0 is for 1024 × 1024; on this 28 × 28 model the best shift and guidance are different (sections 6 and 7). Sweep them on a few hundred samples.

10. Interview questions

What does a flow-matching model predict, and what is its loss?

A velocity vθ(xt, t). Training: xt = (1 − t) noise + t data, loss ‖vθ − (data − noise)‖², t uniform (or logit-normal). Sampling: integrate dx/dt = v from noise to data.

Why is it called simulation-free, and why does that matter?

Training never solves the ODE: each step builds xt in closed form from a random pair and does one regression. Older continuous normalizing flows had to simulate the ODE (and backpropagate through it) every step, which made them slow to train.

Each training path is a straight line. Why are the sampled paths curved?

The network learns the average of all straight lines passing through each (x, t); pairs cross, so the average direction changes along a path. On the toy, random-pair paths have straightness (1 = straight), reflowed ones .

Are flow matching and diffusion different models?

Same family: Gaussian paths between data and noise, a network that can be read as predicting noise, data or velocity, and a deterministic ODE for sampling (DDIM solves the diffusion model's). They differ in the schedule, the output parameterisation (hence loss weighting) and the t distribution, which change which errors are cheap and how many steps are needed.

Why did the flow model need fewer steps than the DDPM in this lab?

Three things: the DDPM path is an arc by construction (straightness about 0.90 even with a perfect model; measured against for flow); the linear schedule is nearly pure noise for most of its range, so evenly spaced steps waste most of their budget; and x̂0 from an ε prediction divides by a tiny √ā at the noisy end, while a velocity stays bounded. Straightness alone does not decide it: on the 2-D toy the flow was less straight than DDIM and still needed fewer steps.

What is reflow and what does it cost?

Generate (noise, sample) pairs with the trained flow, retrain on them: the pairs do not cross, so the new paths are nearly straight and 1 to 2 Euler steps work. Cost: a generation pass over many pairs and a second training; the pairs carry the first model's errors, so many-step quality can drop slightly. Usually followed by distillation for one step.

Design: your product needs a 4-step image generator; you have a 50-step flow model.

Options, cheapest first: tune the solver and shift at 4 steps (minutes; limited gain); a second-order or multistep solver at equal NFE; reflow then 4-step Euler (one pair pass plus a fine-tune); distillation into a few-step student (progressive, consistency or adversarial, as FLUX.1 [schnell]'s latent adversarial diffusion distillation); drop CFG at inference by distilling it in (halves evaluations). Measure FD or a preference score at 4 steps against the 50-step teacher, plus time per image including the VAE decode.

Euler or Heun?

Compare at equal network evaluations. Second-order solvers' error falls with h², so they usually win from moderate budgets up, but how the solver treats the data end matters as much: on this lab's images midpoint won at every budget, while plain Heun lost to Euler until its last step was made Euler (it evaluates the net at t = 1, where the learned field is just the input). With guidance each evaluation is already two forward passes, so a second-order step with CFG costs four.

A flow model that trained fine samples garbage. What do you check?

Time direction (which end is noise), the scale of t passed to the network ([0, 1] or ×1000), the sign of the velocity, the shift and step grid, the null label for guidance, and dtype. Then one known-good test: the 1-step output should be a recognisable blur of the class; if it is not, the convention is wrong, not the model.

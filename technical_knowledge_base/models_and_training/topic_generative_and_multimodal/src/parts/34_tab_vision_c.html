4. Zero-shot classification: a classifier written in sentences

How it works. Write one sentence per class (“a photo of a sneaker.”), embed the ten sentences once with the text tower, embed the image, and pick the class whose sentence has the highest cosine. No training on the task: the ten text vectors are the classifier’s weights. In code, from src/vision/zeroshot.py (embeddings unit length):

s = et @ T[k].T          # [10,000 test images] x [10 class sentences] cosines
pred = s.argmax(1)       # nearest sentence wins
acc = (pred == yt).mean()

The test. Fashion-MNIST, the topic’s running example: 10,000 grey 28 × 28 test images of clothing, 1,000 per class, upscaled by each processor to 224. These look nothing like web photos, which makes them a fair test of transfer. Two class wordings (the dataset’s official names, such as “T-shirt/top”, and plain ones, such as “t-shirt”) times five templates, plus the average of the five template embeddings (prompt ensembling, which the CLIP paper used), all measured.

40 test images: the first four of each class, in class order (T-shirt/top, trouser, pullover, dress, coat, sandal, shirt, sneaker, bag, ankle boot). Cosines recorded from the real models; the page only applies the softmax.

Every prompt set on all 10,000 test images

What the table shows measured. Chance is 10%. CLIP B/32 lands between 61.3% and 66.7% whatever the wording: the template moves it by about 5 points, and the ensemble (64.8%) is not its best. Its tokenizer lowercases everything, so “official” and “official, lowercased” are identical. SigLIP 2 B/16 reaches 74.5% with plain names and the ensemble, the best of any set, but scores 20.0% to 22.8% with the official names as written: the capital letters in “T-shirt/top”, “Trouser”, “Pullover” (tokenised as Trou, ser and Pull, over instead of tr, ouser and pullover) send it far from the lowercase text it was trained on. Lowercasing the same names brings it back to 66.9% to 73.1%. A cross-check with one template (“a photo of a ….”) isolates the cause: SigLIP 2 scores 22.3% with official names, 70.2% lowercased, 72.9% with plain names and 22.4% with the plain names capitalised; CLIP scores 63.3%, 63.3%, 61.3% and 61.3% (diag_case.py). A silent preprocessing difference cost 50 points.

The errors are the ones a person would make, and they show what the text side cannot separate. CLIP’s best set sends 564 of 1,000 coats and 438 of 1,000 shirts to “pullover” and gets only 6.6% of shirts right; SigLIP 2’s best set gets 66% of shirts but sends 453 coats to “pullover” and 392 T-shirts to “shirt”. At 28 pixels, grey, a shirt, a pullover and a coat differ by collar and buttons, and their names overlap in web captions. With the capitalised official names SigLIP 2 calls 5,725 of the 10,000 images “T-shirt/top”: one class name absorbs everything, the signature of a text embedding gone wrong rather than an image embedding.

The embedding knows more than the prompts get out

Predict first: a linear classifier trained on the frozen image embeddings with only 16 labelled images per class (160 in all). Better or worse than the best zero-shot prompt set?
Better, for both. With 16 labels per class a linear probe reaches 79.0% on CLIP features (best prompt set 66.7%) and 84.0% on SigLIP 2 features (74.5%). Even one label per class (10 images) roughly matches zero-shot on average (58.8% and 62.2%) but swings by 15 to 16 points between draws of which ten images are labelled.

Linear probe: multinomial logistic regression on the frozen, centred embeddings, trained on k labelled validation images per class (5 random draws for k ≤ 64; the black line spans the worst and best draw), tested on the same 10,000 test images. Reference: the topic’s small CNN classifier, 550,890 parameters, trained from scratch for two minutes on all 55,000 training images (root’s smoke test). measured

Reading it measured. With 500 labels per class the probe reaches 87.8% (CLIP) and 89.3% (SigLIP 2): the class information is in the frozen image embedding, and ten sentences extract only part of it. A 550,890-parameter CNN trained from scratch on 55,000 labelled images for two minutes still wins (92.98%): with enough in-domain labels, a small specialised model beats a general encoder, and the encoder’s value is in needing few or no labels. A mistake made on the way, worth knowing: a first probe fed the unit-length embeddings to gradient descent without rescaling (every coordinate is around 1/√768) and underfit, reporting 79.8% and 84.6% at 500 per class; dividing by the features’ standard deviation fixed it. Settings (fixed before looking at test accuracy, not tuned): full-batch gradient descent, learning rate 0.5, L2 1e-3, 1,000 steps. Published comparison: none; the CLIP paper’s tables (transcribed on the CLIP page) include MNIST but not Fashion-MNIST, so these numbers stand on their own and are not a benchmark of either model.

5. What the embeddings capture, and what they miss

A contrastive embedding keeps whatever helps pick the right caption out of a batch of web captions, and nothing else. Web captions name objects and often repeat the text written in the picture; they rarely state counts, left and right, or which attribute goes with which object. So the embedding is strong on “what is it” and “what does it say”, and weak on structure. Researchers found CLIP-like models behave partly like bags of words on such questions ({{Yuksekgonul et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01936}}), and OpenAI called the first failure below a typographic attack, noting it needs “no more technology than pen and paper” ({{OpenAI, March 2021, archived|https://web.archive.org/web/2021/https://openai.com/blog/multimodal-neurons/}}).

The typographic attack: the same cat, before and after a sticker

Images: the scikit-image cat photo (CC0) with a white label drawn by src/vision/make_probes.py; the DOG version is the root’s test image s04. Four texts: a photo of a cat, dog, bird, car. measured

What it shows measured. CLIP B/32 flips from cat to the written word for DOG (cosine 0.2855 for dog against 0.2775 for cat) and for BIRD (0.2837 against 0.2659), whether the DOG sticker is small or large; it resists CAR, narrowly (cat 0.2890, car 0.2827). The CAT sticker raises the cat score (0.2926 against 0.2846 plain): the written word adds to the image’s meaning in the same direction as the object. SigLIP 2 never flips, but the sticker still moves it: “dog” rises from 0.0684 to 0.1112 with the DOG sticker while “cat” stays near 0.13. Both models read text; SigLIP 2’s image of the cat is strong enough to win. These numbers match the root’s float16 smoke test on the DOG image to within 0.0001 in cosine. The VLM lab asks SmolVLM the same question in words: it answered “There is a cat in this image.” (root smoke test).

Counting, left and right, binding

What it shows measured. Counting: CLIP gets 3 of 6 (one, three and six), calls two “three” and four and five “six”, with margins of 0.001 to 0.008 in cosine; SigLIP 2 gets 4 of 6 (one to three, and six), calling four “five” and five “six”, with larger margins on the small counts (0.02 to 0.03). Small counts of simple shapes are within reach of the newer model, larger ones are not; six probes are a hint, not an accuracy. Left and right: both models pick the same sentence for the scene and its mirror image, so each is right once by construction, a coin toss; the margins are a few thousandths. Binding: CLIP gets both swapped-colour scenes wrong; SigLIP 2 picks the same sentence for both. The sentences contain the same words in a different order, and the embeddings barely see the order. Larger sets of such minimal pairs ({{Winoground|https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03162}}, {{ARO|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.01936}}) found the same for many CLIP-like models.

How to check a model for these. Build minimal pairs: two images that differ in one property (a sticker, a mirror flip, swapped colours) or two captions that differ in one word, and see whether the score moves the right way. Count how often the right one wins and how large the margin is; a margin of a few thousandths of a cosine is a coin toss. Benchmarks built this way exist (Winoground, ARO, SugarCrepe), and VLM-level versions are on {{Long-context and multimodal benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d810bb4dfc98919c9be8a}}. The fix is usually not a better prompt: it is training data with hard negatives (captions with swapped words), a model that reads the image tokens with a language model ({{VLM lab|#t-vlm}}), or both.

6. Common mistakes and interview questions

Comparing raw cosines across models
CLIP’s matching pairs here sit around 0.3 and SigLIP 2’s around 0.1 to 0.2; neither number means “30% similar”. Only the order within one model is meaningful, and thresholds (for retrieval or filtering) must be calibrated per model.
Feeding SigLIP text the way CLIP is fed
Two traps. Case: SigLIP 2’s reference code lowercases text before tokenising, the transformers processor does not, and CLIP’s tokenizer lowercases on its own, so code written for CLIP silently passes capitals to SigLIP 2; here that turned 70.2% into 22.3% on Fashion-MNIST (section 4). Padding: SigLIP was trained on text padded to a fixed 64 tokens (padding="max_length", max_length=64 in the processor call); padding to the longest in the batch, as CLIP code does, changes its text embeddings. The root’s loaders and this lab pass it explicitly.
Forgetting what the processor does to the image
CLIP resizes the short side to 224 and centre-crops (edges of a wide image vanish); SigLIP 2’s fixed-resolution processor squashes to 224 × 224; normalisation constants differ (CLIP’s ImageNet-like mean and standard deviation, SigLIP’s 0.5 and 0.5). Using one model’s preprocessing with the other’s weights degrades results silently.
Reading zero-shot accuracy as what the features know
The prompt set alone moved accuracy by many points here, and a linear probe on 16 labels per class beat every prompt set. Report the prompts with any zero-shot number, and use a linear probe to judge the features.
Applying CLIP’s softmax to SigLIP outputs, or a sigmoid to CLIP
SigLIP’s logits come with a bias and are calibrated per pair (a sigmoid gives a yes-or-no probability); CLIP’s are calibrated only relative to the other candidates (softmax). A sigmoid of 100 × cosine is near 1 for every CLIP pair.
Trusting attention maps as explanations
The pooling heat in the patch animation shows where one layer’s weights go, averaged over heads; outputs mix information across all tokens in earlier layers, so a patch with low weight can still decide the answer.

Interview questions

How many tokens does a ViT-B/16 make from a 224 × 224 image, and what happens at 448?
(224/16)² = 196 (plus [CLS] if it has one). At 448: 784, so attention per layer costs 16 times as much; the learned position table must be interpolated to the new grid (transformers’ interpolate_pos_encoding) or the model fine-tuned at the new size.
Why does CLIP need large batches, and how does SigLIP change that?
The softmax’s negatives are the rest of the batch, and chance loss is ln N; hard negatives appear only in large batches. The sigmoid treats each pair as binary classification, needs no batch-wide normalisation, works better at small batches, and allows a chunked implementation holding b × b logits per device instead of the full matrix; both saturate around 32k in the SigLIP paper.
What does the temperature do, and why learn it?
It sets how peaked the softmax is. Too high a temperature (small scale): loss near ln N and weak gradients; too low: only the hardest negatives matter and training is unstable (CLIP clipped its scale at 100). Learning it lets the model sharpen as its embeddings separate.
Zero-shot accuracy is poor on your domain. What do you try, in order?
Check preprocessing; try class wordings and templates and an ensemble; measure a linear probe on a few labels per class (if it is much higher, the features are fine and you need labels or prompt tuning); if the probe is also poor, fine-tune or pick an encoder trained on closer data.
Your image search returns cat photos for the query “dog” when the photos contain the word DOG. Why, and what do you do?
Contrastive embeddings read text in images strongly (a typographic attack). Measure it with minimal pairs; mitigate with an OCR step that detects text, models trained with such hard negatives, or re-ranking with a VLM.
CNN or ViT for a small labelled dataset?
From scratch, a CNN’s inductive bias wins on small data; in practice you start from a pretrained encoder of either kind, and pretrained ViTs (CLIP, SigLIP, DINOv2) are the default backbones for VLMs and retrieval.

What transfers to a datacenter GPU

Everything except the timings: the cosines, accuracies and probe results are properties of the released weights and would match on any hardware up to float rounding (the root’s MPS float16 smoke test and this lab’s CPU float32 run agree on the typographic cosines to four decimals). Images per second here are CPU numbers on a laptop shared with other jobs; a GPU embeds these models hundreds of times faster, and the ratio between CLIP B/32 and SigLIP 2 B/16 there follows the token count and kernel quality, not this run.

1. Why generate in a latent, and the two kinds of latent

A diffusion model runs its network 25 to 50 times per image and an autoregressive model predicts one symbol at a time, so both pay for every number they work on, and most of a 512 × 512 image's 786,432 numbers are detail the eye barely registers (the LDM paper's rate-distortion argument, {{LDM paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81bb98adc0daf8462cb6}}). So: train an autoencoder once, generate latents, decode once at the end.

The cost, either way. Detail the decoder cannot draw (small text, distant faces, fine texture) cannot appear, however good the generator. Positions set the generator's sequence length or spatial size; channels or codebook size set what each position carries. The trade-off is rate (what the latent carries) against distortion (reconstruction error) against how easy the latent is to model, and reconstruction measurements (encode, decode, compare) and generation measurements (sample, decode, compare distributions) often disagree.

2. Why a plain autoencoder does not generate, and what the VAE changes

To generate, you pick a code and decode it, but a plain autoencoder never says which codes are valid: training only decodes real images' codes, a scattered cloud at whatever scale training chose. Three readings. N(0, I) fails for the autoencoder. A Gaussian fitted to its own codes beats the VAE: what makes a latent usable is knowing where the codes are, which can be learned after training (Ghosh et al., {{From Variational to Deterministic Autoencoders, 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.12436}}) published; the VAE's gain is that the density is N(0, I) by construction, with no second stage. And all are far from the floor: a decoder fed a simple Gaussian cannot model the data's many modes, which is why real systems put a diffusion or autoregressive model on top of the latent.

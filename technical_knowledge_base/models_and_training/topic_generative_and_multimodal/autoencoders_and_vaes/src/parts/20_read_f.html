9. FSQ and LFQ: quantizers with nothing to collapse

Codebook collapse happens because the codebook is learned. Remove it: fix the code grid and let the encoder learn to use it (the toy's FSQ and LFQ modes in section 8 show the two grids).

FSQ: finite scalar quantization

Squash each of a few channels (3 to 10) with tanh and round it to one of Li levels; the codebook is the product of the levels ([8, 5, 5, 5]: 1,000 codes from 4 channels). No codebook or commitment loss, straight-through rounding ({{Mentzer et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15505}}): usage "≈100% for most models, without relying on any auxiliary losses", Li ≥ 5 recommended published.

LFQ: lookup-free quantization

log2 K channels, each quantized to its sign; the token is the binary number the signs spell (18 channels: 262,144 codes, no lookup). An entropy penalty, E[H(q(z))] − H[E(q(z))], makes each token confident and all tokens used (MAGVIT-v2, {{Yu et al. 2023, eq. 3 to 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05737}}; weight 0.1, commitment 0.25) published.

FSQ's bounding function, as ported here (code)
def bound(self, z):                     # FSQ paper appendix A.1, def bound, ported to PyTorch (src/quant/quantizers.py)
    eps = 1e-3
    half_l = (self.L - 1) * (1 - eps) / 2
    offset = torch.where(self.L % 2 == 1, 0.0, 0.5)   # even L needs an asymmetric range
    shift = torch.tan(offset / half_l)
    return torch.tanh(z + shift) * half_l - offset

q = round_ste(self.bound(z))            # integers; round_ste(x) = x + (round(x) - x).detach()
zq = q / (self.L // 2)                  # what the decoder sees, about [-1, 1]

Reading it. FSQ [8, 5, 5, 5] used of 1,000 codes with no auxiliary loss, was best on every measure (rFD ) and trained fastest ( s against : no distances to K codes). At 125 codes FSQ used all of them and landed with the VQ variants. LFQ did worst: at 10 bits only of 1,024 codes were used, and its entropy penalty matters (7-bit perplexity without it, with it). Sign bits on 7 to 10 channels suit a tiny model poorly; MAGVIT-v2 uses 18 bits and a far larger encoder, so this does not test its result. measured

The cost, and when to choose them. Fewer numbers per token (3 to 18 channels against VQ's 8 to 256) on fixed levels. In return large vocabularies become usable: MAGVIT-v2 reports reconstruction and generation both improving as LFQ's vocabulary grows, "a property not observed in current VQ-VAE methods", and FSQ finds VQ "is actually worse for large codebooks" published. Choose them for thousands to hundreds of thousands of codes, or when VQ's collapse keeps returning; check usage, and generation (section 10).

10. VQGAN, and how many tokens an image costs

A pixel-loss VQ-VAE blurs (section 7). VQGAN ({{Esser, Rombach, Ommer 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.09841}}) kept the VQ-VAE and changed the loss; it is still the recipe behind nearly every image tokenizer.

loss = L1 or L2 pixel loss + LPIPS
+ codebook and commitment losses + λ · patch-GAN loss
patch GAN
a small convolutional discriminator scores every patch as real or reconstructed; the autoencoder is trained to fool it, so texture must look real, not merely average
λ
set each step from the ratio of the two losses' gradient norms at the decoder's last layer, so the GAN term never swamps reconstruction (calculate_adaptive_weight, the same function in the LDM code, lines 32 to 43); the discriminator starts only after the autoencoder can reconstruct (LDM's disc_start 50,001)

The catch at large codebooks. The 16,384-code VQGAN reconstructs better but uses only 5.9% of its codes (1,024 codes: 44%), per {{IBQ Table 1, arXiv 2412.02692v2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02692v2}} independent. Section 8's fixes made large codebooks work: in ViT-VQGAN's ablation at 8,192 codes, usage rises from 4% with 256-dimensional codes to 96% with 32-dimensional L2-normalised ones (rFID 3.68 to 1.55), and removing the normalisation at 32 dimensions drops usage to 2% ({{Yu et al. 2021, Table 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.04627v3}}) published.

Tokens per image is sequence length. The token count sets an autoregressive model's cost (attention roughly with its square); the vocabulary sets what each token carries (log2 K bits) and how hard prediction is. Most settled on f = 16 and 1,024 to 16,384 codes; halving f quadruples the sequence.

Predict, then pick: what does going from f = 16 to f = 8 do to attention cost at 512 × 512?

Tokens = (resolution / f)2; bits = tokens × log2 K; attention pairs = tokens2, against Chameleon's 1,024 tokens (512 × 512, f = 16), which "encodes a 512x512 image into 1024 discrete tokens from a codebook of size 8192" ({{arXiv 2405.09818v2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09818v2}}). derived

Larger vocabularies reconstruct better but are harder to predict. MAGVIT-v2's Figure 1 shows generation first improving, then worsening as a VQ vocabulary grows, its reason "the vocabulary size of most language models for visual generation is around 1-8k" published. Chameleon names its ceiling: "A core weakness of our tokenizer is in reconstructing images with a large amount of text, therefore upper bounding the capability of our models" published. And tokens made for reconstruction are not features made for understanding: Janus keeps SigLIP for understanding and a VQ tokenizer for generation, and one VQ tokenizer for both scores far worse on understanding (POPE 60.1 against 87.0, {{arXiv 2410.13848v1, Table 5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13848v1}}) published.

Relatives: residual, multi-scale and 1-D tokenizers (depth)

Residual quantization (RQ-VAE, {{Lee et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.01941v2}}) quantizes the leftover error again with the same codebook, D times, so each position holds D codes: at 8 × 8 positions, depth 4 reaches rFID 4.73 where a one-code VQGAN at 8 × 8 is at 17.95 even with 131,072 codes; the paper: "increasing the quantization depth D is more effective ... than increasing the codebook size K" published. Neural audio codecs use the same idea (the sibling {{Video and audio generation|n:3c65c17b0d0d8198bd7be111a3dc4c3a}}). Multi-scale tokenizers (VAR, {{Tian et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02905v2}}) quantize the residual at growing resolutions, 1 × 1 up to 16 × 16 in its code (10 scales, 680 tokens, one shared codebook of 4,096), so the generator predicts coarse to fine. 1-D tokenizers (TiTok, {{Yu et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07550v1}}) drop the grid: 32 tokens for a 256 × 256 image at rFID 2.21 published.

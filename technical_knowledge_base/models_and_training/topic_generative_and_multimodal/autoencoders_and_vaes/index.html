An autoencoder is a pair of networks: an encoder that turns an image into a much smaller latent (the code), and a decoder that turns the latent back into an image. Almost every image and video generator of 2026 has one in front of it, and the quality of everything it generates is capped by how well that decoder draws. This page is the depth behind section 2 of the parent topic page and its Latent lab: how these autoencoders are trained, how they fail, and how to tell a good one from a bad one. 1. Two jobs: a compressor for diffusion, a tokenizer for autoregression

The problem. A 512 × 512 colour image is 786,432 numbers. Generators do not work on them directly: a diffusion or flow model runs its network tens of times per image, and an autoregressive model predicts one symbol at a time, so both want a much shorter description. An autoencoder provides it, in one of two forms.

Continuous latent (for diffusion and flow)

A small grid of real numbers: Stable Diffusion 1.5 keeps 64 × 64 × 4 = 16,384 numbers for a 512 × 512 image, FLUX.1 64 × 64 × 16 = 65,536. The generator learns to produce such grids; the decoder draws the image once at the end. The autoencoder is a VAE in name, trained with a tiny KL weight plus perceptual and adversarial losses (section 9).

Discrete tokens (for autoregressive and masked models)

A small grid of integers, each one of K codebook entries: Chameleon turns a 512 × 512 image into 1,024 tokens from a codebook of 8,192 (section 8). A transformer predicts them like words, so images and text can share one sequence. The autoencoder is a VQ-VAE, VQGAN, FSQ or LFQ tokenizer.

The cost, either way. The decoder's ceiling binds every generated image: detail it cannot draw (small text, faces at a distance, fine texture) cannot appear, however good the generator. And the latent's shape sets the generator's cost: the number of positions sets the sequence length (tokens) or the denoiser's spatial size, the channels or codebook size set how much each position carries. Choosing that shape is the central design decision of an image tokenizer, and the trade-off has a measured name: rate (how much the latent carries) against distortion (how far the reconstruction is from the image) against how easy the latent is to model.

Check it. Every claim about an autoencoder on this page is a reconstruction measurement (encode a held-out image, decode, compare) or a generation measurement (sample latents, decode, compare the distribution with real images). They answer different questions and disagree often; section 10 is about which to trust.

2. The ELBO in working form: two numbers, and their units

The problem. The VAE's loss is the negative ELBO (evidence lower bound): a reconstruction term plus a KL term. The derivation is on the Math page (section 4). In practice the trouble is not the derivation but the units: the two terms are only balanced as the derivation intends when both are log-probabilities of a whole image, in the same unit. Change how either is summed or averaged and you have silently changed β.

loss per image =
−log p(x | z)  [summed over all pixels]
+ β · KL(q(z | x) ‖ N(0, I))  [summed over all latent dimensions]
−log p(x | z)
the reconstruction term, in nats (natural-log units) for the whole image: Bernoulli cross-entropy for the topic's tiny models, a Gaussian (squared error) or Laplace (absolute error) likelihood elsewhere
KL
the rate: how many nats of information the code carries about this image, on average; 0 means the code says nothing
β
1 for the true ELBO; the Latent lab sweeps it from 0.1 to 10

Reading the two numbers. The parent lab's 16-number VAE at β = 1 ends at nats of reconstruction plus nats of KL per test image: nats, or bits per pixel (divide by 1,024 pixels and by ln 2) derived. The KL is the code's budget: bits per image, about the size of a short sentence, and the reconstruction term says how much of the image those bits fail to pin down.

The Gaussian decoder and σ. With a squared-error loss the decoder is implicitly a Gaussian with a fixed standard deviation σ per pixel: −log p(x | z) = Σ (x − x̂)2 / (2σ2) + a constant. Writing plain "MSE summed + KL" fixes σ2 = 1/2. So σ is a β: relative to the summed squared error, the KL's weight is 2σ2 derived. Measured on pixels scaled to [−1, 1]: with σ = 1 the code carries nats and reconstructs at dB; with σ = 0.1, which makes squared error 100 times more expensive, it carries nats and reconstructs at dB, but its random-code samples get worse (prior FD against ): the β trade-off of the Latent lab, reached by changing a "noise level" nobody thinks of as a weight. All 16 dimensions stay active in both. measured Learning σ instead of fixing it is one fix; the LDM autoencoder learns a single log-variance for its whole reconstruction term (nll_loss = rec_loss / torch.exp(self.logvar) + self.logvar, contperceptual.py line 53), so its effective weight between reconstruction and KL moves during training.

A detail of that loss worth knowing. In the same function the LPIPS distance, one number per image, is added to the L1 error of every pixel before the sum (lines 48 to 51 and 57: rec_loss = rec_loss + self.perceptual_weight * p_loss, then torch.sum(...) / shape[0]), so the perceptual term counts once per pixel and channel, 786,432 times at 512 × 512. The KL, summed over the latent and weighted by 10−6 (line 83), is tiny against it by design. source read at CompVis/latent-diffusion a506df575647

When it matters, how to check. Whenever you write a VAE loss by hand or port one: log the reconstruction and the KL separately and in nats per image, check that the reconstruction's reduction is a sum over pixels (or that β has been rescaled to match), print the KL per latent dimension, and count active units. A KL near zero with a fine-looking loss curve is the signature of every case above.

3. Posterior collapse: when the decoder does not need the code

The problem. The KL term is smallest, exactly 0, when the encoder ignores the image and outputs N(0, I) for everyone. If the decoder can reconstruct well without the code, nothing pays for leaving that optimum, and the code goes unused: posterior collapse. The Math page explains the mechanism with Bowman et al.'s LSTM text decoder (section 10); here it is on images, with the three standard fixes compared on the same model.

A decoder that does not need z. A PixelCNN decoder predicts each pixel from the pixels above and to its left (masked convolutions), plus the code. During training it sees the true previous pixels, so it can predict most of an image from its own left-and-above context: a garment's edge continues, the background stays black. The model here: the topic's VAE encoder (16 numbers), a PixelCNN decoder of one 7 × 7 and five 3 × 3 masked layers, 64 channels, the code added as a learned bias to every layer (src/collapse/pixelvae.py), parameters, trained with the same recipe as every other model here.

KL nats per test image, of 16 active units. The direct check: give every test image another image's code (a shuffle) and the reconstruction term gets worse by only nats. The decoder learned to do the job itself. measured

Three fixes, same model, same data.

  • KL annealing (Bowman et al. 2016, arXiv 1511.06349): start with the KL weight at 0 and raise it linearly to 1 (here over the first half of training), so the encoder learns to carry information before the KL starts charging for it ("annealing from a vanilla autoencoder to a vae", in the paper's words). DALL-E's dVAE used the same idea, raising its KL weight from 0 to 6.6 over the first 5,000 updates (Ramesh et al. 2021, appendix A.2).
  • Free bits (Kingma et al. 2016, IAF, arXiv 1606.04934): do not charge for the first λ nats of each latent dimension: the KL term becomes Σj max(λ, KLj), with KLj averaged over the minibatch (here λ = 0.5 nats per dimension, so up to 8 nats in total are free). The paper's appendix C.8 found λ from 0.125 to 2 helpful on CIFAR-10.
  • Weaken the decoder, or drop the PixelCNN entirely (the ordinary decoder of the Latent lab, which cannot reconstruct anything without the code). Chen et al.'s Variational Lossy Autoencoder explains collapse in exactly these terms: information the decoder can model locally is not worth sending through z, so limit the decoder's receptive field to what you want the code to leave out.

Removing the strong decoder costs likelihood: an ordinary decoder's negative ELBO is far worse than a PixelCNN's.

When it matters, how to check. Any VAE with an autoregressive or otherwise powerful decoder (text VAEs, PixelCNN and transformer decoders, some video and audio models), and any VAE whose loss units are off (section 2). Check: KL per dimension and active units over training; the shuffle test (reconstruction with another image's code); decode the same code twice with different random draws and different codes once: if the code does not change the output, it is not being used.

4. Why reconstructions blur, and what actually fixes it

The problem. Autoencoders trained with a pixel loss give soft, smeared reconstructions, and VAEs more so. The Latent lab shows the effect; here are its four causes, because each has a different fix.

  1. The pixel loss rewards the average. When the code does not pin down a detail (the exact position of an edge, the pattern of a texture), the squared error is minimised by the mean of all the images consistent with the code, and the mean of slightly different sharp edges is a soft edge. Absolute error (L1) gives the median, a little sharper, but the same in kind. Fix: a loss that does not reward averaging, a perceptual or adversarial one.
  2. Too little rate. A small latent, a high β or a Gaussian σ that is too large (section 2) means the code pins down less, so there is more to average over. Fix: more channels or tokens, a smaller KL weight. Generator autoencoders use a KL weight of about 10−6.
  3. The VAE's sampling noise. Training decodes z = μ + σε, not μ, so the decoder learns to expect a jittered code and hedges. Decoding the mean at test time helps only a little.
  4. The downsampling factor. Detail finer than one latent cell has to be redrawn by the decoder from nothing. At 8 × downsampling a 512 × 512 image's 1-pixel strokes share a cell with 63 other pixels.

The trade-off you cannot escape. Blau and Michaeli proved that, at a given rate, the lowest possible distortion (pixel error) and the most realistic-looking output are different targets, and pushing one costs the other (The perception-distortion trade-off, 2018). A decoder that draws plausible fur where the code was unsure will score a worse PSNR than one that draws the average blur. So "sharper" and "lower pixel error" are not the same goal, and a tokenizer trained for generation should be judged on how realistic its reconstructions look, not on PSNR alone.

The fix, measured on the tiny tokenizer

The same 128-code VQ tokenizer (with dead-code restarts, as the Latent lab's vq_restart) trained three ways: the pixel loss only; plus a perceptual loss (LPIPS, the distance between the two images' features in a pretrained AlexNet, Zhang et al. 2018); plus a patch adversarial loss from step 2,500 (a small discriminator that labels every 8 × 8 region of the 32 × 32 image as real or reconstructed, hinge loss, weighted adaptively as in the LDM code). This is the VQGAN recipe at toy scale (section 7).

5. VQ-VAE training: the straight-through gradient and keeping the codebook alive

How it works, in brief (the Latent lab builds it step by step). The encoder outputs a vector ze at each latent position; the quantizer replaces it by the nearest of K codebook vectors e; the decoder sees e. Nearest-neighbour lookup has no gradient, so the decoder's gradient with respect to e is copied to ze unchanged, the straight-through estimator; a codebook loss ‖sg[ze] − e‖2 moves the chosen codes towards the encoder's outputs and a commitment loss β‖ze − sg[e]‖2 (β = 0.25) keeps the encoder near its codes (van den Oord et al. 2017, eq. 3).

The problem: dead codes. A code that is never the nearest to any encoder output receives no gradient from either loss, so it never moves and is never chosen: codebook collapse. With the usual initialisation (uniform in ±1/K, far smaller than the encoder's outputs) most codes die in the first few hundred steps. The fixes in use, each run here on the same tokenizer:

  • Dead-code restarts: re-seed unused codes from random encoder outputs of the current batch (Jukebox, Dhariwal et al. 2020, section 3.1). The Latent lab's vq_restart.
  • EMA codebook: no codebook loss; each code is set to a moving average of the encoder outputs assigned to it (decay 0.99), with Laplace smoothing of the counts (VQ-VAE appendix A.1). Faster and more stable updates, but a code that wins nothing still does not move.
  • Normalised codes: L2-normalise both ze and the codebook before the lookup, so distances become angles and every code lives on the same sphere as the data (ViT-VQGAN, Yu et al. 2021, section 3.2); usually combined with low-dimensional codes (8 to 32 numbers), the "factorized codes" of the same paper.
  • Data-dependent initialisation (k-means on the first training batch's encoder outputs, as in SoundStream, which also uses EMA codebooks and restarts: Zeghidour et al. 2021, section III-C) and the rotation trick for a better-than-copied gradient (Fifty et al. 2024): not run here.

6. FSQ and LFQ: quantizers with nothing to collapse

The idea. Codebook collapse happens because the codebook is learned. Remove it: make the code grid fixed and let the encoder learn to use it.

FSQ: finite scalar quantization

Project the encoder output to a few channels d (3 to 10), squash each into a bounded range with tanh, and round it to one of Li levels. The codebook is implicit, the product of the levels: [8, 5, 5, 5] gives 1,000 codes from 4 channels. No codebook loss, no commitment loss, gradients by straight-through rounding (Mentzer et al. 2023). The paper reports codebook usage "≈100% for most models, without relying on any auxiliary losses" and recommends Li ≥ 5 for every channel published.

LFQ: lookup-free quantization

The extreme case: log2 K channels, each quantized to its sign, ±1. The token is the binary number the signs spell, so 18 channels give 218 = 262,144 codes with no lookup at all. An entropy penalty, E[H(q(z))] − H[E(q(z))], makes each token confident and all tokens used (MAGVIT-v2, Yu et al. 2023, eq. 3 to 5; weight 0.1, commitment 0.25 in its appendix) published.

The FSQ bounding function, ported from the paper's appendix A.1 to PyTorch and used for every FSQ run here (src/quant/quantizers.py):

def bound(self, z):                     # paper appendix A.1, def bound
    eps = 1e-3
    half_l = (self.L - 1) * (1 - eps) / 2
    offset = torch.where(self.L % 2 == 1, 0.0, 0.5)   # even L needs an asymmetric range
    shift = torch.tan(offset / half_l)
    return torch.tanh(z + shift) * half_l - offset

q = round_ste(self.bound(z))            # integers; round_ste(x) = x + (round(x) - x).detach()
zq = q / (self.L // 2)                  # what the decoder sees, about [-1, 1]

The cost. FSQ and LFQ put fewer numbers into each token (3 to 18 channels against VQ's 8 to 256), so one token carries less, and the levels are fixed rather than fitted to the data. In return large vocabularies become usable: MAGVIT-v2 reports reconstruction and generation both improving as LFQ's vocabulary grows, "a property not observed in current VQ-VAE methods" published, and FSQ reports VQ "is actually worse for large codebooks" published.

When it matters, how to check. Choose FSQ or LFQ when you need a vocabulary of thousands to hundreds of thousands of codes, or when VQ's collapse keeps coming back. Check codes used and perplexity exactly as for VQ; a large vocabulary is also harder for the generator to model (section 8), so check generation, not only reconstruction.

7. VQGAN: the recipe behind nearly every image tokenizer

The problem. A VQ-VAE trained with a pixel loss blurs (section 4), and a blurry tokenizer caps every image a generator makes from its tokens. VQGAN (Esser, Rombach, Ommer 2020) kept the VQ-VAE and changed the loss.

loss = L1 or L2 pixel loss + LPIPS perceptual loss
+ codebook and commitment losses + λ · patch-GAN loss
LPIPS
distance between the features of the two images inside a pretrained network (AlexNet or VGG), with per-layer weights fitted to human similarity judgements (Zhang et al. 2018); 0 means identical features
patch GAN
a small convolutional discriminator scores every patch of the image as real or reconstructed; the autoencoder is trained to fool it, so texture must look real, not merely average
λ
set adaptively each step: the ratio of the two losses' gradient norms at the decoder's last layer, so the GAN term never swamps the reconstruction (calculate_adaptive_weight, the same function in the LDM code, lines 32 to 43); the discriminator is switched on only after the autoencoder has learned to reconstruct (LDM's disc_start 50,001)

What it bought, in the paper's own table published: reconstruction FID (rFID: the Fréchet distance between the reconstructions of the ImageNet validation set and the originals, at 256 × 256) against the earlier tokenizers.

The catch at large codebooks. The 16,384-code VQGAN reconstructs better, but later papers measured that it uses only 5.9% of its codes (1,024 codes: 44%), per IBQ Table 1, arXiv 2412.02692v2 independent. Section 5's fixes are what made large codebooks work: ViT-VQGAN's ablation, at 8,192 codes, reports usage rising from 4% with 256-dimensional codes to 96% with 32-dimensional L2-normalised ones (rFID 3.68 to 1.55), and removing the normalisation at 32 dimensions drops usage to 2% (Yu et al. 2021, Table 4) published.

At toy scale here, adding LPIPS to the 128-code tokenizer cut rFD from to at a cost of dB of PSNR, and the patch GAN added nothing measurable on top (section 4).

Relatives worth knowing. Residual quantization (RQ-VAE, Lee et al. 2022) quantizes the leftover error again with the same codebook, D times, so each position holds D codes: at 8 × 8 positions, depth 4 reaches rFID 4.73 where a one-code VQGAN at 8 × 8 is at 17.95 even with 131,072 codes; the paper: "increasing the quantization depth D is more effective ... than increasing the codebook size K" published. Neural audio codecs use the same idea (the sibling page Video and audio generation). Multi-scale tokenizers (VAR, Tian et al. 2024) quantize the residual at growing resolutions, 1 × 1 up to 16 × 16 in its code (10 scales, 680 tokens in total, one shared codebook of 4,096), so the generator predicts coarse to fine. 1-D tokenizers (TiTok, Yu et al. 2024) drop the grid: 32 tokens for a 256 × 256 image at rFID 2.21 published.

8. Image tokenizers in production: tokens per image and the vocabulary

The trade-off. For an autoregressive model, tokens per image is sequence length, and cost grows with it (attention roughly with its square); vocabulary size sets how much each token carries (log2 K bits) and how hard the next-token prediction is. Every tokenizer below picks a point.

What the numbers say. Most autoregressive image models settled on f = 16 and 1,024 to 16,384 codes (256 tokens at 256 × 256, 1,024 at 512 × 512): halving f quadruples the sequence. Larger vocabularies reconstruct better but are harder to predict; MAGVIT-v2's Figure 1 shows generation quality first improving and then getting worse as a VQ vocabulary grows, which it offers as the reason "the vocabulary size of most language models for visual generation is around 1-8k" published, and LFQ as the way past it. Chameleon names the cost of its choice: "A core weakness of our tokenizer is in reconstructing images with a large amount of text, therefore upper bounding the capability of our models" (arXiv 2405.09818v2) published. Janus keeps two image encoders, SigLIP for understanding and the LlamaGen VQ tokenizer for generation, and its ablation shows one VQ tokenizer for both tasks scoring far worse on understanding (POPE 60.1 against 87.0, arXiv 2410.13848v1, Table 5) published: tokens made for reconstruction are not the features made for understanding. The sibling page Native multimodal and any-to-any models covers the generators that use these tokens.

9. The autoencoders inside real image generators

What they are. The latent diffusion and flow models in this table use continuous autoencoders of one family, descended from the LDM autoencoder: a convolutional encoder and decoder (DC-AE adds attention-like blocks), a KL weight so small the latent is barely regularised (about 10−6 in the LDM recipe), and VQGAN-style losses (L1 + LPIPS + patch GAN in the LDM code; Qwen-Image's decoder fine-tune dropped the GAN). More channels keep more detail at the same f. SD3's own ablation at f = 8 published (Esser et al. 2024, Table 3; dataset and resolution not stated in the paper):

The cost is real at smaller scales. VA-VAE measured it directly at f = 16 on ImageNet 256 (Yao et al. 2025, Table 2) published: going from 16 to 32 to 64 channels improves reconstruction FID from 0.49 to 0.26 to 0.17 but worsens generation FID (with the same LightningDiT-B generator) from 16.24 to 22.62 to 36.83; aligning the latent to DINOv2 features during autoencoder training (their "VF loss") recovers most of it (f16d32: 15.82). Other levers. SDXL kept SD's architecture and retrained it "at a larger batch-size (256 vs 9)" with an EMA of the weights, beating SD 1.x on every reconstruction metric on COCO at 256 × 256 (rFID 4.4 against 5.0; Podell et al. 2023, Table 3) published. Qwen-Image froze the Wan 2.1 encoder and fine-tuned only the image decoder on text-rich images (documents, slides, posters), with reconstruction and perceptual losses but no adversarial loss ("the discriminator is unable to provide effective guidance" at that quality): text-image PSNR 36.63 against 26.77 for the Wan 2.1 decoder it started from (Qwen-Image report, Table 2) vendor. DC-AE goes the other way, compressing 32 × per side with residual shortcuts and a staged high-resolution adaptation; at f = 64 on ImageNet 512 it reports rFID 0.22 where the SD-VAE design trained at the same latent shape reaches 16.84, and 19.1 × faster inference for the same generator (Chen et al. 2024, Table 2) published.

10. Evaluating an autoencoder or tokenizer

Fidelity to the image

PSNR: 10 log10(2552 / mean squared error), in dB; pixel-exact, so it prefers blur over plausible detail. SSIM: compares local means, contrasts and correlations in small windows, 1 is identical; still a local pixel statistic. LPIPS: distance between deep features (AlexNet or VGG layers, weighted by a model fitted to human judgements), 0 is identical; tracks what people notice better than either.

Realism of the set

rFID: the Fréchet distance between Inception features of the reconstructions and of the originals over a whole set, usually the 50,000 ImageNet validation images at 256 × 256 (LlamaGen: "r-FID, reconstruction-FID on 256×256 ImageNet 50k validation set", arXiv 2406.06525v1). It asks whether reconstructions look like real images as a distribution, so it punishes blur that PSNR forgives. It needs thousands of images (see Common mistakes), and protocols differ: FSQ computes it against the training set rather than the validation set (arXiv 2309.15505v2).

The tokens themselves

Codes used and perplexity (section 5); tokens or numbers per image and bits per image (section 8); encode and decode time and memory. For a continuous latent: its per-channel statistics (section 9's normalisations exist because they are not unit-scale).

What it is for

Generation FID of a generator trained on the latents, at fixed compute. The only measure of "easy to model", and the one sections 8 and 9 show can move against reconstruction.

Metrics disagree, measured. On the comparator's 16 images the three fidelity measures rank the nine autoencoders differently at the top and in the middle: PSNR puts FLUX.1 first, SSIM and LPIPS put FLUX.2 first; MoVQ is fourth by PSNR but seventh by SSIM; Qwen-Image is third by PSNR and SSIM but fourth by LPIPS, behind SD 1.5. The parent's Latent lab shows the deeper version: a Gaussian blur with exactly the SD reconstruction's PSNR looks far worse. For reference, LPIPS as computed here (torchmetrics 1.9.0, AlexNet, v0.1 weights) ranges from to across the nine models. measured

Comparing papers. Numbers are only comparable at the same resolution, dataset, image count and metric implementation. Even the scale of LPIPS differs between papers: SDXL's Table 3 reports LPIPS of 0.88 to 0.96 and BFL's table 0.27 to 1.67, far above the 0.01 to 0.08 the standard implementation gives for nine similar autoencoders on this page's images, without saying how they were scaled; NVIDIA's Cosmos report re-ran rivals under its own protocol and got different rFIDs from the rivals' own papers (LlamaGen at f = 16: 1.657 in Cosmos's Table 8 against 2.19 in LlamaGen's). Treat cross-paper tables as rough, and re-measure the candidates you are choosing between on your own images.

How to check a tokenizer you are about to use: round-trip a few hundred of your own images, look at full-resolution crops of text, faces and fine texture, compute PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS, check float16 against float32 once, print the normalised latent's mean and standard deviation (near 0 and 1 if the recipe is right), and for discrete tokenizers count the codes used.

Common mistakes, with what they look like

Averaging the reconstruction over pixels while summing the KL
An effective β equal to the number of pixels: of 16 active units and every reconstruction the average garment on the running example (section 2). Nothing errors; the loss curve looks fine. Check: KL per image in nats, and active units.
Plain MSE plus KL, without asking what σ that implies
Summed squared error plus KL is a Gaussian decoder with σ2 = 1/2, and σ is a β (section 2). Moving σ from 1 to 0.1 on [−1, 1] pixels took the KL from to nats and the prior FD from to (section 2). Choose σ deliberately, or learn it.
A powerful decoder and a KL that drops to zero
Posterior collapse: the PixelCNN decoder here ended with a KL of nats and decoded the same picture whatever the code (section 3). Run the shuffle test; use free bits or annealing, or limit the decoder.
Training a large codebook with the default initialisation and no usage logging
At K = 1,024 the plain VQ recipe used codes on the test set (section 5). Log codes used and perplexity; restart dead codes, use EMA with k-means initialisation or normalised codes, or switch to FSQ.
Running an autoencoder in float16 without checking for NaNs
The SDXL VAE in float16 returned NaN (not-a-number) values on all of the 16 test images here, already in the encoder's output; the fp16-fix VAE, with the same architecture and rescaled internal activations, did not (section 9). diffusers' force_upcast config flag exists for this. Check one image in float32 against float16 before a batch run.
Using a model's scaling factor but forgetting its shift
FLUX.1's VAE needs (z − 0.1159) × 0.3611 on the way in and the inverse on the way out. Decoding without adding the shift back moved PSNR from to dB on the 16 test images: a small, silent error here (a constant offset in every latent value), easy to miss in a test and harmful wherever exact colours matter, as in editing. The scaling factor is the loud version of the same bug (the parent's Latent lab).
Comparing PSNR across resolutions, crops or colour spaces
PSNR depends on what is averaged: grey 28 × 28 crops (this page's tiny tokenizers, 22 to 25 dB) and 512 × 512 colour images through SD 1.5's VAE (20.6 to 37.5 dB in the parent's Latent lab, depending on the image) are not on one scale, and neither are papers that compute it at 256 or on the luminance channel only. Compare tokenizers only on the same images at the same resolution.
Quoting an rFID from a few hundred images
Fréchet distances are biased upwards at small N: the topic's real-against-real floor is 2.54 at 10,000 images and about 15.6 at 1,000 (root src/data/README.md). Tokenizer papers use the 50,000 ImageNet validation images; use thousands at least, and state N.
Choosing the tokenizer by reconstruction alone
Better reconstruction does not mean better generation: more channels or a larger vocabulary reconstruct better and can be harder for the generator to model (section 9, section 8). Train a small generator on the candidate latents and measure generation before committing.

Interview questions

Your VAE's KL went to zero and the samples ignore the input. What happened and what do you do?
Posterior collapse: the decoder can model the data without the code (an autoregressive or otherwise strong decoder), or the loss units make the KL far too expensive (a per-pixel mean reconstruction against a summed KL). First check the units; then use KL annealing, free bits (do not charge the first λ nats per dimension), a weaker or local decoder, or a discrete latent. Confirm with the shuffle test: decoding from another image's code should cost many nats.
Why are VAE reconstructions blurry, and why does turning β down not fully fix it?
A pixel loss is minimised by the average of the images consistent with the code, and averages are soft. Lower β gives the code more rate, so there is less to average over, but whatever the code still leaves open is averaged. The fix is a loss that does not reward averaging: perceptual (LPIPS) plus adversarial, as in VQGAN and every generator autoencoder. It costs some PSNR (the perception-distortion trade-off).
Explain the straight-through estimator and the two extra losses of VQ-VAE.
Nearest-code lookup has no gradient, so the decoder's gradient with respect to the quantized vector is copied to the encoder output (ze + sg[e − ze] in code). The codebook loss ‖sg[ze] − e‖2 moves codes towards encoder outputs; the commitment loss β‖ze − sg[e]‖2 (β = 0.25) stops the encoder outputs from drifting away from the codebook.
What is codebook collapse, and name four fixes.
Most codes are never the nearest to any encoder output, get no gradient and stay unused, so the effective vocabulary shrinks. Fixes: dead-code restarts from encoder outputs; k-means initialisation; EMA codebook updates; L2-normalised low-dimensional codes; or remove the learned codebook (FSQ, LFQ). Monitor codes used and perplexity.
How does FSQ differ from VQ, and what does it give up?
FSQ bounds each of a few channels with tanh and rounds it to a fixed number of levels; the codebook is the implicit grid (product of the levels), so there is nothing to learn and nothing to collapse, and no auxiliary losses. It gives up flexible code placement and puts fewer numbers in each token; the encoder must adapt to the grid.
What is SD's 0.18215, and why do newer VAEs have a shift or per-channel statistics instead?
One over the standard deviation of SD 1.x's raw latents, so the diffusion process sees unit-scale inputs. Later latents are not zero-mean or not equally scaled per channel, so FLUX.1 subtracts a shift before scaling, Qwen-Image normalises each of 16 channels with its own mean and std, and FLUX.2 uses batch-norm running statistics after 2 × 2 patchification. Each must be undone exactly before decoding.
Why did generator VAEs move from 4 to 16 or 32 channels, and what is the catch?
More channels keep more detail at the same 8 × downsampling (better text, faces and texture). The catch: a richer latent is harder for the generator to learn, so more channels can reconstruct better and generate worse unless the generator is larger or the latent is regularised or aligned to a pretrained encoder (section 9).
How do you evaluate a tokenizer?
Reconstruction: PSNR and SSIM (pixel fidelity), LPIPS (perceptual), rFID on thousands of held-out images (does the set of reconstructions look like real data). Usage: codes used, perplexity. Cost: tokens or numbers per image, encode and decode time, memory. And the one that matters: generation FID of a generator trained on the tokens, at matched compute.
Design: you are building an autoregressive text-to-image model for 512 × 512 images. Choose the tokenizer.
Sequence length sets cost: f = 16 gives 32 × 32 = 1,024 tokens per image (Chameleon's choice), f = 8 gives 4,096. Pick f = 16 unless the budget allows more. Vocabulary: 8k to 16k VQ codes with L2-normalised low-dimensional codes and restarts, or FSQ/LFQ for larger vocabularies; train with L1/L2 + LPIPS + patch GAN (VQGAN) on data like the target (text-heavy if text rendering matters, since the decoder's ceiling binds). Measure rFID, LPIPS, usage, and a small AR model's generation FID for two or three candidates; budget a decoder fine-tune on faces and text. Trade-offs to state: fewer tokens are faster but lose detail; a larger vocabulary reconstructs better but is harder to predict.
Design: a latent diffusion model's outputs have mangled small text. Is the generator or the autoencoder at fault?
Round-trip real images with small text through the autoencoder alone. If the reconstructions already mangle it, the decoder's ceiling is the cause (as SD 1.5's VAE does on text in the Latent lab); a generator cannot fix that. Options: a VAE with more channels or a text-fine-tuned decoder (Qwen-Image fine-tuned its decoder on text-rich images, per its report), or higher resolution. If reconstructions are fine, look at the generator (data, text encoder, guidance).

What to take from it

  • Channels buy detail. At the same 8 × downsampling, the 16-channel FLUX.1 and Qwen-Image VAEs and the 32-channel FLUX.2 VAE beat the 4-channel SD and SDXL VAEs by about 4 to 5 dB of PSNR on average, and by 7 to 13 dB on the cat photo, the sticker image, the clock and the text card (compare rows in the per-image table). Reading section 9 explains why that is not free for the generator.
  • Text is where they differ most. On the "LATENT 42" card PSNR runs from to dB across the nine; look at its crop.
  • Every model's hardest image is the same (g04, grass and fur): texture the decoder must invent.
  • Precision is a property of the weights. The SDXL VAE in float16 returns NaN for every image; the fp16-fix weights, fine-tuned to keep activations small, run in float16 within a few hundredths of a dB of float32. Run float32 once before trusting float16.
  • Normalisation recipes differ (the statistics table): the SD, SDXL, FLUX and DC-AE recipes bring these images' latents to a standard deviation between 0.86 and 1.2, Qwen-Image's per-channel statistics to 0.64, each by a different route, from raw scales between about 1.4 and 7.3. Using one model's recipe with another's weights fails silently.
  • Limits. 16 images, so rankings between close models are not reliable, and rFID (which needs thousands of images) is not computed; published rFIDs are in Reading sections 7 to 9. DC-AE ran in bfloat16 (312M parameters, over the page's 300M rule for float32); the others in float32 except the two fp16 rows. Qwen-Image's 3-D causal VAE peaked at about 9.6 GiB of MPS memory for one 512 × 512 frame, the most of the nine.

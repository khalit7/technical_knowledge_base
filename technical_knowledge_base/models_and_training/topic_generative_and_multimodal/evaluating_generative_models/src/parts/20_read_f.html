6. Prompt alignment: did it draw what was asked?

Distribution metrics ignore the prompt: a model that draws beautiful random scenes scores well. For text-to-image each image is also checked against its prompt, either with one similarity or with questions.

CLIPScore ({{Hessel et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08718}}) is CLIP's cosine between image and text, CLIP-S = 2.5 · max(cos, 0), with CLIP ViT-B/32 (the 2.5 stretches the usual 0 to 0.4 range of cosines; the paper scored captions, prefixed "A photo depicts"). Many text-to-image papers instead report 100 · cos with a larger CLIP and call it "CLIP score": the same idea on another scale. It needs no reference image and inherits CLIP's blind spots: CLIP's text side behaves much like a "bag of words" (the VQAScore authors' phrase), so counts, relations and text are weak, as {{Vision encoders|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c99f60ceb2f02cf5ea}} shows with a typographic image (its section 6).

Question-answering checks ask instead. TIFA ({{Hu et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11897}}) turns the prompt into question-answer pairs with a language model and asks a VQA model about the image; with mPLUG it correlated with human faithfulness ratings at Spearman 0.60 against 0.33 for CLIPScore (Table 2). DSG ({{Cho et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18235}}) orders the questions in a dependency graph, so "is the motorcycle blue?" is skipped when there is no motorcycle. VQAScore ({{Lin et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01291}}) asks one question, "Does this figure show '{text}'? Please answer yes or no.", and uses the probability of "Yes"; on Winoground its group score was 46.0 against 7.8 for CLIPScore.

Compositional benchmarks fix the prompt suite and the judge. GenEval ({{Ghosh et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11513}}) uses an object detector instead of a VLM for six tasks (single object, two objects, counting, colours, position, attribute binding): SD v1.5 scores 0.43 overall, SDXL 0.55, IF-XL 0.61, with position at most 0.15 (Table 2), and it agrees with human annotators on 83% of images against 88% between annotators. T2I-CompBench ({{Huang et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06350}}; its extended ++ version: 8,000 compositional prompts in four groups, attribute binding, object relationships, generative numeracy and complex compositions) scores spatial relations and counts with a detector and the rest with multimodal LLMs. DPG-Bench ({{Hu et al. 2024, ELLA|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.05135}}) is about 1,000 dense, long prompts for "dense prompt following". Each reports a per-skill breakdown, which is the useful part.

7. Human preference: arenas and reward models

People are the final judge. Arenas show two anonymous models' images for the same prompt, collect a vote, and fit a Bradley-Terry (Elo-style) rating; the mechanics, confidence intervals and critiques (style, self-selected prompts, private variants) are on {{Human preference and arenas|n:3ef5c17b0d0d814db49ff3b5c9fc3034}}. The text-to-image boards move monthly; a dated reading is in the closed block at the end of this section.

Reward models learn to predict those votes, so they can score thousands of images (or serve as a reward for fine-tuning). They are measured by the share of held-out human pairwise choices they predict:

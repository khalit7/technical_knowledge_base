8. Resolution and aspect ratio

The problem. A denoiser learns the statistics of the latent grids it was trained on: their size, their shape, and how much of a subject one position covers. SD 1.5 was fine-tuned at 512 × 512 (its model card: 595k steps at that size). Ask it for another size and nothing in the code stops you: the U-Net is convolutional and its attention takes any number of positions.

One prompt, one seed, four sizes (SD 1.5)

What the four sizes show. At 512, the training size, one puppy fills the frame. At 768 (2.25 times the area) one puppy still holds together, with more background. At 1024 (four times the area) the model draws four puppies: each region of the 128 × 128 latent is denoised with the statistics of a 64 × 64 training image, so the canvas fills with several subjects of the usual size; the U-Net's attention reaches the whole grid, but nothing in its training taught it what one large subject looks like at this scale. At 256 the puppy is cropped at the edge and its proportions are off: too few positions for the layout it learned. The same seed and settings throughout; no sampler change fixes this.

How production models handle it.

  • Train at the sizes you will sample. SDXL pretrained at 256 × 256, then 512, then fine-tuned on "different aspect ratios ... of ~1024 × 1024 pixel area" ({{Podell et al. 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01952}}, section 2.5).
  • Aspect-ratio bucketing (from NovelAI's 2022 work, which SDXL and SD3 cite): sort the training images into buckets of similar shape, keep the pixel count as close to 10242 as the shape allows, with height and width in multiples of 64, and build each batch from one bucket (SDXL section 2.3, which also feeds the bucket's size to the model as a condition). For a transformer, equal area means a similar number of tokens, so the cost per batch stays level.
  • Tell the model what it is looking at. SDXL's micro-conditioning feeds the original image size, the crop offsets and the target size as Fourier features added to the time embedding ({{pipeline line 734|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/pipelines/stable_diffusion_xl/pipeline_stable_diffusion_xl.py#L734}}). Without the size condition, 39% of SDXL's pretraining data would have been discarded as smaller than 256 pixels, and the paper blames random crops in training for SD 1.5's cut-off heads; with the crop offsets as an input, sampling at (0, 0) asks for an uncropped image and other values "simulate the amount of cropping" (section 2.2, Figures 4 and 5; vendor figures).
  • Shift the noise schedule with resolution. More pixels carry more redundant signal, so the same noise level destroys less of the image; SD3 shifts its timesteps by a factor of √(m / n) (m pixels at the new resolution, n at the base) and FLUX computes the shift from the image's token count ({{pipeline_flux.py line 73|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/pipelines/flux/pipeline_flux.py#L73}}). The sibling page Flow matching owns the details.

9. What one image costs

The formula. One image = text encoder once (twice with guidance and a negative or empty prompt) + steps × passes per step × FLOPs per pass + one VAE decode. Passes per step is 2 with classifier-free guidance (the conditional and the unconditional prediction, batched together) and 1 for a guidance-distilled model (FLUX.1 [dev]) or a model sampled without guidance (FLUX.1 [schnell], Z-Image-Turbo). FLOPs per pass grow with the image tokens N (linear layers, convolutions) and with N2 (attention), so at high resolution attention takes over: counted here, attention scores are of SD 1.5's FLOPs at 512 pixels and at 2,048.

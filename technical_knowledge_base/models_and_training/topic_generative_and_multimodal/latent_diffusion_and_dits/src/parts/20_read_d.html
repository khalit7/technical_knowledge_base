3. How the text gets in: encoders, pooled vectors and cross-attention

The problem. The denoiser must draw what the prompt says, so the prompt has to reach every position of the latent at every step. Two questions: what vectors stand for the prompt (the text encoder and which of its outputs), and how the image positions read them (cross-attention, joint attention or modulation).

Step 1: tokens. SD 1.5's tokenizer (CLIP's byte-pair encoding) turns the running example's first prompt into a start token, 11 word pieces and an end token, then pads to exactly 77 positions with the end token's id (49407) again. 77 is the context length CLIP was trained with; anything longer is cut, and diffusers says so in a warning ({{pipeline_stable_diffusion.py line 413|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/pipelines/stable_diffusion/pipeline_stable_diffusion.py#L413}}). Step 2: the encoder's outputs. CLIP's text transformer is causal (each position sees only itself and earlier positions). Three consequences, each checked here on three prompts:




    

  • The start token's output is a constant. Position 0 sees only itself, so its 768-number output is identical for every prompt (cosine , largest difference ). It carries no information about the prompt, yet the U-Net's cross-attention puts most of its weight there (below): a slot to attend to when nothing in the prompt concerns a position, the same "attention sink" seen in language models.
    • 

  • The pooled vector is the end token's output. CLIP's single summary vector (pooler_output) is the final-layer output at the end token (equal here to within 10−5). SD 1.5 ignores it and passes the whole 77 × 768 sequence to cross-attention.
    • 

  • Padding is not empty. The positions after the end token have seen the whole prompt; their outputs stay close to the end token's (cosine 1.00 just after it, falling to  at the last position). SD 1.5's U-Net applies no mask, so the image attends to them.
    • 




Step 3: cross-attention. In each transformer block of the U-Net, the latent positions form the queries and the 77 text vectors the keys and values: position i computes a softmax over the 77 tokens and takes the weighted mix of their values. The weights are a map per token: where in the image that word is being used. Two published results explain the trend. Imagen found that scaling the frozen text encoder improved fidelity and alignment more than scaling the image model (quoted on the parent page). SD3 trained with each of its three encoders dropped independently, which buys a choice at inference: against the full model, the version without T5-XXL (4.7B parameters) won 50% of human comparisons on aesthetics (no difference), 46% on prompt adherence and 38% on written text, i.e. T5 matters most for spelling ({{Esser et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03206}}, section 5.3.3; vendor, Stability AI's own raters). The same paper gives the drop rate as 46.3% in one place and 46.4% in another; 0.4643 = 0.0999, so 46.4% is the value that makes "roughly an unconditional model in 10% of all steps" true. derived



The cost. The encoder runs once per prompt (twice with classifier-free guidance, for the empty prompt), so even a 7B language model is a small share of an image's compute next to 25 to 50 denoiser steps; it is a large share of the memory, which is why SD3 made T5 droppable and why FLUX.2 [klein] uses a 4B encoder. Inside the denoiser, cross-attention costs N × T (positions times text tokens) per layer and joint attention (N + T)2: at 1,024 image tokens and 512 text tokens, the text half of a joint sequence is not small (section 9).

3. How the text gets in: encoders, pooled vectors and cross-attention

The problem. The denoiser must draw what the prompt says, so the prompt has to reach every position of the latent at every step. Two questions: what vectors stand for the prompt (the text encoder and which of its outputs), and how the image positions read them (cross-attention, joint attention or modulation).

Step 1: tokens. SD 1.5's tokenizer (CLIP's byte-pair encoding) turns the running example's first prompt into a start token, 11 word pieces and an end token, then pads to exactly 77 positions with the end token's id (49407) again. 77 is the context length CLIP was trained with; anything longer is cut, with only a logged warning ({{pipeline_stable_diffusion.py line 413|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/pipelines/stable_diffusion/pipeline_stable_diffusion.py#L413}}). Step 2: the encoder's outputs. CLIP's text transformer is causal (each position sees only itself and earlier positions). Three consequences, each checked here on three prompts:




    

  • The start token's output is a constant. Position 0 sees only itself, so its 768-number output is identical for every prompt (cosine , largest difference ). It carries nothing about the prompt, yet the U-Net's cross-attention puts most of its weight there (below): a slot to attend to when no word concerns a position, the same "attention sink" seen in language models.
    • 

  • The pooled vector is the end token's output, and SD 1.5 ignores it: the whole 77 × 768 sequence goes to cross-attention, padding included.
    • 



The pooled vector and the padding, checked (depth)
CLIP's single summary vector (pooler_output) is the final-layer output at the end token (equal here to within 10−5). Padding is not empty: the positions after the end token have seen the whole prompt; their outputs stay close to the end token's (cosine 1.00 just after it, falling to  at the last position). SD 1.5's U-Net applies no mask, so the image attends to them.



Step 3: cross-attention. In each transformer block of the U-Net, the latent positions form the queries and the 77 text vectors the keys and values: position i computes a softmax over the 77 tokens and takes the weighted mix of their values. The weights are a map per token: where in the image that word is being used. How the maps were recorded, and the code (provenance)


Every attn2 processor of the U-Net replaced by one that keeps the softmax output (src/sd/sd_ld.py): diffusers routes every attention layer through a processor object, so swapping the processors records every map without touching the model. The conditional half of the guided batch, mean over heads, summed per resolution; maps stored as 8-bit, each token scaled to its own maximum (the mass bars use the unscaled values). Two published results explain the trend. Imagen found that scaling the frozen text encoder improved fidelity and alignment more than scaling the image model (quoted on the parent page). SD3 trained with each of its three encoders dropped independently, which buys a choice at inference: against the full model, the version without T5-XXL (4.7B parameters) won 50% of human comparisons on aesthetics (no difference), 46% on prompt adherence and 38% on written text, i.e. T5 matters most for spelling ({{Esser et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03206}}, section 5.3.3; vendor, Stability AI's own raters). The same paper credits the joint text-image transformer itself (section 5) with better typography, spelling words inside the image.

The cost. The encoder runs once per prompt (twice with guidance, for the empty prompt), a small share of compute next to 25 to 50 denoiser steps even at 7B, but a large share of memory: why SD3 made T5 droppable and FLUX.2 [klein] uses a 4B encoder. Inside the denoiser, cross-attention costs N × T per layer and joint attention (N + T)2 (section 9).

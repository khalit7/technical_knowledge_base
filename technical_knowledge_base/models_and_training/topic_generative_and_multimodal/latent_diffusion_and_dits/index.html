A text-to-image model runs one large network, the denoiser, tens of times on a small VAE latent while a text encoder steers it, so the price of an image is FLOPs per denoiser pass × passes per image. Every design on this page changes one of those two factors or how the text reaches the image: the latent shrinks the pass, the U-Net and the diffusion transformer (DiT) are two ways to build it, cross-attention and joint attention are two ways to read the prompt, and distillation cuts the passes.

1. Why latent diffusion: the compute arithmetic

The problem. The denoiser runs tens of times per image (at the topic's settings: 25 steps, each two passes with guidance), so everything it costs is multiplied. A 512 × 512 colour image is 786,432 numbers, while the details that matter to a viewer (shapes, layout, texture statistics) are far fewer.

How it works. Train an autoencoder once, then train and run the denoiser on its latent. Stable Diffusion's VAE maps 512 × 512 × 3 to 64 × 64 × 4 = 16,384 numbers, 48 times fewer (the downsampling factor f = 8 per side, 4 channels). The decoder runs once at the end; the denoiser never sees a pixel. Rombach et al. called this splitting "perceptual compression" (the autoencoder) from "semantic compression" (the generator); the paper's own f sweep is rebuilt on the LDM paper page, with a live toy of latent against pixel diffusion at fixed compute.

The same U-Net costs hundreds of times more on pixels than on the latent

Two parts that scale differently. Convolutions and linear layers cost in proportion to the positions N, attention scores to N2. SD 1.5's top level already compares 4,096 positions with 4,096; on pixels it would hold 262,144, an attention matrix of 6.9 × 1010 entries per head per layer. That is why pixel-space models (DDPM, ADM, Imagen's base model) worked mostly at 64 to 256 pixels and reached higher resolutions with separate upsampling models.

Predict: double the image side, 512 to 1024 pixels (four times the positions). How much more does one SD 1.5 U-Net pass cost?

The price. Latents cap quality at what the decoder can draw (small text, faces at a distance: Autoencoders and VAEs measures nine decoders), and the denoiser must be fed a latent with the statistics it was trained on, which is what the scaling factor is for (same page, section 11). Since 2023 the saved compute has gone into a bigger denoiser and deeper compression with more channels (FLUX.1 and SD3: 16 channels at f = 8; Sana's DC-AE: 32 channels at f = 32), not back to pixels.

2. The U-Net, read from Stable Diffusion 1.5's config

The problem. The denoiser takes a noisy latent and the step's noise level (the timestep) and predicts the noise (or the velocity, for a flow model) at every position. It must see fine detail (to remove noise position by position) and the whole layout (to know that the blob in the corner is a wheel). A U-Net (Ronneberger et al. 2015, arXiv:1505.04597) gets both by shrinking the grid level by level, so that a 3 × 3 convolution at the bottom covers a large part of the image, then growing it back, with skip connections that hand each level's features straight across to the matching level on the way up.

How it works, read from the config. SD 1.5's unet/config.json at the pinned revision says everything: block_out_channels [320, 640, 1280, 1280] (four levels, at 64, 32, 16 and 8 positions per side for a 512 image), down_block_types three CrossAttnDownBlock2D then one plain DownBlock2D (no attention at 8 × 8, except in the middle block), layers_per_block 2 (two ResNet blocks per level on the way down, three on the way up), cross_attention_dim 768 (the width of CLIP ViT-L's text vectors) and attention_head_dim 8, which diffusers reads as the number of heads, so heads are 40, 80 and 160 wide (unet_2d_condition.py line 249; a classic mistake, closed block at the end).

ResNet block (the convolutional part)

GroupNorm, SiLU, 3 × 3 convolution; then the time embedding is projected to the block's channels and added to every position (time_emb_proj); GroupNorm, SiLU, 3 × 3 convolution; plus the input (a residual). The time embedding is a 320-wide sinusoid of the timestep (line 569) through a two-layer MLP to 1,280.

Transformer block (the attention part)

Flatten the grid into a sequence; self-attention (attn1: every position looks at every other), cross-attention (attn2: every position looks at the 77 text vectors, attention.py line 1043), a GEGLU feed-forward, each with LayerNorm and a residual; reshape back to a grid. One per ResNet block at the 64, 32 and 16 levels, one in the middle.

Skip connections are concatenations, not sums: each up-block ResNet receives its input with the matching down-level features stacked on as extra channels (unet_2d_blocks.py line 2444). Twelve feature maps go across (the input convolution, two ResNets and a downsampler at each of the first three levels, two ResNets at the last), and the up path consumes three per level. That is why the up blocks hold most of the parameters: their first convolutions see twice the channels.

Parameters sit where the grid is small; FLOPs and time sit where it is large

The lesson of the three views: parameters sit where the grid is small and the channels many (the 16 × 16 and 8 × 8 levels hold most of them), while the FLOPs sit where the grid is large (the two 64 × 64 levels do 38% of the work with 3.4% of the weights). A parameter count says what the model can store; FLOPs per pass say what an image costs. DiT's authors measured complexity in Gflops for this reason (section 4).

The cost of the design. The U-Net's convolutions build in locality and multi-scale structure, which makes it data-efficient at small scale (this page's tiny experiment in section 4 shows it) but heterogeneous: every level has its own width, block mix and attention cost, so scaling it means hand-redistributing blocks. SDXL did exactly that: "we omit the transformer block at the highest feature level, use 2 and 10 blocks at the lower levels, and remove the lowest level" (Podell et al. 2023, section 2.1), for a 2.6B U-Net (counted here: 2,567,463,684).

3. How the text gets in: encoders, pooled vectors and cross-attention


The problem. The denoiser must draw what the prompt says, so the prompt has to reach every position of the latent at every step. Two questions: what vectors stand for the prompt (the text encoder and which of its outputs), and how the image positions read them (cross-attention, joint attention or modulation).



Step 1: tokens. SD 1.5's tokenizer (CLIP's byte-pair encoding) turns the running example's first prompt into a start token, 11 word pieces and an end token, then pads to exactly 77 positions with the end token's id (49407) again. 77 is the context length CLIP was trained with; anything longer is cut, with only a logged warning (pipeline_stable_diffusion.py line 413). CLIP's single summary vector (pooler_output) is the final-layer output at the end token (equal here to within 10−5). Padding is not empty: the positions after the end token have seen the whole prompt; their outputs stay close to the end token's (cosine 1.00 just after it, falling to  at the last position). SD 1.5's U-Net applies no mask, so the image attends to them.



Step 3: cross-attention. In each transformer block of the U-Net, the latent positions form the queries and the 77 text vectors the keys and values: position i computes a softmax over the 77 tokens and takes the weighted mix of their values. The weights are a map per token: where in the image that word is being used. 4. The diffusion transformer (DiT): patches, adaLN-Zero, and scaling with compute


The problem. The U-Net is a hand-built stack of different blocks; a transformer is one block repeated, with well-understood scaling and years of systems work behind it. Peebles and Xie asked whether a plain transformer can replace the U-Net inside latent diffusion, and what to scale (Scalable Diffusion Models with Transformers, Dec 2022).



How it works. Four pieces, all visible in the animation of section 2 (switch it to DiT):


    

  1. Patchify. Cut the I × I × C latent into p × p patches and embed each linearly (a convolution with kernel and stride p): T = (I / p)2 tokens of width d. At 256 × 256 the latent is 32 × 32 × 4, so p = 2 gives 256 tokens; at 512, 1,024 tokens.
    2. 

  2. Positions. Add a fixed 2-D sine-cosine embedding (as in ViT and MAE): the tokens carry where they came from (section 7).
    3. 

  3. N identical DiT blocks with adaLN-Zero conditioning (below).
    4. 

  4. Unpatchify. A final adaptive LayerNorm and a linear layer map each token back to p × p × 2C numbers (the noise and, for DiT, a learned variance), reshaped into the latent grid (models.py line 218).
    5. 



adaLN-Zero. The timestep and class embeddings are summed into one vector c. Each block feeds c through SiLU and one linear layer to 6d numbers: a shift and scale for the LayerNorm before attention and before the MLP (adaptive LayerNorm, the same trick as FiLM and StyleGAN), and a gate that multiplies each branch before its residual add. That linear layer is initialised to zero, so every gate starts at 0 and every block starts as the identity. The authors' code, as it ran here for counting (models.py lines 119 to 121 and 207):


def forward(self, x, c):
    shift_msa, scale_msa, gate_msa, shift_mlp, scale_mlp, gate_mlp = self.adaLN_modulation(c).chunk(6, dim=1)
    x = x + gate_msa.unsqueeze(1) * self.attn(modulate(self.norm1(x), shift_msa, scale_msa))
    x = x + gate_mlp.unsqueeze(1) * self.mlp(modulate(self.norm2(x), shift_mlp, scale_mlp))
    return x

Cheaper modulation: adaLN-single, and attention without N2


adaLN-Zero has a price the paper's Gflops hide: each block's modulation layer is d × 6d, a third of the block (attention 4d2, MLP 8d2, modulation 6d2). Counted here, DiT-XL/2's modulation layers hold 225.8M of its 675.1M parameters (33.4%). PixArt-α found the same, "a substantial proportion (27%) of the parameters" in its text-conditioned DiT, and replaced them with adaLN-single: one MLP computes the six vectors from the timestep once, shared by all blocks, plus a small learned per-block offset (Chen et al. 2023). Sana (section 1's 32 × DC-AE) went further and replaced softmax attention by ReLU linear attention (attention_processor.py line 5371), so its cost grows linearly with tokens: counted here, attention scores are 2% of Sana's FLOPs at 1024 pixels, against 15 to 37% for the softmax transformers on this page.

5. MMDiT: text and image in one attention


The problem. In cross-attention the text is read but never changes: the 77 vectors are the same in every layer, so the text cannot adapt to what the image has become. DiT's class conditioning through adaLN carries only one vector, too little for a sentence. Stable Diffusion 3 wanted the text processed alongside the image (Esser et al., Mar 2024).


How it works. Concatenate the text tokens and the image tokens into one sequence and run one attention over all of it, but give each modality its own weights: "two separate sets of weights for the two modalities ... equivalent to having two independent transformers for each modality, but joining the sequences of the two modalities for the attention operation" (section 4). Each block has, for image and for text separately, the Q, K, V and output projections, the MLP and the adaLN modulation (driven by the timestep and the pooled text vector); only the softmax is shared. In diffusers the join is one line (attention_processor.py line 1480): query = torch.cat([query, encoder_hidden_states_query_proj], dim=2), the same for keys and values, then split again after attention.





Who attends to whom: only cross-attention leaves the text unchanged













What it bought, by the paper's own comparison. On CC12M at matched settings, "Our MM-DiT variant significantly outperforms the cross-attention and vanilla variants" (DiT with concatenated text, CrossDiT with cross-attention, and UViT), on validation loss, CLIP score and FID (section 5.2.3, Figure 4; vendor, the authors' runs). QK-normalisation came with it: at scale, attention logits grew until the attention entropy collapsed in the last blocks; RMSNorm on Q and K in both streams prevented it and "enables efficient training at bf16-mixed precision" (section 5.3.2, "QK-Normalization"). Modulation is a third of the model: each stream has its own d × 6d modulation per block, so counted here SD3.5 Large's modulation layers hold 2,686.3M of 8,056.6M parameters (33.3%) and Qwen-Image's (60 MMDiT blocks) 6,815.9M of 20,430.4M (33.4%).


The cost. Joint attention costs (N + T)2 per layer and the text stream's weights run on every text token, so text length is no longer nearly free: SD3.5 Large at 1024 pixels costs 28.95 TFLOP per pass with 77 text tokens and 31.12 with diffusers' 333 (a 7.5% difference, counted).

6. FLUX-style double and single stream blocks


The problem. Two full weight sets per block double the parameters per unit of depth. Once text and image have been mixed for a while, do they still need separate weights?


How it works. FLUX.1 (Black Forest Labs, August 2024; no paper, but the FLUX.1 Kontext report describes it) : "Double stream blocks employ separate weights for image and text tokens ... we concatenate them and apply 38 single stream blocks to the image and text tokens. Finally, we discard the text tokens and decode the image tokens" (Labs et al. 2025, section 2). The released code has 19 double blocks and 38 single blocks (BFL flux util.py: depth 19, depth_single_blocks 38, hidden size 3,072, 24 heads). A single-stream block is a parallel transformer block (as in ViT-22B): one LayerNorm and one modulation, then attention and the MLP computed side by side from the same input, their outputs concatenated and projected once (transformer_flux.py line 404), which halves the modulation parameters and makes larger matrix multiplications.

7. Positions for image tokens: tables, crops and 2-D RoPE


The problem. Attention is blind to order: shuffle the tokens and the outputs shuffle with them. An image transformer must be told where each patch sits, in two dimensions, ideally in a way that still works at a resolution or aspect ratio it was not trained at. Three answers are in use:


    

  • A fixed 2-D sine-cosine table (DiT, PixArt): half the channels encode the row, half the column, at geometric frequencies. It is computed for one grid; the DiT code builds it at construction for one input size, which is why this page's FLOP counter had to rebuild DiT-XL/2 for every resolution.
    • 

  • A large table, center-cropped (SD3): one table for a 192 × 192 patch grid, cropped to the image's grid at run time (embeddings.py line 336), so any size up to 192 × 2 × 8 = 3,072 pixels per side has positions. For mixed aspect ratios the paper builds "a combination of extended and interpolated position grids" and center-crops them (section 5.3.2).
    • 

  • Rotary embeddings in 2-D or 3-D (FLUX, Qwen-Image, Z-Image, FLUX.2): rotate each query and key by angles proportional to the token's position, so their dot product depends only on the offset between two tokens. FLUX splits each 128-wide head into 16 channels for a third axis (time or reference image), 56 for the row and 56 for the column (axes_dim [16, 56, 56]); image token ids are (0, row, column) (pipeline_flux.py line 508) and the text tokens' ids are all zero, so text sits at "no position" and is told apart by its own weights. Qwen-Image's MSRoPE instead places the text "along the diagonal of the image" grid (Qwen-Image report, section 2.4). Z-Image uses 32/48/48 channels; FLUX.2 four axes of 32. There is no table to outgrow, which helps with new sizes but does not by itself make the model good at them (section 8).
    • 





FLUX's position ids: image tokens get (0, row, column), text tokens all zero










RoPE itself (why rotating q and k makes attention depend on relative position, and how frequencies are chosen) is on Positional Encodings and the RoFormer paper page; here only the 2-D and 3-D split is new.






8. Resolution and aspect ratio


The problem. A denoiser learns the statistics of the latent grids it was trained on: their size, their shape, and how much of a subject one position covers. SD 1.5 was fine-tuned at 512 × 512 (its model card: 595k steps at that size). Ask it for another size and nothing in the code stops you: the U-Net is convolutional and its attention takes any number of positions.




One prompt, one seed, four sizes: at four times the training area SD 1.5 draws four puppies










What the four sizes show. At 512, the training size, one puppy fills the frame. At 768 (2.25 times the area) one puppy still holds together, with more background. At 1024 (four times the area) the model draws four puppies: each region of the 128 × 128 latent is denoised with the statistics of a 64 × 64 training image, so the canvas fills with several subjects of the usual size; the U-Net's attention reaches the whole grid, but nothing in its training taught it what one large subject looks like at this scale. At 256 the puppy is cropped at the edge and its proportions are off: too few positions for the layout it learned. The same seed and settings throughout; no sampler change fixes this.


How production models handle it.


    

  • Train at the sizes you will sample. SDXL pretrained at 256 × 256, then 512, then fine-tuned on "different aspect ratios ... of ~1024 × 1024 pixel area" (Podell et al. 2023, section 2.5).
    • 

  • Aspect-ratio bucketing (from NovelAI's 2022 work, which SDXL and SD3 cite): sort the training images into buckets of similar shape, keep the pixel count as close to 10242 as the shape allows, with height and width in multiples of 64, and build each batch from one bucket (SDXL section 2.3, which also feeds the bucket's size to the model as a condition). For a transformer, equal area means a similar number of tokens, so the cost per batch stays level.
    • 

  • Tell the model what it is looking at. SDXL's micro-conditioning adds the original size, crop offsets and target size to the time embedding as Fourier features (pipeline line 734). The size condition kept the 39% of pretraining data smaller than 256 pixels that would otherwise be discarded; the crop condition targets SD 1.5's cut-off heads, which the paper blames on random crops: sampling at (0, 0) asks for an uncropped image, other values "simulate the amount of cropping" (section 2.2, Figures 4 and 5; vendor).
    • 

  • Shift the noise schedule with resolution. More pixels carry more redundant signal, so the same noise level destroys less of the image; SD3 shifts its timesteps by a factor of √(m / n) (m pixels at the new resolution, n at the base) and FLUX computes the shift from the image's token count (pipeline_flux.py line 73). Flow matching, section 11, owns the details.
    • 








9. What one image costs


The formula. One image = text encoder once (twice with guidance and a negative or empty prompt) + steps × passes per step × FLOPs per pass + one VAE decode. Passes per step is 2 with classifier-free guidance (the conditional and the unconditional prediction, batched together) and 1 for a guidance-distilled model (FLUX.1 [dev]) or a model sampled without guidance (FLUX.1 [schnell], Z-Image-Turbo). FLOPs per pass grow with the image tokens N (linear layers, convolutions) and with N2 (attention), so at high resolution attention takes over: counted here, attention scores are  of SD 1.5's FLOPs at 512 pixels and  at 2,048. A video clip multiplies the image tokens by its frames, so the N2 term grows with the square of the duration: the clip-length slider of Video and audio generation, section 4 ("Duration is the expensive axis"), shows it overtaking everything else.

10. Few steps: distillation


The problem. The cost formula's biggest lever is passes per image: 50 for SD 1.5 at the topic's settings, 100 for SDXL at its pipeline default, against one decode. Better solvers bring 1,000 steps down to 10 to 25 (Diffusion from zero, section 5), but no solver follows a curved path in one or two straight steps. Below about 8 steps you need a second model trained to jump: distillation, where a student learns to do in a few passes what the teacher (the original model plus its sampler) does in many. A student removes one of two things.




Distilled students cut passes per image 7 to 100 times; FLOPs per pass stay the same








One square is one denoiser pass; blue conditional, orange the unconditional passes guidance adds. Teachers at the topic's settings (SD 1.5) or the diffusers pipeline defaults. Students: LCM-LoRA 4 steps, guidance off (measured on Diffusion from zero, section 7); SDXL-Turbo 1 step, guidance_scale=0.0 (card: "1 to 4 steps"); SD3-Turbo "four unguided sampling steps" (LADD); FLUX.1 [schnell] 4 steps (card: "1 to 4 steps"). [dev] and [schnell] share one 11.9B transformer, 74.38 TFLOP per pass at 1024: 28 passes are 2,083 TFLOP, 4 are 298. derived




    

  • Remove the second pass per step: guidance distillation. Train a student that takes the guidance scale w as an input and outputs the guided prediction in one call (Meng et al. 2022). FLUX.1 [dev] is such a model ("Trained using guidance distillation", its model card), and it shows in the weights: the 10,229,760-parameter guidance embedder of section 6. Halves the cost at the same steps; negative prompts lose their slot, since there is no unconditional pass to replace.
    • 

  • Remove steps: four families of students.

      

    • Progressive distillation (Salimans and Ho 2022): a student does in one step what the teacher does in two; repeat, halving each round.
      • 

    • Consistency distillation (Song et al. 2023): train a map from any point on the sampling path straight to its end. Latent consistency models do it in SD's latent with guidance baked in (Luo et al. 2023); LCM-LoRA packs the result into a 67.5M-parameter LoRA that plugs into an unchanged SD 1.5 (Luo et al. 2023).
      • 

    • Adversarial distillation: add a GAN loss so one or two steps give sharp images. ADD (Sauer et al. 2023, SDXL-Turbo) judges decoded pixels with a frozen DINOv2 discriminator; LADD (Sauer et al. 2024) judges in the latent, with generative features of a pretrained latent diffusion model instead of a pixel-space discriminator, which enables "high-resolution multi-aspect ratio image synthesis": SD3-Turbo, and FLUX.1 [schnell] ("Trained using latent adversarial diffusion distillation", its card).
      • 

    • Distribution matching (DMD, Yin et al. 2023; DMD2, 2024): match the student's output distribution to the teacher's, using two score models.
      • 

    • 



What it costs. A student is a new model: training it takes compute and data (LCM about 32 A100 GPU hours), it is tied to its teacher's weights, and the few-step regime has its own failures. Measured on SD 1.5 by Diffusion from zero (section 7): "LCM-LoRA is sharp at 1 to 4 steps but drops the lighthouse; DPM-Solver++ needs 4 steps to keep it". Flow-based shortcuts (rectified flow, reflow, one-step distillation of a flow) are on Flow matching, sections 8 and 9.

Depth: mistakes, rehearsal, what transfers


Common mistakes, each from a real run or a real config (9)


Reading attention_head_dim in an SD 1.x or 2.x config as the head width. In UNet2DConditionModel configs it is the number of heads (SD 1.5: 8 heads of width 40, 80 and 160; SDXL's [5, 10, 20]: heads of width 64). diffusers keeps the old name for "40,000+ configurations" and maps it at unet_2d_condition.py line 254. Transformer configs (SD3Transformer2DModel, FluxTransformer2DModel) mean what they say: 64 and 128.


Writing prompts longer than 77 tokens for a CLIP-conditioned model. Everything past position 77 is dropped, with only a logged warning (the real one is in section 3). SD 1.5 and SDXL never see the end of a long prompt; SD3 sees it through T5 only. Fixes: shorten, put what matters first, or use a long-prompt helper that encodes chunks of 75 and concatenates them (the model then sees several start and end tokens, which it was not trained on). A T5- or LLM-conditioned model accepts 256 to 512 tokens.


Sampling far from the training resolution. SD 1.5 at 1024 pixels (section 8) is a real run on this page: asked for one puppy on a canvas four times its training area, it drew four puppies of the usual size. It is not a bug in the sampler; it is the denoiser's learned statistics. Use a model trained near the target size and aspect ratio (bucketing), or generate at the native size and upscale (a second img2img pass at the high resolution is the common recipe).


Comparing models by parameter count when the question is cost. DiT-XL/8 and DiT-XL/2 have the same 675M parameters and differ 16 times in compute and 5 times in FID. FLUX.1 [dev] has 1.5 times SD3.5 Large's parameters but costs about 1.2 times as much per image because it needs one pass per step instead of two. Count FLOPs per pass, multiply by steps and passes (section 9).


Trusting a parameter count without knowing what was counted. Three cases found while counting for this page: SD3's checkpoints include a 192 × 192 position table that diffusers holds as a buffer (56.6M numbers for SD3 Medium, 89.7M for SD3.5 Large, section 5); diffusers' port of DiT-XL/2 has 749,826,464 parameters against the authors' 675,129,632, because its conversion script copies the timestep and class embedders into every one of the 28 blocks (convert_dit_to_diffusers.py line 34), 27 extra copies of 2,777,472 parameters, and holds the position table as a buffer where the authors' code has a frozen parameter (256 × 1,152 = 294,912 at 256 pixels): 27 × 2,777,472 − 294,912 = 74,696,832, the whole gap; and model cards round ("12 billion" for 11.9B). State what a number includes.


Running classifier-free guidance on a guidance-distilled model. FLUX.1 [dev]'s guidance_scale is an input to the network (an embedding, default 3.5), not the CFG weight; the diffusers pipeline runs one pass per step unless true_cfg_scale is set above its default 1.0 with a negative prompt, which doubles the cost and was not what the model was distilled for. [schnell] has no guidance embedder at all (10,229,760 fewer parameters, section 6).


Feeding a model text features from the wrong layer. SD 1.5 was trained on CLIP's last layer, SDXL on the penultimate layers of two encoders (line 414), Z-Image on Qwen3's second-to-last layer. The two layers differ: measured here on SD 1.5's encoder, the cosine between the last and penultimate layer's output is  across the prompt's positions. "Clip skip" settings in user interfaces swap these; on a model trained with one, the other shifts every cross-attention input.


Running a DiT at a size its position table was not built for. The authors' DiT code builds its sine-cosine table for one latent size; giving it another fails in the patch embedding before attention is reached. The real error, from counting DiT-XL/2 at 1024 pixels on this page before rebuilding the model per size: 
AssertionError: Input height (128) doesn't match model (32).
 Rebuild (or interpolate) the table for the new grid; RoPE models (section 7) have no table to rebuild, but still need training at the new size.


Expecting a small transformer to beat a U-Net on a small budget. The tiny experiment in section 4: at equal training compute the topic's 1M-parameter U-Net beat a 4M-parameter DiT by a wide margin. DiT's advantage is in how it scales with compute and how well it fits modern hardware and systems, not in data efficiency at toy scale.




Interview rehearsal (optional, 9 questions)


Why do most image generators run diffusion in a VAE latent instead of on pixels? A denoiser runs 20 to 50 times per image, so its cost per pass dominates. An f = 8 VAE cuts positions 64 times (48 times fewer numbers for SD's 4 channels), and attention cost falls with the square: counted here, SD 1.5's U-Net would cost about 680 times more per pass on a 512 × 512 pixel grid. The price is a quality ceiling set by the decoder and the need to normalise the latent (scaling factor).


Where are a U-Net's parameters, and where are its FLOPs? Parameters at the low-resolution, high-channel levels (weights grow with channels squared); FLOPs at the high-resolution levels (cost grows with positions). For SD 1.5 at 512: the two 64 × 64 stages do 38% of the FLOPs with 3.4% of the weights, while up_blocks.1 (16 × 16) holds 258M parameters.


What is adaLN-Zero and why initialise it at zero? The conditioning vector (timestep plus class or pooled text) is mapped per block to a shift and scale for each LayerNorm and a gate on each residual branch. Zero-initialising that mapping makes every block the identity at the start, so a deep stack trains stably from a well-behaved function; in DiT's ablation it beat plain adaLN (FID 19.5 vs 25.2), cross-attention (26.1) and in-context tokens (35.2). Its cost: the modulation layer is about a third of each block's parameters, which PixArt cut with adaLN-single and FLUX.2 with shared modulation.


Cross-attention or joint attention (MMDiT) for text: what are the trade-offs? Cross-attention: text is read-only, cost N × T per layer, a cheap way to add text to any backbone. Joint attention: one softmax over image and text, so text tokens are updated by the image and both modalities interact symmetrically; cost (N + T)2 and a second set of weights per block (or shared weights in single-stream blocks). SD3 found MMDiT better than cross-attention DiT and UViT at matched settings; nearly every 2025 to 2026 open model uses joint attention.


Why did the DiT paper report Gflops rather than parameters? Because quality tracked compute per forward pass, not parameters: shrinking the patch size quadruples tokens and Gflops without changing the weights, and FID improved accordingly. Their "Gflops" are multiply-accumulates (counted here at exactly half the FLOPs).


FLUX.1 [dev] against [schnell]: what differs in the weights and in the cost? [dev] has a guidance embedder (10.2M parameters) because it was distilled to take the guidance scale as an input: one pass per step, default 28 steps. [schnell] was distilled further (latent adversarial diffusion distillation, per its card) to 1 to 4 steps without guidance. Same transformer otherwise: 19 double and 38 single-stream blocks.


How would you support many aspect ratios and resolutions? Bucketed training at roughly constant area (similar token counts per batch), size and crop conditioning or their equivalent, position encodings that extend (RoPE or a large cropped table), a resolution-dependent timestep shift, and progressive training from low to high resolution. Check by sampling at every bucket and at an unseen size.


Design question: a text-to-image service must return 1024 × 1024 images in about a second on one datacenter GPU. Sketch the model and the arithmetic. Work backwards from compute. If the GPU sustains S TFLOP/s on the denoiser, one second buys S TFLOP. A FLUX.1-sized model costs 74 TFLOP per pass at 1024 (counted), so even 4 passes need 300 TFLOP: plausible only with a few-step distilled model and a fast kernel. Levers in order of size: (1) steps and passes: a distilled few-step model (adversarial or consistency distillation) with guidance distilled in, 1 pass per step; (2) tokens: a deeper-compression VAE (f = 16 or 32 with more channels, as in Sana's DC-AE) cuts tokens 4 to 16 times; (3) attention cost: linear or windowed attention at high resolution; (4) model size: a 2 to 5B denoiser rather than 12B; (5) text encoder: a 2 to 4B LLM or T5, run once and cached for repeated prompts. Then measure: the calculator gives FLOPs, but kernel efficiency decides the seconds. Keep a quality check (a preference or VQA-based metric) in the loop, since every lever trades quality.


Why does SD 1.5's cross-attention put so much weight on the start token? CLIP's text encoder is causal, so the start token's output is the same constant for every prompt. Softmax attention has to put its weight somewhere; a position whose content no word concerns puts it on that constant slot, which acts as a no-op (an attention sink). Measured here: about  of the 16 × 16 layers' cross-attention for the bicycle prompt.

FLOPs per pass against image size, all eleven






The formula: FLOPs(N, T) = k0 + k1N + k2T + k3N2 + k4NT + k5T2, N = image tokens (or the U-Net's top-level latent positions), T = text tokens, with the six coefficients fitted to counts on meta tensors at 10 square sizes and 1 to 5 text lengths per model (src/arch/flops_all.py).

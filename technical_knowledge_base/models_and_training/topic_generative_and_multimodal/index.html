The idea. Making images and reading them are two pipelines built from the same few parts: a generator turns random noise into a small latent through tens of denoising passes and decodes it to pixels once; a reader cuts an image into patch tokens that a language model attends to like words; a native multimodal model runs both inside one transformer.

1. Five ways to generate, three ways to understand

An image of 28 × 28 grey pixels is a point in a 784-dimensional space; almost every point is static, and the images that look like clothing fill a tiny, oddly shaped region of it. We never get a formula for that region (the data distribution p(x)), only examples. A generative model is a recipe for drawing new points from it; a conditional one draws from p(x | c), with c a class or a prompt. Understanding, not generating. A vision encoder turns an image into vectors (ViT, CLIP, SigLIP). A vision-language model (VLM) feeds those vectors to a language model, which answers in text. A native multimodal model puts text and image tokens into one transformer, which can read and write both.

2. Latents: generate something smaller than the image

A 512 × 512 colour image is 786,432 numbers; Stable Diffusion v1 generates a 64 × 64 × 4 latent instead, 16,384 numbers, 48 times fewer derived, and the denoiser pays for every number at every step. An autoencoder makes the latent: an encoder squeezes the image, a decoder rebuilds it, trained on the difference. Three kinds matter, and they differ in what you can do with the code:

KindThe codeCan you sample a new image from it?Used by
Plain autoencoderAny vector the training put thereNo: a random code falls in a gap and decodes to nonsenseCompression only
VAEA distribution pulled towards a standard Gaussian by a KL penaltyYes, but blurry: the decoder averages what the code cannot pin downThe compressor of latent diffusion (with the KL weight near zero)
VQ-VAE and its successorsThe nearest entries of a learned codebook: a grid of integersYes, with a second model that predicts the integers like wordsAutoregressive and native multimodal models

The autoencoder of a latent diffusion model is a compressor, not a generator: Stable Diffusion’s weights its KL term at about 10−6 and adds perceptual and adversarial losses for sharp texture, and its latents are rescaled by a scaling factor (0.18215 for Stable Diffusion v1, 0.3611 for FLUX’s 16 channels) so the denoiser sees unit-scale numbers.

3. Diffusion and flow matching, side by side

Diffusion. Mix an image with Gaussian noise in closed form, xt = √āt x0 + √(1 − āt) ε, train a denoiser to predict the noise from xt with a squared error, and generate by starting from pure noise and removing the predicted noise a little per step; the rule per step is the sampler. Flow matching. Draw the straight line from an image to a noise sample, xt = (1 − t) x0 + t ε, train the network to predict its direction ε − x0 (the velocity), and generate by stepping against the predicted velocity with an ODE solver. Classifier-free guidance works the same in both: run the network with and without the prompt and push past the difference, ε = εuncond + w (εcond − εuncond), which doubles the passes per step.

They are one family. Flow matching “is compatible with a general family of Gaussian probability paths”, “which subsumes existing diffusion paths as specific instances” (Lipman et al. 2022), and a trained diffusion model also defines an ODE, the probability-flow ODE that DDIM and the solvers integrate (Song et al. 2021). So the choice is the path, the prediction target and how training weights the noise levels; straighter paths survive larger steps. On the topic’s tiny model, same network, data and training steps, flow with Euler scored FD 158.3 at 4 steps against DDIM’s 478.9 and beat every diffusion sampler from 1 to 50 steps, yet DDPM at 1,000 steps (34.9) still beat any unguided flow sampler measured: fewer steps, not a better end point. Cutting to 1 to 4 steps is distillation, owned by Latent diffusion and DiTs section 10 (reflow and one-step distillation of a flow: Flow matching sections 8 and 9).

5. Vision encoders: how a model sees

A Vision Transformer cuts the image into square patches, maps each to a vector with one learned matrix and adds a position vector: a 224 × 224 image in patches of 16 is 14 × 14 = 196 tokens derived, and from then on the image is a sequence like a sentence (ViT page). What the encoder learns depends on its training signal: labels, the pixels themselves (MAE, DINO), or captions. Caption training is what links images to words: CLIP scores every image against every caption in a batch with a softmax (Radford et al., Feb 2021); SigLIP scores each pair on its own with a sigmoid, so it needs no batch-wide sum and shards more cheaply (Zhai et al. 2023). The shared space gives zero-shot classification: write one sentence per class and pick the nearest. On the 10,000 Fashion-MNIST test images CLIP B/32 reaches 66.7% at best and SigLIP 2 74.5% (chance 10%); a linear probe on 16 labelled images per class reaches 79.0% and 84.0%; SigLIP 2 drops to about 22% if class names keep their capitals measured (Zero-shot lab). The embedding keeps what helps match captions and loses much else: a written word can override the object, and counting, left and right and attribute binding are close to guesses.

Most open VLMs follow LLaVA (Liu et al., Apr 2023): the projected patch vectors replace a placeholder in the prompt and are attended to like words; the alternative, cross-attention from a frozen language model to the image (Flamingo), keeps images out of the context. An image costs tokens: LLaVA-1.5’s CLIP ViT-L/14 at 336 pixels gives (336 / 14)2 = 576 derived; SmolVLM-500M gives 1,088 for any square image (16 tiles plus one whole view, 64 tokens each after a pixel shuffle) measured (VLM lab). More tokens read smaller text and cost context and time quadratically, which is why models tile, run at native resolution or merge neighbouring patches. Training aligns the connector first with the rest frozen, then tunes more of the model on conversations about images; the signature failure is object hallucination, naming what is not there because the language model’s habits outvote weak visual evidence.

8. Video and audio: the same four moves

Video is latent diffusion or flow with a time axis: a causal VAE compresses space and time, and a transformer denoises space-time patches (Sora is “trained on and subsequently generates videos within this compressed latent space”, OpenAI, Feb 2024, archived vendor; Movie Gen is a 30B-parameter transformer trained with flow matching, Polyak et al. 2024). Attention grows with the square of the patch count, so duration is the expensive axis, and the hard failures are temporal: identities that change and objects that vanish between frames. Audio takes two routes: a neural audio codec turns the waveform into parallel streams of discrete tokens that a language model predicts (AudioLM, MusicGen), or diffusion and flow generate a continuous latent or spectrogram (Stable Audio, flow-matching speech).

Depth: mistakes that cross pages, facts kept from the labs, two design questions

Mistakes that cross pages (one line each, with the page that shows it)
  • Counting steps instead of passes. Guidance doubles the passes per step and some solvers make two per step (Heun’s method, as in EDM): compare samplers and models by passes and by time on the same hardware, not by the step count in a config (section 10).
  • Decoding with the wrong latent scale. Every autoencoder has its own scaling factor and the pinned Stable Diffusion v1.5 vae/config.json has no scaling_factor entry, so diffusers falls back to its class default: Autoencoders and VAEs section 11.
  • Mixing up the two guidance-scale conventions, and a scheduler that does not match how the model was trained (prediction target, direction of time, schedule): Diffusion from zero sections 5, 6 and 8; for flows, Flow matching section 14.
  • Comparing FIDs computed at different sample counts or with different code, and reading CLIPScore as image quality: Evaluating generative models sections 3, 5 and 6.
  • Judging a VLM by its captions, and shrinking the image until its text is unreadable: Vision-language models sections 3 and 7.
Kept from the retired labs: facts no child page carries yet

The Latent, Diffusion, Flow and Vision labs were retired on 2026-10-24 because their children now teach the same mechanisms; these few facts are not yet on the owning child and stay here until it absorbs them.

  • Vision encoders. A convolutional network (CNN) slides small filters (3 × 3 pixels, say) over the image, so each layer only mixes neighbours and the same filter is used everywhere: that inductive bias makes it learn from little data and limits what one layer sees; a stack of L such layers (stride 1) sees a window of only 2L + 1 pixels derived. From scratch, a CNN’s inductive bias wins on small data; in practice you start from a pretrained encoder. Raw cosines do not compare across models: CLIP’s matching pairs sit around 0.3 and SigLIP 2’s around 0.1 to 0.2, so thresholds for retrieval or filtering must be calibrated per model. The CAT sticker raises CLIP’s cat score (0.2926 against 0.2846 plain) measured. Temperature: too high (small scale) leaves the loss near ln N with weak gradients, too low lets only the hardest negatives matter and destabilises training (CLIP clipped its scale at 100).
  • Vision-language models. Flamingo’s largest model builds on the 70B Chinchilla language model and is called Flamingo-80B; DeepMind’s announcement calls it “an 80B parameter VLM”.
  • Flow matching. Training the topic’s flow model took 893.3 s (4.48 steps/s) against the DDPM’s 906.7 s measured: the objective costs nothing extra. diffusers implements the resolution shift as sigmas = self.shift * sigmas / (1 + (self.shift - 1) * sigmas) (FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler, v0.41.0, line 351).
  • Diffusion from zero. DPM-Solver++ (2M) is the diffusers default for Stable Diffusion 1.5 (scheduler line 227, v0.41.0); it reuses the previous step’s prediction to take second-order steps at no extra network cost.
  • Autoencoders and VAEs. On the 128-code VQ-VAE’s token map a test image takes 28 to 43 distinct codes for its 64 positions, and the same blank corner gets the same few codes across images (117, 72, 26 recur) measured.
  • Evaluating generative models. FID is biased upwards at small counts even with nothing wrong. Two samples drawn from the same distribution still get a positive FID, and it shrinks as the count grows: 
    import numpy as np
from scipy import linalg

def fid(a, b):  # the Frechet distance FID uses, on any features
    m1, m2 = a.mean(0), b.mean(0)
    s1, s2 = np.cov(a, rowvar=False), np.cov(b, rowvar=False)
    covmean = linalg.sqrtm(s1 @ s2).real
    return float(((m1 - m2) ** 2).sum() + np.trace(s1 + s2 - 2 * covmean))

d = 64                                   # Inception features have 2,048 dimensions; 64 keeps this fast
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
A = rng.normal(size=(d, d)) / np.sqrt(d)
cov = A @ A.T + 0.1 * np.eye(d)          # one fixed "true" feature distribution
L = np.linalg.cholesky(cov)
draw = lambda n: rng.normal(size=(n, d)) @ L.T

for n in [500, 2000, 10000, 50000]:

    print(f"n = {n:>6}: FID between two samples of the SAME distribution = {np.mean(scores):.4f} (3 draws: {', '.join(f'{s:.4f}' for s in scores)})")

Design: a text-to-image system that returns an image in under 1 second.
Budget the passes: time one denoiser pass at the target resolution on the target GPU and multiply. 50 steps with guidance is 100 passes, so unless a pass takes under 10 ms it cannot meet 1 s, before the text encoder and decoder. Use a model distilled to 1 to 4 steps with guidance folded in (adversarial or consistency distillation), bf16 or fp8 weights, a compiled graph, the text encoder output cached for repeated prompts, and a fast decoder (or a tiny distilled decoder for previews). Generate at 512 to 768 and upscale if needed; batch requests per GPU; keep a few-step preview then refine path for quality. Measure the latency distribution, and check quality against the slow model with preference tests, because distillation loses variety.

Discrete tokens reuse the language-model stack, loss and serving (Chameleon, and, per its system card, GPT-4o image generation), but cost one pass per token and lose detail in the tokenizer. Diffusion or flow on continuous latents gives better images per FLOP and runs in tens of passes, but needs a second objective and sampler (Transfusion puts both in one transformer; others attach a separate diffusion decoder). Choose by what you must share (one context, editing in conversation), the latency budget and the quality bar.

In one screen
  • A vision-language model (VLM) reads images and text and writes text. Pixel shuffle, in plain words. The encoder gives one vector per 16-pixel patch, 1,024 per tile. Feeding all of them would cost 1,024 tokens per tile. Pixel shuffle (also called space-to-depth) takes each 4 × 4 block of neighbouring vectors and lays them side by side into one long vector: 16 × 768 = 12,288 numbers. The token count falls 16-fold to 64 while every number is kept; the projector then has to learn to read 16 positions out of one vector. The SmolVLM paper reports that “smaller VLMs benefit from more aggressive compression (r=4)” (Marafioti et al., April 2025, section 2.2) published. The code:

# transformers v5.20.1, models/idefics3/modeling_idefics3.py lines 398-407
def pixel_shuffle(self, x, scale_factor=2):
    bsz, seq, embed_dim = x.size()
    height = width = int(seq**0.5)
    x = x.view(bsz, height, width, embed_dim)
    x = x.view(bsz, height, int(width / scale_factor), embed_dim * scale_factor)
    x = x.permute(0, 2, 1, 3)
    x = x.reshape(bsz, int(width / scale_factor), int(height / scale_factor), embed_dim * (scale_factor**2))
    x = x.permute(0, 2, 1, 3)
    x = x.reshape(bsz, int(seq / (scale_factor**2)), embed_dim * (scale_factor**2))
    return x

Other merges do the same job: Qwen2-VL merges 2 × 2 neighbours with an MLP; Gemma 4 pools 3 × 3 (both as the Model map reads their configs). Flamingo’s resampler instead lets 64 learned queries attend to all the features.

3. How many tokens is an image?

The problem. More image tokens let a model see finer detail (small text, a chart’s labels), but each one is a token the language model must attend over, in context and in time. A fixed low resolution (LLaVA-1.5’s one 336-pixel view) misses detail; full resolution at one token per 16-pixel patch would make a 2,048-pixel square 16,384 tokens derived: (2,048 / 16)². Every VLM picks a point between the two.

How SmolVLM does it. Its image processor (transformers v5.20.1, models/idefics3/image_processing_idefics3.py) does three things: it scales the image so its long side equals size.longest_edge (2,048 by default, written N × 512 below with N = 4), keeping the aspect ratio (lines 58–92); it rounds both sides up to whole 512-pixel tiles by resizing, not padding (resize_for_vision_encoder, lines 319–348); and it cuts the tiles and appends the whole image shrunk to 512 as a global view (split_images, lines 286–317). Each view becomes 64 tokens. The processor then writes one placeholder per token into the prompt, with a tag naming each tile’s row and column (processing_idefics3.py lines 216–243).

Three things the counting showed measured
  • Input resolution does not change the count. A 64 × 64 icon and a 3,000 × 3,000 photo both become 17 views and 1,088 image tokens: small images are upscaled to 2,048 first. Only the aspect ratio and the N setting matter.
  • Shapes are stretched to whole tiles. A 1920 × 1080 photo becomes 2,048 × 1,152 and then 2,048 × 1,536 (3 × 4 tiles): height stretched by a third.
  • N above 6 breaks the tile tags. The vocabulary has <row_1_col_1> to <row_6_col_6> as single learned tokens; <row_7_col_1> is split into 9 ordinary text pieces (<, row, _, 7, ...). At N = 8 a square image takes 4,538 prompt tokens, of which 4,160 are image tokens and 378 are markers.

    Image tokens are not words. measured The projected image vectors have a median length (L2 norm) of 101 (10th to 90th percentile 83 to 158); the language model’s own word embeddings have a median length of 3.5.     7. Cost per image: time and memory
    

    Where the time goes. Answering a question about an image has three parts: the vision encoder and projector run once per view; prefill runs the language model over the whole prompt once (image tokens included) and fills its key-value cache; decode then produces one token at a time, each reading that cache. More image tokens make the first two dearer and each decoded token a little dearer (a longer cache to read).
    

    

    What the timing shows measured (median of three warm runs, on the cat photo). With the default 17 views, the encoder and projector take 1.61 s and the language model’s prefill of 1,142 tokens 0.44 s; each decoded token then takes 14 ms. With one view (82 prompt tokens) the same steps take 0.11 s and 0.07 s, and 13 ms per token. For short answers the vision encoder is the cost: it runs 17 tiles of 1,024 patches through 12 layers, about 17,400 patch positions against 1,142 language-model positions. Decoding barely changes with the prompt length at this size. Peak GPU memory 2,338 to 2,444 MiB (2.45 to 2.56 GB; the MPS driver’s allocation, sampled every 0.1 s), against the SmolVLM paper’s “1.2GB for the 500M” (section 4.3) published; the paper does not say how it measured, and the MPS driver figure counts everything allocated, so the two are not directly comparable.
    

    What transfers. The token counts, the answers (greedy decoding, bfloat16) and the shape of the trade-off transfer to any machine; the seconds do not. A datacenter GPU runs the encoder and prefill many times faster and batches many images; MPS on an M1 Pro is a laptop GPU sharing memory with the CPU, and these runs were at low priority on a machine shared with other jobs.
    

    


    

    8. Mistakes that ran here, and two design questions
    

    Assuming a small image costs few tokens
    SmolVLM upscales to its tile grid: a 64-pixel icon costs the same 1,088 image tokens as a 3,000-pixel photo (section 3). Set size={"longest_edge": N*512} or turn splitting off for small or simple images; measure with the processor, not from the file size.
    

    Writing the prompt by hand
    The placeholders, tile tags and wrappers must match what the model was trained on and the number of image vectors exactly. Use processor.apply_chat_template and then the processor on text and image together; never paste <image> yourself.
    

    Raising resolution past what the model was trained for
    More tiles are not always better: past 6 × 6 SmolVLM’s tile tags stop being single tokens (section 3), and any VLM degrades on token counts and layouts it never saw in training. Check the model card’s recommended settings and measure on your own questions.
    

    Judging a VLM by its captions
    Fluent descriptions hide errors: section 6’s long descriptions add details that are not there. Score with questions that have known answers, including absent objects (POPE-style) and traps, and report the yes rate on absent objects, not only accuracy.
    

    Treating image tokens like words
    Projected image vectors are far from every word embedding (section 4: norms about 30 times larger, nearest vocabulary entries meaningless). Tricks designed for text tokens (pruning by similarity to the vocabulary, reusing text-only quantisation statistics) need checking on image tokens separately.
    

    Forgetting the aspect ratio
    Processors that resize to whole tiles or to a square change the shape of the image (a 16:9 photo stretched by a third here; squashed to a square with splitting off). Thin text and circles suffer first. Pad to a tile-friendly shape yourself if geometry matters.
    

    Two design questions
    



    Your VLM cannot read small text in screenshots. What do you try?
    More visual tokens where the text is: higher resolution or tiling (more tiles), a smaller merge factor, or cropping to the region; check the processor is not downscaling first; check the aspect ratio is preserved. Then data: OCR-heavy instruction data. Measure the token and latency cost of each.
    


    Design: a VLM that answers questions about phone screenshots in under one second on a laptop
    Small language model (0.5–2B), SigLIP-class encoder, tiles sized to the screenshot’s aspect ratio, aggressive token merging (pixel shuffle) to keep the prompt near 1,000 tokens, prefix caching of the system prompt, short answers. Here SmolVLM-500M answered a short question about one image in about 2.1 s, 1.6 s of it in the vision encoder on an M1 Pro (section 7), so the budget is the image tokens: count them, then trade tiles for latency on a held-out question set.

    Reading a model licence in one minute
    

    "Open" covers four different things, and the difference decides whether you can ship. Open source in the strict sense means the weights, code and (ideally) data are under an OSI-approved licence such as Apache 2.0 or MIT. Open weights means you can download the weights but the licence may restrict use. Read four clauses:
    

      

    • Field of use: "non-commercial" or "research only" (FLUX.1 [dev], FLUX.2 [dev], Qwen-Image-2.1, Ideogram 4, MusicGen, F5-TTS, XTTS-v2).
      • 

    • Revenue or user thresholds: free until US$1M annual revenue (Stability AI Community License), US$5M (FLUX Kommunity), US$10M (LTX-2.x), or 700 million monthly users (Llama 4), or 1 million visits a month (CogVideoX).
      • 

    • Territory: the Tencent Hunyuan licence "does not apply in the European Union, United Kingdom and South Korea"; Llama 3.2 and Llama 4 withhold multimodal rights from EU-domiciled companies.
      • 

    • Use restrictions and naming: OpenRAIL licences list banned uses; DINOv3 bans military uses and asks for "Built with DINOv3".
      • 

    

    Same family, different licence
    Licences change between versions and sizes: Qwen-Image is Apache 2.0, Qwen-Image-2.1 is research-only; FLUX.2 [klein] 4B is Apache 2.0, the 9B is non-commercial; Gemma 3 used Google's own terms, Gemma 4 is Apache 2.0; DINOv2 is Apache 2.0, DINOv3 has its own licence. Check the licence of the exact checkpoint, not the family. This page summarises; it is not legal advice.

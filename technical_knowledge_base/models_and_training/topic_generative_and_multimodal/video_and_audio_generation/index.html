The idea in one screen

A video or audio generator is an image generator with a time axis, and almost every design choice exists to make that axis affordable: a causal video VAE folds 4 frames into one latent frame so that a 5 s clip becomes about 33,000 space-time tokens for a flow-matching transformer, and a neural codec turns each second of sound into 100 to 600 discrete tokens for a language model, or into a short continuous latent for flow.

Same four moves for both media: compress, cut into tokens, generate, decode

Causal video VAE
a VAE with 3-D convolutions that only look backwards in time; frame 1 alone, then each group of 4 (or 8) frames becomes one latent frame.
Space-time patch
one token: a small block of the latent across space and time (1 × 2 × 2 latent cells in Wan).
Full against factorised attention
every token attends to every token in the clip, against attention within a frame followed by attention across frames at one position.
Exposure bias, drift
a model trained on real frames conditions on its own imperfect ones when extending a video, and errors pile up.
Mel spectrogram
sound as an image: log power in frequency bands spaced like human hearing, one column every 10 to 20 ms.
Vocoder
a network that turns a spectrogram back into a waveform (it must invent the phase).
Neural codec, RVQ
an autoencoder for sound whose bottleneck is a chain of small codebooks, each quantising what the previous ones missed (residual vector quantisation).
Quantiser dropout
training with a random number of codebooks, so any prefix of the chain decodes (one model, many bitrates).
Semantic tokens
codes that carry what is said rather than how it sounds (Mimi distils a speech model into its first codebook).
Delay pattern
codebook k shifted k − 1 steps, so one transformer emits every codebook of a frame in order, one step per frame.

1. Video as space-time latents

The problem. Wan 2.1's default clip (480 × 832 pixels, 81 frames, about 5 s at 16 frames per second) is 97 million numbers, and neighbouring frames are almost the same picture. An image VAE applied to every frame (8 × 8 smaller in space) still leaves 81 latent frames; with 2 × 2 patches that is 81 × 30 × 52 = 126,360 tokens, most of them repeating the frame before. Attention cost grows with the square of the token count, so redundancy across time is the first thing to remove.

A causal video VAE. The image VAE (Autoencoders and VAEs) with 3-D convolutions, so it also shrinks the time axis: every open model on this page compresses time by 4 (or 6, or 8) as well as space by 8 (or 16, or 32). Two details recur:

  • Causal in time. The encoder's convolutions only look at the current and earlier frames (all the padding goes at the start of the time axis, CogVideoX, Sec 2.1), so the latent of frame group t never depends on later frames. One model then encodes an image (a 1-frame video), and a decoder can stream.
  • The first frame alone. Wan's VAE encodes frame 1 on its own and then each further group of 4 frames into one latent frame, carrying a cache of features from earlier groups. So a clip must have 4k + 1 frames, and 81 frames become 1 + 80 / 4 = 21 latent frames. Wan's paper: "the first frame is only spatially compressed to better handle the image data" (Wan 2025, Sec 4.1).

Then patches. The denoiser cuts the latent into space-time patches: Wan, HunyuanVideo and Movie Gen use 1 × 2 × 2 (one latent frame, 2 × 2 latent pixels), so each token stands for 4 × 16 × 16 = 1,024 pixels across 4 frames. Sora's report: "spacetime patches which act as transformer tokens. This scheme works for images too since images are just videos with a single frame" (OpenAI, Feb 2024, archived) vendor. With time compression ct, space compression cs and patch size p, a clip of F frames at H × W becomes


tokens = (1 + (F − 1) / ct) × H / (cs p) × W / (cs p)


For Wan 2.1's default: 21 × 30 × 52 = 32,760, about a quarter of the per-frame count. The pipeline computes the first factor the same way (num_latent_frames = (num_frames - 1) // self.vae_scale_factor_temporal + 1, pipeline_wan.py:341).

2. Attention across space and time


The problem. Each token must see the rest of its frame (to draw a coherent picture) and the same object in other frames (to move smoothly). Full 3-D attention lets every token attend to every other token in the clip, at a cost that grows with the square of the token count N: for Wan 2.1 1.3B's default clip, N = 32,760, so one layer forms N2 = 1.07 billion query-key pairs per head.


The older answer: factorise. Factorised attention splits one layer into spatial attention (within its own latent frame) followed by temporal attention (the same position in the other frames). Video Diffusion Models did this inside a U-Net: "we insert a temporal attention block that performs attention over the first axis" with the spatial axes treated as batch (Ho et al. 2022); Latte compared four ways of splitting the two inside a video DiT, and alternating spatial and temporal blocks "performs the best" (a plot, not a table: Ma et al. 2024, Fig 5). With f latent frames of s tokens, the pairs fall from (fs)2 to fs2 + sf2: about 21 times fewer for Wan's default clip.


The current answer: full attention. Every open model from 2024 on uses it (CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo, Mochi, LTX-Video, Wan, Movie Gen). CogVideoX's argument: separated attention "requires extensive implicit transmission of visual information" (Yang et al. 2024, Sec 2.2): an object moving diagonally is at a different position in the next frame, so a temporal layer that looks only at the same position cannot follow it directly. Full attention became affordable because the VAE took most of the tokens out (section 1) and FlashAttention-style kernels never store the N2 matrix; the price is compute, and section 4 shows attention becomes the largest term.

4. What one clip costs


The arithmetic. One denoiser pass over N video tokens costs about


FLOPs per pass ≈ 2 P N + 4 L N2 D


where P is the parameter count (every weight used once per token: one multiply and one add), L the number of blocks and D the attention width (query-key scores plus the weighted sum of values, each N2D multiply-adds per layer). A clip costs that times the sampling steps times the passes per step (2 with classifier-free guidance). The first term grows with the clip's length, the second with its square. The image version, fitted to eleven image denoisers, is on Latent diffusion and diffusion transformers (section 9).

5. Keeping a video consistent, and making it longer


The problem. Inside one clip, full attention keeps things consistent: every frame sees every other. The failures are temporal: identity drift (a face or a shirt slowly changing), objects that appear or vanish, physics that does not hold. Sora's own report: it "does not accurately model the physics of many basic interactions, like glass shattering", and shows "incoherencies that develop in long duration samples or spontaneous appearances of objects" (OpenAI, Feb 2024, archived) vendor. Independent physics tests agree: on Physics-IQ "the best model scoring 29.5%" where real videos score 100 (Motamed et al. 2025) independent; on VideoPhy the best model, CogVideoX-5B, follows both the caption and physical laws in 39.6% of cases (Bansal et al. 2024) independent.


Why length is hard. Models train on clips of 5 to 16 s because tokens, and attention with their square, grow with duration (section 4). A longer video needs a bigger window (Movie Gen trained to 16 s; LTX-2 reaches 20 s with audio) or generation in pieces, each conditioned on the frames before. That works like a language model writing text, with the same weakness: the model trained on clean real frames now conditions on its own imperfect ones, errors accumulate, and quality drifts (exposure bias; FramePack calls the result "drifting").

6. Sound as data: waveforms and spectrograms


The problem. Audio is a waveform: air pressure measured tens of thousands of times a second (the sample rate: 16 kHz for speech recognition, 24 kHz for most speech generation, 44.1 or 48 kHz for music). The Shaggy sentence is 3.67 s, so 58,720 samples at 16 kHz or 88,080 at 24 kHz; ten seconds of speech is 240,000 numbers, against a few dozen text tokens for the same words. Nothing can attend over that directly.


The spectrogram. Cut the waveform into short overlapping windows, take each window's Fourier transform, keep the magnitude, pool the frequencies into bands spaced like human hearing (the mel scale: fine at low frequencies, coarse at high), and take the log. Whisper: "an 80-channel log-magnitude Mel spectrogram representation is computed on 25-millisecond windows with a stride of 10 milliseconds" (Radford et al. 2022, Sec 2.2); large-v3 uses 128 bins. The result is an image, 80 numbers per 10 ms, about 30 times shorter than the 16 kHz waveform.


The same 3.67 s as 88,080 samples and as an image of frequency against time







Top: the waveform at 24 kHz drawn as its minimum and maximum in every 10 ms. Below: 64 mel bands from 0 to 12 kHz (bottom to top) at 20 ms steps, brightness = log power over 70 dB, computed on this page's data (codec/make_assets.py). The source is a 16 kHz recording, so it holds nothing above 8 kHz (the top eighth of the bands holds only faint energy leaked by the resampler, 37 dB below the total).




The catch: phase. A magnitude spectrogram throws away each frequency's phase, so it cannot be played back directly. A vocoder turns a spectrogram back into a waveform; HiFi-GAN "uses a mel-spectrogram as input and upsamples it through transposed convolutions until the length of the output sequence matches the temporal resolution of raw waveforms", reaching MOS 4.36 against 4.45 for the real recordings, "167.9 times faster than real-time on a single V100 GPU" (Kong et al. 2020) vendor. Because phase is free, a vocoder's output can sound right while matching the original waveform poorly sample by sample, which is why waveform error (SNR) is a poor quality measure for generated audio (section 7).


From spectrograms to today's two representations. Text-to-speech from 2017 to 2022 predicted a mel spectrogram and called a vocoder (Tacotron 2: 80 mel channels, 12.5 ms hop, WaveNet vocoder). Today two representations dominate: discrete codec tokens (section 7), which turn audio into a language-model problem, and continuous latents from an audio VAE (Stable Audio Open: 64 numbers per frame at 21.5 frames a second for 44.1 kHz stereo, Evans et al. 2024), which turn it into a diffusion problem, exactly like an image VAE.






7. Neural audio codecs and residual vector quantisation


What a neural codec is. An autoencoder for sound with a discrete bottleneck. A convolutional encoder strides through the waveform (EnCodec: 320 samples per step, so 24,000 / 320 = 75 frames a second, each a 128-number vector); a quantiser replaces each vector with codebook indices; a decoder turns the indices back into a waveform. It is trained with reconstruction losses on waveform and mel spectrograms plus adversarial losses from discriminators that look at the spectrogram at several scales, which is what makes it sound natural rather than merely close (SoundStream 2021, EnCodec 2022, DAC 2023, Mimi 2024).


Why residual quantisation. One codebook of 1,024 gives each frame log2(1,024) = 10 bits; spending 80 bits a frame (6 kbps) with one codebook would need 280 entries. Residual vector quantisation (RVQ) chains several small codebooks: the first quantises the vector, the second quantises what the first missed, and so on, so 8 codebooks of 1,024 give 80 bits with 8,192 stored vectors. Bits per codebook per second in EnCodec's code: bw_per_q = math.log2(self.codebook_size) * self.frame_rate = 10 × 75 = 750 bit/s, so 1.5 kbps is 2 codebooks and 24 kbps is 32. The image version (RQ-VAE) and VQ training tricks are on Autoencoders and VAEs.

Bitrate scalability for free. During training the number of codebooks is drawn at random per example (quantiser dropout, introduced with SoundStream), so the decoder learns to work from any prefix of the chain. That is why one model serves 1.5 to 24 kbps, and why a token generator can predict the first codebooks first: they carry the most.

8. Generating audio: token language models and flow


Route 1: a language model over codec tokens. Once audio is tokens, generating it is next-token prediction, and everything known about language models applies (prompts, sampling temperature, KV caches, streaming). The difficulty is that each frame has several codebooks, so the question is in what order to predict them.


    

  • AudioLM (Google, 2022) used a hierarchy: first semantic tokens (k-means clusters of a speech model's features at 25 a second) for long-range structure, then the first 4 SoundStream codebooks, then the remaining 8, each by its own 0.3B decoder; listeners told its continuations from real speech 51.2% of the time, "not statistically significantly different" from chance (Borsos et al. 2022) vendor.
    • 

  • VALL-E (Microsoft, 2023) made text-to-speech a language-model task: phonemes plus a "3-second enrolled recording" of a voice in, EnCodec tokens out (8 codebooks at 6 kbps). The first codebook is predicted autoregressively, codebooks 2 to 8 by a second, non-autoregressive model, all trained on 60K hours of audiobooks (Wang et al. 2023).
    • 

  • MusicGen (Meta, 2023) predicts all 4 codebooks with one transformer using a delay pattern: codebook k is shifted k − 1 steps, so each step emits one token per codebook and still sees the coarser codebooks of the same frame from earlier steps. "This translates 30 seconds of audio into 1500 autoregressive steps" (50 frames a second; Copet et al. 2023); trained on 20K hours of licensed music; weights CC-BY-NC.
    • 



Three orders for 4 codebooks: the delay pattern keeps one step per frame and still predicts coarse before fine










Moshi (Kyutai, 2024) is full duplex: it "always listens and always generates sound", modelling its own Mimi tokens and the user's as parallel streams plus a text stream it writes as an "inner monologue", 17 tokens per 80 ms step (1 text + 8 + 8). A 7B temporal transformer runs once per step and a small depth transformer generates the codebooks within the step; "a theoretical latency of 160ms, 200ms in practice" (Défossez et al. 2024) vendor. Mimi's 12.5 frames a second are what make one big forward pass per 80 ms affordable.

Route 2: flow or diffusion over continuous audio. The image recipe, applied to a spectrogram or an audio latent. F5-TTS trains a DiT with flow matching to fill in masked mel frames; the text is "simply padded with filler tokens to the same length as input speech", with no duration model or aligner; 336M parameters, about 95K hours of speech, "an inference RTF of 0.15" (real-time factor: 10 s of speech in 1.5 s, on an RTX 3090 at 16 steps) (Chen et al. 2024) vendor. Stable Audio Open generates "variable-length (up to 47s) stereo audio at 44.1kHz" with a 156M VAE, a 109M T5 text encoder and a 1,057M DiT, trained on Creative Commons recordings; it cannot produce intelligible speech or lyrics (Evans et al. 2024).


F5-TTS needs no aligner: text padded to the length of the audio, the missing frames filled by flow




Training: a random span of the mel frames is masked and the model learns the flow that fills it, seeing the rest of the audio and the whole padded text. Inference: the prompt recording is the unmasked part, its transcript plus the new text is padded with filler tokens, and the total length must be chosen before sampling (the "fixed or estimated length" weakness in the table below). Common mistakes, with the real errors (9 items)


A frame count that is not 4k + 1 (or 8k + 1)
A causal video VAE needs the first frame plus whole groups. diffusers does not fail, it changes your request: . Ask for 80 frames from Wan and you get 81; from LTX-Video (8 per group) the rule is 8k + 1. Check the frame count of the output, and pick 4k + 1 yourself.


A size the latent grid cannot divide
Height and width must be multiples of the VAE factor times the patch:  (Wan, 8 × 2), and 32 for LTX-Video. 720p is 1280 × 720 for Wan 2.1 (720 / 16 = 45) but 1280 × 704 for Wan 2.2's 5B model, whose VAE plus patch divides by 32.


Feeding audio at the wrong sample rate
Measured: the 16 kHz LibriSpeech samples passed straight to EnCodec 24 kHz encode and decode without error, and come out as  s instead of  s, 1.5 times faster and higher: . The feature extractor would have stopped it: . Resample first (this page uses scipy.signal.resample_poly), and assert the rate wherever audio enters a model.


Asking a codec for a bitrate or depth it does not have
Real errors from this page's run:  and . Bitrate is codebooks × frames per second × bits per code, so only a few values exist per model.


Judging a codec or vocoder by waveform error
Measured: EnCodec at 24 kbps has a lower SNR than Opus at 12 kbps on the same sentence but a closer spectrogram, and its own listening test ranks it far above (section 7). Adversarially trained decoders do not reproduce phase. Use mel distance for quick checks, MUSHRA or MOS for decisions.


Counting cost in frames or parameters instead of tokens
Wan 2.2's 5B model makes 720p clips with fewer tokens (27,280 for 121 frames) than Wan 2.1's 1.3B makes at 480p and 81 frames (32,760), because its VAE plus patch is 32 × 32 in space. And attention is about two thirds of the compute at these sizes, so doubling the duration roughly quadruples it. Count tokens with the formula in section 1, then FLOPs (section 4).


Trusting a headline compression ratio
Mochi's card says its VAE "causally compresses videos to a 128x smaller size, with an 8x8 spatial and a 6x temporal compression to a 12-channel latent space"; those factors give 3 × 8 × 8 × 6 / 12 = 96, not 128 (the card does not show its arithmetic). LTX-Video's "1:192" and Wan 2.2's "64" do reproduce (section 1, closed table). Recompute from the factors.


Comparing FVD across settings, or trusting it alone
FVD depends on frame count, resolution, the feature network and the number of samples, and it rewards good single frames over motion: a frozen video can score well (section 9). Compare only at identical settings, add a motion measure (VBench's dynamic degree), and run a human or arena comparison for decisions.


Building on a closed model as if it will stay
Sora's app closed in April 2026 and its API was scheduled to end in September 2026 (secondary reports; the root's Model map tracks status). Keep an open fallback and your own evaluation set, so switching is a measured decision.

Interview rehearsal (10 questions with answers)


Why compress time in the VAE rather than just using bigger patches in the transformer?
Both cut tokens, but the VAE is trained to reconstruct, so it learns what to keep (a moving edge) and what to drop (a static background repeated over 4 frames); a bigger patch is a fixed linear projection that loses detail uniformly. Latte found tubelet-style patches "notably" worse than per-frame patches; LTX-Video moved patching into the VAE for the same reason.


Full 3-D attention or factorised spatial and temporal attention?
Factorised is about 20 times cheaper at Wan's default size but a token can only see its own frame or its own position over time, so diagonal motion must be relayed through layers. Full attention sees everything and is what every 2024 to 2026 open model uses; it is affordable because the VAE leaves tens of thousands of tokens, not hundreds of thousands, and FlashAttention avoids storing N2. Above that, sparse or sliding-tile attention (HunyuanVideo 1.5) is the middle ground.


Why is the first frame encoded on its own?
So that an image is a valid 1-frame video (joint image and video training, and image-to-video conditioning on a clean first latent), and so the encoder can be causal: each later group of 4 frames is encoded with a cache of features from earlier frames only. Hence 4k + 1 frames.


How many tokens is 10 s of 720p video for Wan 2.1 14B, and what does it do to attention?
161 frames at 16 fps become 1 + 160 / 4 = 41 latent frames of 90 × 160, patched 2 × 2: 41 × 45 × 80 = 147,600 tokens, against 75,600 for 5 s. Tokens about double, attention FLOPs about quadruple (3.8 times).


Why residual VQ rather than one big codebook?
Bits add up across stages while storage adds linearly: 8 codebooks of 1,024 give 80 bits per frame with 8,192 vectors, where one codebook would need 280. Quantiser dropout makes every prefix decodable, so one model gives many bitrates and a generator can predict coarse codebooks first.


Codec language model or flow matching for text-to-speech?
Tokens and a language model when you need streaming, voice cloning by prompt, or one model shared with text (dialogue agents, any-to-any). Flow or diffusion over mel or latents when you can generate the whole utterance at once and want speed and robustness (F5-TTS: RTF 0.15, no aligner). The deciding factors are usually latency, length control and how the model is served.


Why can a codec with worse SNR sound better?
SNR compares waveforms sample by sample, so any phase shift counts as error; hearing is mostly insensitive to phase. Neural codecs are trained with spectral and adversarial losses that reward sounding right, so their waveforms differ while their spectrograms match.


Design: a text-to-video API must return 5 s of 720p in under a minute on one datacenter GPU. Sketch the model and the arithmetic.
Work backwards from tokens. At Wan 2.1 14B's design (8 × 8 × 4 VAE, 2 × 2 patches) 5 s at 720p is 75,600 tokens and about 6,800 TFLOP per pass, so 50 steps with guidance is about 680,000 TFLOP: far beyond a minute on any single GPU. Levers in order of size: a stronger VAE (32 × 32 in space, as Wan 2.2 5B or LTX: 4 to 10 times fewer tokens, quadratic gain in attention); a smaller denoiser (2 to 5B); step and guidance distillation (50 × 2 passes down to 4 to 8 single passes); sparse attention; generate at 480p and upsample. Verify with the calculator, then measure: a published check is LTX-Video's 2 s for 121 frames at 768 × 512 on an H100 with a 1.9B model and 1:8192 compression.


Design: a real-time voice agent with sub-300 ms response. What audio representation and model?
A causal codec with a low frame rate and semantic first codebook (Mimi: 12.5 Hz, 80 ms frames, 8 codebooks), a model that runs once per frame and generates the codebooks within the frame with a small depth model, full duplex so it can listen while speaking, and text tokens predicted alongside audio for content. Moshi's budget: 80 ms frame plus 80 ms acoustic delay = 160 ms theoretical, 200 ms measured on an L4. A cascade (ASR, LLM, TTS) is simpler to build but adds each stage's latency and loses tone.


How would you get a 1-minute video from a model trained on 5 s clips?
Extend in chunks conditioned on previous frames, and fight drift: train with per-frame noise levels (Diffusion Forcing) or on your own rollouts (Self Forcing), compress old context (FramePack), keep identity anchors (a reference image or keyframes generated first and filled in between). Check with a per-frame identity score over time.

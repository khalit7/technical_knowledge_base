4. What one clip costs

The arithmetic. One denoiser pass over N video tokens costs about

FLOPs per pass ≈ 2 P N + 4 L N2 D

where P is the parameter count (every weight is used once per token: one multiply and one add), L the number of blocks and D the attention width (the second term is the query-key scores plus the weighted sum of values, each N2D multiply-adds per layer). A clip costs that times the sampling steps times the passes per step (2 with classifier-free guidance). The first term grows with the clip's length; the second with its square. The image version of this formula, fitted to eleven image denoisers, is on the sibling page {{Latent diffusion and diffusion transformers|n:3fc5c17b0d0d814fbcb5e0c8df5289f3}} (section 9).

Where else the time goes. The text encoder runs once, and the VAE decoder once per clip, but a video decoder is heavy: in the measured run above, decoding 17 small frames took longer than encoding them. The published speed-ups attack each factor: fewer steps by distillation (CausVid distils 50 steps to 4; HunyuanVideo 1.5 ships 8 or 12 step variants), fewer tokens by a stronger VAE (LTX-Video, Wan 2.2's 5B model), cheaper attention by sparsity (HunyuanVideo 1.5's SSTA, 1.87 times end to end, vendor), and lower resolution plus a separate upsampler (Movie Gen to 1080p, HunyuanVideo 1.5's few-step super-resolution).

5. Keeping a video consistent, and making it longer

The problem. Inside one generated clip, full attention keeps things consistent: every frame sees every other. The failures are temporal: identity drift (a face or a shirt slowly changing), objects that appear or vanish, physics that does not hold. Sora's own report lists them: it "does not accurately model the physics of many basic interactions, like glass shattering", and shows "incoherencies that develop in long duration samples or spontaneous appearances of objects" ({{OpenAI, Feb 2024, archived|https://web.archive.org/web/20250105124831/https://openai.com/index/video-generation-models-as-world-simulators/}}) vendor. Independent physics tests agree: on Physics-IQ "the best model scoring 29.5%" where real videos score 100 ({{Motamed et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09038}}) independent; on VideoPhy the best model, CogVideoX-5B, follows both the caption and physical laws in 39.6% of cases ({{Bansal et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03520}}) independent.

Why length is hard. Models train on clips of 5 to 16 s because tokens, and attention with their square, grow with duration (section 4). A longer video needs either a bigger window (Movie Gen trained to 16 s; LTX-2 reaches 20 s with audio) or generation in pieces. Generating in pieces, each conditioned on the frames before, works like a language model writing text, with the same weakness: the model trained on clean real frames now conditions on its own imperfect ones, errors accumulate, and quality drifts. That train-test mismatch is called exposure bias; FramePack calls the result "drifting".

Three ways to schedule the noise over a long video

Each row is one denoising step, each column one chunk of frames; darker means noisier. Illustration of the published schemes (no run here).

  • Diffusion Forcing gives every frame its own noise level during training, so at sampling time later frames can be noisier than earlier ones; it rolls out "with lengths past the training horizon, where baselines diverge" ({{Chen et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01392}}). MAGI-1 generates "in chunks of 24 frames, where each chunk attends to all previously denoised chunks", denoising several chunks at once at rising noise levels ({{Sand AI 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13211}}).
  • Causal students. CausVid distils a bidirectional model into a few-step causal one that streams with a KV cache: "an initial latency of only 1.3 seconds, after which frames are generated continuously ... at approximately 9.4 FPS" ({{Yin et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.07772}}) vendor. Self Forcing trains on its own rollouts to close the train-test gap: "real-time video generation at 17 FPS with sub-second latency on a single H100 GPU"; in its Table 1 the many-step Wan2.1 1.3B it starts from runs at 0.78 FPS with 103 s latency ({{Huang et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08009}}) vendor.
  • Compressed context. FramePack encodes older frames with fewer tokens so the context stays bounded however long the video, and samples in an order that limits drift ({{Zhang and Agrawala 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.12626}}).

How to check. Generate past the training length and plot a per-frame quality score and an identity score (similarity of a face or object embedding to frame 1) against time: drift shows as a slope.

2. What a perfect model would do in few steps

The problem. Plain flow matching needs many steps: on the toy one Euler step puts no points on a blob, and on the images one step gives a blurry class average (FD against at 50 steps, the root's numbers). Is that a training failure, or would even a perfectly trained model do it?

How to tell. Sample the exact field with the same solver and step counts as the trained network. Whatever the exact field gets wrong is the path's fault (discretisation error); the extra error of the network is learning error.

What it shows. One Euler step of the exact field lands every point on the ring's centre (mean distance from the centre 0.0): at t = 0 the field is "the average image minus where you are", so x0 + u(x0, 0) is the average image for every x0. At 2 steps the exact field puts on a blob and the trained network ; at 4 steps against . Most of the few-step error is the field's own. The exact paths are as bent as the learned ones: straightness (straight-line distance over path length, 1 = straight) for the exact field, for the network. After one reflow round (section 6) the network is no longer learning this field at all, which is why it can beat the "perfect" model at 1 step.

Predict: you train the same flow-matching model for 10 times longer. At 2 Euler steps, how close can it get to 90% on a blob?
Training longer moves the network towards the exact field, and the exact field itself scores at 2 steps. To do better at 2 steps you must change what is being learned (the pairing: sections 5 and 6), the solver or the step placement (sections 4 and 10), or distil (section 7).

When it matters. Every time someone proposes "train longer" or "a bigger network" to fix few-step quality. How to check on your own problem: a toy with a closed-form field (a Gaussian mixture), or, on real data, compare N-step samples with a many-step solve from the same noise; if the network's many-step samples are good and its few-step ones are blurry, the problem is the path, not the fit.

3. The perfect model only copies its training set

The problem. Section 1 said a perfectly trained network outputs the exact marginal field. For real training data the "data distribution" the loss sees is the training set itself: 55,000 Fashion-MNIST images, each a single point. Its exact field also has a closed form (the blobs have zero width): xt given image j is Gaussian around t · imagej with spread 1 − t, the weights are a softmax over images, and u(x, t) = (E[x1 | x] − x) / (1 − t). Where does that field take noise?

Measured. src/runs/fm_memo.py samples this exact field (class-conditional: the 5,500 training images of the requested class) from the root's noises, and samples the root's trained model from the same noises, then finds each sample's nearest training image (root-mean-square pixel difference on the 28 × 28 crop, pixels in [−1, 1]). The network did not reach the optimum of its own loss, and that failure is what lets it generate new garments.

Published. The same fact for diffusion: denoising score matching "has a closed-form optimal solution that can only generate training data replicating samples" (Gu et al., arXiv 2310.02664, Oct 2023), who also find memorisation "tend[s] to occur on smaller-sized datasets". Kamb and Ganguli (arXiv 2412.20292, Dec 2024) explain where the novelty comes from: convolutional networks are local and translation-equivariant, so they cannot implement the optimal (global) answer and instead stitch "local training set patches"; their patch-mosaic model predicts trained models' outputs with median r² 0.94 on Fashion-MNIST published.

When it matters. Memorisation and privacy (a model that fits its small fine-tuning set too well reproduces it), evaluation (a memorising model scores a superb FID: FID cannot see copying; the {{Topic: generative-and-multimodal|n:3c65c17b0d0d817ab6ade318917bff55}} Reading tab and the sibling page {{Evaluating generative models|n:3fc5c17b0d0d81c890f4f62543312faa}} cover metrics) and theory (a "better optimiser" is not always better). How to check: nearest-training-neighbour distances of generated samples against those of held-out real images, as above.

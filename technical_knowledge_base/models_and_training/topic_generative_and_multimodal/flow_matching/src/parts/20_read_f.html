8. Which t to train on: timestep sampling and the resolution shift

The problem. Each training step draws one t per image. Uniform t spends equal effort on every noise level, but section 1 showed the levels are not equally useful: near pure noise the target is mostly unpredictable, near clean data the input almost gives the answer away, and the middle is where the network has the most to learn. Which distribution of t gives the best model for a fixed budget?

How it works. Draw u uniform in [0, 1] and map it. SD3 (Esser et al., arXiv 2403.03206, section 3.1) compared, in its own convention (t = 0 data, t = 1 noise; this page flips them): logit-normal lognorm(m, s), the logistic of a normal with mean m and scale s (m > 0 leans towards noise); mode sampling fmode(u; s) = 1 − u − s (cos²(πu/2) − 1 + u), which keeps some weight at the ends; and CosMap t = 1 − 1/(tan(πu/2) + 1). Densities are drawn in the {{Timestep explorer|#t-fmts}} tab.

Published. SD3 trained 61 variants on ImageNet and CC12M; ranked over two datasets, EMA and plain weights and several step counts, "rf/lognorm(0.00, 1.00) consistently achieves a good rank": first overall (rank 1.54), with plain uniform rectified flow at 5.67 and the classic eps/linear diffusion at 2.88 (Table 1). At 25 steps on ImageNet, FID 45.78 for lognorm(0, 1) against 49.70 for uniform (Table 2), while lognorm(0.5, 0.6), which leans hard towards noise, had the best CLIP score (0.256) and a much worse FID (80.41): a scheme can buy prompt adherence at the cost of image statistics published. Logit-normal(0, 1) then became the default: Movie Gen (arXiv 2410.13720: "zero mean and unit standard deviation"), HunyuanVideo (2412.03603) and Wan (2503.20314) all state logit-normal t in their training sections published.

Measured on the toy. Six schemes, three seeds each, at a short budget (2,000 steps) and the root's full budget (12,000), scored against the exact field (learning error by t) and by samples:

What it shows. The distribution of t moves the network's accuracy to where t is drawn: at 12,000 steps logit-normal(0, 1) cuts the mid-path error ( to ) and multiplies the error near pure noise ( to ), because it almost never trains there. An honest summary: on a 2-D toy the choice is nearly free; its published value comes at image scale, where the middle of the path carries most of the perceptual work and training budgets are never "converged".

The resolution shift. A bigger image has more pixels to average over, so the same noise level destroys less of it: at a given t a 1024-pixel image is "less noisy" than a 256-pixel one. SD3 shifts the time to compensate, tm = √(m/n) tn / (1 + (√(m/n) − 1) tn) on the noise level (section 5.3.2, eq. 23), and uses "a shift value of α = 3.0 both during training and sampling at resolution 1024×1024" published. diffusers implements two forms (v0.41.0, schedulers/scheduling_flow_match_euler_discrete.py): a fixed shift (line 351) and, when use_dynamic_shifting is set, math.exp(mu) / (math.exp(mu) + (1 / t - 1) ** sigma) (lines 646 to 647) with μ growing linearly with the number of image tokens (calculate_shift, pipelines/flux/pipeline_flux.py lines 73 to 83). With sigma = 1 the two are the same formula with shift = eμ (divide top and bottom by t) derived: FLUX's defaults (μ from 0.5 at 256 tokens to 1.15 at 4,096) mean shift 1.65 at 512 × 512 and 3.16 at 1024 × 1024, next to SD3's 3.0. The explorer tab computes it for any resolution; the root's Flow lab showed what a shift does at 4 steps on the tiny model.

When it matters: training from scratch (pick logit-normal(0, 1) unless you have a reason), fine-tuning at a new resolution (the shift should change with it), and sampling with few steps (HunyuanVideo: "we set a larger shifting factor s for a lower inference step"). How to check: per-t validation loss against a reference run, and samples at your production step count.

9. Adding noise back: stochastic sampling with the same marginals

The problem. The flow ODE is deterministic. Diffusion's DDPM sampler adds fresh noise every step, and the root's Diffusion lab found DDPM beating DDIM on the tiny model from 4 steps up. Can a flow model sample stochastically too, and should it?

How it works. From a velocity you also get the score (the gradient of the log density, which points towards more likely images): on the straight path E[x0 | x] = x − t v, so score = −(x − t v)/(1 − t) derived. Then for any noise level ε ≥ 0 the stochastic differential equation dx = (v + ε/2 · score) dt + √ε dW has the same distribution at every t as the ODE (the extra drift exactly cancels the spreading the noise causes). Albergo, Boffi and Vanden-Eijnden's "Stochastic Interpolants" (arXiv 2303.08797, Mar 2023) build flows and diffusions as one family "with an adjustable level of noise" published; the ODE is ε = 0. What it shows. With a perfect field, noise only costs steps: every ε reaches the data with enough steps, and more noise needs more steps. Noise helps when the field is imperfect: each injection lets the sampler forget part of the error it accumulated. SiT measured exactly that on ImageNet: SDE sampling beats the ODE for every path (FID 39.8 to 37.8 on VP, 34.8 to 33.6 on the straight path, 34.6 to 32.9 on GVP; Table 5, with many steps) published, and their best SiT-XL result (2.06) uses an SDE sampler.

1. Training: regress the straight-line velocity

The problem. Noise is easy to sample; data is not. Flow matching looks for a velocity field v(x, t) that, followed for one unit of time, carries the whole cloud of noise onto the cloud of data. Sampling is then solving an ordinary differential equation (ODE): start at x(0) drawn from a standard Gaussian and move with dx/dt = vθ(x, t) until t = 1. Nothing is random after the start, so the same noise always gives the same output. Such a model is a continuous normalising flow; what 2022 added is a cheap way to train one.

One training example, in numbers (illustrative). Draw a noise x0 = (0.5, −1.2), a data point x1 = (3, 0) (a blob centre of the toy) and t = 0.3. The point on their straight line is xt = 0.7 x0 + 0.3 x1 = (1.25, −0.84), and the line's velocity, the same at every t, is x1 − x0 = (2.5, 1.2). A network that answers (2.0, 1.0) at that input pays (0.5² + 0.2²) = 0.29. That is the whole training step (panel 1 of the figure above):

xt = (1 − t) x0 + t x1,   loss = ‖ vθ(xt, t) − (x1 − x0) ‖²,   t uniform in [0, 1]
x0
Gaussian noise, the same shape as a data point
x1
a training example (an image scaled to [−1, 1])
x1 − x0
the target: the constant velocity of the straight path between them

Liu, Gong and Liu (arXiv 2209.03003) call it rectified flow; Lipman et al. (arXiv 2210.02747) the conditional optimal-transport path; both 2022 published. It is simulation-free: older continuous flows solved the ODE and backpropagated through it at every training step, while here xt is built in closed form, so a step costs one network call and one squared error, exactly like a DDPM step. 2. What the network learns: the marginal field


The problem. The same point x at the same t lies on many lines, from different noises to different images (panel 2 of the figure). The network sees x and t only, so it cannot know which line it is on. A network trained with a squared error predicts the average of its targets given its inputs, so it learns the marginal vector field (averaged over all pairs, as opposed to the conditional field of one pair):


u(x, t) = E[ x1 − x0 | xt = x ] = Σpairs (posterior weight of the pair at x) × (its velocity)


weight
how likely it is that a line through x at time t came from that pair
u
what a perfectly trained network outputs; following it carries the whole noise cloud onto the data cloud (Lipman et al. 2023)


For the toy this average has a closed form (given which blob an example came from, xt is Gaussian), so the page can draw the exact u beside the trained network. Your browser runs a line-by-line port of the script that computed every exact number here.

Any Gaussian path xt = at x1 + bt x0 on a mixture of Gaussians: posterior weights over blobs, then E[x1 | x] and E[x0 | x] in closed form, u = a′ E[x1 | x] + b′ E[x0 | x].


Why training on single lines works. We never compute u while training. Lipman et al.'s Theorem 2: the per-pair loss and the loss against u differ by a constant independent of the network, so they have the same gradient (published; derived on the {{Math page, derivation D8|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}). The constant is the spread of the lines around their average, and with the exact field it can be measured:

3. Few steps fail even for a perfect model


The problem. On the toy one Euler step puts no points on a blob; on images it gives the blur of section 1. Training failure, or would a perfect model do it too? Sample the exact field with the same solver and steps: what it gets wrong is the path's fault (discretisation error); the network's extra error is learning error.

Predict: you train the same model 10 times longer. At 2 Euler steps, can it reach 90% on a blob?




Longer training moves the network towards the exact field, which itself scores  at 2 steps. To do better you change what is learned (the pairing: sections 7 and 8), the solver or step placement (sections 5 and 6), or distil (section 9). After one reflow round the network no longer learns this field at all, which is why it beats the "perfect" model at 1 step.


How to check on real data: compare N-step samples with a many-step solve from the same noise; good many-step and blurry few-step samples mean the path, not the fit, is the problem.

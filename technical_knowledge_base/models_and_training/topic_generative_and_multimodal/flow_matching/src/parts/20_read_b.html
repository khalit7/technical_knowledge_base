1. The target: the marginal vector field, computed exactly

The problem. Training draws a noise x0, an image x1 and a time t, puts a point on the straight line between them, xt = (1 − t) x0 + t x1, and asks the network for that line's velocity x1 − x0 (the root's Flow lab, section 2). But the same point x at the same t lies on many lines: from different noises to different images. The network sees x and t only, so it cannot know which line it is on. What does it end up predicting?

How it works. A network trained with a squared error predicts the average of its targets given its inputs. So it learns the marginal vector field (marginal: averaged over all pairs, as opposed to the conditional field of one pair)

u(x, t) = E[ x1 − x0 | xt = x ] = Σpairs (weight of the pair at x) × (its velocity)
weight
how likely it is that a line through x at time t came from that pair: the posterior (probability after seeing x) of the pair
u
what a perfectly trained network outputs; following it from noise carries the whole noise cloud onto the data cloud (Lipman et al. 2023)

For the root's 2-D toy (noise in the plane, data a ring of 8 Gaussian blobs of radius 3 and spread 0.25) this average has a closed form, because given which blob an image came from, xt is Gaussian. The page's script computes it for any path xt = at x1 + bt x0; this is the function that ran (src/runs/fm_toy.py), and your browser runs a line-by-line port of it in every toy visual below:

Try: click or drag anywhere to move the point. At t near 0 every one of the 80 sampled data points is about equally likely to be the line's end, so the average (thick arrow) points at the ring's centre, where there is no data. Slide t towards 1: the weights collapse onto one blob and the average turns into that blob's direction. The thick arrow from the 80 samples, the exact field (dashed, from the formula) and the trained network (the root's toy model, optional) should agree. Each arrow is drawn (1 − t) times the velocity, so it ends where the straight line through the point would end: the model's current guess of the final image, E[x1 | x].




Why the conditional objective works. We never compute u while training; we regress on single lines. Lipman et al.'s Theorem 2 says the two losses differ by a constant that does not depend on the network, so they have identical gradients (published; derived on the {{Math page, section 18|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}). The constant is the spread of the lines around their average, and with the exact field it can be measured:

The flow-matching loss is a poor progress meter: almost all of it is the variance of a random target. The root's Flow lab found the same on images from the other side (its per-t validation loss is about 1 at t = 1).


What the network gets wrong. The right-hand chart is the honest measure of learning: the trained toy model is within  of the exact field at t = 0.1 but  at t = 1, where the field is steep (near the data end each blob pulls hard towards its centre). That is where a sampler's last steps land, and why a solver that evaluates the network exactly at t = 1 is fragile (the root's Heun finding).


Cost and when it matters. Nothing extra to pay: this is the standard loss. It matters when you read training curves (a flat loss does not mean converged), when you compare objectives (different paths have different floors, so their raw losses are not comparable) and when you debug (a closed-form toy tells learning error from sampling error). How to check: on any Gaussian-mixture toy, compute u as above and plot |vθ − u|² by t, as here.

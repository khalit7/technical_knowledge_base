The idea in one screen

A diffusion model is one network, the denoiser, trained to undo a known amount of Gaussian noise at every noise level; generating is starting from pure noise and calling that network again and again. Four choices around the network decide whether it works: how noise is added (the schedule), what the network is asked to output (the target and its loss), how its answers are chained into a path (the sampler and its step count), and how hard each step is pushed toward the prompt (guidance).

New terms

  • āt ("alpha bar"): share of the image's variance left at step t.
  • SNR, λ: signal-to-noise ratio ā/(1 − ā) and its log.
  • Target: what the network outputs: noise ε, clean image x0, or a mix v.
  • Sampler: the rule that chains predictions into a path; NFE, network calls per image.
  • Deterministic (ODE) / stochastic: whether the sampler adds fresh noise as it goes.
  • CFG: classifier-free guidance, extrapolating from the unconditional prediction.
  • Distillation: training a second model to take one to four big steps.

1. The forward process: destroying an image on purpose

The problem. Nobody can write down the distribution of real images, so nobody can sample it directly. The other direction is easy: take a real image and add noise until nothing is left. A diffusion model learns to reverse that destruction in small steps; then it can start from pure noise, which anyone can sample, and walk back to an image.

Concretely. DDPM's schedule, which the tiny model uses, destroys an image over T = 1,000 steps: step t shrinks the image slightly and adds Gaussian noise of variance βt, rising linearly from 10−4 to 0.02, so the total variance stays 1.

The formula. Gaussians add up to a Gaussian, so the steps never have to be simulated one by one: the noisy image at any step has a closed form,

xt = √āt · x0 + √(1 − āt) · ε,   ε ~ N(0, I)

where x0 is the clean image with pixels in [−1, 1], αt = 1 − βt is the share of variance one step keeps, and āt = α1 α2 … αt is the share of the original signal's variance that survives to step t, falling from about 1 to about 0. This one line is the whole data pipeline of training: pick an image, a random t and random noise, and the noisy input is ready (diffusers' add_noise, scheduling_ddpm.py line 608). The derivation of the closed form is on the math page, derivation D3.

2. Noise schedules: where the steps go, and what survives the last one

The problem. The schedule (the list of βt, or equivalently āt) fixes two things at once: how the 1,000 training timesteps are spread over noise levels, and what the model sees at the very last one. Both quietly limit what the model can generate.

One axis for all schedules. Write the noise level as the log signal-to-noise ratio λ = ln(ā / (1 − ā)); SNR = 1 (λ = 0) is where signal and noise are equal. A schedule is a way of spending timesteps along λ: the linear schedule spends 74% of its timesteps where noise dominates (SNR < 1 from t = 259); the cosine schedule (Nichol and Dhariwal 2021) falls slowly at both ends and crosses at t = 496, about half; Stable Diffusion 1.5's scaled-linear (β from 0.00085 to 0.012, linear in √β) crosses at t = 354, spending 65% derived. Two schedules that cover the same λ range differ only in how densely they sample it, which is why EDM (section 3) drops the timestep and samples noise levels directly.

The end point: what survives "pure noise"

Predict: at SD 1.5's last training step, √ā = 0.068, so each latent value keeps 6.8% of its amplitude and its SNR is 0.0047. How visible is the image's average brightness (the mean of one latent channel over its 64 × 64 values) to the model at that step?
Clearly visible. Averaging 4,096 independent noise values shrinks the noise's standard deviation 64 times but leaves the signal's average alone, so the SNR of the average is 4,096 × 0.0047 = 19.2 derived (recompute.py), the per-value SNR of step 52 of 1,000, an almost clean image. So in training, even at t = 999, the model reads the image's overall brightness off its input and learns to keep it; at sampling the start is real noise whose average is about 0, so the model faithfully keeps "medium brightness". Lin et al. (2023) traced SD's inability to make very dark or very bright images to exactly this non-zero terminal SNR.

Two fixes, one hack and one cure.

  • Offset noise (Guttenberg, Crosslabs, 2023): during training add a random constant per image and channel to the noise, k · ξ with k around 0.1 (diffusers' fine-tuning script: --noise_offset, train_text_to_image.py lines 973 to 976). The averaged noise now has variance (1 − ā)(1/n + k²), so the brightness SNR at the last step falls from 19.2 to 0.46 at k = 0.1 derived. It works, but it trains on a noise distribution the sampler never starts from, and it reduces the brightness signal rather than removing it.
  • Zero terminal SNR (Lin et al. 2023, Algorithm 1): rescale √ā so its last value is exactly 0 and its first is unchanged, then rebuild the βs; diffusers' rescale_betas_zero_snr=True.

The cost of the cure. At SNR exactly 0 the input is the noise, so an ε-predicting network has nothing to learn there, and the clean-image guess x̂0 = (xt − √(1 − ā) ε̂) / √ā divides by zero. The paper therefore pairs it with v-prediction (section 3), fine-tuning, sampling that starts at the last timestep ("trailing", section 5) and CFG rescale (section 6). Resolution: the same schedule is gentler on bigger images

The averaging argument applies to any patch, not just the whole image: at 4 × the pixels, every 2 × 2 block's average has 4 × the SNR of a single value, so coarse structure survives more noise, and a schedule tuned at 32 × 32 destroys too little of a 512 × 512 image's layout at its noisiest steps. Hoogeboom, Heek and Salimans (2023) make it a rule: take a schedule designed at 64 × 64 and, at d × d, multiply its SNR by (64/d)², a shift of log SNR by 2 log(64/d); on ImageNet 256 that moved their training FID from 7.65 to 3.76 published. Stable Diffusion 3 does the same for flow matching with a timestep shift (Esser et al. 2024); that belongs to the flow-matching page.

3. The denoiser, what it predicts, and the training loss

The network. The denoiser takes the noisy image xt, the timestep t and (here) the class label, and returns a tensor of the image's shape. The tiny model is the shared TinyUNet, 1,040,353 parameters: a convolutional U-Net that shrinks the image from 32 to 16 to 8 pixels and grows it back, with skip connections between matching sizes and self-attention at 8 × 8; t enters as a sinusoidal embedding and the label as a learned embedding with an 11th "no label" row, both added inside every residual block. Production denoisers (SD's U-Net, diffusion transformers) are the latent-diffusion page's subject.

Three things it can be asked for. Given xt and t, the clean image and the noise determine each other, so the network can be trained to output either, or a mix, and the sampler converts. The choice does not change what a perfect network knows; it changes which noise levels the loss cares about and how errors are amplified at sampling time:

The label is replaced by the null class 10% of the time, so one network also learns the unconditional model that guidance needs (section 6).

def loss_fn(model, b):
    x0, y = b
    t = torch.randint(0, sched.T, (x0.shape[0],), device=dev)
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    pred = model(sched.q_sample(x0, t, eps), t.float() / sched.T, drop_labels(y, 0.1))
    loss = F.mse_loss(pred, target(x0, eps, t))     # target: eps, or v = sqrt(abar) eps - sqrt(1 - abar) x0
    return loss, {"loss": loss.item()}

Recipe: batch 128, AdamW at 5 × 10−4 after 200 warm-up steps, gradient clipping at 1, an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights with decay 0.999 used for all sampling, 4,000 steps in 15.1 minutes (4.41 steps per second on the M1 Pro's GPU) measured. Two models were trained this way, one per target: ε and v. Why predicting noise is the right loss for DDPM, from the ELBO, is on the DDPM paper page and the math page, derivations D4 and D5.

Training details that change samples

EMA of the weights. An exponential moving average keeps a second copy of the weights, updated as wema ← d wema + (1 − d) w after every step; with d = 0.999 it averages roughly the last 1,000 steps, and sampling uses it. Diffusion training is noisy (every step sees a random noise level), and the average sits closer to the bottom of the loss valley than any single iterate. measured: the lab's raw weights at step 4,000 score FD 141.9 with DDIM 50 and 87.0 with DDPM 50, against 112.7 and 62.3 for the EMA copy of the same run. diffusers' EMAModel defaults to d = 0.9999 with optional warm-up (training_utils.py line 632); EDM2 (Karras et al. 2023) stores snapshots so the EMA length can be chosen after training. EDM2 reports ImageNet-512 FID 1.81 with this machinery and guidance (EDM2-XXL), 1.91 without published.

Condition dropout. Guidance needs the unconditional model, so 10% of training labels (or prompts) are replaced by a null label. Ho and Salimans found dropping 10% or 20% of conditions about equally good and 50% worse published; the tiny model uses 10%. SD 1.5 used 10% text dropout (the model card).

Training length. Doubling the tiny model's training (4,000 to 8,000 steps, 14.8 more minutes) moved DDIM at 50 steps from FD 112.7 to 41.6 and DDPM at 1,000 steps from 34.9 to 20.1 measured; the model was far from converged, which is the context for every sampler result here.

What transfers from the tiny model. Mechanisms transfer: how targets weight noise levels, what each sampler does with a model's errors, how guidance trades fidelity for variety, what each bug looks like. Absolute numbers do not: a 1M-parameter model trained for 15 minutes on 28 × 28 grey images is far less accurate than a production model, which is exactly why the deterministic sampler loses here (section 5); well-trained models favour DPM-Solver-class ODE samplers at 20 to 30 steps.

4. One network, three views: denoiser, score, ODE

The problem. Papers describe the same trained network in three vocabularies: it "predicts the noise", it "estimates the score", it "defines an ODE". Samplers, guidance and distillation are each written in one of them. The translation is two lines of algebra; with it, DDIM, Euler, DPM-Solver and Karras' samplers stop being separate inventions.

View 1: the denoiser

Given xt, the best guess of the clean image under squared error is the posterior mean D(xt, t) = E[x0 | xt]: the average of every clean image that could have produced this noisy one, weighted by how likely each is. At high noise many images could have, so the guess is a blurry average; at low noise only one could, so it is sharp. Predicting ε is the same thing rearranged: ε̂ = (xt − √ā D) / √(1 − ā).

View 2: the score

The score is ∇x log pt(x): at any point, the direction in which noisy images become more likely. Tweedie's formula links it to the denoiser: D = (xt + (1 − ā) ∇ log pt(xt)) / √ā, so ε̂ = −√(1 − ā) × score. A noise predictor is a scaled score estimator; the proof is on the math page, derivation D5.

View 3: the ODE

Divide xt by √ā: x̃ = x0 + σ ε with noise level σ = √((1 − ā)/ā). Sampling is now moving σ from its largest value to 0. The probability-flow ODE (Song et al. 2021) says how: dx̃/dσ = (x̃ − D) / σ = ε̂. Followed from pure noise it lands exactly on the data distribution if D is exact; adding noise as you go gives the stochastic version (an SDE) with the same end point.

Why it matters in practice. One Euler step of that ODE from σ to a smaller σ′ is x̃′ = x̃ + (σ′ − σ) ε̂ = D + σ′ ε̂. Multiply back by √ā′ and it is DDIM's update, line for line. That is why diffusers' EulerDiscreteScheduler and DDIM give the same samples on the tiny model (FD within 0.2 at every step count in the lab) measured: one method in two coordinate systems. Every "Karras" or "k-diffusion" sampler works in σ; every "DDIM-style" one in xt. The σ range is the schedule seen from the ODE's side: the tiny model's linear schedule runs from σ = 0.01 to 157, Stable Diffusion 1.5's from 0.029 to 14.6 derived. A σ-space sampler must start from noise of that size, not unit noise (a real bug, section 8).

5. Samplers: walking back from noise

The problem. The trained network answers one question: "at this noise level, what is the clean image (or the noise)?". A sampler turns those answers into a path from noise to an image. Every sampler below uses the same trained weights; only the rule changes, and one network evaluation (NFE) is the unit of cost.

  • DDPM, ancestral (Ho et al. 2020): estimate x̂0, move to the average of where xt could have come from given x̂0 (the posterior mean), then add fresh noise. Built for the 1,000 small steps it was trained on; the same start gives a different image each run.
  • DDIM (Song, Meng and Ermon 2020): the same model, no fresh noise: keep the predicted noise as the direction and jump straight to the previous noise level, xprev = √āprev x̂0 + √(1 − āprev) ε̂. Deterministic and valid for any number of steps, because it follows the probability-flow ODE (section 4).
  • DPM-Solver++ and Euler (Lu et al. 2022; Karras et al. 2022): treat that ODE as an ODE and integrate it with better numerical methods.

What a call costs. Every step is one network evaluation, two with guidance: one tiny U-Net call takes 6.0 ms for one image and 332 ms for a batch of 1,000 on the M1 Pro's GPU; one SD 1.5 U-Net pass at 512 × 512 takes 0.319 s measured (root lab recordings). Samplers differ in three ways only: which coordinates they step in (xt, σ or λ, section 4), how many calls each step costs and how they combine them (the solver's order), and whether they add fresh noise.

Why the stochastic sampler won here

Ancestral DDPM beats DDIM from 4 steps up on this model, the opposite of the DDIM paper's CIFAR-10 result. The lab's explanation was that fresh noise lets later steps correct earlier errors (Karras et al. 2022, section 4, make the same argument). Three tests of that explanation:

Test 1. Predict: on the exact-score toy, give the model a typical under-training error, a denoiser that hedges between bumps it cannot yet tell apart (its posterior over bumps is flattened, temperature 2). Which sampler suffers more?
DDIM. With the hedging denoiser DDIM's distance to the truth stops falling at about 0.233 however many steps it takes (0.231 at 50, 0.233 at 1,000), with 2.5% of samples stranded between bumps; DDPM keeps improving to 0.131 with 0.8% between bumps measured (gmm_toy.py, 20,000 samples): the same plateau the tiny model shows. Why: the ODE transports noise to exactly the distribution the model's denoiser implies, errors included, and a hedging denoiser implies a smeared one. The stochastic sampler re-noises and re-denoises at every step; each re-denoise at lower noise pulls the sample toward wherever the denoiser is confident, which is on a bump.

6. Guidance: trading variety for adherence

The problem. A conditional model asked for "sandal" produces something sandal-like, but its samples drift: some are ambiguous, some look like another class. We want a knob that trades variety for adherence to the condition. Its predecessor, classifier guidance (Dhariwal and Nichol 2021), pushed along the gradient of a separate classifier trained on noisy images; classifier-free guidance (CFG, Ho and Salimans 2022) removes the classifier by letting the model be its own.

How it works. Train one network both with and without the label (the 10% dropout of section 3). At sampling, evaluate it both ways and extrapolate from the unconditional prediction past the conditional one:

ε̂ = εu + s · (εc − εu)

where εc and εu are the predictions with the label (or prompt) and with the null label (or empty prompt), and s is diffusers' guidance_scale: 0 is the unconditional model, 1 the plain conditional model, above 1 pushes further in the direction the condition points (pipeline_stable_diffusion.py line 1055). Two traps: the pipeline runs the unconditional branch only when the scale is above 1 (line 764), so guidance_scale=0 there is the plain conditional model, not the unconditional one; and papers often write εc + w (εc − εu) with w = s − 1. The cost: two evaluations per step, run as one batch of two (line 1037). The derivation is on the math page, derivation D7.

7. Few steps: distillation

The price of an image is network calls (steps × 2 with guidance: SD 1.5 at 25 guided steps is 50 U-Net passes, 15.8 s per 512 × 512 image here measured, root lab recordings), and below about 8 steps no sampler of section 5 is enough, since one straight step from pure noise lands on the blurry average guess of section 4; the fix is a second, distilled student model trained to jump, and its methods, published results and costs are taught on Latent diffusion and diffusion transformers, section 10 (flow-based shortcuts, reflow and one-step distillation of a flow, on Flow matching, sections 8 and 9). Grey fog: a σ-space scheduler started from unit noise

Cause. Euler, Heun, DPM-Solver with Karras sigmas and the other k-diffusion-style schedulers work on x̃ = xt / √ā, whose starting noise has standard deviation σmax (157.4 for the tiny model's schedule with trailing spacing, about 14.6 for SD 1.5). A hand-written loop that skips x = x * scheduler.init_noise_sigma starts with noise 157.4 times too small, and scale_model_input then shrinks it again. Check: print scheduler.init_noise_sigma; the pipeline does the multiplication at pipeline_stable_diffusion.py line 713. measured: FD 1,339.5, diversity 0.69 (real images 28.87): every sample collapses to nearly the same faint shape.

Noise or blobs at every step count: the timestep in the wrong units

Cause. The network was trained with t / T in [0, 1) (the shared TinyUNet) and is fed the integer t, or a diffusers U-Net trained on integer t is fed a fraction. The time embedding then describes a noise level the network never saw. Check: compare the range of t in the training loop with the sampler's call. measured: FD 1,065.6, accuracy 19.4% (chance is 10%).

Plausible but wrong: a schedule mismatch between training and sampling

Cause. Sampling with ā from a different schedule than the model was trained with (a cosine scheduler config on a linear-schedule model, or SD 1.5's scaled-linear on a model fine-tuned with something else). Each step's conversion from ε̂ to x̂0 then uses the wrong noise level. Check: diff the scheduler config against the training config (beta_schedule, beta_start, beta_end, num_train_timesteps, rescale_betas_zero_snr). In diffusers the scheduler's prediction_type and schedule come from the checkpoint's scheduler/scheduler_config.json; swap samplers with from_config so they inherit it. measured: FD 1,284.8, accuracy 21.0%: the cosine schedule claims far more signal at middle timesteps than the model was trained with, so each step removes the wrong amount of noise.

NaN or black images: zero terminal SNR switched on for an ε-model

Cause. rescale_betas_zero_snr=True makes ā at the last step exactly 0, and turning ε̂ into x̂0 divides by √ā. Zero terminal SNR is for v-prediction models fine-tuned on that schedule (section 2). On SD 1.5 it produced an all-black image (section 2) measured.

Clipping that helps pixels and breaks latents: clip_sample

Cause. diffusers' DDPMScheduler and DDIMScheduler default to clip_sample=True, clamping x̂0 to [−1, 1] at every step. That suits pixel models trained on [−1, 1] images; a latent model's latents are not bounded that way, which is why SD 1.5's scheduler config sets clip_sample false. On the tiny pixel model clipping did not help measured: DDPM 50 steps FD 70.7 with it, 62.3 without (250 steps: 44.7 and 39.7).

Blotchy samples from a model whose loss looks fine: sampling the raw weights

Cause. The training loop's own weights are noisier than their EMA; checkpoints often hold both. measured: DDIM 50 steps FD 141.9 raw against 112.7 EMA.

"DDIM is always better than DDPM"

True for a well-trained model at 10 to 100 steps (DDIM Table 1), false for an under-trained one (section 5). A sampler ranking measured on one checkpoint does not transfer to another; re-run the sampler sweep after each training run that matters.

Interview rehearsal (optional)

Twelve questions with short answers

Why can training use any timestep without running the earlier ones?

The forward process is a chain of Gaussians, so xt given x0 is Gaussian in closed form: xt = √āt x0 + √(1 − āt) ε. One line makes the training input for any t.

Explain how a network trained to predict noise is a score model.

Tweedie's formula: the posterior mean is E[x0|xt] = (xt + (1 − ā) ∇ log pt) / √ā. Rearranging the noise model gives ε̂ = −√(1 − ā) ∇ log pt(xt). So minimising the ε loss is denoising score matching with a per-level scale; the sampler can use the network as a score in an SDE or an ODE.

ε, x0 or v: which would you predict, and why?

They are interchangeable given xt; what differs is the loss weighting across noise levels and the numerical behaviour at high noise. ε is the DDPM default and fine at many steps; v avoids dividing by √ā near pure noise, which matters for few-step sampling, distillation and zero-terminal-SNR schedules.

What does the ε loss weight, and what does Min-SNR change?

In clean-image units the ε loss weights each level by its SNR, so nearly clean timesteps dominate the gradient; x0 does the opposite. Min-SNR caps the weight at γ = 5, which takes effort away from easy low-noise steps and conflicting gradients; Hang et al. report 3.4 × faster convergence on ImageNet 256 (published).

Why does SD 1.5 struggle to make very dark or very bright images, and what would you do in a fine-tune?

Its schedule leaves √ā = 0.068 at the last step; per value that is little, but the per-channel average over 4,096 values keeps an SNR of about 19, so the model learns to read brightness off its input even at t = 999, and pure noise at sampling has average 0. Fine-tune with zero terminal SNR plus v-prediction, sample from the last timestep (trailing) and use CFG rescale (Lin et al.); the cheap patch is offset noise (about 0.1) in the fine-tune.

Why does zero terminal SNR force v-prediction?

At SNR 0 the input equals the noise, so ε is trivially the input (nothing to learn) and the clean-image guess divides by √ā = 0. v = √ā ε − √(1 − ā) x0 becomes −x0 there: a well-posed target (predict the average image), and no division.

DDPM, DDIM or DPM-Solver++: which would you ship, and when would you pick a stochastic sampler?

Same training for all; DDIM drops the fresh noise and follows the probability-flow path, so it works at 10 to 50 steps and a seed reproduces an image. For a well-trained text-to-image model: DPM-Solver++ 2M (or UniPC) at 20 to 30 steps with trailing or Karras spacing. Pick a stochastic sampler (ancestral, SDE variants, EDM churn) when the model's errors dominate (under-trained, small, or out of distribution) and you can afford more steps; measure both, since the ranking depends on the checkpoint (here DDPM won).

Your 4-step samples are noise but 50-step samples are fine. What do you check first?

The timesteps the scheduler actually walks (scheduler.timesteps): with "leading" spacing the first step is far below the last training step. Then the prediction type, and whether the model was trained for few steps at all.

What does classifier-free guidance cost, and what does it buy?

Two denoiser evaluations per step (usually one batch of two) and 10 to 20% of training steps with the condition dropped. It buys adherence to the condition and cleaner samples at the cost of diversity; too high a scale oversaturates. On the tiny model class accuracy rose from 77.8% at s = 1 to 96.4% at s = 5 while diversity fell.

Your guided samples are oversaturated at scale 10. Three fixes and their costs?

CFG rescale (free, φ = 0.7; slightly flatter images, and little effect on an ε-model like SD 1.5), a guidance interval (free, saves calls; needs the interval tuned), a lower scale (loses prompt adherence). For pixel models, dynamic thresholding. If you can train a second model, autoguidance avoids the trade-off between diversity and quality that CFG makes.

How do consistency models and LCMs get to 1 to 4 steps, and what do they lose?

They train a student to map any point on a teacher's ODE trajectory straight to its end point (self-consistency along the trajectory), with cskip/cout so the map is the identity at σ = 0; sampling is one jump, optionally re-noise and jump again. They lose some diversity and fine detail, and classifier-free guidance becomes a training-time setting (LCM bakes in a guidance range; run at scale 1 to 2).

Design: serve a text-to-image model at under 1 second per 1024 × 1024 image on one GPU, starting from a base model that needs 30 guided steps.

Count calls: 30 × 2 = 60 denoiser passes plus a VAE decode. Steps: switch to DPM-Solver++ 2M at 20 to 25 steps (no training). Guidance: distil it into the model (one call per step) or guide only within an interval (fewer double calls). Then distil steps: LCM/LCM-LoRA or adversarial distillation (ADD, DMD2) to 4 steps, which is 4 calls; check prompt adherence and diversity against the teacher on a fixed prompt set (CLIPScore, a preference model, FID on a held-out set). Then per-call cost: bf16, compiled graphs, a fast VAE decoder, batching. Trade-offs to state: distillation loses some diversity and editability; keep the base model for high-quality modes.

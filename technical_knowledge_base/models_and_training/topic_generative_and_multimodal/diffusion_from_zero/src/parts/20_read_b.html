1. One network, three views: denoiser, score, ODE

The problem. Papers describe the same trained network in three vocabularies: it "predicts the noise", it "estimates the score", it "defines an ODE". Samplers, guidance and distillation methods are each written in one of them, so reading the literature means translating. The translation is two lines of algebra; once you have it, DDIM, Euler, DPM-Solver and Karras' samplers stop being separate inventions.

Notation (as the {{Diffusion lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d817ab6ade318917bff55}}): the noisy image at step t is xt = √āt x0 + √(1 − āt) ε, where x0 is the clean image, ε standard Gaussian noise, and āt ("alpha bar") the fraction of the signal's variance that survives to step t.

View 1: the denoiser

Given xt, the best guess of the clean image under squared error is the posterior mean D(xt, t) = E[x0 | xt]: the average of every clean image that could have produced this noisy one, weighted by how likely each is. At high noise many images could have, so the guess is a blurry average; at low noise only one could, so the guess is sharp. Predicting ε is the same thing rearranged: ε̂ = (xt − √ā D) / √(1 − ā).

View 2: the score

The score is ∇x log pt(x): at any point, the direction in which noisy images become more likely. Tweedie's formula links it to the denoiser: D = (xt + (1 − ā) ∇ log pt(xt)) / √ā, so ε̂ = −√(1 − ā) × score. A noise predictor is a scaled score estimator; the proof is on the {{math page, section 15|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}.

View 3: the ODE

Divide xt by √ā: x̃ = x0 + σ ε with noise level σ = √((1 − ā)/ā). Now sampling is moving σ from its largest value to 0. The probability-flow ODE (Song et al. 2021) says how: dx̃/dσ = (x̃ − D) / σ = ε̂. Following it from pure noise lands exactly on the data distribution if D is exact; adding noise as you go gives the stochastic version (an SDE) with the same end point.

Why it matters in practice. Take one Euler step of that ODE from σ to a smaller σ′: x̃′ = x̃ + (σ′ − σ) ε̂ = D + σ′ ε̂. Multiply back by √ā′ and it is DDIM's update, line for line. That is why diffusers' EulerDiscreteScheduler and DDIM give the same samples on the tiny model (FD within 0.2 at every step count in the lab) measured: they are one method in two coordinate systems. Every "Karras" or "k-diffusion" sampler works in σ; every "DDIM-style" one works in xt. The σ range is the schedule seen from the ODE's side: the tiny model's linear schedule runs from σ = to , Stable Diffusion 1.5's from to derived. An σ-space sampler must start from noise of that size, not unit noise (a real bug in section "Mistakes").

Watch it on a model you can check: the exact-score toy

Images are too big to see a distribution. So the page uses a one-dimensional stand-in for the running example: three "garment classes" as three narrow bumps on a line (weights 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 at −1.5, 0.6 and 2.0). For a mixture of Gaussians the noisy density pt and the denoiser D are known in closed form, so this toy has a perfect network, and the same linear schedule and trailing timesteps as the tiny model. Below, 160 starting noise values walk to data; the shaded background is pt(x) at each moment (three bumps on the right merge into one Gaussian on the left). The code is src/runs/gmm_toy.py; the page runs its JavaScript port, checked against it to 3 × 10−15.

The same 160 noise values, deterministic ODE against stochastic sampler, exact denoiser, 50 steps

What to notice. For the first third of the steps nothing happens that you could call a decision: the paths drift with the shrinking Gaussian. Between roughly t = 700 and t = 300 (on this schedule, where the signal-to-noise ratio crosses 1 at t = 259) each path commits to a bump; after that the steps only sharpen. Coarse structure is decided at high noise, detail at low noise, the same lesson the lab's image trajectories show. The ODE paths never cross (a deterministic flow cannot send two points through the same place at the same time); the stochastic paths jitter, and some switch bumps late.

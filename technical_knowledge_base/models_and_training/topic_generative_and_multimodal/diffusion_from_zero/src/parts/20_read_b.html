1. The forward process: destroying an image on purpose

The problem. Nobody can write down the distribution of real images, so nobody can sample it directly. The other direction is easy: take a real image and add noise until nothing is left. A diffusion model learns to reverse that destruction in small steps; then it can start from pure noise, which anyone can sample, and walk back to an image.

Concretely. DDPM's schedule, which the tiny model uses, destroys an image over T = 1,000 steps: step t shrinks the image slightly and adds Gaussian noise of variance βt, rising linearly from 10−4 to 0.02, so the total variance stays 1.

The formula. Gaussians add up to a Gaussian, so the steps never have to be simulated one by one: the noisy image at any step has a closed form,

xt = √āt · x0 + √(1 − āt) · ε,   ε ~ N(0, I)

where x0 is the clean image with pixels in [−1, 1], αt = 1 − βt is the share of variance one step keeps, and āt = α1 α2 … αt is the share of the original signal's variance that survives to step t, falling from about 1 to about 0. This one line is the whole data pipeline of training: pick an image, a random t and random noise, and the noisy input is ready (diffusers' add_noise, {{scheduling_ddpm.py line 608|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/schedulers/scheduling_ddpm.py#L608}}). The derivation of the closed form is on the {{math page, derivation D3|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}.

2. Noise schedules: where the steps go, and what survives the last one

The problem. The schedule (the list of βt, or equivalently āt) fixes two things at once: how the 1,000 training timesteps are spread over noise levels, and what the model sees at the very last one. Both quietly limit what the model can generate.

One axis for all schedules. Write the noise level as the log signal-to-noise ratio λ = ln(ā / (1 − ā)); SNR = 1 (λ = 0) is where signal and noise are equal. A schedule is a way of spending timesteps along λ: the linear schedule spends of its timesteps where noise dominates (SNR < 1 from t = 259); the cosine schedule ({{Nichol and Dhariwal 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09672}}) falls slowly at both ends and crosses at t = 496, about half; Stable Diffusion 1.5's scaled-linear (β from 0.00085 to 0.012, linear in √β) crosses at t = 354, spending derived. Two schedules that cover the same λ range differ only in how densely they sample it, which is why EDM (section 3) drops the timestep and samples noise levels directly.

The end point: what survives "pure noise"

Predict: at SD 1.5's last training step, √ā = 0.068, so each latent value keeps 6.8% of its amplitude and its SNR is 0.0047. How visible is the image's average brightness (the mean of one latent channel over its 64 × 64 values) to the model at that step?
Clearly visible. Averaging 4,096 independent noise values shrinks the noise's standard deviation 64 times but leaves the signal's average alone, so the SNR of the average is 4,096 × 0.0047 = derived (recompute.py), the per-value SNR of step of 1,000, an almost clean image. So in training, even at t = 999, the model reads the image's overall brightness off its input and learns to keep it; at sampling the start is real noise whose average is about 0, so the model faithfully keeps "medium brightness". {{Lin et al. (2023)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.08891}} traced SD's inability to make very dark or very bright images to exactly this non-zero terminal SNR.

Two fixes, one hack and one cure.

  • Offset noise ({{Guttenberg, Crosslabs, 2023|https://www.crosslabs.org/blog/diffusion-with-offset-noise}}): during training add a random constant per image and channel to the noise, k · ξ with k around 0.1 (diffusers' fine-tuning script: --noise_offset, {{train_text_to_image.py lines 973 to 976|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/examples/text_to_image/train_text_to_image.py#L973}}). The averaged noise now has variance (1 − ā)(1/n + k²), so the brightness SNR at the last step falls from to at k = 0.1 derived. It works, but it trains on a noise distribution the sampler never starts from, and it reduces the brightness signal rather than removing it.
  • Zero terminal SNR (Lin et al. 2023, Algorithm 1): rescale √ā so its last value is exactly 0 and its first is unchanged, then rebuild the βs; diffusers' rescale_betas_zero_snr=True.
Zero terminal SNR in code (two lines)
alphas_bar_sqrt -= alphas_bar_sqrt_T                                        # shift so the last timestep is zero
alphas_bar_sqrt *= alphas_bar_sqrt_0 / (alphas_bar_sqrt_0 - alphas_bar_sqrt_T)  # scale so the first is unchanged
{{diffusers v0.41.0, schedulers/scheduling_ddim.py lines 103 to 137|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/v0.41.0/src/diffusers/schedulers/scheduling_ddim.py#L103}} (rescale_zero_terminal_snr).

The cost of the cure. At SNR exactly 0 the input is the noise, so an ε-predicting network has nothing to learn there, and the clean-image guess x̂0 = (xt − √(1 − ā) ε̂) / √ā divides by zero. The paper therefore pairs it with v-prediction (section 3), fine-tuning, sampling that starts at the last timestep ("trailing", section 5) and CFG rescale (section 6). Resolution: the same schedule is gentler on bigger images

The averaging argument applies to any patch, not just the whole image: at 4 × the pixels, every 2 × 2 block's average has 4 × the SNR of a single value, so coarse structure survives more noise, and a schedule tuned at 32 × 32 destroys too little of a 512 × 512 image's layout at its noisiest steps. Stable Diffusion 3 does the same for flow matching with a timestep shift ({{Esser et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03206}}); that belongs to the {{flow-matching page|n:3fc5c17b0d0d8136a1efc73aaa446ceb}}.

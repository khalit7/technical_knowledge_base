4. One network, three views: denoiser, score, ODE

The problem. Papers describe the same trained network in three vocabularies: it "predicts the noise", it "estimates the score", it "defines an ODE". Samplers, guidance and distillation are each written in one of them. The translation is two lines of algebra; with it, DDIM, Euler, DPM-Solver and Karras' samplers stop being separate inventions.

View 1: the denoiser

Given xt, the best guess of the clean image under squared error is the posterior mean D(xt, t) = E[x0 | xt]: the average of every clean image that could have produced this noisy one, weighted by how likely each is. At high noise many images could have, so the guess is a blurry average; at low noise only one could, so it is sharp. Predicting ε is the same thing rearranged: ε̂ = (xt − √ā D) / √(1 − ā).

View 2: the score

The score is ∇x log pt(x): at any point, the direction in which noisy images become more likely. Tweedie's formula links it to the denoiser: D = (xt + (1 − ā) ∇ log pt(xt)) / √ā, so ε̂ = −√(1 − ā) × score. A noise predictor is a scaled score estimator; the proof is on the {{math page, derivation D5|n:3f05c17b0d0d81aa9857eb5fa1bac530}}.

View 3: the ODE

Divide xt by √ā: x̃ = x0 + σ ε with noise level σ = √((1 − ā)/ā). Sampling is now moving σ from its largest value to 0. The probability-flow ODE ({{Song et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456}}) says how: dx̃/dσ = (x̃ − D) / σ = ε̂. Followed from pure noise it lands exactly on the data distribution if D is exact; adding noise as you go gives the stochastic version (an SDE) with the same end point.

Why it matters in practice. One Euler step of that ODE from σ to a smaller σ′ is x̃′ = x̃ + (σ′ − σ) ε̂ = D + σ′ ε̂. Multiply back by √ā′ and it is DDIM's update, line for line. That is why diffusers' EulerDiscreteScheduler and DDIM give the same samples on the tiny model (FD within 0.2 at every step count in the lab) measured: one method in two coordinate systems. Every "Karras" or "k-diffusion" sampler works in σ; every "DDIM-style" one in xt. The σ range is the schedule seen from the ODE's side: the tiny model's linear schedule runs from σ = to , Stable Diffusion 1.5's from to derived. A σ-space sampler must start from noise of that size, not unit noise (a real bug, section 8).

5. Samplers: walking back from noise

The problem. The trained network answers one question: "at this noise level, what is the clean image (or the noise)?". A sampler turns those answers into a path from noise to an image. Every sampler below uses the same trained weights; only the rule changes, and one network evaluation (NFE) is the unit of cost.

  • DDPM, ancestral ({{Ho et al. 2020|n:3c65c17b0d0d811481e4e36333454f7a}}): estimate x̂0, move to the average of where xt could have come from given x̂0 (the posterior mean), then add fresh noise. Built for the 1,000 small steps it was trained on; the same start gives a different image each run.
  • DDIM ({{Song, Meng and Ermon 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02502}}): the same model, no fresh noise: keep the predicted noise as the direction and jump straight to the previous noise level, xprev = √āprev x̂0 + √(1 − āprev) ε̂. Deterministic and valid for any number of steps, because it follows the probability-flow ODE (section 4).
  • DPM-Solver++ and Euler ({{Lu et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01095}}; {{Karras et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00364}}): treat that ODE as an ODE and integrate it with better numerical methods.

What a call costs. Every step is one network evaluation, two with guidance: one tiny U-Net call takes ms for one image and ms for a batch of 1,000 on the M1 Pro's GPU; one SD 1.5 U-Net pass at 512 × 512 takes s measured (root lab recordings). Samplers differ in three ways only: which coordinates they step in (xt, σ or λ, section 4), how many calls each step costs and how they combine them (the solver's order), and whether they add fresh noise.

One family, one knob: DDIM's η. DDIM's general update interpolates between the deterministic path and DDPM: keep a share of the predicted noise as the direction and replace the rest by fresh noise; η = 0 is DDIM, η = 1 is DDPM.

DDIM with η, in code, and the check against the lab
for i, t in enumerate(ts):                       # trailing timesteps, e.g. 999, 899, ... for 10 steps
    ab = abar[t]
    ab_prev = abar[ts[i + 1]] if i + 1 < n else torch.ones_like(ab)
    eps = eps_fn(x, t)
    x0 = (x - (1 - ab).sqrt() * eps) / ab.sqrt()
    sigma = eta * ((1 - ab_prev) / (1 - ab) * (1 - ab / ab_prev)).sqrt() if i + 1 < n else 0 * ab
    x = ab_prev.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - ab_prev - sigma ** 2).clamp(min=0).sqrt() * eps
    if i + 1 < n and eta > 0:
        x = x + sigma * torch.randn(x.shape, generator=gen).to(x.device)

DPM-Solver++: solve the easy part exactly. In λ coordinates the ODE has a linear part, which can be integrated exactly, and a nonlinear part that is just the network's clean-image guess. DPM-Solver++ integrates the first exactly and approximates the second: first order (the guess held constant over the step) is exactly DDIM, and "2M" fits a line through this step's and the previous step's guesses, a second-order step for one network call.

Where the steps land. At few steps the spacing matters as much as the solver. "Trailing" spaces timesteps evenly from 999; SD 1.5's scheduler config makes diffusers' DDIM fall back to "leading", which never calls the network at 999, so its first step treats pure noise as a partly clean image: at 4 steps the root lab's recorded run got a pale, foggy scene with no lighthouse where trailing spacing gave a dark but recognisable one, same model, same noise, same number of calls measured. Karras et al.'s spacing crowds steps at low noise instead.

Why the stochastic sampler won here

Ancestral DDPM beats DDIM from 4 steps up on this model, the opposite of the DDIM paper's CIFAR-10 result. The lab's explanation was that fresh noise lets later steps correct earlier errors ({{Karras et al. 2022|https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.00364}}, section 4, make the same argument). Three tests of that explanation:

Test 1. Predict: on the exact-score toy, give the model a typical under-training error, a denoiser that hedges between bumps it cannot yet tell apart (its posterior over bumps is flattened, temperature 2). Which sampler suffers more?
DDIM. With the hedging denoiser DDIM's distance to the truth stops falling at about however many steps it takes ( at 50, at 1,000), with of samples stranded between bumps; DDPM keeps improving to with between bumps measured (gmm_toy.py, 20,000 samples): the same plateau the tiny model shows. Why: the ODE transports noise to exactly the distribution the model's denoiser implies, errors included, and a hedging denoiser implies a smeared one. The stochastic sampler re-noises and re-denoises at every step; each re-denoise at lower noise pulls the sample toward wherever the denoiser is confident, which is on a bump.

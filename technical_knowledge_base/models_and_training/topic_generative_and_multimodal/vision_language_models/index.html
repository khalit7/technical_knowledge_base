0. In one screen

A vision-language model (VLM) is a language model that has been given eyes. A vision encoder (a vision transformer, see the sibling page Vision encoders) turns an image into one vector per small square of pixels, a patch; a connector turns those vectors into a few hundred or a few thousand image tokens of the language model’s width; and the language model reads them, either as part of its input sequence or through extra attention layers. Everything on this page is a choice inside that path: how many tokens, cut from which pixels, at which positions, and trained to mean what.

The root page’s VLM lab follows one image through SmolVLM-500M step by step, counts its tokens, shows the exact prompt, and scores 30 questions and a yes/no probe. This page assumes that walk-through and goes further: the other connector designs, native resolution and 3-D positions, text and boxes and video, training recipes, hallucination, and cost. It runs a second model to make each of those concrete: Qwen3.5-0.8B (Apache 2.0, 1.75 GB in bfloat16), chosen because it differs from SmolVLM in exactly the places this page teaches (native resolution instead of tiles, 3-D positions instead of 1-D, trained to output boxes and read video, and a hybrid language model whose cache does not grow in most layers). Both run on an Apple M1 Pro laptop GPU, one at a time, in bfloat16.

1. Connectors: five ways to hand patches to a language model

The problem. The encoder gives a grid of vectors, for example 576 vectors of 1,024 numbers for LLaVA-1.5’s CLIP ViT-L at 336 pixels, or 1,024 vectors of 768 numbers per 512-pixel tile for SmolVLM. The language model expects tokens of its own width (4,096 numbers for a 7B Llama, 960 for SmolVLM’s, 1,024 for Qwen3.5-0.8B’s) and pays for every token it reads: context length, attention, cache memory and time. A connector must make the vectors readable (in the language model’s space), few (cheap), and faithful (small text and positions survive). The five designs below trade those three against each other.

One square is one vector or token, drawn to scale within each design. Pick a design, then step through: the encoder’s patch grid, what the connector does, how many tokens come out, and where the language model reads them. Grids and counts come from each model’s configuration or paper; SmolVLM’s and Qwen3.5’s are measured on the same 512 × 512 photo.

1a. Projector (LLaVA)

How it works. Each patch vector goes through the same small network, independently: first a single linear layer (“a simple linear layer to connect image features into the word embedding space”, LLaVA, Liu et al., April 2023, section 4.1), then in LLaVA-1.5 “a two-layer MLP” which “can improve LLaVA’s multimodal capabilities” (Liu et al., October 2023, section 3.3) paper. Nothing is merged: 576 patches become 576 tokens. Which encoder layer feeds it matters: LLaVA-1.5 reads the second-to-last block and drops the [CLS] token (the sibling page Vision encoders quotes the code and measures why a late, not last, layer is better).

Cost and when it matters. The cheapest connector to train (stage 1 of LLaVA trains only it) and the most faithful (every patch keeps its own token), but the most expensive to read: the token count is the patch count, so resolution is paid for quadratically. It is the right default when images are few and small.

1b. Query transformer (BLIP-2’s Q-Former)

How it works. A fixed set of learned queries (vectors that are parameters of the model, the same for every image) attend to the frozen encoder’s features through cross-attention and to each other through self-attention. BLIP-2 “use[s] 32 queries where each query has a dimension of 768”; the output “(32×768) is much smaller than the size of frozen image features (e.g. 257×1024 for ViT-L/14)”; the Q-Former is initialised from BERT-base, has cross-attention “inserted every other transformer block”, and “contains 188M parameters”. A fully connected layer then projects the 32 outputs to the language model’s width and they are “prepended to the input text embeddings” as “soft visual prompts” (Li et al., January 2023, section 3) paper.

Cost and when it matters. Thirty-two tokens whatever the image: cheap to read, but a bottleneck the paper names as such (“an information bottleneck between the frozen image encoder and the frozen LLM”). Fine text, counts and positions have to squeeze through 32 vectors chosen during pretraining, and the Q-Former needs its own pretraining stage (section 6). It suits a frozen language model and a tight budget, not documents.

1c. Resampler and gated cross-attention (Flamingo)

How it works. As the root lab quotes from the paper and checks word for word (Alayrac et al., April 2022, sections 2.1 to 2.3) paper: a frozen vision encoder; a Perceiver Resampler that “produces a fixed number of visual outputs (64)” from however many features the image or video gave; new gated cross-attention blocks between the frozen language model’s layers, each output multiplied by tanh(α) with α “a layer-specific learnable scalar initialized to 0”, so training starts exactly at the frozen language model; and each text token “attends to the visual tokens of the image that appeared just before it”. The image never enters the token sequence.

Cost and when it matters. Context stays free (many images or long video cost no sequence length) and the language model is untouched at the start. The price is new layers (a quarter of all parameters trained in Idefics2’s comparison below) and a less standard architecture that serving stacks support less well.

Read the ablation, not the headline: cross-attention against tokens in the sequence

Idefics2 is the one controlled comparison (Laurençon et al., May 2024, section 3.2 and Table 3) paper. Same backbones, same data, same updates; average score over its benchmarks:

With frozen backbones cross-attention wins; once the backbones can adapt (through LoRA, since full unfreezing “would yield loss divergences”), tokens in the sequence win, “even though the latter has more parameters”. That is why almost every open VLM since 2024 puts image tokens in the sequence. Two of the prose numbers do not match the table: the text says cross-attention “performs 7 points better” (table: 66.7 − 60.3 = 6.4) and LoRA gives “a 12.9 points increase under the fully autoregressive architecture” (table: 69.5 − 60.3 = 9.2; 0.6 for cross-attention matches) derived. The ranking holds either way; quote the table. The same paper finds its learned resampler both “increases the performance by 8.5 points” (60.3 − 51.8, matches) and cuts tokens “from 729 to 64”, and sees “no gains when using more than 64 visual tokens” (Table 4: 71.2 at 128, 71.7 at 64).

1d. Merging neighbours (pixel shuffle, patch merger)

How it works. Instead of learned queries, take each block of neighbouring patch vectors and lay them side by side into one longer vector (pixel shuffle or space-to-depth), then project. Nothing is averaged away; the projector must learn to read several positions out of one vector. InternVL 1.5 uses “a simple pixel shuffle to reduce the number of visual tokens to one-quarter”, so “a 448×448 image is represented by 256 visual tokens” (Chen et al., April 2024, section 3.1) paper. SmolVLM merges 4 × 4 = 16 patches (1,024 to 64 per tile; the root lab shows the code). Qwen2-VL merges 2 × 2 with an MLP: “a simple MLP layer is employed after the ViT to compress adjacent 2×2 tokens into a single token”, so a 224 × 224 image with 14-pixel patches becomes “66 tokens” (Wang et al., September 2024, section 2.1) paper: (224 / 14)² / 4 = 64, plus the start and end markers derived.

Cost and when it matters. A fixed compression factor (4 or 16) with no extra pretraining stage and every number kept. Combined with tiling or native resolution (section 2) it is today’s default. The more aggressive the merge, the more each token has to carry: the SmolVLM paper reports that “smaller VLMs benefit from more aggressive compression (r=4)”; section 8 measures what happens when tokens are merged further without retraining.

2. Resolution and positions: tiles against native resolution

The problem. An encoder trained on 224 or 384-pixel squares sees a phone screenshot or an A4 page squashed into that square: thin text vanishes and the aspect ratio is wrong. How native resolution works. Three changes make an encoder accept any size. (1) The image is resized only enough that both sides are multiples of the merged patch (32 pixels for Qwen3.5: 16-pixel patches, 2 × 2 merge), and its pixel count is kept inside a minimum and a maximum (Qwen3.5’s processor: 65,536 to 16,777,216 pixels, that is 64 to 16,384 tokens). The rule is smart_resize (transformers v5.20.1, models/qwen2_vl/image_processing_qwen2_vl.py line 63; ported in the Token and cache budget tab and checked against the processor on 24 sizes). (2) The encoder’s fixed position table is replaced or supplemented by 2-D RoPE (rotary position embeddings that rotate by row and by column), so any grid is legal: Qwen2-VL “modified ViT by removing the original absolute position embeddings and introducing 2D-RoPE” paper. (3) The language model is told where each token sat in the image, through 3-D positions (below).

2a. Positions in 1-D and 3-D

A language model with rotary position embeddings (RoPE, see Positional Encodings) gives each token one integer position. SmolVLM’s language model does exactly that for image tokens: they are positions k, k+1, ..., so the token below a given one is 8 positions away inside a tile and the global view sits far from the tiles; the row and column survive only through the <row_r_col_c> text tags the processor inserts. M-RoPE (multimodal RoPE) splits the rotary dimensions into three groups for time, height and width: “For text inputs, these components utilize identical position IDs, making M-RoPE functionally equivalent to 1D-RoPE”, while for an image “the temporal IDs of each visual token remain constant, while distinct IDs are assigned to the height and width components”, and the next modality starts at “the maximum position ID of the preceding modality” plus one (Wang et al., section 2.1) paper. Qwen3-VL keeps the three groups but interleaves them across the frequency bands, because “chunking the embedding dimensions into temporal (t), horizontal (h), and vertical (w) groups induces an imbalanced frequency spectrum” (Qwen3-VL Technical Report, section 1) paper; Qwen3.5-0.8B’s config says "mrope_interleaved": true, "mrope_section": [11, 11, 10].

Predict, then reveal. Qwen3.5 reads a 512 × 512 photo as 256 image tokens (16 × 16) starting at position 4. Which position does the first text token after the image get?

Why it matters. Positions are a budget too. With 1-D positions a 4,096-token image consumes 4,096 positions of the trained context; with 3-D positions it consumes about 64. The paper gives this as a reason: M-RoPE “reduces the value of position IDs for images and videos, enabling the model to extrapolate to longer sequences during inference”. The context window in tokens (memory, compute) is unchanged: every image token is still attended to.

How to check it. Open the What the model saw tab: pick any token of any image and read its position, 1-D for SmolVLM and (t, h, w) for Qwen3.5, as the models’ own code computes them (get_rope_index in models/qwen3_5/modeling_qwen3_5.py, line 1364).

3. Reading text in images: OCR and documents

The problem. Reading text (OCR, optical character recognition) is where a VLM’s resolution budget shows first. A character must cover enough pixels, after every resize the processor does, for the encoder’s 14 or 16-pixel patches to tell an 8 from a B; and after merging, several characters share one token. A model can describe a photo well from 64 tokens and still be unable to read a receipt.

The test here measured. 21 synthetic 1,024 × 1,024 pages, each with one random 6-character code (letters and digits, no 0/O or 1/I so the answer is unambiguous, no words so the language model cannot guess) at 7 font sizes, from 5 to 32 pixels tall for capitals; each read at three token budgets per model. The question: “What is the 6-character code written in the image? Answer with the code only.” Scored by exact match and by character error rate (CER: edits needed to turn the answer into the code, divided by 6). The crops show each size at 1:1:

Why the budgets behave this way. What decides legibility is pixels per character after the processor, and how many characters share a token. At SmolVLM’s 64-token setting the 1,024-pixel page is shrunk to 512, so a 9-pixel capital becomes 4.5 pixels, under a third of a 16-pixel patch, and each token covers 64 × 64 pixels of the shrunk page derived: 16-pixel patches × 4 × 4 merge. At its default setting the page is enlarged to 2,048 (two pixels per original pixel) and cut into 16 tiles. Qwen3.5 at 1,024 tokens sees the page at its own size, one token per 32 × 32 pixels. Equal token budgets can therefore give different legibility: tiles enlarge, native resolution does not.

When it matters, and the published picture. Documents, charts, screenshots and GUIs are the workloads where VLMs are bought, so model cards lead with OCR benchmarks: Qwen3.5-0.8B’s card gives OCRBench 74.5 / 79.1 (thinking / non-thinking) and OmniDocBench1.5 61.0 / 70.6 (Qwen3.5-0.8B card, read 2026-10-17) vendor; what those benchmarks measure, and their pitfalls, is on Long-context and multimodal benchmarks. Turning PDFs into text for retrieval (where a VLM is one option among layout parsers and OCR engines) is on Document ingestion and multimodal retrieval.

How to check it on your own documents. Render a page at the resolution your users send, count tokens with the processor, and test the smallest text that matters at two or three budgets; a code like the ones above (no words, no prior) separates seeing from guessing.

4. Pointing: grounding and bounding boxes

The problem. Grounding means tying words to places in the image: “where is the red circle?” answered with a bounding box (left, top, right, bottom). A language model can only answer in text, so boxes are written as numbers in text, and the model and the code that draws the box must agree on what the numbers mean. Qwen’s own models have changed that meaning twice:

The test here measured. 12 synthetic scenes in four shapes (640 × 480, 480 × 640, 800 × 400, 512 × 512) with two of a red circle, a blue square and a green triangle each, exact boxes known. Prompt: The same raw answers are scored under three readings of the numbers: 0 to 1,000 of the image, pixels of the resized image the encoder saw, and pixels of the original. IoU (intersection over union) is the overlap of predicted and true box divided by their union: 1 is perfect, above 0.5 is the usual “correct” threshold.

How to check it. Before trusting a model’s boxes, draw them on two or three images of different aspect ratios under each documented convention; the right one lines up on all of them, and a wrong one fails worst on the non-square images. When the processor resized the image, keep its output size (image_grid_thw × patch size) next to the answer.

5. Video: frames become tokens

The problem. A minute of video at 30 frames per second is 1,800 images. Even at 256 tokens each that is 460,800 tokens derived. Video VLMs sample a few frames per second, merge pairs of frames into one token, and cap the total.

How Qwen does it. Frames are sampled (Qwen2-VL: “two frames per second”), and the encoder’s patch embedding is a 3-D convolution of depth 2 over consecutive frames, “allowing the model to handle 3D tubes instead of 2D patches”; a still image is “treated as two identical frames”, and Qwen2-VL’s training limited “the total number of tokens per video to 16384” (section 2.1) paper. Time positions moved from frame counting (Qwen2-VL) to absolute time inside M-RoPE (Qwen2.5-VL) to plain text: Qwen3-VL prefixes each temporal patch with “a timestamp expressed as a formatted text string”, for example “<3.0 seconds>” (Qwen3-VL report, section 2.3) paper. Qwen3.5’s processor does the same; this is the real prompt for one 4-second clip sampled at 2 frames per second, with each run of video tokens written as “x80” measured:



Token arithmetic. Tokens per second = (frames sampled per second / 2) × (height / 32) × (width / 32), plus a few timestamp tokens per pair of frames derived. A 320 × 240 clip at 2 frames per second costs 80 tokens per second; a 1,280 × 720 clip costs 720, because the processor caps each frame at 768 tokens and shrinks the frames to 1,152 × 640 measured with the processor (the Token and cache budget tab computes any case).

6. Training recipes: stages, frozen parts, data


The problem. A VLM is built from two pretrained parts that have never met, joined by a randomly initialised connector. Training everything at once lets the random connector send noise into a good language model; training too little leaves the image tokens unreadable. Every recipe answers three questions: which parts train in which stage, on what data, and how long. The root lab explains LLaVA’s two stages (align the projector with everything frozen, then instruction-tune projector and language model); the recipes since then differ in what they unfreeze and how much data they use paper:

The pattern. A short alignment stage with only the connector training survives everywhere (LLaVA, Qwen3-VL’s S0); after it, recent recipes train everything, the encoder included, on a trillion tokens or more, with text-only data mixed in so the language model does not forget. Image-caption pairs teach what is in an image; interleaved web pages teach several images in context; OCR, chart, document and grounding data teach reading and pointing; instruction and conversation data teach answering. The loss is on text tokens only: the model never predicts image tokens (that is the subject of the sibling page Native multimodal and any-to-any models).


6a. Was SmolVLM’s encoder trained inside the VLM? measured


The SmolVLM paper names its encoder (“SigLIP-B/16”) but the passages read do not say whether it was frozen. The weights can say. SmolVLM’s vision tower has the same shape as Google’s public google/siglip-base-patch16-512 (12 blocks, width 768, 1,024 positions; the public checkpoint adds a pooling head a VLM does not use), so each tensor can be compared directly: the relative change ‖WSmolVLM − WSigLIP‖ / ‖WSigLIP‖, averaged over each block weighted by size.






Why freeze or not. Freezing the encoder saves memory (no optimizer state for it) and protects features learned from billions of pairs; training it lets the encoder learn what the language model needs (small text, positions, the processor’s tiles). Idefics2 found that adapting the backbones (with LoRA) beat freezing them by 9.2 points in its own table (section 1), and the recipes above that train the encoder do so after an alignment stage, usually at a lower learning rate.






7. Hallucination: when the language model answers instead of the image


The problem. A VLM writes the most likely text given the image tokens and the words so far. When the visual evidence is weak (a small object, a low budget, a confusing scene) or the question suggests an answer, the language model’s habits win: it names objects that usually appear with what is there, or agrees with the question. This is object hallucination. POPE (Li et al., May 2023) turned it into a yes/no test, “Is there a <object> in the image?”, over present objects and absent ones drawn at random, from the most frequent objects (popular) or from objects that co-occur with what is there (adversarial), and showed that “LVLMs are prone to hallucinate objects which frequently appear or co-occur in the visual instruction dataset” paper. The older caption metric CHAIR (share of mentioned objects that are not in the image) moved with the prompt: two similar instructions gave up to “doubled values of CHAIR metrics”, one reason POPE asks short questions.


Measuring it better than counting answers measured. The root lab parsed SmolVLM’s typed answers to the 72 yes/no questions. Here both models are scored by the probability they put on “yes” at the first answer token, P(yes) = mass on yes tokens / (mass on yes + no tokens), which shows how sure each answer is and lets the threshold move. And each question is asked four ways: with the image; with a plain grey image; with no image at all (the language model’s prior alone); and with the leading phrasing “There is <object> in the image, isn’t there?”.






What reduces it published, not run here. More image tokens where it matters (LLaVA-NeXT’s blog says higher resolution “reduces the model hallucination that conjectures the imagined visual content when confronted with low-resolution images”); training data with negative and “not visible” answers; preference training against hallucinated captions; shorter answers. The full table, per model and condition, with a threshold you can move, is the Hallucination probe tab.






8. What image tokens cost: context, cache, time


The problem. Image tokens are ordinary tokens to the language model: they take context, they are attended to by every later token, and their keys and values are cached for the whole conversation. On top of that the encoder runs once per image.


The cache. For a standard transformer the KV cache (the stored keys and values of every past token) costs per token: 2 (key and value) × layers × KV heads × head width × bytes per number derived. From the configs: SmolVLM-500M 2 × 32 × 5 × 64 × 2 = 40,960 bytes per token; LLaVA-1.5-7B (32 layers, 32 KV heads of 128) 524,288 bytes, so one 576-token image holds 302 MB of cache. Qwen3.5-0.8B is a hybrid: of its 24 layers only 6 are attention layers with a growing cache (“6 × (3 × (Gated DeltaNet → FFN) → 1 × (Gated Attention → FFN))”, card vendor); the other 18 are linear-attention layers (Gated DeltaNet, a relative of the state-space models on the Mamba page) that keep a fixed-size state whatever the length. 8a. Fewer image tokens without retraining measured


If most image tokens carry little, can they be dropped at inference? FastV (Chen et al., March 2024) observed that after the second layer image tokens receive little attention in LLaVA-1.5 (“image tokens take up most of the input tokens (64%)”) and pruned half of them at layer 2, ranked by “the average attention-score one token received from all other tokens”, reporting “a 45% reduction in FLOPs for LLaVA-1.5-13B” without loss on its benchmarks paper. Here SmolVLM answers the root’s 30 questions with its 1,088 image tokens cut four ways before the language model: at random; by FastV’s attention ranking (computed at layer 2, but the tokens are removed from the input rather than after layer 2, a simplification); by averaging each 2 × 2 block of tokens (a merge the model was never trained on); and by dropping the 64-token global view.






When it matters. Token count drives prefill time and cache size linearly (and attention quadratically at long lengths), so a VLM serving screenshots or video is usually bound by image tokens. The cheapest lever is the processor setting (fewer tiles, lower maximum pixels), then a model trained with a stronger merge; training-free pruning is a third option whose loss must be measured on your questions, as above.






9. Serving: what a server caches and shares


A serving engine (see Topic: inference-and-serving and Batching, KV cache and scheduling in depth) handles a VLM request in three costs: preprocessing (decode, resize, tile), the encoder, and the language model’s prefill and decode. vLLM’s documentation (latest, read 2026-10-17) vendor describes what it does about each:


    

  • Preprocessing and encoder outputs are cached by content: “vLLM normally hashes each media item by content to enable caching across requests”; a client may pass its own stable IDs (multi_modal_uuids) to skip re-hashing, or even skip sending the image when a cache hit is expected (multimodal inputs). The processor cache defaults to 4 GiB (mm_processor_cache_gb) (optimization).
    • 

  • The encoder is small next to the language model, so with tensor parallelism vLLM can shard the batch of images instead of the encoder’s weights (mm_encoder_tp_mode="data"), which “has been shown to improve the throughput and TTFT by around 10% for tensor_parallel_size=8”.
    • 

  • Limits per prompt (limit_mm_per_prompt) bound how many images or videos a request may carry, since each one is a block of tokens and cache.
    • 

  • Video tokens can be pruned after the encoder (--video-pruning-rate), “at some cost in accuracy”, by dropping tokens that barely change from the previous frame.
    • 



Prefix caching with images. A second question about the same image reuses the cached keys and values of the shared prefix (system prompt, image, earlier turns) only if the image tokens are identical, which content hashing guarantees; putting the image first and the question after it makes the shared prefix longest. Measured here at the scale of one laptop, the encoder is a large share of a short answer (section 8), which is exactly the share a cache hit removes.






Common mistakes (with the real outputs)


Calling the image processor instead of the processor (InternVL)
The InternVL3-1B-hf repository ships "crop_to_patches": false in its image-processor config; the InternVLProcessor overrides it to True (models/internvl/processing_internvl.py line 34, v5.20.1). Call the image processor alone and dynamic tiling silently disappears:  measured with the processors. Always build inputs with the model’s processor and count the tokens it produced.


Drawing boxes in the wrong coordinate system
Qwen2-VL and Qwen3-VL write boxes on a 0 to 1,000 scale; Qwen2.5-VL writes pixels of the resized image (section 4). 


Passing video frames without their frame rate
Qwen3.5’s video processor needs to know the clip’s frame rate to sample at 2 frames per second. Without metadata transformers warns, verbatim: “Asked to sample `fps` frames per second but no video metadata was provided which is required when sampling with `fps`. Defaulting to `fps=24`.” On this page’s 16-frame, 4-second clip that made the processor treat it as 0.67 seconds: 2 temporal patches (4 frames kept of 16) and a first timestamp of <0.1 seconds>, against 4 patches spread over 0.2 to 3.5 seconds with video_metadata set measured with the processor. Pass the real frame rate and duration.


Writing the prompt by hand
The processor expands one placeholder per image into hundreds of image tokens; without the placeholder there is nowhere to put the image. SmolVLM’s processor refuses, verbatim: “ValueError: The total number of <image> tokens in the prompts should be the same as the number of images passed. Found [0] <image> tokens and [0, 1] images per sample.” Qwen3.5’s processor accepts the same mistake without a word and returns a prompt with no image tokens;  Use apply_chat_template with an image entry, then the processor.


Expecting a training-free token cut to be free
 Methods that report no loss (FastV) were measured on larger models and on benchmarks; measure on your own questions before shipping a pruning rate.


Scoring yes/no answers by parsing text only
A parsed “yes” hides how close the call was, and a model that answers neither yes nor no gets miscounted. Read the probability on yes and no tokens at the first answer position (section 7): the yes rate on absent objects, its confidence, and the share of probability on neither are all visible.


Comparing models at their default settings and calling it a model comparison
At defaults SmolVLM spends 1,088 tokens on a 512-pixel square and Qwen3.5 spends 256; on a 1,024-pixel page, 1,088 and 1,024; on a 64-pixel icon, 1,088 and 64 (section 2, measured). Compare at matched token budgets as well as at defaults, and say which.


Running a model with a decoding setting it was not tuned for, without saying so
Qwen3.5-0.8B’s card recommends sampling for vision tasks (“temperature=0.7, top_p=0.80, top_k=20, min_p=0.0, presence_penalty=1.5”) vendor; this page decodes greedily, so every answer is reproducible but may differ from the vendor’s setting. Report the decoding with every score.






Interview questions


Why do most open VLMs put image tokens in the sequence instead of using Flamingo-style cross-attention?
Idefics2’s controlled comparison: with frozen backbones cross-attention wins (66.7 against 60.3), but once the backbones adapt (LoRA) tokens in the sequence win (69.5 against 67.3) with fewer new parameters, and any language model can be used unchanged by serving stacks. Cross-attention keeps context free, which still matters for many images or long video.


Q-Former against an MLP projector: what do you gain and lose?
The Q-Former gives a fixed 32 tokens per image (cheap context, works with a frozen language model) at the price of a bottleneck the paper names, a 188M-parameter module and an extra pretraining stage; text and fine detail suffer. An MLP projector keeps every patch (576 for LLaVA-1.5), so it is faithful but expensive per image; merging neighbours (pixel shuffle) is the middle path used today.


How many tokens does a 1,920 × 1,080 screenshot cost, and how would you cut it?
It depends on the processor: 576 squashed (LLaVA-1.5), 832 (SmolVLM, stretched to 4 × 3 tiles), 2,304 (InternVL, 8 tiles plus a thumbnail), 2,040 (Qwen3.5 native) here. Cut with the processor first (fewer tiles, a lower max-pixels cap), then a model trained with a stronger merge; training-free pruning last, measured on your questions.


What is M-RoPE and what does it buy?
Rotary positions split into time, height and width groups; text tokens get the same value in all three (so it is ordinary RoPE for text), image tokens get their row and column, video frames their time. An image advances the position counter by its side length, not its token count (256 tokens advanced it by 16 here), so long multimodal inputs use far fewer positions; Qwen3-VL interleaves the three groups across frequencies.


How do you measure hallucination in a VLM?
POPE-style yes/no questions on present and absent objects (random, popular, adversarial), scored by the yes rate on absent objects, ideally from P(yes) so confidence is visible; a no-image and a blank-image baseline to expose the language prior; a leading-question variant; and CHAIR-style counting of non-existent objects in free descriptions, knowing it moves with the prompt.


Estimate the KV cache of one image in a 7B VLM.
Bytes per token = 2 × layers × KV heads × head width × 2 bytes: LLaVA-1.5-7B is 2 × 32 × 32 × 128 × 2 = 524,288 bytes, so 576 image tokens hold 302 MB. Grouped-query attention divides that by the head-sharing factor; a hybrid with linear-attention layers (Qwen3.5) caches only its attention layers.


Design: a VLM that extracts fields and their locations from invoices.
Native-resolution or tiled encoder sized so the smallest printed text has several pixels per stroke after resizing (test legibility first, section 3); count tokens per page and cap them; a model trained for grounding, with its coordinate convention verified on non-square pages; training data of documents with boxes, plus negatives (“field absent”) against hallucinated fields; evaluate exact match per field, box IoU, and the absent-field false-positive rate; cache the page encoding so several questions about one invoice reuse it.


Design: question answering over 10-minute videos on one GPU.
Sample 1 to 2 frames per second, merge frame pairs and 2 × 2 patches, and cap tokens per video (Qwen2-VL trained with 16,384); add timestamps so answers can cite times. Know what your processor does to long inputs: at 320 × 240 and 2 frames per second a short clip costs 80 tokens per second, but for 10 minutes Qwen3.5’s processor keeps at most 768 frames and shrinks each to 192 × 128, so the whole video becomes 9,216 tokens (measured with the processor), which may be too blurry to answer. Choose fps and resolution per question type, prune near-duplicate frames or tokens (vLLM’s video pruning), cache the encoded video across questions, and measure accuracy against tokens.

5. Video: frames become tokens, time becomes text

A minute of video at 30 frames per second is 1,800 images; even at 256 tokens each that is 460,800 tokens derived. Video VLMs sample a few frames per second, merge pairs of frames into one token, and cap the total. Qwen2-VL samples “two frames per second”; its patch embedding is a 3-D convolution of depth 2 over consecutive frames (“3D tubes instead of 2D patches”; a still image is “treated as two identical frames”), and training capped “the total number of tokens per video to 16384” ({{section 2.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12191}}) paper. Time positions moved from frame counting (Qwen2-VL) to absolute time inside M-RoPE (Qwen2.5-VL) to plain text: Qwen3-VL prefixes each temporal patch with “a timestamp expressed as a formatted text string”, such as “<3.0 seconds>” ({{Qwen3-VL report, section 2.3|https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21631}}) paper.

Qwen3.5’s processor does the same. The real prompt for one 4-second clip sampled at 2 frames per second, timestamps <0.2 seconds> to <3.5 seconds>, each run of 80 video tokens written “x80” measured:



Token arithmetic derived: tokens per second = (frames sampled per second / 2) × (height / 32) × (width / 32), plus a few timestamp tokens per pair of frames. A 320 × 240 clip at 2 frames per second costs 80 tokens per second; a 1,280 × 720 clip costs 720, because the processor caps each frame at 768 tokens and shrinks the frames to 1,152 × 640 measured with the processor. The {{Token budget|#t-vmbud}} tab computes any case.


The test measured: 8 synthetic clips (16 frames, 4 seconds) of a red or blue dot crossing the frame in one of four directions, asked which way it moves; as a control, the same question about the first frame alone.




Qwen3.5 got the direction right in 0 of 8 clips; in 7 it named exactly the opposite direction. Each clip is 320 video tokens (4 temporal patches of 80), 400 prompt tokens in all. The one-frame control got 4 of 8, all horizontal: with the dot near one edge, “it moves away from that edge” is a good guess. The processor keeps the frames in order (decoding the dot from pixel_values_videos, it moves from x = 40 to 248 in the left-to-right clip), so the reversal is the model’s. A 0.8B model on synthetic dots is a narrow probe (the vendor reports VideoMME 63.8, thinking, with subtitles vendor), but direction of motion is what a video model must not get backwards, and checking it costs 16 frames.






6. Training: align the connector, then train everything


A VLM joins two pretrained parts that have never met through a randomly initialised connector. Training everything at once lets the random connector send noise into a good language model; training too little leaves the image tokens unreadable. Every recipe answers which parts train in which stage, on what data, and for how long:




After a short connector-only stage, recent recipes train the encoder too. One row per published recipe, one column per stage; E encoder, C connector, L language model paper. “Not stated” means the paper’s text does not say for that part; BLIP-2’s first stage has no language model at all; Qwen3-VL’s S2 and S3 then extend the context to 32,768 and 262,144 tokens; SmolVLM’s two stages (“(1) a vision stage, and (2) a video stage”) are left out because the passages read do not say what is frozen.


The pattern. A short alignment stage with only the connector training survives everywhere (LLaVA’s stage 1, Qwen3-VL’s S0); after it, recent recipes train everything, the encoder included, on a trillion tokens or more, with text-only data mixed in so the language model does not forget. Image-caption pairs teach what is in an image; interleaved web pages teach several images in context; OCR, chart, document and grounding data teach reading and pointing; instruction and conversation data teach answering. The loss is on text tokens only: the model never predicts image tokens (that is {{Native multimodal and any-to-any models|n:3c65c17b0d0d8139bd6cd4137dd2a4d8}}).


Freeze or train the encoder? Freezing saves memory (no optimiser state for it) and protects features learned from billions of pairs; training lets the encoder learn what the language model needs (small text, positions, the processor’s tiles). Idefics2 found that adapting the backbones with LoRA beat freezing them by 9.2 points in its own table (section 1), and the recipes that train the encoder do so after an alignment stage, usually at a lower learning rate. The weights show it happened in SmolVLM: its encoder moved 0.23 to 0.37 of its norm away from the public SigLIP checkpoint it started from (below).

Was SmolVLM’s encoder trained inside the VLM? The weights say yes (measured)


The SmolVLM paper names its encoder (“SigLIP-B/16”) but the passages read do not say whether it was frozen. Its vision tower has the same shape as Google’s public google/siglip-base-patch16-512 (12 blocks, width 768, 1,024 positions; the public checkpoint adds a pooling head a VLM does not use), so each tensor can be compared directly: the relative change ‖WSmolVLM − WSigLIP‖ / ‖WSigLIP‖, averaged over each block weighted by size measured.




Every tensor changed: the transformer blocks by 0.23 (block 0) to 0.37 (block 11) of their norm, rising with depth, the patch and position embeddings by 0.30, the final LayerNorm by 0.02. Rounding to bfloat16 accounts for well under 0.01, and two unrelated checkpoints of this shape would differ by about 1.4 (the norm of a difference of two independent tensors of equal norm is √2 times the norm). So SmolVLM’s encoder started from this checkpoint (or one very close to it; inferred from the match, not stated in the passages read) and was trained inside the VLM, the deep blocks most; it also lost the public checkpoint’s mild fixed-position outliers (section 1).

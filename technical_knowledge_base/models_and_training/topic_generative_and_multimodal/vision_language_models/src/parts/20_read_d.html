3. Reading text in images: legibility is pixels per character after the processor

Reading text (OCR, optical character recognition) is where the resolution budget shows first. A character must cover enough pixels, after every resize the processor does, for 14 or 16-pixel patches to tell an 8 from a B; after merging, several characters share one token. A model can describe a photo well from 64 tokens and still be unable to read a receipt.

The test measured: 21 synthetic 1,024 × 1,024 pages, each with one random 6-character code (no 0/O or 1/I, no words, so the language model cannot guess) at 7 font sizes, from 5 to 32 pixels tall for capitals, each read at three token budgets per model; “What is the 6-character code written in the image? Answer with the code only.” Scored by exact match and character error rate (CER: edits needed to turn the answer into the code, over 6).

Fig. 4: one 20-pixel code (14-pixel capitals) at the scale each setting gives the page

  1. Qwen3.5 at 1,024 tokens: the page at its own size. Capitals are 14 pixels tall, a little under one 16-pixel patch (a grid cell); one token covers 2 × 2 patches, 32 × 32 pixels. It read this code, and all 3 codes at this size.
  2. Qwen3.5 at 256 tokens: the page halved to 512. Capitals 7 pixels, under half a patch; still 3 of 3 read exactly.
  3. Qwen3.5 at 64 tokens: the page quartered to 256. Capitals 3.5 pixels, and one token now covers 128 × 128 pixels of the original page derived. 0 of 3; for PRZ6C7 it answered “PREDCT”.
  4. SmolVLM at 64 tokens: one 512 view. The same 7-pixel capitals as Qwen3.5 at 256, but each SmolVLM token merges 4 × 4 patches (64 × 64 pixels of the shrunk page): 0 of 3 exact, answers one or two characters off (“PR28C7.”).
  5. SmolVLM at 1,088 tokens: enlarged to 2,048 and cut into 16 tiles. Capitals 28 pixels, nearly two patches; 1 of 3 exact. Enlarging adds no detail: once the characters clear the patch, what is left is the model.

The crop is the page’s 256 × 128 pixels around the code at 1:1, resampled in the browser at each setting’s scale (the scale is exact; the interpolation only approximates the processor’s). Answers and counts measured.

Why the budgets behave this way. At SmolVLM’s 64-token setting the page is shrunk to 512, so a 9-pixel capital becomes 4.5 pixels, under a third of a 16-pixel patch, and each token covers 64 × 64 pixels of the shrunk page derived: 16-pixel patches × 4 × 4 merge. At its default setting the page is enlarged to 2,048 and cut into 16 tiles. Qwen3.5 at 1,024 tokens sees the page at its own size, one token per 32 × 32 pixels. Equal token budgets can give different legibility: tiles enlarge, native resolution does not. So SmolVLM at 64 tokens still reads 20-pixel text almost as well as at 1,088 (mean CER 0.22 against 0.11), because its single view keeps 512 pixels where Qwen3.5’s 64-token budget keeps 256; and Qwen3.5 at 64 tokens reads nothing below 28 px, answering “000000”, “666666” or invented strings of the right length.

Codes read exactly, at every size and budget

Smallest legible font, an inversion at 1,024 tokens, and the vendor’s OCR numbers (detail)

Smallest font from which at least 2 of 3 codes are read exactly at every larger size: SmolVLM 28 px (19 px capitals) at 1,088 tokens; Qwen3.5 20 px (14 px capitals) at 256 and none at 1,024, because Qwen3.5 at 1,024 tokens fails the largest text (0 of 3 at 40 px, answering “600000”) while reading it at 64 and 256: a 152-pixel-wide code on a 1,024-pixel page at full resolution is a case its training apparently covered less. More tokens are not monotonically better measured.

Model cards lead with OCR benchmarks because documents, charts, screenshots and GUIs are where VLMs are bought: Qwen3.5-0.8B’s card gives OCRBench 74.5 / 79.1 (thinking / non-thinking) and OmniDocBench1.5 61.0 / 70.6 ({{Qwen3.5-0.8B card|https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B}}, read 2026-10-17) vendor. What those benchmarks measure is on {{Long-context and multimodal benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d810bb4dfc98919c9be8a}}; turning PDFs into text for retrieval, where a VLM is one option among layout parsers and OCR engines, is on {{Document ingestion and multimodal retrieval|n:3f85c17b0d0d81279079f614f382f2e7}}.

How to check it on your documents. Render a page at the resolution your users send, count tokens with the processor, and test the smallest text that matters at two or three budgets; a code with no words and no prior separates seeing from guessing.

4. Pointing: grounding and the coordinate convention

Grounding ties words to places: “where is the red circle?” answered with a bounding box (left, top, right, bottom). A language model answers in text, so boxes are numbers in text, and the model and the code that draws the box must agree on what the numbers mean. Qwen’s own models changed that meaning twice:

ModelBox coordinates in the textSource
Qwen2-VL“normalized within [0, 1000)”, written “(Xtop left, Ytop left),(Xbottom right, Ybottom right)” between box tokens{{section 2.2.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2409.12191}} paper
Qwen2.5-VL“Unlike traditional approaches that normalize coordinates, our model directly uses the actual dimensions of the input image”: pixels of the image as resized by the processor{{section 2.1.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2502.13923}} paper
Qwen3-VL“Different from Qwen2.5-VL, we adopt a normalized coordinate system scaled to the range [0,1000]”{{section 3.2.4|https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21631}} paper

The test measured: 12 synthetic scenes in four shapes (640 × 480, 480 × 640, 800 × 400, 512 × 512), each with two of a red circle, a blue square and a green triangle, exact boxes known; prompt Locate the <shape> in the image. Report its bounding box as JSON: {"bbox_2d": [x1, y1, x2, y2]}. The same raw answers are scored under three readings of the numbers: 0 to 1,000 of the image, pixels of the resized image the encoder saw, and pixels of the original. IoU (intersection over union) is the overlap of predicted and true box over their union: 1 is perfect, above 0.5 the usual “correct”.

Fig. 5: the same four numbers, read three ways

  1. The question. Scene 3 of the 12, 800 × 400 pixels, which Qwen3.5’s processor resizes to 800 × 384; the true box of the red circle is dashed green.
  2. The answer is text. Qwen3.5 writes four numbers, [316, 300, 368, 360], in its JSON answer; nothing in them says what they are measured in.
  3. Read as 0 to 1,000 of the image (Qwen2-VL’s and Qwen3-VL’s convention): the box shrinks towards the top-left corner and misses the circle, IoU 0.00.
  4. Read as pixels of the original 800 × 400: close, but the box sits too high, IoU 0.54.
  5. Read as pixels of the resized 800 × 384 image, mapped back (Qwen2.5-VL’s convention): IoU 0.78, the best of the three.
  6. SmolVLM. It writes [0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 1.0], a dot at the origin under every reading: IoU 0.

Qwen3.5 writes boxes in pixels, not on Qwen3-VL’s 0 to 1,000 scale measured: mean IoU over the 24 boxes is 0.49 read as resized-image pixels, 0.49 as original pixels and 0.06 as 0 to 1,000; 11 of 24 boxes reach IoU 0.5 under the best reading. The two pixel readings differ only on the 800 × 400 scenes (the processor makes them 800 × 384), and there the resized reading fits better: the Qwen2.5-VL convention. The misses are mostly a right edge or bottom far too large (a blue square of 143 pixels reported 650 wide). SmolVLM writes boxes that mean nothing: all 24 answers are tiny boxes at the origin such as {"bbox_2d": [0, 0, 2, 2]}, IoU 0 under every reading; its training data did not teach it boxes.

All 12 scenes and 24 boxes, under each reading (the full test)

How to check it. Draw a model’s boxes on two or three images of different aspect ratios under each documented convention: the right one lines up on all of them, and a wrong one fails worst on non-square images. When the processor resized the image, keep its output size (image_grid_thw × patch size) next to the answer.

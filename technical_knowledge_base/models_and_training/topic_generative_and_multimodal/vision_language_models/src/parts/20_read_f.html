7. Hallucination: when the language model answers instead of the image

The problem. A VLM writes the most likely text given the image tokens and the words so far. When the visual evidence is weak (a small object, a low budget, a confusing scene) or the question suggests an answer, the language model’s habits win: it names objects that usually appear with what is there, or agrees with the question. This is object hallucination. POPE ({{Li et al., May 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10355}}) turned it into a yes/no test, “Is there a <object> in the image?”, over present objects and absent ones drawn at random, from the most frequent objects (popular) or from objects that co-occur with what is there (adversarial), and showed that “LVLMs are prone to hallucinate objects which frequently appear or co-occur in the visual instruction dataset” paper. The older caption metric CHAIR (share of mentioned objects that are not in the image) moved with the prompt: two similar instructions gave up to “doubled values of CHAIR metrics”, one reason POPE asks short questions.

Measuring it better than counting answers measured. The root lab parsed SmolVLM’s typed answers to the 72 yes/no questions. Here both models are scored by the probability they put on “yes” at the first answer token, P(yes) = mass on yes tokens / (mass on yes + no tokens), which shows how sure each answer is and lets the threshold move. And each question is asked four ways: with the image; with a plain grey image; with no image at all (the language model’s prior alone); and with the leading phrasing “There is <object> in the image, isn’t there?”.

What reduces it published, not run here. More image tokens where it matters (LLaVA-NeXT’s blog says higher resolution “reduces the model hallucination that conjectures the imagined visual content when confronted with low-resolution images”); training data with negative and “not visible” answers; preference training against hallucinated captions; shorter answers. The full table, per model and condition, with a threshold you can move, is the {{Hallucination probe|#t-vmhal}} tab.

8. What image tokens cost: context, cache, time

The problem. Image tokens are ordinary tokens to the language model: they take context, they are attended to by every later token, and their keys and values are cached for the whole conversation. On top of that the encoder runs once per image.

The cache. For a standard transformer the KV cache (the stored keys and values of every past token) costs per token: 2 (key and value) × layers × KV heads × head width × bytes per number derived. From the configs: SmolVLM-500M 2 × 32 × 5 × 64 × 2 = 40,960 bytes per token; LLaVA-1.5-7B (32 layers, 32 KV heads of 128) 524,288 bytes, so one 576-token image holds 302 MB of cache. Qwen3.5-0.8B is a hybrid: of its 24 layers only 6 are attention layers with a growing cache (“6 × (3 × (Gated DeltaNet → FFN) → 1 × (Gated Attention → FFN))”, {{card|https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B}} vendor); the other 18 are linear-attention layers (Gated DeltaNet, a relative of the state-space models on the {{Mamba|n:3c65c17b0d0d8179984fc3a1fee4c36a}} page) that keep a fixed-size state whatever the length. 8a. Fewer image tokens without retraining measured

If most image tokens carry little, can they be dropped at inference? FastV ({{Chen et al., March 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06764}}) observed that after the second layer image tokens receive little attention in LLaVA-1.5 (“image tokens take up most of the input tokens (64%)”) and pruned half of them at layer 2, ranked by “the average attention-score one token received from all other tokens”, reporting “a 45% reduction in FLOPs for LLaVA-1.5-13B” without loss on its benchmarks paper. Here SmolVLM answers the root’s 30 questions with its 1,088 image tokens cut four ways before the language model: at random; by FastV’s attention ranking (computed at layer 2, but the tokens are removed from the input rather than after layer 2, a simplification); by averaging each 2 × 2 block of tokens (a merge the model was never trained on); and by dropping the 64-token global view.

When it matters. Token count drives prefill time and cache size linearly (and attention quadratically at long lengths), so a VLM serving screenshots or video is usually bound by image tokens. The cheapest lever is the processor setting (fewer tiles, lower maximum pixels), then a model trained with a stronger merge; training-free pruning is a third option whose loss must be measured on your questions, as above.

9. Serving: what a server caches and shares

A serving engine (see {{Topic: inference-and-serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}} and {{Batching, KV cache and scheduling in depth|n:3c65c17b0d0d819db53bd2c48909e5b2}}) handles a VLM request in three costs: preprocessing (decode, resize, tile), the encoder, and the language model’s prefill and decode. vLLM’s documentation (latest, read 2026-10-17) vendor describes what it does about each:

  • Preprocessing and encoder outputs are cached by content: “vLLM normally hashes each media item by content to enable caching across requests”; a client may pass its own stable IDs (multi_modal_uuids) to skip re-hashing, or even skip sending the image when a cache hit is expected ({{multimodal inputs|https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/multimodal_inputs.html}}). The processor cache defaults to 4 GiB (mm_processor_cache_gb) ({{optimization|https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/configuration/optimization.html}}).
  • The encoder is small next to the language model, so with tensor parallelism vLLM can shard the batch of images instead of the encoder’s weights (mm_encoder_tp_mode="data"), which “has been shown to improve the throughput and TTFT by around 10% for tensor_parallel_size=8”.
  • Limits per prompt (limit_mm_per_prompt) bound how many images or videos a request may carry, since each one is a block of tokens and cache.
  • Video tokens can be pruned after the encoder (--video-pruning-rate), “at some cost in accuracy”, by dropping tokens that barely change from the previous frame.

Prefix caching with images. A second question about the same image reuses the cached keys and values of the shared prefix (system prompt, image, earlier turns) only if the image tokens are identical, which content hashing guarantees; putting the image first and the question after it makes the shared prefix longest. Measured here at the scale of one laptop, the encoder is a large share of a short answer (section 8), which is exactly the share a cache hit removes.

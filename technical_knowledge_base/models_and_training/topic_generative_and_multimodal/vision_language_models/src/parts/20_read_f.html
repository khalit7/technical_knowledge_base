7. Hallucination: when the language model answers instead of the image

A VLM writes the most likely text given the image tokens and the words so far. When the visual evidence is weak (a small object, a low budget, a confusing scene) or the question suggests an answer, the language model’s habits win: it names objects that usually appear with what is there, or agrees with the question. This is object hallucination. POPE ({{Li et al., May 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.10355}}) made it a yes/no test, “Is there a <object> in the image?”, over present objects and absent ones drawn at random, from the most frequent objects (popular) or from objects that co-occur with what is there (adversarial), and showed that “LVLMs are prone to hallucinate objects which frequently appear or co-occur in the visual instruction dataset” paper. The older caption metric CHAIR (the share of mentioned objects not in the image) moved with the prompt, up to “doubled values of CHAIR metrics” between two similar instructions, one reason POPE asks short questions.

Measured better than by counting answers measured. The root lab’s 72 yes/no questions on its 16 test images (25 present objects, 16 absent at random, 31 absent but adversarial), scored by P(yes) = probability on yes tokens / (probability on yes + no tokens) at the first answer token, which shows how sure each answer is. Each question is asked four ways: with the image, with a plain grey image, with no image at all (the language model’s prior alone), and with the leading phrasing “There is <object> in the image, isn’t there?”.

Both models look: a grey image drives yes to nearly zero, and without any image the prior answers. With the image, SmolVLM says yes (P(yes) ≥ 0.5) to 24 of 25 present objects and 2 of 47 absent ones, Qwen3.5 to 25 and 4; every absent yes is adversarial, an object that goes with the scene (a dog in the typographic-attack image for both; a legend in the chart; numbers and a second hand on the clock; a person at the lighthouse). On a grey image mean P(yes) on present objects is 0.02 and 0.01. With no image SmolVLM keeps about 0.3 on yes whatever the object (0.31 present, 0.33 absent; 13 of 72 yes), Qwen3.5 about 0.15 (0.15 and 0.15; 0 yes). The leading phrasing moved absent objects only slightly: 0.05 to 0.05 (SmolVLM), 0.06 to 0.14 (Qwen3.5). These are 16 easy images, not POPE’s COCO scenes: the method transfers, the rates do not.

What reduces it published, not run here: more image tokens where it matters (LLaVA-NeXT’s blog says higher resolution “reduces the model hallucination that conjectures the imagined visual content when confronted with low-resolution images”); training data with negative and “not visible” answers; preference training against hallucinated captions; shorter answers.

Evaluating a VLM. The failure modes on this page each have a cheap check: a no-word code for reading (section 3), boxes under every convention (section 4), a direction test for video (section 5), P(yes) with blank and no-image baselines for hallucination (above), and matched token budgets for any comparison. Public benchmark scores (MMMU, DocVQA, OCRBench, POPE, video suites) and their pitfalls are on {{Long-context and multimodal benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d810bb4dfc98919c9be8a}}; how generative and VLM outputs are judged is on {{Evaluating generative models|n:3fc5c17b0d0d81c890f4f62543312faa}}.

8. What image tokens cost, and cutting them without retraining

Image tokens are ordinary tokens to the language model: they take context, every later token attends to them, and their keys and values are cached for the whole conversation; on top of that the encoder runs once per image. The cache per token is the model’s, whatever the token is: SmolVLM-500M keeps 2 × 32 × 5 × 64 × 2 = 40,960 bytes per token (measured slope equal to the config arithmetic), Qwen3.5-0.8B 12,288 bytes in its 6 attention layers plus a fixed state in the other 18, so its cache grows 3.3 times more slowly measured. Sizing a whole model at any context is the {{KV-cache calculator|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81059ee1ce20c0326dc5#t-kv}} on Long context, which carries both models and LLaVA-1.5-7B as presets.

The measured cache after prefill, and where the time goes (measured)

Cache tensors summed by walking the cache object after a real prefill (bfloat16 keys and values, float32 recurrent state) measured. Qwen3.5-0.8B is a hybrid: of its 24 layers only 6 are attention layers with a growing cache (“6 × (3 × (Gated DeltaNet → FFN) → 1 × (Gated Attention → FFN))”, {{card|https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B}} vendor); the other 18 are linear-attention layers (Gated DeltaNet, a relative of the state-space models on the {{Mamba|n:3c65c17b0d0d8179984fc3a1fee4c36a}} page) that keep a fixed-size state.

SmolVLM’s cache grows by 40,960 bytes per token with nothing fixed (3.4 MB at 82 prompt tokens, 46.8 MB at 1,142). Qwen3.5’s grows by 12,288 bytes per token on top of a fixed 19.8 MB held by the 18 linear-attention layers (20.8 MB at 85 tokens, 32.6 MB at 1,045). The fixed state is worth about 1,608 tokens of its attention cache: at 1,000 tokens SmolVLM holds 41.0 MB and Qwen3.5 32.0 MB; at 8,000 tokens (near SmolVLM’s 8,192 context) 327.7 MB against 118.1 MB. For scale, LLaVA-1.5-7B (32 layers, 32 KV heads of 128) keeps 524,288 bytes per token, so one 576-token image holds 302 MB.

Time measured. SmolVLM at 1,088 image tokens (the root lab’s measurement): encoder and connector 1.61 s, a full forward of the 1,142-token prompt 2.04 s, so about 0.44 s in the language model, decoding 13.9 ms per token. Qwen3.5 at 64, 256, 1,024 image tokens: encoder 0.05, 0.13, 0.64 s; full forward (encoder included) 0.31, 0.86, 3.10 s; decoding 24, 24, 25 ms per token; peak GPU memory 2,365, 2,349, 2,341 MiB. Its linear-attention layers run transformers’ reference PyTorch code here (the optimised kernels, flash-linear-attention and causal-conv1d, are not installed and target CUDA GPUs), so its times say little about a datacenter GPU; the token counts and cache sizes transfer exactly.

8a. Fewer image tokens without retraining measured

If most image tokens carry little, can they be dropped at inference? FastV ({{Chen et al., March 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06764}}) observed that after layer 2 image tokens get little attention in LLaVA-1.5 (“image tokens take up most of the input tokens (64%)”) and pruned half of them there, ranked by “the average attention-score one token received from all other tokens”, reporting “a 45% reduction in FLOPs for LLaVA-1.5-13B” without loss on its benchmarks paper. Here SmolVLM answers the root’s 30 questions with its 1,088 image tokens cut four ways before the language model: at random; by FastV’s ranking (computed at layer 2, but the tokens are removed from the input rather than after layer 2, a simplification); by averaging each 2 × 2 block (a merge the model never saw); and by dropping the 64-token global view.

Every cut costs answers, and the 64 global tokens matter more than the 1,024 tile tokens. Right answers of 30: all 1,088 tokens 26 (the root lab’s 26, reproduced through this page’s token-editing path); random half 20, quarter 17, eighth 15; attention-ranked half 16, quarter 12, eighth 9; 2 × 2 average pooling (272 tokens) 19; dropping only the global view and keeping all 1,024 tile tokens: 15. Language-model prefill on the cat question fell from 0.43 s to 0.15 s at a quarter of the tokens.

Three lessons. (1) The model was trained to read the global view first and the tiles as detail, so counting and charts fail without it. (2) Pooling the projector’s outputs, a merge the model never saw, loses 7 answers; the same compression trained into the connector (SmolVLM’s pixel shuffle, Idefics2’s resampler) costs little. (3) FastV’s ranking did worse than random: averaged over all later queries, attention favours early tokens simply because more queries can see them, so it kept mostly the first tiles (for “How many cats are in this image?”, 150 of the 272 kept tokens came from the first four of 16 tiles and 6 from the global view). FastV prunes after layer 2 inside a 7B or 13B model; a 500M model with the pruning moved to the input is a harsher test, so read this as “measure before trusting”, not as a refutation.

When it matters. Token count drives prefill time and cache size linearly (and attention quadratically at long lengths), so a VLM serving screenshots or video is usually bound by image tokens. The cheapest lever is the processor setting (fewer tiles, lower maximum pixels), then a model trained with a stronger merge; training-free pruning is a third option whose loss must be measured on your own questions.

9. Serving: what a server caches and shares

A serving engine (see {{Topic: inference-and-serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c08b3bc95ff45c7b13}} and {{Batching, KV cache and scheduling in depth|n:3c65c17b0d0d819db53bd2c48909e5b2}}) handles a VLM request in four costs, and vLLM (documentation, latest, read 2026-10-17) vendor has a lever for each:

Preprocessdecode, resize, tilecached by content hash; processor cache 4 GiB by default
→
Encoderpatches to image tokenswith tensor parallelism, shard the batch of images, not the weights
→
Prefilltext and image tokensprefix cache reuses identical image tokens; limit images per prompt
→
Decodeanswer tokensreads every cached image token per step
  • Content hashing: “vLLM normally hashes each media item by content to enable caching across requests”; a client may pass stable IDs (multi_modal_uuids) to skip re-hashing, or skip sending the image when a cache hit is expected ({{multimodal inputs|https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/multimodal_inputs.html}}). The processor cache defaults to 4 GiB (mm_processor_cache_gb, {{optimization|https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/configuration/optimization.html}}).
  • The encoder is small next to the language model, so mm_encoder_tp_mode="data" shards the images instead of the encoder’s weights, which “has been shown to improve the throughput and TTFT by around 10% for tensor_parallel_size=8”.
  • Limits and pruning: limit_mm_per_prompt bounds the images or videos per request; --video-pruning-rate drops video tokens that barely change from the previous frame, “at some cost in accuracy”.
  • Prefix caching with images: a second question about the same image reuses the cached keys and values of the shared prefix (system prompt, image, earlier turns) only if the image tokens are identical, which content hashing guarantees; putting the image before the question makes the shared prefix longest. At laptop scale the encoder is a large share of a short answer (section 8), exactly the share a cache hit removes.

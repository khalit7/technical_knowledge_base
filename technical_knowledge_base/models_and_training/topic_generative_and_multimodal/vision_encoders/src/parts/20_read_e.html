4. Training on captions: the contrastive loss

There are hundreds of millions of image-caption pairs on the web and no labels saying how similar any two are. Contrastive training uses the pairing itself as the label: in a batch of N pairs, each image's own caption is the positive and the other N − 1 captions are negatives. Two towers (a ViT for images, a transformer for text) each end in a vector scaled to length 1, so their dot product is the cosine similarity.

A real batch. The root's 16 colour test images against their 16 captions: through CLIP B/32 the matching pairs average a cosine of 0.333 and the other pairs 0.166; it puts the right caption first for 15 of 16 images (its miss is the typographic image s04, section 6). SigLIP 2 gets all 16, after its text is lowercased as its reference code does; its cosines average 0.194 and −0.019 measured. Cosines that small still make a confident choice because each is multiplied by a learned scale t (the inverse of the temperature) before the softmax: CLIP's released B/32 sits at t = 100.0, so a gap of 0.05 in cosine becomes a factor of e5 ≈ 150 in probability.

Predict first: as the scale t goes to 0, what does CLIP's softmax loss on a batch of N tend to? And as t grows very large?
At t = 0 every logit is 0, every softmax is uniform, and the loss is exactly ln N whatever the embeddings: the chance level (ln 8 = 2.079 for 8 pairs). As t grows the softmax approaches an argmax: rows the model gets right cost almost nothing, but a row where a negative beats the positive costs about t times the gap. On the first 8 pairs CLIP's loss falls to its minimum, 0.071, near t = 100 (its learned value) and rises again to 0.121 at t = 316, because one row (s04) is wrong; on SigLIP 2's embeddings, where all 8 rows are right, it keeps falling measured. Move the sliders to see it.

the N × N matrix of cosines, images in rows, captions in columns; the diagonal holds the true pairs
CE
cross-entropy of a softmax with the diagonal as the right answer: each image must pick its caption out of the row, each caption its image out of the column (the InfoNCE loss)
t, b

zij

CLIP's t = 100.0 is exactly where its paper clipped the scale; SigLIP 2 learned t = 112.67 with b = −16.77 read from the weights in float32. Because SigLIP's logits carry a bias they are calibrated per pair (a sigmoid gives a yes-or-no probability); CLIP's are calibrated only relative to the other candidates.

The two losses in code (depth)

In transformers 5.20.1: CLIP's contrastive_loss is a cross-entropy against torch.arange(len(logits)) (models/clip/modeling_clip.py:47), applied to the logits and their transpose and averaged; SigLIP's logits are logits_per_text * logit_scale.exp() + logit_bias (models/siglip/modeling_siglip.py:805) and the loss is logsigmoid(m1_diag1 * logits_per_text) summed over each row and averaged (:814). CLIP's scale started at 1 / 0.07 and was clipped at 100 (CLIP paper §2.5); the released B/32 sits exactly at that clip, and SigLIP 2 learned t = 112.67 with b = −16.77 read from the weights in float32. A sigmoid of 100 × cosine is near 1 for every CLIP pair, so applying one model's rule to the other's logits is meaningless.

Batch size, and why the sigmoid scales

The softmax learns only from the negatives in the batch, and its chance level ln N grows with it: CLIP's batch of 32,768 asked each image to pick 1 caption out of 32,768 ({{CLIP page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8194a85dfc5f3bf3f799}}), and hard negatives (two cats, two similar captions) only show up in large batches. Split over D devices, the softmax normalises each row and column over the whole batch, so every device needs every other device's embeddings (an all-gather). The sigmoid scores each cell alone, so SigLIP's "chunked" implementation keeps each device's images in place and passes text embeddings round the devices in D steps: "the memory cost at any given moment is reduced from |B|2 to b2", with b = |B| / D (SigLIP §3.3).

Before and after: the softmax needs every embedding on every device, the chunked sigmoid passes one block at a time

Drawn for D = 4 devices. Numbers for a global batch |B| = 32,768 on D = 256 (b = 128 per device), float32 logits: the full matrix is 4.3 GB; one device's rows b × |B| are 16.8 MB; one chunk b × b is 65.5 kB derived from SigLIP §3.3.

What the paper measured published: with locked image features (SigLiT), "when the batch size is smaller than 16 k, sigmoid loss outperforms softmax loss by a large margin", but "performance for both sigmoid and softmax saturate at around 32 k batch size" (they went up to one million), and for SigLIP proper "a very large batch size hurts both losses" (§4.1, Figure 2). With four TPUv4 chips a SigLiT model reached 84.5% ImageNet zero-shot in two days. So the sigmoid buys cost and small-batch quality, not a higher ceiling.

Why the data is the model

CLIP chose the contrastive loss for efficiency (it learned ImageNet-recognisable features 4 times faster than predicting the caption's bag of words, CLIP Figure 2), but almost every gain since 2021 came with a new dataset rather than a new loss. At fixed compute, curation moved accuracy by several points: MetaCLIP's ViT-B/32 goes from 57.4% on raw English pairs to 65.5% with its metadata balancing, and DataComp's best filter adds 6.9 points over its unfiltered pool published.

Locking a tower: LiT (depth)

Contrastive training does not have to start from scratch. LiT (locked-image tuning) freezes a pre-trained image model and trains only a text tower to read it, and found "locked pre-trained image models with unlocked text models work best"; with a ViT-g/14 image tower it reached 85.2% ImageNet zero-shot (Zhai et al. 2021) published. SigLIP's 84.5% in two days is a LiT-style run. SigLIP 2's caption decoder comes from LocCa.

5. Training without captions: labels, pixels, other views

Labels, the original recipe (ImageNet-21k, Google's private JFT-300M), teach only the distinctions their 1,000 or 21,000 classes ask for, and labels are expensive; captions and self-supervision both remove the labeller.

DeiT: a ViT on ImageNet alone (depth)

DeiT showed the ViT's data requirement was partly a recipe problem: "competitive convolution-free transformers by training on Imagenet only", 83.1% top-1 for an 86M model with a distillation token that learns from a CNN teacher, "on a single computer in less than 3 days" (Touvron et al. 2020) published.

Images alone. How the methods work (with MAE's real reconstructions and the collapse problem) is on {{Contrastive and self-supervised learning|n:3c65c17b0d0d811db6a7d02d0ee7215c}}. In one line each: MAE hides 75% of the patches and trains the encoder plus a small decoder to redraw the missing pixels; DINO trains a student network so that its output for one crop matches a slowly averaged teacher copy's output for another crop, and DINOv2 adds a masked-patch version of the same loss (iBOT) that is applied to every patch token. As encoders they differ like this:

  • MAE's features are not linearly separable. Its paper: "the MAE representations are less linearly separable, they are stronger non-linear features" and do well "when a non-linear head is tuned"; ViT-B linear probe 68.0% on ImageNet against 83.6% fine-tuned (He et al. 2021, §4.3, Tables 3 and 12) published. MAE is a starting point for fine-tuning, not a frozen feature extractor.
  • DINOv2 was built to be used frozen. A 1.1B ViT-g trained on LVD-142M, 142 million images chosen by retrieval "close to those in several curated datasets" from an uncurated pool, then distilled into S, B and L; "the linear probe performance is lower-bounded by the k-NN performance" in their experience. Its Table 4 (frozen features, ImageNet-1k): DINOv2 B/14 82.1% kNN and 84.5% linear, against MAE ViT-H/14 49.4% kNN and 76.6% linear, and OpenAI CLIP ViT-L/14 79.8% and 84.3% (Oquab et al. 2023) published.

DINOv3 (2025) scaled the recipe to a 6.7B-parameter ViT-7B and added Gram anchoring to stop dense features degrading in long training; its weights are under Meta's own licence, so it is not run here.

DINOv3 in detail (depth)

DINOv3 scaled it again. A 6.7B-parameter ViT-7B with patch 16, RoPE and 4 registers, trained on LVD-1689M (1,689 million curated images), with a new Gram anchoring loss that fixes "the known yet unsolved issue of dense feature maps degrading during long training schedules" by keeping the patch-to-patch similarity matrix close to that of an earlier checkpoint (Siméoni et al. 2025, §3 to 4, Table 2) published. Its weights are gated under Meta's own DINOv3 licence, so it is not run here (DINOv2 is Apache 2.0).

2. Three ways to train the same backbone

The architecture barely differs between the five encoders; the training signal does. There are three, and each leaves a different mark on the vectors.

Labelspredict a class name; needs labelled images (ImageNet-21k, JFT)
Captionsmatch each image to its own web caption (CLIP, SigLIP)
Images alonepredict hidden parts (MAE) or match views of one image (DINO)

Labels: the original recipe, and why it lost the lead

The first ViT was pre-trained to classify, on ImageNet-21k or Google's private JFT-300M, and only beat the best CNNs at the largest scale ({{ViT page|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa231cfcde6c1954d}}). DeiT showed the data requirement was partly a recipe problem: "competitive convolution-free transformers by training on Imagenet only", 83.1% top-1 for an 86M model with a distillation token that learns from a CNN teacher, "on a single computer in less than 3 days" (Touvron et al. 2020) published. The limit is the label set itself: a classifier learns only the distinctions its 1,000 or 21,000 classes ask for, and labels are expensive. Captions and self-supervision both remove the labeller.

Captions: contrastive training, and why the data is the model

The loss (each image must pick its own caption out of the batch, by a softmax in CLIP or a pairwise sigmoid in SigLIP), the temperature and the batch size are taught with real numbers in the root's {{Vision lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d817ab6ade318917bff55}} (section 3 of that tab) and with a toy CLIP on the {{CLIP page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8194a85dfc5f3bf3f799}}. What those leave out is the data. CLIP chose the contrastive loss for efficiency (in its Figure 2 it learned ImageNet-recognisable features 4 times faster than predicting the caption's bag of words, on the CLIP page), but almost every gain since 2021 has come with a new dataset rather than a new loss:

The CLIP-score filter, on the running example. LAION's filter used OpenAI's CLIP ViT-B/32, the very model the Vision lab runs, so its rule can be applied to the lab's real 16 × 16 matrix of image-caption cosines. Move the threshold and count what survives:

Dots: the 16 true pairs (top row) and the 240 mismatched image-caption pairs (bottom row) of the root's 16 colour test images, CLIP ViT-B/32 cosines from the Vision lab measured; the line is the threshold. LAION used 0.28 for English.

At 0.28 all 16 true pairs pass and 1 of the 240 wrong pairs passes too measured. Two lessons. A good caption of a simple image scores only about 0.28 to 0.38 with this model, so the threshold sits just under real matches, and the typographic image s04 (0.284) barely survives. And a filter built from one model passes that model's blind spots into the next model's data: LAION's authors note that filtering with a small CLIP "may leave more image-text pairs with weak or no semantic connection" and remove good ones that a stronger model would keep (§3.1).

Locking a tower. Contrastive training does not have to start from scratch. LiT (locked-image tuning) freezes a pre-trained image model and trains only a text tower to read it, and found "locked pre-trained image models with unlocked text models work best"; with a ViT-g/14 image tower it reached 85.2% ImageNet zero-shot (Zhai et al. 2021) published. The SigLIP paper's 84.5% in two days on four TPUv4 chips is a LiT-style run (root Vision lab).

More than one loss. SigLIP 2 keeps the sigmoid loss and adds a caption decoder that reads the un-pooled patch tokens (from LocCa), then, for the last 20% of training, self-distillation and masked prediction "to improve the local semantics of the (un-pooled) feature representation" (§2.2, 2.3). The result is a caption-trained encoder whose patch tokens are much better for dense tasks: in its Table 2, ADE20K linear segmentation rises from 37.6 (SigLIP So/14, 224) to 41.8 mIoU published.

Images alone: masked autoencoders and self-distillation

How MAE and DINO work (with MAE's real reconstructions and the collapse problem) is on {{Contrastive and self-supervised learning|n:3c65c17b0d0d811db6a7d02d0ee7215c}}. In one line each: MAE hides 75% of the patches and trains the encoder plus a small decoder to redraw the missing pixels; DINO trains a student network so that its output for one crop of an image matches a slowly averaged teacher copy's output for another crop, and DINOv2 adds a masked-patch version of the same loss (iBOT). What matters here is how they differ as encoders:

  • MAE's features are not linearly separable. Its paper: "the MAE representations are less linearly separable, they are stronger non-linear features" and do well "when a non-linear head is tuned"; ViT-B linear probe 68.0% on ImageNet against 83.6% fine-tuned (He et al. 2021, §4.3, Tables 3 and 12) published. MAE is a starting point for fine-tuning, not a frozen feature extractor.
  • DINOv2 was built to be used frozen. A 1.1B ViT-g trained on LVD-142M, 142 million images chosen by retrieval "close to those in several curated datasets" from an uncurated pool, then distilled into S, B and L; "the linear probe performance is lower-bounded by the k-NN performance" in their experience. Its Table 4 (frozen features, ImageNet-1k): DINOv2 B/14 82.1% kNN and 84.5% linear, against MAE ViT-H/14 49.4% kNN and 76.6% linear, and OpenAI CLIP ViT-L/14 79.8% and 84.3% (Oquab et al. 2023) published.
  • DINOv3 scaled it again. A 6.7B-parameter ViT-7B with patch 16, RoPE and 4 registers, trained on LVD-1689M (1,689 million curated images), with a new Gram anchoring loss that fixes "the known yet unsolved issue of dense feature maps degrading during long training schedules" by keeping the patch-to-patch similarity matrix close to that of an earlier checkpoint (Siméoni et al. 2025, §3 to 4, Table 2) published. Its weights are gated under Meta's own DINOv3 licence, so it is not run here (DINOv2 is Apache 2.0).

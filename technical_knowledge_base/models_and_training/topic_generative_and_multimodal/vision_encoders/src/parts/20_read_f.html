3. What each encoder is good at, measured on the same images

An encoder is used in one of four ways: its pooled vector for classification and search (global features), its per-patch tokens for segmentation, depth or matching (dense features), its text tower for zero-shot work (text), or all its patch tokens as the eyes of a VLM (backbone). Each training signal favours some of these. The test here is the topic's running example, Fashion-MNIST: grey 28-pixel clothing, upscaled to 224, unlike anything any of the five was trained on, so it measures transfer rather than memory.

Global features: a linear probe, nearest neighbours, search

A linear probe trains only a linear classifier on frozen features (here with the Vision lab's exact settings and draws); a kNN classifier labels each test image by a vote of its 20 most similar labelled images (DINO's weighted protocol), with no training at all; image-to-image search asks whether each test image's nearest neighbours share its class. A probe can rescale and rotate the features; kNN and search take the geometry as it is.

The last layers specialise for the training objective (matching a caption); general features peak earlier. Meta's Perception Encoder paper makes the same point at scale: contrastive training "can produce strong, general embeddings", but "these embeddings are hidden within the intermediate layers of the network" (Bolya et al. 2025) published. It is why LLaVA reads the second-to-last block.

Dense features: the same image through four encoders

The problem. Segmentation, depth and matching need a good vector at every patch, not one per image. Nothing in a contrastive loss asks for that: only the pooled vector is compared with text. Self-distillation with masked patches (DINOv2's iBOT term) does ask for it. How to see it. Take one block's patch tokens, find their three main directions of variation (principal components) and paint each patch with its three coordinates as red, green and blue: patches with similar vectors get similar colours. The animation steps through the blocks of all four models on the same image; switch images with the buttons.

Published, at scale. With a linear layer on frozen patch tokens, ADE20K semantic segmentation (150 classes) reaches 47.3 mIoU for DINOv2 B/14, 39.3 for OpenCLIP ViT-G/14 and 33.3 for MAE ViT-H/14 (DINOv2 Table 10); SigLIP 2's extra losses lift a caption-trained encoder to 41.8 (So/14, 224) from SigLIP's 37.6 (SigLIP 2 Table 2) published. Dense quality is where self-supervised encoders still lead.

As the eyes of a vision-language model

A VLM takes the patch tokens (not the pooled vector) of a pre-trained encoder, maps them into the language model's embedding space with a small projector, and treats them as words. How images become tokens there is the subject of the sibling page {{Vision-language models|n:3fc5c17b0d0d81adbb7be5bc41c4d9d0}} and of the root's {{VLM lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d817ab6ade318917bff55}}; the encoder side, from configs and papers:

Contrastive encoders (CLIP, then SigLIP) won this role because their patch tokens already line up with language; DINO-style features carry more spatial detail but no link to words, and VLM builders have mixed the two (an ablation question for the {{VLM page|n:3fc5c17b0d0d81adbb7be5bc41c4d9d0}}). Qwen2.5-VL shows the other route: train the encoder inside the VLM at native resolution, with LLM-style blocks.

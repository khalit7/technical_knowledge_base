6. What each encoder is good and bad at, on the same images

An encoder is used in one of four ways: its pooled vector for classification and search (global features), its per-patch tokens for segmentation, depth or matching (dense features), its text tower for zero-shot work (text), or all its patch tokens as the eyes of a VLM (backbone). Each training signal favours some of these.

Global features: a linear probe, nearest neighbours, search

A linear probe trains only a linear classifier on frozen features (the root Vision lab's settings and draws); kNN labels each test image by a vote of its 20 most similar labelled images (DINO's weighted protocol); image-to-image search asks whether a query's nearest neighbours share its class. A probe can rescale and rotate the features; kNN and search take the geometry as it is.

Dense features: the same image through four encoders

Segmentation, depth and matching need a good vector at every patch. Nothing in a contrastive loss asks for that: only the pooled vector is compared with text. Self-distillation with masked patches (DINOv2's iBOT term) does. To see it, take one block's patch tokens, find their three main directions of variation (principal components) and paint each patch with its three coordinates as red, green and blue: patches with similar vectors get similar colours.

Published, at scale. With a linear layer on frozen patch tokens, ADE20K semantic segmentation (150 classes) reaches 47.3 mIoU for DINOv2 B/14, 39.3 for OpenCLIP ViT-G/14 and 33.3 for MAE ViT-H/14 (DINOv2 Table 10); SigLIP 2's extra losses lift a caption-trained encoder to 41.8 (section 4) published. Dense quality is where self-supervised encoders still lead.

What a caption-trained embedding misses

A contrastive embedding keeps whatever helps pick the right caption out of a batch. Web captions name objects and often repeat the text in the picture; they rarely state counts, left and right, or which attribute goes with which object. So the embedding is strong on "what is it" and "what does it say", weak on structure, partly a bag of words (Yuksekgonul et al. 2022). OpenAI called the first failure a typographic attack, needing "no more technology than pen and paper" (OpenAI, March 2021, archived).

To check a model: build minimal pairs like these and see whether the score moves the right way, and by more than a few thousandths. The fix is rarely a better prompt: it is training data with hard negatives (captions with swapped words), a VLM that reads the image tokens with a language model, or both.

As the eyes of a vision-language model

A VLM maps an encoder's patch tokens into the language model with a small projector (the {{Vision-language models|n:3fc5c17b0d0d81adbb7be5bc41c4d9d0}} page owns how). Contrastive encoders (CLIP, then SigLIP) won this role because their patch tokens already line up with language; DINO-style features carry more spatial detail but no link to words. Qwen2.5-VL shows the other route: train the encoder inside the VLM at native resolution, with windowed attention.

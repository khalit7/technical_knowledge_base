5 · Regression: MAE, RMSE, R² and MAPE

For a numeric target the errors are residuals, y − ŷ, and the metric decides how to add them up. MAE is the mean absolute residual, in the target's units; RMSE is the square root of the mean squared residual, also in those units but dominated by the largest misses, and RMSE ≥ MAE always (Willmott and Matsuura 2005 argue for MAE as the typical error; Chai and Draxler 2014 for RMSE when errors are Gaussian). R² = 1 − SSE / SST compares the squared error with always predicting the test mean: 0 is that baseline, 1 is perfect, and it goes negative for a model worse than the mean. MAPE, the mean of |y − ŷ| / |y|, is scale-free but explodes when a true value is near zero (Hyndman and Koehler 2006) and is lopsided: for a positive target and a non-negative prediction, an under-prediction costs at most 100%, an over-prediction without limit. Minimising MSE targets the conditional mean and minimising MAE the conditional median, which is why the loss and the reported metric should match (losses: {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}).

Report MAE when every unit of error costs the same, RMSE when large misses cost disproportionately, R² to compare against the trivial model on the same data (never across datasets, since it depends on the target's variance), and a relative error only when no true value is near zero.

6 · Ranked lists: precision@k, recall@k, MRR, MAP, nDCG

Search, retrieval and recommendation return an ordered list per query, and the metrics look only at the top k. Precision@k is the share of the top k that is relevant and recall@k the share of all relevant items that made the top k: the threshold metrics of section 1, with the threshold at rank k. Reciprocal rank is 1 / (rank of the first relevant item), and MRR its mean over queries (Voorhees 1999, TREC-8 question answering); it ignores everything after the first hit. Average precision averages precision@i at the rank i of each relevant item, divided by the number of relevant items, and MAP is its mean over queries. nDCG handles graded relevance: DCG@k = Σi≤k gaini / log2(i + 1), divided by the same sum for the ideal ordering so that 1 is perfect (Järvelin and Kekäläinen 2002). The gain is the grade itself (scikit-learn's ndcg_score) or 2grade − 1, which favours highly relevant items (Burges et al. 2005); the two give different numbers, so say which.

7 · Text against a reference: BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, chrF

When the output is text, the classic metrics count what it shares with one or more human references.

MetricMechanismOrigin, notes
BLEUFor n = 1 to 4, the share of the output's n-grams found in the reference, each clipped to the count the reference has ("modified precision"); their geometric mean, times a brevity penalty BP = exp(1 − r/c) when the output length c is below the reference length r, else 1Machine translation; precision-oriented; defined over a whole test corpus (Papineni et al. 2002)
ROUGE-NPrecision, recall and F1 of matching n-gramsSummarisation; recall-oriented (Lin 2004)
ROUGE-LLongest common subsequence of the two word sequences, as precision, recall and F1Order-sensitive without requiring contiguity
ROUGE-SSkip-bigram co-occurrence: every ordered pair of words, any gap apartAllows gaps within bigrams; rarely reported now
METEOROne-to-one unigram alignment in stages (exact words, then Porter stems, then WordNet synonyms); Fmean = 10PR / (R + 9P); penalty 0.5 × (chunks / matches)3, where a chunk is a run of matches adjacent and in the same order in both; score = Fmean × (1 − penalty)Built to score single sentences (Banerjee and Lavie 2005)
chrFCharacter 1- to 6-gram precision and recall (spaces removed), averaged over orders, combined as Fβ with β = 2Robust to word forms and spelling (Popović 2015)

BLEU is a corpus metric: summed over a test set, a sentence with no 4-gram match does not zero the score. On one sentence it needs smoothing; sacreBLEU's sentence_bleu uses the "exp" method (each zero precision becomes 1 / (2k × total), Chen and Cherry 2014, method 3) and drops n-gram orders longer than the output, so never compare sentence BLEU with corpus BLEU. Tokenisation changes the number too: Post (2018) measured differences as high as 1.8 BLEU between commonly used configurations, which is why sacreBLEU fixes the tokenizer (13a) and prints a signature to quote. 8 · Perplexity, and why it depends on the tokenizer

Perplexity is the reciprocal geometric mean of the probabilities a model gives each token: PPL = (∏i p(wi | w<i))−1/N = exp(mean negative log-likelihood per token), the training cross entropy read as a score on held-out text. The catch is N, a count of tokens: the total log-probability of a text is comparable across models, but dividing it by a model-specific token count is not. A tokenizer that splits finely makes each token easier to predict, because the later pieces of a word follow almost surely from the first, and per-token perplexity drops without the model knowing anything more. Normalise by something the tokenizer does not choose: bits per UTF-8 byte, or perplexity per word. The Pile's authors prefer bits per byte "due to its invariance to different tokenization schemes" (Gao et al. 2020, section 3.1).

Try: "Digits of pi", then switch between GPT-2 and Qwen2.5. Each bar's width is the bytes its token covers and its height the bits per byte the model paid, so its area is the token's bits and the total area is comparable across models. Step back to watch the tokens arrive one at a time.

Two more things change perplexity without changing the model: the text (GPT-2's perplexity on the French article is 11 times its perplexity on the same article in English), and the context each token gets: text longer than the window is scored with a sliding window, and a larger stride gives each token less context and a higher perplexity (Hugging Face, perplexity of fixed-length models). And low perplexity is not good generation: on GPT-2 Large, beam search output has perplexity 1.48 against 12.38 for human text, because the most probable text is degenerate ({{Sampling and Decoding|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bafcda29a2c7086e}}, after Holtzman et al.). How tokenizers split languages differently is on {{Tokenizers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817187bcd94a1b0f15ea}}.

Entropy, cross entropy and KL behind log-loss and perplexity (owned by the root's thread 1)
Entropy H(P) = −Σ P log P is the average bits to encode samples of P with P's own optimal code; cross entropy H(P, Q) = −Σ P log Q uses a code built for Q; their difference is the KL divergence DKL(P‖Q) = Σ P log(P/Q), the extra bits for the wrong code (asymmetric, no triangle inequality). Minimising cross entropy over Q is minimising KL, since H(P) is fixed; with one-hot labels H(P) = 0 and cross entropy = KL = the negative log-likelihood of the true class, which is log-loss (section 3) and log perplexity (above). KL is the workhorse for divergence between distributions: VAE regularisers, distillation, RLHF policy constraints. 9 · Embedding scores, learned metrics and judges

BERTScore embeds both texts with a contextual encoder, takes the cosine similarity of every candidate token with every reference token, and matches greedily: precision averages, over candidate tokens, the best similarity found in the reference; recall does the same from the reference side; F1 combines them; optional idf weights make rare words count more (Zhang et al. 2019, ICLR 2020). The English default in the bert-score package is roberta-large, layer 17. Raw scores crowd into a narrow band (here 0.90 for shuffled words to 1.00 for a copy), so the package can rescale them linearly against a baseline from unrelated sentence pairs (rescale_baseline.md), (score − b) / (1 − b), which spreads the range without changing any ranking. The BERTScore view of the step animation in section 7 draws the real similarity matrix and the greedy matches.

MoverScore replaces greedy one-to-one matching with an earth mover's distance between the two sets of embeddings, a softer many-to-one alignment (Zhao et al. 2019); it is not computed here (it needs corpus idf statistics and an optimal-transport solve, and would add a second embedding score with the same blind spot). Learned metrics such as COMET train on human quality judgements; they are the "neural metrics" WMT22 found better than overlap. For open-ended LLM output, evaluation has largely moved to LLM judges and task benchmarks, a measuring instrument with its own biases and calibration: {{Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges|n:3c65c17b0d0d8181b351e1b8fc6a1546}}, judge design on {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}, and two paper pages with data: {{RocketEval|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81ffa8e5f8fa5a5806c8}} (a small judge grading checklists re-ranks a leaderboard almost exactly) and {{Re-grading six physics benchmarks|n:3e25c17b0d0d8165b0c8d15600421259}} (what expert re-grading does to headline scores).

10 · How much to trust a number: error bars

A test set is a sample, so every metric on it has sampling error. For accuracy p on n examples the standard error is √(p(1 − p)/n): at 80% on 872 sentences that is 1.4 points, so a 95% interval is about ±2.7. On the hero's data naive Bayes is 1.1 points more accurate than logistic regression, and the paired 95% interval (−1.0 to +3.4 points, src/recompute.py) still crosses zero: on 872 examples a 1-point gap is inside the noise. For metrics with no simple formula (F1, AUC, BLEU) the bootstrap resamples the test set with replacement and reads the interval off the spread (Efron 1979); comparing two models on the same items, resample the pairs (Koehn 2004 for MT; Miller 2024 for LLM evals). Report the interval, say what was resampled (sentences, documents, users: whatever was drawn independently), and size the test set before collecting it. Paired tests, clustered questions and sample-size planning are owned by {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}.

Before reporting a metric: ten checks (each from a section above)
  • Accuracy on an imbalanced task: on the rare-positive subset "always negative" scores 90.5%, above the real classifier; report MCC or per-class precision and recall, and the base rate.
  • Threshold chosen on the test set: picking the threshold that maximises F1 or MCC on the data you then report inflates the score; choose it on validation data, or report a threshold-free metric.
  • ROC-AUC read as "good" when positives are rare: it barely moves with prevalence; look at AUPRC or precision at the recall you need.
  • A trapezoidal PR area called "average precision": linear interpolation in PR space is optimistic (Davis and Goadrich 2006).
  • A confident score treated as a probability: naive Bayes here is the more accurate model and has twice the log-loss; check a reliability diagram and the Brier score first.
  • "F1" without macro, micro or weighted: 0.57, 0.63 and 0.65 on the same glass predictions.
  • RMSE without MAE, or R² across datasets: one unit slip moves RMSE far more than MAE; R² depends on the target's variance.
  • BLEU across papers: tokenisation alone moves it by up to 1.8 points; quote the sacreBLEU signature.
  • Overlap scores trusted on meaning: a one-word negation beats a faithful paraphrase on BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, chrF and BERTScore.
  • Perplexity across tokenizers or texts: compare bits per byte, or perplexity only with the same tokenizer, text and context.

In one screen

A metric is a decision about which errors count and how much each one costs. Every score below is blind to something, and the useful question is never "which is best" but "what can this one not see, here".

Three ideas carry the whole page. A threshold turns scores into decisions, and most classification metrics describe one threshold; ROC-AUC, AUPRC, log-loss and the Brier score describe all of them, or the probabilities themselves. An average hides whom it averages over: over examples (accuracy, micro), over classes (macro), over tokens (perplexity), over n-gram orders (BLEU). Every number from a finite test set has an error bar, and on a few hundred examples it is often wider than the gap being reported. 1 · Classification at a threshold

A binary classifier outputs a score; a threshold turns it into a decision, and the decisions against the truth fill the confusion matrix: true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), false negatives (FN). Every metric in this section is a ratio of those four counts at one threshold (Google ML Crash Course).

MetricFormulaReads as
Accuracy(TP + TN) / allShare of decisions that were right
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)Of everything I flagged positive, how much was right
Recall (true positive rate, sensitivity)TP / (TP + FN)Of all actual positives, how many I caught
F12PR / (P + R)Harmonic mean of precision and recall
Fβ(1 + β²)PR / (β²P + R)Recall counted β times as important as precision (van Rijsbergen 1979, chapter 7)
False positive rate (FPR)FP / (FP + TN)Fraction of actual negatives wrongly flagged
Specificity (true negative rate)TN / (TN + FP)1 − FPR
Balanced accuracy(recall + specificity) / 2Accuracy with both classes weighted equally
MCC(TP·TN − FP·FN) / √((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN))Correlation between decision and truth, from −1 to 1, 0 for guessing (Matthews 1975)

F1 never looks at TN: it is the score of the class you act on. That is right for search or fraud alerts, but it means F1 changes if you swap which class is called positive. MCC is symmetric in the two classes and is high only when all four cells are good, which is why Chicco and Jurman (2020) argue for it over F1 and accuracy on imbalanced binary tasks.

2 · Ranking: ROC and precision-recall curves

Sweeping the threshold from high to low traces two curves. The ROC curve plots recall (TPR) against FPR; its area, ROC-AUC, equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative, with ties counted half (Hanley and McNeil 1982; Fawcett 2006). The precision-recall (PR) curve records precision and recall at each threshold, and its area is AUPRC. A random ranker scores ROC-AUC 0.5 whatever the classes, but AUPRC equal to the share of positives, so the two areas are not on the same scale.

Why ROC misleads when positives are rare comes down to one denominator. FPR divides by all negatives, which is a huge number, so even thousands of extra false positives barely move it while recall moves quickly; the ROC curve looks good regardless. Precision divides by the model's own positive predictions, so it is hit by every one of those false positives (Davis and Goadrich 2006). Use AUPRC when positives are rare. The root page's widget shows it with a fixed classifier as positives get rarer: ROC-AUC stays at 0.856 while AUPRC falls from 0.85 to 0.11 ({{Topic: ml-fundamentals|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}}, Reading, step 7). Two results from Davis and Goadrich are worth keeping. One curve dominates another in ROC space if and only if it dominates in PR space, so a ranking that is better everywhere is better on both. But points in PR space cannot be joined by straight lines: precision does not interpolate linearly, so the trapezoidal area under a PR curve is optimistic. That is why scikit-learn's average_precision_score sums precision times each step in recall with no interpolation; "AUPRC" in papers may be either, and the two differ.

3 · Probabilities: calibration, log-loss and Brier

Ranking metrics ignore what a score means. A classifier is calibrated when, of all the examples it gives probability 0.8, about 80% are positive. Two scores measure probabilities directly. Log-loss is the cross entropy of the predicted probability of the true class, −mean log p(true class), which is the training loss read as a measurement. The Brier score is the mean squared error of the probability against the 0 or 1 outcome (Brier 1950). Both are proper scoring rules: the expected score is best when the forecast equals the true probability, so neither can be gamed by hedging (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). A reliability diagram bins predictions by confidence and plots the observed rate in each bin; the expected calibration error (ECE) is the sample-weighted mean gap between confidence and observed rate over the bins (Guo et al. 2017, who found that modern deep networks, unlike those of a decade earlier, are poorly calibrated, and that temperature scaling, one parameter, fixes most of it). ECE depends on the binning (here 10 equal-width bins) and is not a proper scoring rule.

The more accurate model is the worse forecaster. A multinomial naive Bayes classifier on the same SST-2 features is slightly more accurate than the logistic regression ( against at threshold 0.5) and ranks almost as well, yet its probabilities are far worse: naive Bayes multiplies word evidence as if words were independent, which pushes its outputs towards 0 and 1 (Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana 2005). Isotonic regression, a monotone step function fitted on cross-validated predictions (Zadrozny and Elkan 2002), maps them back towards observed frequencies without changing their order. Calibration is a separate axis from accuracy, so measure it whenever a probability is used as a probability: a risk threshold, an expected cost, an abstention rule, a confidence shown to a user, or a score fed to another model. 4 · More than two classes: macro, micro, weighted

With K classes each class gets its own precision, recall and F1 (one class against the rest), and a single number needs an average (scikit-learn user guide). Macro takes the plain mean over classes, so a 9-example class counts as much as a 76-example one. Weighted weights each class by its support (true count). Micro pools the counts first; in single-label multi-class classification every false positive for one class is a false negative for another, so micro precision, micro recall and micro F1 all equal accuracy.

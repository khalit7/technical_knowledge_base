In one screen

A metric is a decision about which errors count and how much each one costs. Every score below is blind to something, and the useful question is never "which is best" but "what can this one not see, here".

Three ideas carry the whole page. A threshold turns scores into decisions, and most classification metrics describe one threshold; ROC-AUC, AUPRC, log-loss and the Brier score describe all of them, or the probabilities themselves. An average hides whom it averages over: over examples (accuracy, micro), over classes (macro), over tokens (perplexity), over n-gram orders (BLEU). Every number from a finite test set has an error bar, and on a few hundred examples it is often wider than the gap being reported. This page is the depth behind step 7 of Topic: ml-fundamentals (Reading tab, "7 · Measure"), which already shows ROC against precision-recall as positives get rarer and the entropy, cross entropy and KL trio; both are linked below rather than rebuilt.

1 · Classification at a threshold

A binary classifier outputs a score; a threshold turns it into a decision, and the decisions against the truth fill the confusion matrix: true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), false negatives (FN). Every metric in this section is a ratio of those four counts at one threshold (Google ML Crash Course).

MetricFormulaReads as
Accuracy(TP + TN) / allShare of decisions that were right
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)Of everything I flagged positive, how much was right
Recall (true positive rate, sensitivity)TP / (TP + FN)Of all actual positives, how many I caught
F12PR / (P + R)Harmonic mean of precision and recall
Fβ(1 + β²)PR / (β²P + R)Recall counted β times as important as precision (van Rijsbergen 1979, chapter 7)
False positive rate (FPR)FP / (FP + TN)Fraction of actual negatives wrongly flagged
Specificity (true negative rate)TN / (TN + FP)1 − FPR
Balanced accuracy(recall + specificity) / 2Accuracy with both classes weighted equally
MCC(TP·TN − FP·FN) / √((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN))Correlation between decision and truth, from −1 to 1, 0 for guessing (Matthews 1975)

F1 never looks at TN: it is the score of the class you act on. That is right for search or fraud alerts, but it means F1 changes if you swap which class is called positive. MCC is symmetric in the two classes and is high only when all four cells are good, which is why Chicco and Jurman (2020) argue for it over F1 and accuracy on imbalanced binary tasks.

2 · Ranking: ROC and precision-recall curves

Sweeping the threshold from high to low traces two curves. The ROC curve plots recall (TPR) against FPR; its area, ROC-AUC, equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative, with ties counted half (Hanley and McNeil 1982; Fawcett 2006). The precision-recall (PR) curve records precision and recall at each threshold, and its area is AUPRC. A random ranker scores ROC-AUC 0.5 whatever the classes, but AUPRC equal to the share of positives, so the two areas are not on the same scale.

Why ROC misleads when positives are rare comes down to one denominator. FPR divides by all negatives, which is a huge number, so even thousands of extra false positives barely move it while recall moves quickly; the ROC curve looks good regardless. Precision divides by the model's own positive predictions, so it is hit by every one of those false positives (Davis and Goadrich 2006). Use AUPRC when positives are rare. The root page's widget shows it with a fixed classifier as positives get rarer: ROC-AUC stays at 0.856 while AUPRC falls from 0.85 to 0.11 (Topic: ml-fundamentals, Reading, step 7). The SST-2 classifier does the same on real data: ROC-AUC on the full set and on the rare-positive subset, average precision and .

Two results from Davis and Goadrich are worth keeping. One curve dominates another in ROC space if and only if it dominates in PR space, so a ranking that is better everywhere is better on both. But points in PR space cannot be joined by straight lines: precision does not interpolate linearly, so the trapezoidal area under a PR curve is optimistic. That is why scikit-learn's average_precision_score sums precision times each step in recall with no interpolation; "AUPRC" in papers may be either, and the two differ.

3 · Probabilities: calibration, log-loss and Brier

Ranking metrics ignore what a score means. A classifier is calibrated when, of all the examples it gives probability 0.8, about 80% are positive. Two scores measure probabilities directly. Log-loss is the cross entropy of the predicted probability of the true class, −mean log p(true class), which is the training loss read as a measurement. The Brier score is the mean squared error of the probability against the 0 or 1 outcome (Brier 1950). Both are proper scoring rules: the expected score is best when the forecast equals the true probability, so neither can be gamed by hedging (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). A reliability diagram bins predictions by confidence and plots the observed rate in each bin; the expected calibration error (ECE) is the sample-weighted mean gap between confidence and observed rate over the bins (Guo et al. 2017, who found that modern deep networks, unlike those of a decade earlier, are poorly calibrated, and that temperature scaling, one parameter, fixes most of it). ECE depends on the binning (here 10 equal-width bins) and is not a proper scoring rule.

The more accurate model is the worse forecaster. A multinomial naive Bayes classifier on the same SST-2 features is slightly more accurate than the logistic regression ( against at threshold 0.5) and ranks almost as well, yet its probabilities are far worse: naive Bayes multiplies word evidence as if words were independent, which pushes its outputs towards 0 and 1 (Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana 2005). Isotonic regression, a monotone step function fitted on cross-validated predictions (Zadrozny and Elkan 2002), maps them back towards observed frequencies without changing their order. 4 · More than two classes: macro, micro, weighted

With K classes each class gets its own precision, recall and F1 (one class against the rest), and a single number needs an average (scikit-learn user guide). Macro takes the plain mean over classes, so a 9-example class counts as much as a 76-example one. Weighted weights each class by its support (true count). Micro pools the counts first; in single-label multi-class classification every false positive for one class is a false negative for another, so micro precision, micro recall and micro F1 all equal accuracy.

Try: switch the averaging and watch the bar widths, which are each class's weight. The rare "vehicle window" class scores F1 = 0 and drags only the macro average down.

5 · Text against a reference: BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, chrF

When the output is text, the classic metrics compare it with one or more human references by counting what they share.

MetricMechanismOrigin, notes
BLEUFor n = 1 to 4, the share of the output's n-grams found in the reference, each clipped to the count the reference has ("modified precision"); their geometric mean, times a brevity penalty BP = exp(1 − r/c) when the output length c is below the reference length r, else 1Machine translation; precision-oriented; defined over a whole test corpus (Papineni et al. 2002)
ROUGE-NPrecision, recall and F1 of matching n-gramsSummarisation; recall-oriented (Lin 2004)
ROUGE-LLongest common subsequence of the two word sequences, as precision, recall and F1Order-sensitive without requiring contiguity
ROUGE-SSkip-bigram co-occurrence: every ordered pair of words, any gap apartAllows gaps within bigrams; rarely reported now
METEOROne-to-one unigram alignment in stages (exact words, then Porter stems, then WordNet synonyms); Fmean = 10PR / (R + 9P); penalty 0.5 × (chunks / matches)3, where a chunk is a run of matches adjacent and in the same order in both; score = Fmean × (1 − penalty)Built to score single sentences (Banerjee and Lavie 2005)
chrFCharacter 1- to 6-gram precision and recall (spaces removed), averaged over orders, combined as Fβ with β = 2Robust to word forms and spelling (Popović 2015)

BLEU is a corpus metric: summed over a test set, a sentence with no 4-gram match does not zero the score. Scored on one sentence it needs smoothing; sacreBLEU's sentence_bleu uses the "exp" method (each zero precision becomes 1 / (2k × total), Chen and Cherry 2014, method 3) and drops n-gram orders longer than the output. Tokenisation changes the number too: Post (2018) measured differences as high as 1.8 BLEU between commonly used configurations, which is why sacreBLEU fixes the tokenizer (13a) and prints a signature. Correction to the old page: what METEOR's paper measured
The old page said METEOR "correlates with humans better than BLEU at sentence level". The paper's only comparison with BLEU is at the system level, on the Chinese portion of the TIDES 2003 data: Pearson correlation with human judgement 0.964 for METEOR against 0.817 for BLEU (Table 1). At the segment level it reports R = 0.347 (Arabic) and 0.331 (Chinese) and compares with unigram precision, recall and F1, not with BLEU, which its authors call unfair to score per sentence (Banerjee and Lavie 2005, section 3.2).

6 · Perplexity, and why it depends on the tokenizer

Perplexity is the reciprocal geometric mean of the probabilities a model gives each token of a text: PPL = (∏i p(wi | w<i))−1/N = exp(mean negative log-likelihood per token). Lower is better, and it is the training loss (cross entropy per token) read as a score on held-out text. The catch is N: it counts tokens, and different tokenizers cut the same text into different numbers of tokens. The total log-probability of the text is comparable across models; dividing it by a model-specific token count is not. A tokenizer that splits text finely makes each token easier to predict, because the later pieces of a word follow almost surely from the first, and per-token perplexity drops without the model knowing anything more. The fix is to normalise by something the tokenizer does not choose: bits per UTF-8 byte, or perplexity per word. The Pile's authors prefer bits per byte "due to its invariance to different tokenization schemes" (Gao et al. 2020, section 3.1).

Try: "Digits of pi", then switch between GPT-2 and Qwen2.5. Each bar's width is the bytes its token covers and its height is the bits per byte the model paid, so its area is the token's bits and the total area is comparable across models.

Real per-token log-probabilities from GPT-2 (vocabulary 50,257), SmolLM2-135M (49,152) and Qwen2.5-0.5B (151,665 tokens in the tokenizer), base models in float32, each text scored after the model's own end-of-text token (src/data_ppl.py). Texts: Article 1 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in English and French, a three-line Python function written for this page, and the first 35 decimals of pi. Bars are capped at 10 bits per byte.

Two more things change perplexity without changing the model: the text (GPT-2's perplexity on the French article is 11 times its perplexity on the same article in English), and the context each token is given. Text longer than the context window is scored with a sliding window, and a larger stride gives each token less context and a higher perplexity (Hugging Face, perplexity of fixed-length models). And low perplexity is not good generation: on GPT-2 Large, beam search output has perplexity 1.48 against 12.38 for human text, because the most probable text is degenerate (Sampling and Decoding, after Holtzman et al.). 7 · Embedding scores, learned metrics and judges

BERTScore embeds both texts with a contextual encoder, takes the cosine similarity of every candidate token with every reference token, and matches greedily: precision averages, over candidate tokens, the best similarity found in the reference; recall does the same from the reference side; F1 combines them. Optional idf weights make rare words count more (Zhang et al. 2019, ICLR 2020). The English default in the bert-score package is roberta-large, layer 17. Raw scores crowd into a narrow band (here 0.90 for shuffled words to 1.00 for a copy), so the package can rescale them linearly against a baseline computed from unrelated sentence pairs (rescale_baseline.md): (score − b) / (1 − b), which spreads the range without changing any ranking. The BERTScore view of the animation above draws the real similarity matrix and the greedy matches.

MoverScore replaces greedy one-to-one matching with an earth mover's distance between the two sets of contextual embeddings, a softer, many-to-one alignment (Zhao et al. 2019). It is not computed here: it needs idf statistics from a corpus and an optimal-transport solve on the embeddings, and on these sentences it would add a second embedding score with the same blind spot. Learned metrics such as COMET go further and train on human quality judgements; those are the "neural metrics" WMT22 found better than overlap.

For open-ended LLM output, evaluation has largely moved to LLM-as-judge and task-specific benchmarks: a model grades the answer against a rubric, a checklist or another answer. That is a measuring instrument with its own biases and calibration, and it has its own topic, Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges (judge design and biases on LLM-as-judge). Two paper pages here take it apart with data: RocketEval (a small judge grading checklists re-ranks a leaderboard almost exactly, tab "Re-rank the leaderboard") and Re-grading six physics benchmarks (what expert re-grading does to headline scores, with Wilson intervals).

8 · How much to trust a number: error bars

A test set is a sample, so every metric on it is an estimate with sampling error. For accuracy p on n examples the standard error is √(p(1 − p)/n): at 80% on 872 sentences that is 1.4 points, so a 95% interval is about ±2.7. For metrics with no simple formula (F1, AUC, BLEU) the bootstrap resamples the test set with replacement many times and reads the interval off the spread (Efron 1979). Comparing two models on the same test set, resample the pairs: the paired difference cancels the shared difficulty of each example and is far tighter than two separate intervals (Koehn 2004 for MT; Miller 2024 for LLM evals, which also covers clustered questions).

Try: shrink the test set. At 872 sentences naive Bayes is 1.1 points more accurate than logistic regression, and the paired interval still crosses zero.

And size the test set for the difference you need to detect before collecting it; the error bars on public LLM benchmarks are covered on the Production eval engineering page of the evaluation topic.

Information quantities

Entropy H(P) = −Σ P log P (the average bits to encode samples of P with P's own optimal code), cross entropy H(P, Q) = −Σ P log Q (with a code built for Q) and their difference, the KL divergence DKL(P‖Q) = Σ P log(P/Q) (the extra bits for the wrong code; asymmetric, no triangle inequality), are the root page's thread 1, "Everything is cross entropy", with its slider. On this page they appear as log-loss (section 3) and perplexity (section 6). Minimising cross entropy over Q is minimising KL, since H(P) is fixed; with one-hot labels H(P) = 0 and cross entropy = KL = the negative log-likelihood of the true class. KL is the workhorse for measuring divergence between distributions: VAE regularisers, distillation, RLHF policy constraints (Topic: ml-fundamentals, Reading, "Four threads"; as losses, Loss functions).

Common mistakes

Reporting accuracy on an imbalanced task
On the rare-positive SST-2 subset "always negative" scores 90.5%, above the real classifier. Report MCC or per-class precision and recall, and the base rate (section 1).
Choosing the threshold on the test set
Picking the threshold that maximises F1 or MCC on the data you then report inflates the score. Choose it on validation data, or report a threshold-free metric.
Reading ROC-AUC as "good" when positives are rare
ROC-AUC barely moves with prevalence; precision does. Look at AUPRC or precision at the recall you need (section 2).
Calling a trapezoidal PR area "average precision"
Linear interpolation in PR space is optimistic (Davis and Goadrich 2006). Say which you computed.
Treating a confident score as a probability
Accuracy and AUC cannot see calibration; naive Bayes here is the more accurate model and has twice the log-loss. Check a reliability diagram and the Brier score before using probabilities (section 3).
"F1" without saying macro, micro or weighted
On the glass data the three give 0.57, 0.63 and 0.65 for the same predictions (section 4).
Comparing BLEU across papers
Tokenisation and normalisation alone move it by up to 1.8 points (Post 2018). Use sacreBLEU and quote its signature; never compare sentence BLEU with corpus BLEU.
Trusting overlap scores on meaning
A one-word negation scores higher than a faithful paraphrase on BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, chrF and BERTScore (section 5).
Comparing perplexity across tokenizers or texts
Per-token perplexity rewards fine tokenizers; compare bits per byte, or perplexity only between models with the same tokenizer, text and context (section 6).
A leaderboard gap without an error bar
On 872 examples a 1-point gap is inside the noise; use a paired bootstrap (section 8).

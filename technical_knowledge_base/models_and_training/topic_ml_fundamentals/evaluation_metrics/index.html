The idea in one screen

A metric is a decision about which errors count and how much: most classification scores are ratios of one confusion matrix at one threshold, the rest summarise every threshold (ROC-AUC, average precision) or the probabilities themselves (log-loss, Brier, ECE), and each one is blind to whatever its choice leaves out.

Two more ideas carry the page. An average hides whom it averages over: examples (accuracy, micro), classes (macro), tokens (perplexity), queries (MRR), n-gram orders (BLEU). Every number from a finite test set has an error bar, and on a few hundred examples it is often wider than the gap being reported.

Predict, then drag. Switch to "Rare positives" and the threshold sits at 0.5. Press "Best MCC" (0.79), then drag back to 0.5: the same 97 extra false alarms appear. Which dot moves further, the one on the ROC curve or the one on the precision-recall curve?

    1 · Classification at a threshold

    Every metric here is a ratio of the four counts in the hero's matrix at one threshold (Google ML Crash Course). F1 never looks at TN: it scores the class you act on, which suits search or fraud alerts but means F1 changes if you swap which class is called positive. MCC is symmetric in the two classes and is high only when all four cells are good, which is why Chicco and Jurman (2020) argue for it over F1 and accuracy on imbalanced binary tasks.

    2 · Every threshold at once: ROC and precision-recall

    Sweeping the threshold traces the hero's two curves. ROC-AUC, the area under recall against FPR, equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive is scored above a randomly chosen negative, ties counted half (Hanley and McNeil 1982; Fawcett 2006). The area under precision against recall is AUPRC. A random ranker scores ROC-AUC 0.5 whatever the classes but AUPRC equal to the share of positives, so the two areas are not on the same scale.

    Why ROC misleads when positives are rare is one denominator. FPR divides by all negatives, a huge number, so thousands of extra false positives barely move it; precision divides by the model's own positive calls and is hit by every one (Davis and Goadrich 2006). Use AUPRC, or precision at the recall you need, when positives are rare.

    Two more results from Davis and Goadrich. One curve dominates another in ROC space if and only if it dominates in PR space. But PR points cannot be joined by straight lines, because precision does not interpolate linearly, so a trapezoidal PR area is optimistic. scikit-learn's average_precision_score therefore sums precision times each step in recall with no interpolation; "AUPRC" in papers may be either, and the two differ, so say which you computed.

    3 · Probabilities: calibration, log-loss and Brier

    A classifier is calibrated when, of all the examples it gives probability 0.8, about 80% are positive. Ranking metrics cannot see this. Log-loss is −mean log p(true class), the training loss read as a measurement; the Brier score is the mean squared error of the probability against the 0 or 1 outcome (Brier 1950). Both are proper scoring rules, so neither can be gamed by hedging (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). A reliability diagram bins predictions by confidence and plots the observed rate per bin; the expected calibration error (ECE) is the sample-weighted mean gap over the bins (Guo et al. 2017, who found modern deep networks, unlike those of a decade earlier, poorly calibrated, and that temperature scaling, one parameter, fixes most of it). ECE depends on the binning (here 10 equal-width bins) and is not a proper scoring rule. The same diagram for language models' next-token probabilities, measured on GPT-2 and Qwen2.5-0.5B with temperature scaling, is on Probability for ML, section 8.

    The more accurate model is the worse forecaster. Naive Bayes on the same features is slightly more accurate than logistic regression ( against at 0.5) and ranks almost as well, yet multiplies word evidence as if words were independent, which pushes its outputs towards 0 and 1 (Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana 2005). Isotonic regression, a monotone step function fitted on cross-validated predictions (Zadrozny and Elkan 2002), maps them back towards observed frequencies without changing their order.

    Predict, then watch: between the first and the last naive Bayes step, which of accuracy, ROC-AUC and log-loss moves most?

    1. Naive Bayes, as trained. Each dot is one of the 872 validation sentences, placed at the probability of "positive" the model gives it; blue dots are positive reviews, orange negative. Most dots sit at the two edges: the model is almost always nearly certain.
    2. Bin by confidence. Cut the probability axis into 10 equal bins. Below, each bin's green bar is the share of its sentences that really are positive; a calibrated model's bars would reach the diagonal.
    3. The gaps are the calibration error. Sentences given about 0.98 are positive far less often than 98% of the time, and those given 0.02 far more often than 2%. ECE is the gap per bin (red) weighted by the bin's share of sentences.
    4. Isotonic calibration moves every dot. A monotone step function, fitted on cross-validated training predictions, maps each probability to the observed frequency for scores like it. Order is kept, so the ranking (ROC-AUC) barely moves; the dots leave the edges.
    5. Re-binned: much closer, not perfect. ECE falls from 0.135 to 0.076, log-loss from 0.85 to 0.45, the Brier score from 0.157 to 0.142; accuracy moves by half a point because a few sentences cross 0.5. The top bins are still overconfident: the map was fitted on training phrases, many of them short fragments, and these are full sentences, so calibration learned on one distribution only partly transfers to another.
    6. For reference: logistic regression. Trained by minimising log-loss, logistic regression is close to calibrated without any correction, and has the best Brier score of the three while being the least accurate at 0.5.

    Measure calibration whenever a probability is used as a probability: a risk threshold, an expected cost, an abstention rule, a confidence shown to a user, a score fed to another model. Judge calibration against human labels is on LLM-as-judge.

    4 · More than two classes: macro, micro, weighted

    With K classes each class gets its own precision, recall and F1 (one against the rest), and one number needs an average (scikit-learn user guide). Macro takes the plain mean over classes, so a 9-example class counts as much as a 76-example one. Weighted weights each class by its support (true count). Micro pools the counts first; in single-label multi-class classification every false positive for one class is a false negative for another, so micro precision, recall and F1 all equal accuracy.

    Try: switch the averaging; bar opacity is each class's weight. The rare "vehicle window" class scores F1 = 0 and drags only the macro average down.

    None is "correct". Macro answers "how good on a typical class", weighted "on a typical class, sized as in this data", micro "on a typical example". Report which, and per-class numbers when a class matters on its own.

    5 · Regression: MAE, RMSE, R² and MAPE

    For a numeric target the errors are residuals, y − ŷ, and the metric decides how to add them up. MAE is the mean absolute residual, in the target's units; RMSE is the square root of the mean squared residual, also in those units but dominated by the largest misses, and RMSE ≥ MAE always (Willmott and Matsuura 2005 argue for MAE as the typical error; Chai and Draxler 2014 for RMSE when errors are Gaussian). R² = 1 − SSE / SST compares the squared error with always predicting the test mean: 0 is that baseline, 1 is perfect, and it goes negative for a model worse than the mean. MAPE, the mean of |y − ŷ| / |y|, is scale-free but explodes when a true value is near zero (Hyndman and Koehler 2006) and is lopsided: for a positive target and a non-negative prediction, an under-prediction costs at most 100%, an over-prediction without limit. Minimising MSE targets the conditional mean and minimising MAE the conditional median, which is why the loss and the reported metric should match (losses: Loss functions).

    Predict, then drag: one of 442 predictions gets an extra error of 1,000 (a unit slip). How far does MAE move, and how far does RMSE?

    Report MAE when every unit of error costs the same, RMSE when large misses cost disproportionately, R² to compare against the trivial model on the same data (never across datasets, since it depends on the target's variance), and a relative error only when no true value is near zero.

    6 · Ranked lists: precision@k, recall@k, MRR, MAP, nDCG

    Search, retrieval and recommendation return an ordered list per query, and the metrics look only at the top k. Precision@k is the share of the top k that is relevant and recall@k the share of all relevant items that made the top k: the threshold metrics of section 1, with the threshold at rank k. Reciprocal rank is 1 / (rank of the first relevant item), and MRR its mean over queries (Voorhees 1999, TREC-8 question answering); it ignores everything after the first hit. hit@k (also called "success@k", and "recall@k" in some dense-retrieval papers) is 1 when any relevant item is in the top k and 0 otherwise, averaged over queries: it equals recall@k when every query has exactly one relevant item and is more lenient otherwise, so check which one a paper means before comparing numbers. Average precision averages precision@i at the rank i of each relevant item, divided by the number of relevant items, and MAP is its mean over queries. nDCG handles graded relevance: DCG@k = Σi≤k gaini / log2(i + 1), divided by the same sum for the ideal ordering so that 1 is perfect (Järvelin and Kekäläinen 2002). The gain is the grade itself (scikit-learn's ndcg_score) or 2grade − 1, which favours highly relevant items (Burges et al. 2005); the two give different numbers, so say which.

    Fig. 2, step by step

    1. Ten results, graded. One query's top ten as columns in rank order, each as tall as its relevance grade (0 to 3; an illustrative list); relevant ones are lit. A fifth relevant item (grade 1) was never returned: it stands apart, outside the list, and still counts in recall and in the ideal ordering.
    2. Cut at k = 5. The glass plane keeps the top five. 3 of them are relevant, so precision@5 = 3/5 = 0.6; they are 3 of the 5 relevant items, so recall@5 = 0.6.
    3. The first hit. The first relevant item sits at rank 2, so the reciprocal rank is 1/2 = 0.5, whatever comes after it; MRR averages this number over queries.
    4. Precision at each hit. Average precision reads precision@i at the rank of each relevant item in the top five, 1/2, 2/4 and 3/5, and divides their sum by all 5 relevant items: AP@5 = (0.5 + 0.5 + 0.6) / 5 = 0.32.
    5. Discount by rank. Each grade is divided by log2(rank + 1): the grade 3 at rank 2 keeps 1.89, the grade 1 at rank 5 only 0.39. The discounted gains inside the cut, stacked, are DCG@5 = 3.141.
    6. Against the ideal order. Behind, the same five relevant grades sorted best first (3, 2, 2, 1, 1), discounted the same way and stacked: ideal DCG@5 = 6.079. nDCG@5 = 3.141 / 6.079 = 0.517.

    Try: move k. Precision@k goes up and down as hits and misses arrive; recall@k and nDCG@k only grow; reciprocal rank is fixed by rank 2.

    Two traps. Items nobody judged count as irrelevant, so a new system that finds unjudged good documents is under-scored; and the ideal DCG uses only the judged items, so nDCG is comparable only across systems on the same judgements. The same metrics on real retrieval runs (BM25 against a dense retriever on three BEIR sets, and what a reranker does to them) are on RAG and retrieval evaluation.

    7 · Text against a reference: BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, chrF

    When the output is text, the classic metrics count what it shares with one or more human references.

    MetricMechanismOrigin, notes
    BLEUFor n = 1 to 4, the share of the output's n-grams found in the reference, each clipped to the count the reference has ("modified precision"); their geometric mean, times a brevity penalty BP = exp(1 − r/c) when the output length c is below the reference length r, else 1Machine translation; precision-oriented; defined over a whole test corpus (Papineni et al. 2002)
    ROUGE-NPrecision, recall and F1 of matching n-gramsSummarisation; recall-oriented (Lin 2004)
    ROUGE-LLongest common subsequence of the two word sequences, as precision, recall and F1Order-sensitive without requiring contiguity
    ROUGE-SSkip-bigram co-occurrence: every ordered pair of words, any gap apartAllows gaps within bigrams; rarely reported now
    METEOROne-to-one unigram alignment in stages (exact words, then Porter stems, then WordNet synonyms); Fmean = 10PR / (R + 9P); penalty 0.5 × (chunks / matches)3, where a chunk is a run of matches adjacent and in the same order in both; score = Fmean × (1 − penalty)Built to score single sentences (Banerjee and Lavie 2005)
    chrFCharacter 1- to 6-gram precision and recall (spaces removed), averaged over orders, combined as Fβ with β = 2Robust to word forms and spelling (Popović 2015)

    BLEU is a corpus metric: summed over a test set, a sentence with no 4-gram match does not zero the score. On one sentence it needs smoothing; sacreBLEU's sentence_bleu uses the "exp" method (each zero precision becomes 1 / (2k × total), Chen and Cherry 2014, method 3) and drops n-gram orders longer than the output, so never compare sentence BLEU with corpus BLEU. Tokenisation changes the number too: Post (2018) measured differences as high as 1.8 BLEU between commonly used configurations, which is why sacreBLEU fixes the tokenizer (13a) and prints a signature to quote. Every score below is computed in your browser with ports of the reference code, checked against sacrebleu 2.6.0, rouge-score 0.1.2 and NLTK 3.10.3 on these sentences and on 150 random sentence pairs (all match to 10−9; src/check_core.mjs).

    Correction to the old page: what METEOR's paper measured
    The old page said METEOR "correlates with humans better than BLEU at sentence level". The paper's only comparison with BLEU is at the system level, on the Chinese portion of the TIDES 2003 data: Pearson correlation with human judgement 0.964 for METEOR against 0.817 for BLEU (Table 1). At the segment level it reports R = 0.347 (Arabic) and 0.331 (Chinese) and compares with unigram precision, recall and F1, not with BLEU, which its authors call unfair to score per sentence (Banerjee and Lavie 2005, section 3.2).

    The scorer, step by step

    1. Tokenise (13a). Both sentences are split into tokens with punctuation separated, as WMT's mteval-v13a does.
    2. Unigrams. Each of the candidate's words is looked up in the reference, and each reference word can be used as many times as it occurs there ("clipping"): p1 is the share found.
    3. Bigrams. The same for pairs of adjacent words: p2.
    4. 3-grams. Runs of three: p3. Moving a clause breaks the runs that cross its edges while keeping every word.
    5. 4-grams. Runs of four: p4. A sentence with no 4-gram match would score zero, so sentence BLEU smooths a zero precision by the exp method.
    6. Geometric mean of the four precisions. exp(mean of log pn). A geometric mean is dragged down by its weakest term, so losing the long n-grams costs more than losing words.
    7. Brevity penalty. Precision alone would reward saying little: a candidate shorter than the reference is multiplied by BP = exp(1 − r/c); otherwise BP = 1.
    8. BLEU. BP × geometric mean × 100, checked against sacrebleu's sentence_bleu.
    1. Tokenise (rouge-score). Lower case, every non-alphanumeric character becomes a space, no stemming by default.
    2. Longest common subsequence. The longest run of words appearing in both in the same order, gaps allowed (green). Words outside it count for nothing, even if they appear in both.
    3. ROUGE-L F1. Precision is LCS / candidate length, recall LCS / reference length, F1 their harmonic mean. Order matters, contiguity does not.
    1. Tokenise, lower case. METEOR aligns words, one to one.
    2. Stage 1, exact matches. Each candidate word, last to first, takes the last unused identical reference word.
    3. Stage 2, Porter stems. Left-over words are stemmed and matched again (green). WordNet synonyms would be stage 3, which this page does not run live.
    4. Fragmentation. A chunk is a run of matches adjacent and in the same order in both sentences; colours alternate by chunk. One chunk means the matched words appear as a single block in the same order.
    5. METEOR. Fmean = 10PR / (R + 9P) weights recall 9 to 1; the penalty is 0.5 × (chunks / matches)3; score = Fmean × (1 − penalty).
    1. Remove spaces, read characters. chrF ignores word boundaries, so word forms ("budgets", "approve") still share most of their character n-grams.
    2. Character n-gram precision and recall, n = 1 to 6. For each order, clipped matches over the candidate's n-grams (precision) and over the reference's (recall).
    3. chrF. Average precision and recall over the six orders, then Fβ = (1 + β²)PR / (β²P + R) with β = 2, so recall counts twice as much.
    1. Tokens, in context. roberta-large splits both sentences into word pieces (<s> and </s> mark the ends) and gives every piece a vector from layer 17 that depends on the whole sentence.
    2. Cosine similarity of every pair. Darker cells are more similar. Even unrelated pieces look fairly similar in this layer, which is why raw BERTScores sit in a narrow band.
    3. Precision: each candidate piece takes its best match. Greedy, no one-to-one constraint: two candidate pieces may pick the same reference piece. Precision is the mean of the circled values, special tokens excluded.
    4. Recall: each reference piece takes its best match. The same from the reference side, column by column.
    5. F1, then rescaled. F1 combines the two, then (F − b) / (1 − b) rescales it against the baseline b for this model and layer. Values come from a similarity matrix stored to 3 decimals, so they can differ from the library in the third decimal.

    Overlap rewards the wrong thing. A faithful paraphrase that shares few words with the reference against a copy with one word changed so that it says the opposite: every metric prefers the wrong one, the embedding metric included.

    8 · Perplexity, and why it depends on the tokenizer

    Perplexity is the reciprocal geometric mean of the probabilities a model gives each token: PPL = (∏i p(wi | w<i))−1/N = exp(mean negative log-likelihood per token), the training cross entropy read as a score on held-out text. The catch is N, a count of tokens: the total log-probability of a text is comparable across models, but dividing it by a model-specific token count is not. A tokenizer that splits finely makes each token easier to predict, because the later pieces of a word follow almost surely from the first, and per-token perplexity drops without the model knowing anything more. Normalise by something the tokenizer does not choose: bits per UTF-8 byte, or perplexity per word. The Pile's authors prefer bits per byte "due to its invariance to different tokenization schemes" (Gao et al. 2020, section 3.1).

    Fig. 3, step by step

    1. The text, as bytes. "The first digits of pi are 3.14159265358979323846264338327950288." is 65 UTF-8 bytes and 7 words: the glass strip in front, one cell per byte. Bytes and words are counts no tokenizer chooses.
    2. GPT-2 cuts it into 23 tokens. Each column is one token, scored given everything before it: its width is the bytes the token covers and its height the bits per byte the model paid for it (−log2 p over the token's bytes), so its area is the token's bits. Total: 122.5 bits.
    3. SmolLM2-135M cuts it into 45 tokens and pays 50.5 bits in all: the same text, a different cut, a different total.
    4. Qwen2.5-0.5B cuts it into 45 tokens and pays 48.1 bits. The total bits of a text are comparable across models; the number of tokens is not.
    5. Perplexity per token. exp(mean nats per token), that is 2 to the power of the total bits over the token count: GPT-2 40.11, SmolLM2 2.18, Qwen2.5 2.10 (the side columns, in bits per token). The highest is 19.1 times the lowest, partly better prediction and partly finer cutting: a model that spends more tokens on the same text divides the same kind of total by a bigger N.
    6. Bits per byte. The same total bits divided by the text's bytes: GPT-2 1.884, SmolLM2 0.777, Qwen2.5 0.740. The gap is now 2.5 times: what is left is the models' prediction, not their tokenizers.

    The stage: Real per-token log-probabilities from GPT-2 (vocabulary 50,257), SmolLM2-135M (49,152) and Qwen2.5-0.5B (151,665 tokens in the tokenizer), base models in float32, each text scored after the model's own end-of-text token (src/data_ppl.py). Texts: Article 1 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in English and French, a three-line Python function written for this page, and the first 35 decimals of pi. Columns are capped at 10 bits per byte.

    Two more things change perplexity without changing the model: the text (GPT-2's perplexity on the French article is 11 times its perplexity on the same article in English), and the context each token gets: text longer than the window is scored with a sliding window, and a larger stride gives each token less context and a higher perplexity (Hugging Face, perplexity of fixed-length models). And low perplexity is not good generation: on GPT-2 Large, beam search output has perplexity 1.48 against 12.38 for human text, because the most probable text is degenerate (Sampling and Decoding, after Holtzman et al.). How tokenizers split languages differently is on Tokenizers.

    Entropy, cross entropy and KL behind log-loss and perplexity (owned by the root's thread 1)
    Entropy H(P) = −Σ P log P is the average bits to encode samples of P with P's own optimal code; cross entropy H(P, Q) = −Σ P log Q uses a code built for Q; their difference is the KL divergence DKL(P‖Q) = Σ P log(P/Q), the extra bits for the wrong code (asymmetric, no triangle inequality). Minimising cross entropy over Q is minimising KL, since H(P) is fixed; with one-hot labels H(P) = 0 and cross entropy = KL = the negative log-likelihood of the true class, which is log-loss (section 3) and log perplexity (above). KL is the workhorse for divergence between distributions: VAE regularisers, distillation, RLHF policy constraints. The slider is the root's "Everything is cross entropy" (Topic: ml-fundamentals, Reading, "Four threads"); as losses, Loss functions.

    9 · Embedding scores, learned metrics and judges

    BERTScore embeds both texts with a contextual encoder, takes the cosine similarity of every candidate token with every reference token, and matches greedily: precision averages, over candidate tokens, the best similarity found in the reference; recall does the same from the reference side; F1 combines them; optional idf weights make rare words count more (Zhang et al. 2019, ICLR 2020). The English default in the bert-score package is roberta-large, layer 17. Raw scores crowd into a narrow band (here 0.90 for shuffled words to 1.00 for a copy), so the package can rescale them linearly against a baseline from unrelated sentence pairs (rescale_baseline.md), (score − b) / (1 − b), which spreads the range without changing any ranking. The BERTScore view of the step animation in section 7 draws the real similarity matrix and the greedy matches.

    MoverScore replaces greedy one-to-one matching with an earth mover's distance between the two sets of embeddings, a softer many-to-one alignment (Zhao et al. 2019); it is not computed here (it needs corpus idf statistics and an optimal-transport solve, and would add a second embedding score with the same blind spot). Learned metrics such as COMET train on human quality judgements; they are the "neural metrics" WMT22 found better than overlap. For open-ended LLM output, evaluation has largely moved to LLM judges and task benchmarks, a measuring instrument with its own biases and calibration: Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges, judge design on LLM-as-judge, and two paper pages with data: RocketEval (a small judge grading checklists re-ranks a leaderboard almost exactly) and Re-grading six physics benchmarks (what expert re-grading does to headline scores).

    10 · How much to trust a number: error bars

    A test set is a sample, so every metric on it has sampling error. For accuracy p on n examples the standard error is √(p(1 − p)/n): at 80% on 872 sentences that is 1.4 points, so a 95% interval is about ±2.7. On the hero's data naive Bayes is 1.1 points more accurate than logistic regression, and the paired 95% interval (−1.0 to +3.4 points, src/recompute.py) still crosses zero: on 872 examples a 1-point gap is inside the noise. For metrics with no simple formula (F1, AUC, BLEU) the bootstrap resamples the test set with replacement and reads the interval off the spread (Efron 1979); comparing two models on the same items, resample the pairs (Koehn 2004 for MT; Miller 2024 for LLM evals). Report the interval, say what was resampled (sentences, documents, users: whatever was drawn independently), and size the test set before collecting it. Paired tests, clustered questions and sample-size planning are owned by Eval statistics.

    Before reporting a metric: ten checks (each from a section above)
    • Accuracy on an imbalanced task: on the rare-positive subset "always negative" scores 90.5%, above the real classifier; report MCC or per-class precision and recall, and the base rate.
    • Threshold chosen on the test set: picking the threshold that maximises F1 or MCC on the data you then report inflates the score; choose it on validation data, or report a threshold-free metric.
    • ROC-AUC read as "good" when positives are rare: it barely moves with prevalence; look at AUPRC or precision at the recall you need.
    • A trapezoidal PR area called "average precision": linear interpolation in PR space is optimistic (Davis and Goadrich 2006).
    • A confident score treated as a probability: naive Bayes here is the more accurate model and has twice the log-loss; check a reliability diagram and the Brier score first.
    • "F1" without macro, micro or weighted: 0.57, 0.63 and 0.65 on the same glass predictions.
    • RMSE without MAE, or R² across datasets: one unit slip moves RMSE far more than MAE; R² depends on the target's variance.
    • BLEU across papers: tokenisation alone moves it by up to 1.8 points; quote the sacreBLEU signature.
    • Overlap scores trusted on meaning: a one-word negation beats a faithful paraphrase on BLEU, ROUGE, METEOR, chrF and BERTScore.
    • Perplexity across tokenizers or texts: compare bits per byte, or perplexity only with the same tokenizer, text and context.

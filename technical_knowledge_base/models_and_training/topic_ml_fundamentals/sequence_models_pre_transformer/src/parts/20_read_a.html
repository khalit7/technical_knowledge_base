Words as vectors: word2vec and GloVe

Every model below reads words as vectors. Before 2013 the usual input was a one-hot vector per word, in which every pair of words is equally far apart. word2vec learned a dense vector per word from raw text with a deliberately shallow model, so it could be trained on billions of words (Mikolov et al. 2013a):

  • Skip-gram predicts the words around a word from the word; CBOW (continuous bag of words) predicts a word from the average of its neighbours.
  • The full softmax over a vocabulary of millions is too expensive, so the first paper used a hierarchical softmax (a Huffman tree, about log2 V outputs per word) and the second replaced it with negative sampling (Mikolov et al. 2013b): push the true (word, context) pair together and k random words apart.
log σ(v′c · vw) + Σi=1..k Eni ~ Pn log σ(−v′ni · vw)
vw, v′c
input vector of the word, output vector of a context word
k
negatives per pair: 5 to 20 for small corpora, 2 to 5 for large ones
Pn
the unigram distribution raised to the 3/4 power, which beat both the plain unigram and the uniform distribution (§2.2); frequent words are also subsampled (§2.3)

GloVe took the other route: count first, then fit (Pennington, Socher and Manning 2014). Build the global word-by-word co-occurrence matrix X (pairs d words apart counted as 1/d), then fit vectors so that their dot product matches the log count, by weighted least squares:

J = Σi,j f(Xij) (wi · w̃j + bi + b̃j − log Xij)2,   f(x) = min(1, (x/xmax)α)
f
down-weights rare pairs and caps frequent ones; the paper uses xmax = 100, α = 3/4, AdaGrad, and the sum W + W̃ as the final vectors

The two are closer than they look: skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorises the word-context pointwise mutual information matrix shifted by log k (Levy and Goldberg 2014), so "predictive" (word2vec) against "count-based" (GloVe) is a difference of method more than of what is learned.

The analogy test, and what it hides

Both papers were judged on the analogy test: "a is to b as c is to ?", answered by the word nearest to b − a + c. On it, 300-dimension GloVe trained on 6 billion tokens scored 71.7%, against 69.1% for skip-gram and 65.7% for CBOW trained by the GloVe authors on the same corpus (GloVe paper, Table 2). Run on the real 50-dimension GloVe vectors (the ones in the {{Word vectors|#t-vec}} tab), all 19,544 questions, all 400,000 words:

What "king − man + woman = queen" leaves out
The nearest word to king − man + woman, on these vectors, is king (cosine 0.885); queen is second (0.852). Every published analogy score forbids the three question words as answers, and without that rule the answer is a question word in nearly half of all questions, so the famous result is partly the rule (Linzen 2016). The same rule manufactures bias examples: man : doctor :: woman : ? returns doctor, and "nurse" appears only once doctor is forbidden (Nissim, van Noord and van der Goot 2020). The gender bias in the vectors is real and measurable by other means (Bolukbasi et al. 2016); the analogy is a poor way to show it. On these GloVe vectors man : programmer :: woman : ? gives "prodigy", not Bolukbasi's "homemaker", which came from word2vec's Google News vectors and a constrained search.

Static, then contextual. One vector per word cannot tell the river bank from the money bank, and a word missing from the vocabulary has no vector at all. ELMo (Peters et al. 2018) replaced the lookup with the internal states of a bidirectional LSTM language model, so each word's vector is "a function of the entire input sentence", and {{BERT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e5ad9bd09cbf18ad7c}} did the same with a transformer. The vocabulary problem was solved separately, by splitting words into subword pieces ({{Tokenizers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817187bcd94a1b0f15ea}}). The embedding matrix at the bottom of every LLM is the direct descendant of word2vec's input vectors.

Recurrent networks and backpropagation through time

A recurrent network reads a sequence one element at a time and carries a hidden state forward (Elman 1990):

at = f(U xt + W at−1 + b),   yt = g(V at)
at
the hidden state, the network's whole memory of the past
U, W, V
input, recurrent and output weights, the same at every step: the network has a fixed number of parameters whatever the sequence length
f, g
usually tanh for the state; softmax for a distribution over outputs

It is trained by backpropagation through time (BPTT; Werbos 1990): unroll the network into one layer per time step, all sharing weights, run ordinary backpropagation, and add up the gradients each copy contributes to the shared weights. Long sequences are usually cut into windows (truncated BPTT), which also caps how far back any gradient can reach.

Why long dependencies were hard. The gradient from step t back to step k contains the product of Jacobians ∏ ∂aj/∂aj−1 = ∏ diag(f′)W, one factor per step. Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio made the condition exact: if the largest eigenvalue of W (in absolute value) is below 1/γ, where γ bounds |f′| (1 for tanh, 1/4 for the sigmoid), the long-term terms vanish exponentially; above it is necessary, not sufficient, for them to explode (2013, §2.1). Exploding gradients have a cheap fix, clipping the gradient's norm, which the same paper proposed; vanishing ones need a change of architecture. The difficulty itself was identified in Hochreiter's 1991 diploma thesis and by Bengio, Simard and Frasconi 1994.

The LSTM, gate by gate

The long short-term memory network gives each unit a memory cell C whose update is an addition, guarded by sigmoid gates (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997). Fig. 3 is one step of the toy LSTM (8 units, trained the same way as the other cells on sequences of 5 to 50 symbols), with its real activations on the running example at length 40: b w w z y y x w w ... ?. The input xt and the previous hidden state ht−1 are concatenated and fed to all four in parallel; the cell state runs along the back like a conveyor.

  1. Four layers read the same input. The key "b" and the previous state (zeros) go into four glass plates at once: the forget gate f, the input gate i and the output gate o (sigmoids, bars from 0 to 1) and the candidate C̃ (tanh, bars above or below the plinth). Each makes 8 numbers, one per unit.
  2. The key is written. On unit 4 (outlined) the input gate is 0.80 and the candidate +0.82, so i ⊙ C̃ puts +0.65 on the conveyor. With key "a" the same unit's candidate is −0.59: the key is written as a sign.
  3. A distractor: keep, and add again. At "w" the forget gate keeps 0.90 of the cell and the input gate, 0.86, is not shut: 0.90 × 0.65 + 0.86 × 0.75 = 1.23. The candidate has the key's sign because it reads h, which carries the key.
  4. Forty steps on the conveyor. Unit 4's cell at every step, key "b" in front and key "a" behind, on the same distractors: one grows to +15.2, the other to −5.8. The forget gates average 0.95, so the first write alone would have shrunk to 0.15 of its size; the memory is the running sum, re-written in the key's direction at every step.
  5. At "?", the output gate reads it out. o = 0.99 on unit 4 and tanh of a large cell is ±1, so h = 0.99; the readout over all eight units answers "b" with probability 1.00.

Not the textbook picture: this trained LSTM never shuts its input gate on the distractors (it averages 0.88 on unit 4); it keeps writing in the key's direction, and a saturated tanh reads a large cell the same way. The protected path is what matters for learning: along the cell, ∂Ct/∂Ct−1 is the forget gate alone.

The same four layers as formulas:

PartFormulaRole
Forget gateft = σ(Wf[ht−1, xt] + bf)how much of the old cell Ct−1 to keep
Input gateit = σ(Wi[ht−1, xt] + bi)which units to write
CandidateC̃t = tanh(WC[ht−1, xt] + bC)what would be written
Output gateot = σ(Wo[ht−1, xt] + bo)what part of the cell to expose
UpdatesCt = ft ⊙ Ct−1 + it ⊙ C̃t;   ht = ot ⊙ tanh(Ct)the cell is added to, not rewritten

Why it works. Along the cell, ∂Ct/∂Ct−1 = diag(ft) plus terms through the gates' dependence on h: no weight matrix and no squashing slope on the direct path, only the forget gate, which the network can hold near 1. It is a skip connection through time, the idea residual networks later used through depth. It does not abolish vanishing gradients: the path through h still multiplies Jacobians, and a forget gate of 0.9 still shrinks the cell path by 0.950 ≈ 0.005 over 50 steps. Setting the forget-gate bias to 1 or more at initialisation, so that the cell starts by remembering, is the standard recommendation (Jozefowicz, Zaremba and Sutskever 2015) and is used in the toy.

Correction: the 1997 LSTM had no forget gate
Its input and output gates guarded a "constant error carrousel", a cell whose self-connection was fixed at 1.0 (§3.2, §4). The forget gate came with Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins 2000, and that version is the LSTM in every library today.
Peepholes, and the history of the variants (under 1 min)

"Peephole" connections from the cell to the gates were added by Gers and Schmidhuber in 2000 too; library LSTMs usually leave them out. Greff et al. 2015, §III, has the history of the variants.

The GRU: two gates, one state

The gated recurrent unit merges the cell and the hidden state and uses two gates (Cho et al. 2014, who called it a new hidden unit; the name is from Chung et al. 2014).

ht = (1 − zt) ⊙ ht−1 + zt ⊙ h̃t,   h̃t = tanh(W xt + U(rt ⊙ ht−1))
zt
update gate: one gate doing the forget and input jobs together (keep 1 − z, write z)
rt
reset gate: how much of the old state the candidate may look at

The same interpolating, additive trick with three weight blocks instead of four, so at equal width a GRU has exactly 3/4 of an LSTM's parameters: 3n(n + m + 1) against 4n(n + m + 1) for n units and m inputs derived (PyTorch keeps two bias vectors, so +2 instead of +1: the toy's recurrent layers, n = 8, m = 9, have 456 against 608). Quality is usually comparable, so the GRU's real advantage is a quarter fewer parameters at the same width.

GRU against LSTM: what the side-by-side studies found (under 1 min)

Chung et al. 2014 "could not make concrete conclusion on which of the two gating units was better"; Jozefowicz et al. 2015 found that "adding a bias of 1 to the LSTM's forget gate closes the gap between the LSTM and the GRU"; Greff et al. 2015's search over eight LSTM variants found none significantly better than the standard LSTM, with the forget gate and the output activation its most critical parts.

Measured: who learns to remember

The memory task as an experiment: each cell trained from scratch on sequences up to 10, 20, 50 or 100 symbols long, at three learning rates (0.003, 0.01, 0.03) and three seeds, 3,000 Adam steps of 64 sequences, gradient norm clipped at 1; then tested on 2,000 fresh sequences at each length from 5 to 200.

The plain RNN can hold a memory but cannot learn one from long sequences: trained on lengths up to 10 it solved the task in of 9 runs, and two of those hold the key correctly at every test length up to 200; trained on lengths up to 50 or 100 it solved it in none of 18, ending at 25% (chance) or 50% (one bit of the key kept).

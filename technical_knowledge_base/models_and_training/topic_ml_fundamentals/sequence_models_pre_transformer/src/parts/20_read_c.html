Seq2seq: one vector between two networks

Translation maps a sequence to a sequence of a different length. The encoder-decoder answer of 2014: an encoder RNN reads the source and its final state becomes a fixed-size vector c; a decoder RNN, started from c, writes the target one word at a time, feeding back the word it just wrote (Cho et al. 2014, who also gave c to the decoder at every step; Sutskever, Vinyals and Le 2014). Sutskever et al.'s model was 4 layers of 1,000 LSTM cells with 1,000-dimension embeddings, 384M parameters, so the sentence had to fit in 8,000 numbers (cell and hidden state of every layer). An ensemble of five scored 34.81 BLEU on WMT'14 English to French against 33.30 for the phrase-based baseline: which the paper calls "the first time that a pure neural translation system outperforms a phrase-based SMT baseline on a large scale MT task".

The bottleneck. Everything about the source passes through one vector of fixed size, however long the sentence. Cho et al. found their encoder-decoder's performance "degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence and the number of unknown words increase", and Bahdanau et al. built attention on that observation.

Correction: "quality collapses on long inputs" was not universal
Sutskever et al. reported the opposite for their model: "Surprisingly, the LSTM did not suffer on very long sentences", which they credit to feeding the source sentence reversed. Reversal puts the first source words next to the first target words, introducing "many short term dependencies"; it took test BLEU from 25.9 to 30.6 and perplexity from 5.8 to 4.7 (§3.3). The collapse belongs to smaller, unreversed encoder-decoders (Cho et al.'s, Bahdanau et al.'s RNNencdec). The toy below shows both effects.

Attention: let the decoder look back

Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014, published at ICLR 2015) kept every encoder state hj (from a bidirectional RNN, so each state sees the whole sentence) and, at every output step i, let the decoder choose which to read:

eij = v⊤ tanh(W si−1 + U hj),   αij = softmaxj(eij),   ci = Σj αij hj
si−1
the decoder's state before writing word i
eij
"alignment" score: how well source position j matches what the decoder needs now; a small network, so this is called additive attention
ci
a fresh context vector for every output word, fed to the decoder with the previous word

The decoder now reads as many numbers as the source has states, so nothing has to be squeezed. In the paper's Table 1, trained on sentences up to 50 words, the attention model (RNNsearch-50) scored 26.75 BLEU against 17.82 for the same encoder-decoder without it (RNNencdec-50), and the attention model trained on sentences up to 30 words (21.50) beat the plain one trained up to 50. Their Figure 2 shows the plain model's BLEU falling with sentence length while RNNsearch-50 "shows no performance deterioration even with sentences of length 50 or more". The learned weights α are soft word alignments, readable as a matrix.

Luong, Pham and Manning 2015 simplified and generalised it: score with the decoder's current state ht rather than the previous one, and compare three score functions, dot ht⊤h̄s, general ht⊤Wah̄s and concat (Bahdanau's form), plus "global" attention over all source words against "local" attention over a window. The transformer's scaled dot product, softmax(QK⊤/√dk)V, is Luong's dot score with a scale and separate projections for queries, keys and values.

The same digits, with and without attention

A toy translation that only memory can solve: copy a string of digits. Two models with the same GRU encoder and decoder (16-dimension embeddings, 32 units) and the same training, differing only in the context vector: the encoder's last state at every step (the fixed vector, as Cho et al.) or Bahdanau's attention over all encoder states.

What the toy reproduces, at its scale: the fixed vector copies 6 digits almost always (96%) and 12 digits almost never (4%), so 32 numbers are not enough for a dozen digits; reversing the source, with no other change, lifts 10 digits from 24% to 68%, Sutskever et al.'s effect; and attention copies every length it was trained on perfectly. What it adds: attention fixed the bottleneck, not length generalisation. Past 20 digits the attention model degrades too (93% whole strings at 22, 1% at 26), and Bahdanau et al. single out only RNNsearch-50, the model trained on the longer sentences, as showing no deterioration at 50 words or more. Toy scale: digits, not language, and two seeds.

WaveNet: long context with no recurrence

Recurrence is sequential: step t waits for step t − 1, in training as in use. WaveNet (van den Oord et al., DeepMind 2016) generated raw audio one sample at a time (16,000 samples a second in its speech experiments) with a stack of causal convolutions: each output sees only the past, and every position of a training clip is computed in parallel. A plain causal stack grows its receptive field by one sample per layer (the paper's Figure 2: 4 layers, filter 2, field 5). Dilating the filters (skipping 1, 2, 4, ... inputs) doubles the field every layer instead.

Three more pieces mattered. Audio samples were μ-law companded and quantised to 256 values, so the output is a softmax over 256 classes instead of 65,536 (§2.2). Each layer uses the gated activation z = tanh(Wf ∗ x) ⊙ σ(Wg ∗ x), from gated PixelCNN, which "worked significantly better than the rectified linear activation" for audio (§2.3): the LSTM's gating idea without the recurrence, and an ancestor of the GLU family that ended in SwiGLU ({{Activation functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d819f8049c255e6f206e2}}). And conditioned on linguistic features, its text-to-speech scored a mean opinion score of 4.21 in US English and 4.08 in Mandarin, against 3.86 and 3.79 for the best earlier systems and 4.55 and 4.21 for natural 16-bit speech (Table 1). The text-to-speech models had a 240 ms receptive field = 3,840 samples at 16 kHz.

Correction: the WaveNet in production was not the 2016 model
The old page said WaveNet "powered Google TTS". The 2016 model generates one sample at a time, far too slowly to serve. What shipped in the Google Assistant in 2017 was Parallel WaveNet: a feed-forward student distilled from a trained WaveNet ("probability density distillation"), more than 20 times faster than real time, "deployed online by Google Assistant, including serving multiple English and Japanese voices".

WaveNet's lesson for text came quickly: ByteNet and ConvS2S translated with convolutions (linked from the {{Attention Is All You Need|n:3c65c17b0d0d81999af7f16f8ed8ee9e}} page, whose §4 compares their path lengths, O(logk n) for dilated and O(n/k) for contiguous filters, with self-attention's O(1)).

Where the lineage ends

The transformer (2017) removed the recurrence and kept only attention: self-attention within the encoder and within the decoder, cross-attention from decoder to encoder, so every position is computed at once in training and any two positions are one step apart. Its paper page animates exactly this contrast, one sentence through a recurrent layer (11 sequential steps) against one self-attention layer (one step), and runs a small trained transformer ({{Attention Is All You Need, Reading tab and "Run a Transformer"|n:3c65c17b0d0d81999af7f16f8ed8ee9e}}). Two things the RNN gave for free had to be added back: word order, since attention is blind to it ({{Positional Encodings|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}), and a bound on memory per token, since attention keeps every past state (the KV cache). Everything after lives in {{Topic: llms|n:3c65c17b0d0d812d9e00f6ec89965286}} and {{Topic: llm-training-and-post-training|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}}.

Postscript: recurrence returns. Constant memory and compute per generated token, the RNN's advantage, came back with training that runs in parallel over the sequence:

  • State-space models. {{Mamba|n:3c65c17b0d0d8179984fc3a1fee4c36a}} (Gu and Dao, December 2023) makes the recurrence input-dependent ("selective") and trains it with a parallel scan. Its Theorem 1 says the classical gated RNN is a special case of selection; the paper page has a slider for that gate. In production the winner is the hybrid: a few attention layers for exact recall, SSM layers for cheap length (Jamba, Nemotron-H, Falcon-H1, on the Mamba page).
  • Linear recurrences in RNN form. RWKV (2023) and xLSTM (2024, from Hochreiter's group: exponential gating and a matrix memory) scale recurrent language models to billions of parameters; "Were RNNs all we needed?" (2024) strips the LSTM and GRU gates of their dependence on the previous state so they too train with a parallel scan.

Common mistakes

"The LSTM solved vanishing gradients."
It gave the gradient one protected path, the cell, scaled only by the forget gate; everything through the hidden state still multiplies Jacobians, and a forget gate below 1 still decays geometrically (0.950 ≈ 0.005). Untrained, the toy LSTM's gradient 50 steps back is about 10−6 of its size at the answer (Measured, second view): far better than the plain RNN, nowhere near 1. Training opens the path; it does not start open.
"A plain RNN cannot remember for long."
It can represent a long memory: the toy plain RNN trained on short sequences holds the key for 200 steps. What it cannot do is learn that memory from long sequences, because the gradient that would teach it vanishes first. Representation and learnability are different questions.
"The LSTM was introduced with forget, input and output gates in 1997."
The forget gate came in 2000 (Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins); the 1997 cell had a fixed self-connection of 1.0. See the correction under The LSTM, gate by gate.
"king − man + woman = queen."
Only once king, man and woman are forbidden as answers; otherwise the nearest word is king. Without the rule GloVe 50d scores 30.4% on the analogy test instead of 46.2% ({{Word vectors|#t-vec}}).
"Seq2seq always collapses on long sentences."
Small, unreversed encoder-decoders did; Sutskever et al.'s deep LSTM with reversed sources "did not suffer on very long sentences". Attention removed the need for such tricks, and with it the fixed-size bottleneck.
"Attention was invented by the transformer."
It was added to RNN translation in 2014 (Bahdanau et al.) and refined in 2015 (Luong et al.); the transformer's contribution was to remove everything else. Its paper still describes its dot-product attention against "additive attention", Bahdanau's form.
"GRUs are better than LSTMs" (or the reverse).
Side-by-side studies did not settle it: Chung et al. 2014 could not conclude, Jozefowicz et al. 2015 found that "adding a bias of 1 to the LSTM's forget gate closes the gap between the LSTM and the GRU", and Greff et al. 2015 found no variant significantly better than the standard LSTM. The GRU's real advantage is a quarter fewer parameters at the same width.
"WaveNet is a recurrent network."
It has no recurrence: a stack of dilated causal convolutions, parallel in training. Generation is still one sample at a time because each sample feeds the next, which is why the deployed system was a distilled parallel student.

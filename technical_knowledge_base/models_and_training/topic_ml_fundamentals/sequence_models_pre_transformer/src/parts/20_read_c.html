Seq2seq, the bottleneck, and attention

Translation maps a sequence to a sequence of a different length. The encoder-decoder answer of 2014: an encoder RNN reads the source and its final state becomes a fixed-size vector c; a decoder RNN, started from c, writes the target one word at a time, feeding back the word it just wrote (Cho et al. 2014, who also gave c to the decoder at every step; Sutskever, Vinyals and Le 2014). Everything about the source passes through one vector of fixed size, however long the sentence: Sutskever et al.'s 4-layer, 1,000-cell LSTM (384M parameters) had to fit every sentence in 8,000 numbers (cell and hidden state of every layer). Cho et al. found their smaller encoder-decoder's performance "degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence and the number of unknown words increase", and Bahdanau et al. built attention on that observation.

Sutskever et al.'s result (under 1 min)

The model used 1,000-dimension embeddings. An ensemble of five scored 34.81 BLEU on WMT'14 English to French against 33.30 for the phrase-based baseline, "the first time that a pure neural translation system outperforms a phrase-based SMT baseline on a large scale MT task".

The formula. Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014, published at ICLR 2015) kept every encoder state hj (from a bidirectional RNN, so each state sees the whole sentence) and, at every output step i, let the decoder choose which to read:

eij = v⊤ tanh(W si−1 + U hj),   αij = softmaxj(eij),   ci = Σj αij hj
si−1
the decoder's state before writing word i
eij
"alignment" score: how well source position j matches what the decoder needs now; a small network, so this is called additive attention
ci
a fresh context vector for every output word, fed to the decoder with the previous word

The decoder now reads as many numbers as the source has states, so nothing has to be squeezed. In the paper's Table 1, trained on sentences up to 50 words, the attention model (RNNsearch-50) scored 26.75 BLEU against 17.82 without it (RNNencdec-50), and even the attention model trained up to 30 words (21.50) beat the plain one trained up to 50; RNNsearch-50 "shows no performance deterioration even with sentences of length 50 or more" (Figure 2). The weights α are soft word alignments, readable as a matrix. A year later Luong, Pham and Manning scored with a plain dot product, ht⊤h̄s; the transformer's softmax(QK⊤/√dk)V is that dot score with a scale and separate projections for queries, keys and values.

What the toy reproduces: the fixed vector copies 6 digits almost always (96%) and 12 almost never (4%), so 32 numbers are not enough for a dozen digits; reversing the source alone lifts 10 digits from 24% to 68% (Sutskever et al.'s effect); attention copies every trained length perfectly. What it adds: attention fixed the bottleneck, not length generalisation. Past 20 digits it degrades too (93% whole strings at 22, 1% at 26), and Bahdanau et al. claim no deterioration only for RNNsearch-50, the model trained on the longer sentences. Toy scale: digits, not language, two seeds.

Correction: "seq2seq quality collapses on long inputs" was not universal
Sutskever et al.: "Surprisingly, the LSTM did not suffer on very long sentences", which they credit to feeding the source reversed, so the first source words sit next to the first target words ("many short term dependencies"; test BLEU 25.9 to 30.6, perplexity 5.8 to 4.7, §3.3). The collapse belongs to smaller, unreversed encoder-decoders (Cho et al.'s, Bahdanau et al.'s RNNencdec).

Convolutions for sequences: WaveNet

Recurrence is sequential: step t waits for step t − 1, in training as in use. WaveNet (van den Oord et al., DeepMind 2016) generated raw audio one sample at a time (16,000 samples a second in its speech experiments) with a stack of causal convolutions: each output sees only the past, and every position of a training clip is computed in parallel. A plain causal stack grows its receptive field by one sample per layer (the paper's Figure 2: 4 layers, filter 2, field 5). Dilating the filters (skipping 1, 2, 4, ... inputs) doubles the field every layer instead.

Training is parallel, but generation is still one sample at a time, because each sample feeds the next. WaveNet's lesson for text came quickly: ByteNet and ConvS2S translated with convolutions, whose path between two positions is O(logk n) layers for dilated and O(n/k) for contiguous filters, against self-attention's O(1) (the transformer paper, §4; {{Attention Is All You Need|n:3c65c17b0d0d81999af7f16f8ed8ee9e}}).

Correction: the WaveNet in production was not the 2016 model
The old page said WaveNet "powered Google TTS"; one sample at a time is far too slow to serve. What shipped in the Google Assistant in 2017 was Parallel WaveNet, a feed-forward student distilled from a trained WaveNet ("probability density distillation"), more than 20 times faster than real time, "deployed online by Google Assistant, including serving multiple English and Japanese voices".
WaveNet's other three pieces: μ-law output, the gated activation, the listening test (1 min)

Audio samples were μ-law companded and quantised to 256 values, so the output is a softmax over 256 classes instead of 65,536 (§2.2). Each layer uses the gated activation z = tanh(Wf ∗ x) ⊙ σ(Wg ∗ x), from gated PixelCNN, which "worked significantly better than the rectified linear activation" for audio (§2.3): the LSTM's gating idea without the recurrence, and an ancestor of the GLU family that ended in SwiGLU ({{Activation functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d819f8049c255e6f206e2}}). Conditioned on linguistic features, its text-to-speech scored a mean opinion score of 4.21 in US English and 4.08 in Mandarin, against 3.86 and 3.79 for the best earlier systems and 4.55 and 4.21 for natural 16-bit speech (Table 1). The text-to-speech models had a 240 ms receptive field = 3,840 samples at 16 kHz.

Why the transformer replaced them

The hero figure's last lane. The transformer (2017) kept only attention (self-attention in the encoder and in the decoder, cross-attention between them) and so removed both costs at once: parallelism, since a recurrent layer needs n sequential steps in training and self-attention one, and path length, one hop between any two positions instead of n or logk n. The price is a layer cost of O(n2·d) against the RNN's O(n·d2), cheaper while the sequence is shorter than the width (Table 1 and §4). Its paper page animates one sentence through a recurrent layer (11 sequential steps) against one self-attention layer and runs a small trained transformer ({{Attention Is All You Need, Reading tab and "Run a Transformer"|n:3c65c17b0d0d81999af7f16f8ed8ee9e}}).

Two things the RNN gave for free had to be added back: word order, since attention is blind to it ({{Positional Encodings|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}), and a bound on memory per token, since attention keeps every past state (the KV cache: {{The transformer from zero|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8174980ddde4a20c7b3c}}, section 9). Everything after lives in {{Topic: llm-architecture|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8172b490dc9f9ac281df}}.

Recurrence returns. Constant memory and compute per generated token, the RNN's advantage, came back with recurrences that train in parallel over the sequence: state-space models such as {{Mamba|n:3c65c17b0d0d8179984fc3a1fee4c36a}}, linear attention, RWKV and the hybrids in production are on {{Beyond attention|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8142bc9ecac1fc366966}}.

The modern recurrences named, and how they connect to the LSTM (1 min)
  • Mamba (Gu and Dao, December 2023) makes the recurrence input-dependent ("selective") and trains it with a parallel scan. Its Theorem 1 says the classical gated RNN is a special case of selection; the paper page has a slider for that gate. In production the winner is the hybrid: a few attention layers for exact recall, SSM layers for cheap length (Jamba, Nemotron-H, Falcon-H1).
  • Linear recurrences in RNN form. RWKV (2023) and xLSTM (2024, from Hochreiter's group: exponential gating and a matrix memory) scale recurrent language models to billions of parameters; "Were RNNs all we needed?" (2024) strips the LSTM and GRU gates of their dependence on the previous state so they too train with a parallel scan.

The inputs: one vector per word

Every model above reads words as vectors (the toys use one-hot symbols). Before 2013 a word was a one-hot vector, in which every pair of words is equally far apart. word2vec (Mikolov et al. 2013a) learned a dense vector per word by predicting neighbouring words with a deliberately shallow model, so it could be trained on billions of words; GloVe (Pennington, Socher and Manning 2014) fitted vectors whose dot products match the log co-occurrence counts. The two learn nearly the same thing (details closed below). Both were judged on the analogy test, "a is to b as c is to ?", answered by the word nearest to b − a + c; run here on the real 50-dimension GloVe vectors, all 19,544 questions, all 400,000 words:

What "king − man + woman = queen" leaves out
The nearest word to king − man + woman, on these vectors, is king (cosine 0.885); queen is second (0.852). Every published analogy score forbids the three question words as answers, and without that rule the answer is a question word in nearly half of all questions (Linzen 2016). The same rule manufactures bias examples: man : doctor :: woman : ? returns doctor, and "nurse" appears only once doctor is forbidden (Nissim, van Noord and van der Goot 2020). The bias in the vectors is real and measurable by other means (Bolukbasi et al. 2016); the analogy is a poor way to show it.

Static, then contextual. One vector per word cannot tell the river bank from the money bank, and a word missing from the vocabulary has no vector at all. ELMo (Peters et al. 2018) replaced the lookup with the states of a bidirectional LSTM language model, so each word's vector is "a function of the entire input sentence"; {{BERT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e5ad9bd09cbf18ad7c}} did the same with a transformer, and subword pieces solved the vocabulary problem ({{Tokenizers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817187bcd94a1b0f15ea}}). The embedding matrix at the bottom of every LLM is the direct descendant of word2vec's input vectors. Query the 2,000 real vectors in the {{Word vectors|#t-vec}} tab.

How word2vec and GloVe are trained, and why they agree (2 min)

Skip-gram predicts the words around a word from the word; CBOW (continuous bag of words) predicts a word from the average of its neighbours. The full softmax over a vocabulary of millions is too expensive, so the first word2vec paper used a hierarchical softmax (a Huffman tree, about log2 V outputs per word) and the second (Mikolov et al. 2013b) replaced it with negative sampling: push the true (word, context) pair together and k random words apart.

log σ(v′c · vw) + Σi=1..k Eni ~ Pn log σ(−v′ni · vw)
vw, v′c
input vector of the word, output vector of a context word
k
negatives per pair: 5 to 20 for small corpora, 2 to 5 for large ones
Pn
the unigram distribution raised to the 3/4 power, which beat both the plain unigram and the uniform distribution (§2.2); frequent words are also subsampled (§2.3)

GloVe counts first, then fits: build the global word-by-word co-occurrence matrix X (pairs d words apart counted as 1/d), then fit vectors to the log counts by weighted least squares:

J = Σi,j f(Xij) (wi · w̃j + bi + b̃j − log Xij)2,   f(x) = min(1, (x/xmax)α)
f
down-weights rare pairs and caps frequent ones; the paper uses xmax = 100, α = 3/4, AdaGrad, and the sum W + W̃ as the final vectors

Why they agree. Skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorises the word-context pointwise mutual information matrix shifted by log k (Levy and Goldberg 2014), so "predictive" (word2vec) against "count-based" (GloVe) is a difference of method more than of what is learned. On the analogy test, 300-dimension GloVe trained on 6 billion tokens scored 71.7%, against 69.1% for skip-gram and 65.7% for CBOW trained by the GloVe authors on the same corpus (GloVe paper, Table 2). On these 50-dimension GloVe vectors man : programmer :: woman : ? gives "prodigy", not Bolukbasi's "homemaker", which came from word2vec's Google News vectors and a constrained search.

Negative sampling's descendant is InfoNCE, with the batch as the set of negatives ({{Contrastive and self-supervised learning|n:3c65c17b0d0d811db6a7d02d0ee7215c}}).

Depth

Luong's variants: three scores, global against local (under 1 min)

Luong, Pham and Manning 2015 scored with the decoder's current state ht rather than the previous one, and compared three score functions, dot ht⊤h̄s, general ht⊤Wah̄s and concat (Bahdanau's form), plus "global" attention over all source words against "local" attention over a window. The transformer paper still describes its dot-product attention against "additive attention", Bahdanau's form.

Common misreadings, each answered on this page (1 min)
  • "The LSTM solved vanishing gradients." It gave the gradient one protected path, scaled only by the forget gate; untrained, the toy LSTM's gradient 50 steps back is still about 10−6 of its size at the answer. Training opens the path; it does not start open.
  • "A plain RNN cannot remember for long." It can represent a long memory (the short-trained plain RNN holds the key for 200 steps); it cannot learn that memory from long sequences. Representation and learnability are different questions.
  • "The LSTM had forget, input and output gates in 1997." The forget gate came in 2000.
  • "king − man + woman = queen." Only once king, man and woman are forbidden as answers; without the rule GloVe 50d scores 30.4% on the analogy test instead of 46.2%.
  • "Seq2seq always collapses on long sentences." Small, unreversed encoder-decoders did; Sutskever et al.'s reversed deep LSTM did not.
  • "Attention was invented by the transformer." It was added to RNN translation in 2014 and refined in 2015; the transformer's contribution was to remove everything else.
  • "GRUs are better than LSTMs" (or the reverse). Side-by-side studies did not settle it; the GRU's real advantage is a quarter fewer parameters at the same width.
  • "WaveNet is a recurrent network." It has no recurrence; only its generation is sequential.

Sequence models: the pre-transformer lineage

How sequences were modelled before the transformer, one fix at a time: static word vectors (word2vec, GloVe), recurrent networks and backpropagation through time, the LSTM and GRU gates, seq2seq and the attention that removed its bottleneck, and WaveNet's dilated convolutions. Words as vectors: word2vec and GloVe

Every model below reads words as vectors. Before 2013 the usual input was a one-hot vector per word, in which every pair of words is equally far apart. word2vec learned a dense vector per word from raw text with a deliberately shallow model, so it could be trained on billions of words (Mikolov et al. 2013a):

  • Skip-gram predicts the words around a word from the word; CBOW (continuous bag of words) predicts a word from the average of its neighbours.
  • The full softmax over a vocabulary of millions is too expensive, so the first paper used a hierarchical softmax (a Huffman tree, about log2 V outputs per word) and the second replaced it with negative sampling (Mikolov et al. 2013b): push the true (word, context) pair together and k random words apart.
log σ(v′c · vw) + Σi=1..k Eni ~ Pn log σ(−v′ni · vw)
vw, v′c
input vector of the word, output vector of a context word
k
negatives per pair: 5 to 20 for small corpora, 2 to 5 for large ones
Pn
the unigram distribution raised to the 3/4 power, which beat both the plain unigram and the uniform distribution (§2.2); frequent words are also subsampled (§2.3)

GloVe took the other route: count first, then fit (Pennington, Socher and Manning 2014). Build the global word-by-word co-occurrence matrix X (pairs d words apart counted as 1/d), then fit vectors so that their dot product matches the log count, by weighted least squares:

J = Σi,j f(Xij) (wi · w̃j + bi + b̃j − log Xij)2,   f(x) = min(1, (x/xmax)α)
f
down-weights rare pairs and caps frequent ones; the paper uses xmax = 100, α = 3/4, AdaGrad, and the sum W + W̃ as the final vectors

The two are closer than they look: skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorises the word-context pointwise mutual information matrix shifted by log k (Levy and Goldberg 2014), so "predictive" (word2vec) against "count-based" (GloVe) is a difference of method more than of what is learned.

The analogy test, and what it hides

Both papers were judged on the analogy test: "a is to b as c is to ?", answered by the word nearest to b − a + c. On it, 300-dimension GloVe trained on 6 billion tokens scored 71.7%, against 69.1% for skip-gram and 65.7% for CBOW trained by the GloVe authors on the same corpus (GloVe paper, Table 2). Run on the real 50-dimension GloVe vectors (the ones in the Word vectors tab), all 19,544 questions, all 400,000 words:

What "king − man + woman = queen" leaves out
The nearest word to king − man + woman, on these vectors, is king (cosine 0.885); queen is second (0.852). Every published analogy score forbids the three question words as answers, and without that rule the answer is a question word in nearly half of all questions, so the famous result is partly the rule (Linzen 2016). The same rule manufactures bias examples: man : doctor :: woman : ? returns doctor, and "nurse" appears only once doctor is forbidden (Nissim, van Noord and van der Goot 2020). The gender bias in the vectors is real and measurable by other means (Bolukbasi et al. 2016); the analogy is a poor way to show it. On these GloVe vectors man : programmer :: woman : ? gives "prodigy", not Bolukbasi's "homemaker", which came from word2vec's Google News vectors and a constrained search.

Static, then contextual. One vector per word cannot tell the river bank from the money bank, and a word missing from the vocabulary has no vector at all. ELMo (Peters et al. 2018) replaced the lookup with the internal states of a bidirectional LSTM language model, so each word's vector is "a function of the entire input sentence", and BERT did the same with a transformer. The vocabulary problem was solved separately, by splitting words into subword pieces (Tokenizers). The embedding matrix at the bottom of every LLM is the direct descendant of word2vec's input vectors.

Recurrent networks and backpropagation through time

A recurrent network reads a sequence one element at a time and carries a hidden state forward (Elman 1990):

at = f(U xt + W at−1 + b),   yt = g(V at)
at
the hidden state, the network's whole memory of the past
U, W, V
input, recurrent and output weights, the same at every step: the network has a fixed number of parameters whatever the sequence length
f, g
usually tanh for the state; softmax for a distribution over outputs

It is trained by backpropagation through time (BPTT; Werbos 1990): unroll the network into one layer per time step, all sharing weights, run ordinary backpropagation, and add up the gradients each copy contributes to the shared weights. Long sequences are usually cut into windows (truncated BPTT), which also caps how far back any gradient can reach.

Why long dependencies were hard. The gradient from step t back to step k contains the product of Jacobians ∏ ∂aj/∂aj−1 = ∏ diag(f′)W, one factor per step. Pascanu, Mikolov and Bengio made the condition exact: if the largest eigenvalue of W (in absolute value) is below 1/γ, where γ bounds |f′| (1 for tanh, 1/4 for the sigmoid), the long-term terms vanish exponentially; above it is necessary, not sufficient, for them to explode (2013, §2.1). Exploding gradients have a cheap fix, clipping the gradient's norm, which the same paper proposed; vanishing ones need a change of architecture. The difficulty itself was identified in Hochreiter's 1991 diploma thesis and by Bengio, Simard and Frasconi 1994.

Already built on the parent page: the per-step factor on one 60-step sequence, a tanh RNN against an LSTM cell path, with the recurrent weight and the forget-gate bias as sliders (Topic: ml-fundamentals, Reading tab, thread 3). Below, the same contrast on real trained networks.

The LSTM, gate by gate

The long short-term memory network gives each unit a memory cell C whose update is an addition, guarded by gates (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997). The input xt and the previous hidden state ht−1 are concatenated and fed to four parallel layers; three of them are sigmoid gates, outputs between 0 and 1 that multiply a stream of information element by element.

PartFormulaRole
Forget gateft = σ(Wf[ht−1, xt] + bf)how much of the old cell Ct−1 to keep
Input gateit = σ(Wi[ht−1, xt] + bi)which units to write
CandidateC̃t = tanh(WC[ht−1, xt] + bC)what would be written
Output gateot = σ(Wo[ht−1, xt] + bo)what part of the cell to expose
UpdatesCt = ft ⊙ Ct−1 + it ⊙ C̃t;   ht = ot ⊙ tanh(Ct)the cell is added to, not rewritten

Why it works. Along the cell, ∂Ct/∂Ct−1 = diag(ft) plus terms through the gates' dependence on h: no weight matrix and no squashing slope on the direct path, only the forget gate, which the network can hold near 1. It is a skip connection through time, the same idea residual networks later used through depth. It does not abolish vanishing gradients: the path through h still multiplies Jacobians, and a forget gate of 0.9 still shrinks the cell path by 0.950 ≈ 0.005 over 50 steps. Setting the forget-gate bias to 1 or more at initialisation, so that the cell starts by remembering, is the standard recommendation (Jozefowicz, Zaremba and Sutskever 2015) and is used in the model below.

Correction: the 1997 LSTM had no forget gate
The original LSTM had input and output gates around a "constant error carrousel", a cell whose self-connection was fixed at 1.0 (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997, §3.2 and §4). The forget gate, which lets the cell decay or reset, was added by Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins 2000, and "peephole" connections from the cell to the gates by Gers and Schmidhuber the same year. The LSTM in every library today is the 2000 version, usually without peepholes (Greff et al. 2015, §III, has the history).

The GRU: two gates, one state

The gated recurrent unit merges the cell and the hidden state and uses two gates (Cho et al. 2014, who called it a new hidden unit; the name is from Chung et al. 2014):

ht = (1 − zt) ⊙ ht−1 + zt ⊙ h̃t,   h̃t = tanh(W xt + U(rt ⊙ ht−1))
zt
update gate: one gate doing the forget and input jobs together (keep 1 − z, write z)
rt
reset gate: how much of the old state the candidate may look at

The same interpolating, additive trick, with three weight blocks instead of four, so at equal width a GRU has exactly 3/4 of an LSTM's parameters: 4n(n + m + 1) against 3n(n + m + 1) for n units and m inputs derived (PyTorch keeps two bias vectors, so +2 instead of +1: the toy's recurrent layers, n = 8, m = 9, have 456 against 608). Quality is usually comparable: Chung et al. "could not make concrete conclusion on which of the two gating units was better", and Greff et al.'s search over eight LSTM variants found none significantly better than the standard LSTM, with the forget gate and the output activation its most critical parts.

Seq2seq: one vector between two networks

Translation maps a sequence to a sequence of a different length. The encoder-decoder answer of 2014: an encoder RNN reads the source and its final state becomes a fixed-size vector c; a decoder RNN, started from c, writes the target one word at a time, feeding back the word it just wrote (Cho et al. 2014, who also gave c to the decoder at every step; Sutskever, Vinyals and Le 2014). Sutskever et al.'s model was 4 layers of 1,000 LSTM cells with 1,000-dimension embeddings, 384M parameters, so the sentence had to fit in 8,000 numbers (cell and hidden state of every layer). An ensemble of five scored 34.81 BLEU on WMT'14 English to French against 33.30 for the phrase-based baseline: which the paper calls "the first time that a pure neural translation system outperforms a phrase-based SMT baseline on a large scale MT task".

The bottleneck. Everything about the source passes through one vector of fixed size, however long the sentence. Cho et al. found their encoder-decoder's performance "degrades rapidly as the length of the sentence and the number of unknown words increase", and Bahdanau et al. built attention on that observation.

Correction: "quality collapses on long inputs" was not universal
Sutskever et al. reported the opposite for their model: "Surprisingly, the LSTM did not suffer on very long sentences", which they credit to feeding the source sentence reversed. Reversal puts the first source words next to the first target words, introducing "many short term dependencies"; it took test BLEU from 25.9 to 30.6 and perplexity from 5.8 to 4.7 (§3.3). The collapse belongs to smaller, unreversed encoder-decoders (Cho et al.'s, Bahdanau et al.'s RNNencdec). The toy below shows both effects.

Attention: let the decoder look back

Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014, published at ICLR 2015) kept every encoder state hj (from a bidirectional RNN, so each state sees the whole sentence) and, at every output step i, let the decoder choose which to read:

eij = v⊤ tanh(W si−1 + U hj),   αij = softmaxj(eij),   ci = Σj αij hj
si−1
the decoder's state before writing word i
eij
"alignment" score: how well source position j matches what the decoder needs now; a small network, so this is called additive attention
ci
a fresh context vector for every output word, fed to the decoder with the previous word

The decoder now reads as many numbers as the source has states, so nothing has to be squeezed. In the paper's Table 1, trained on sentences up to 50 words, the attention model (RNNsearch-50) scored 26.75 BLEU against 17.82 for the same encoder-decoder without it (RNNencdec-50), and the attention model trained on sentences up to 30 words (21.50) beat the plain one trained up to 50. Their Figure 2 shows the plain model's BLEU falling with sentence length while RNNsearch-50 "shows no performance deterioration even with sentences of length 50 or more". The learned weights α are soft word alignments, readable as a matrix.

Luong, Pham and Manning 2015 simplified and generalised it: score with the decoder's current state ht rather than the previous one, and compare three score functions, dot ht⊤h̄s, general ht⊤Wah̄s and concat (Bahdanau's form), plus "global" attention over all source words against "local" attention over a window. The transformer's scaled dot product, softmax(QK⊤/√dk)V, is Luong's dot score with a scale and separate projections for queries, keys and values.

WaveNet: long context with no recurrence

Recurrence is sequential: step t waits for step t − 1, in training as in use. WaveNet (van den Oord et al., DeepMind 2016) generated raw audio one sample at a time (16,000 samples a second in its speech experiments) with a stack of causal convolutions: each output sees only the past, and every position of a training clip is computed in parallel. A plain causal stack grows its receptive field by one sample per layer (the paper's Figure 2: 4 layers, filter 2, field 5). Dilating the filters (skipping 1, 2, 4, ... inputs) doubles the field every layer instead.

Try: trace one output back through 4 dilated layers (the paper's Figure 3) and through a plain stack of the same depth, drawn to the same scale. Then raise the layers to 6.

Filter width 2, as in the paper. Receptive field = 1 + (filter width − 1) × sum of dilations derived.

The paper repeats blocks of dilations 1, 2, 4, ..., 512: "each 1, 2, 4, ..., 512 block has receptive field of size 1024" (§2.1). Stack them:

Three more pieces mattered. Audio samples were μ-law companded and quantised to 256 values, so the output is a softmax over 256 classes instead of 65,536 (§2.2). Each layer uses the gated activation z = tanh(Wf ∗ x) ⊙ σ(Wg ∗ x), from gated PixelCNN, which "worked significantly better than the rectified linear activation" for audio (§2.3): the LSTM's gating idea without the recurrence, and an ancestor of the GLU family that ended in SwiGLU (Activation functions). And conditioned on linguistic features, its text-to-speech scored a mean opinion score of 4.21 in US English and 4.08 in Mandarin, against 3.86 and 3.79 for the best earlier systems and 4.55 and 4.21 for natural 16-bit speech (Table 1). The text-to-speech models had a 240 ms receptive field = 3,840 samples at 16 kHz.

Correction: the WaveNet in production was not the 2016 model
The old page said WaveNet "powered Google TTS". The 2016 model generates one sample at a time, far too slowly to serve. What shipped in the Google Assistant in 2017 was Parallel WaveNet: a feed-forward student distilled from a trained WaveNet ("probability density distillation"), more than 20 times faster than real time, "deployed online by Google Assistant, including serving multiple English and Japanese voices".

WaveNet's lesson for text came quickly: ByteNet and ConvS2S translated with convolutions (linked from the Attention Is All You Need page, whose §4 compares their path lengths, O(logk n) for dilated and O(n/k) for contiguous filters, with self-attention's O(1)).

Where the lineage ends

The transformer (2017) removed the recurrence and kept only attention: self-attention within the encoder and within the decoder, cross-attention from decoder to encoder, so every position is computed at once in training and any two positions are one step apart. Its paper page animates exactly this contrast, one sentence through a recurrent layer (11 sequential steps) against one self-attention layer (one step), and runs a small trained transformer (Attention Is All You Need, Reading tab and "Run a Transformer"). Two things the RNN gave for free had to be added back: word order, since attention is blind to it (Positional Encodings), and a bound on memory per token, since attention keeps every past state (the KV cache). Everything after lives in Topic: llms and Topic: llm-training-and-post-training.

Postscript: recurrence returns. Constant memory and compute per generated token, the RNN's advantage, came back with training that runs in parallel over the sequence:

  • State-space models. Mamba (Gu and Dao, December 2023) makes the recurrence input-dependent ("selective") and trains it with a parallel scan. Its Theorem 1 says the classical gated RNN is a special case of selection; the paper page has a slider for that gate. In production the winner is the hybrid: a few attention layers for exact recall, SSM layers for cheap length (Jamba, Nemotron-H, Falcon-H1, on the Mamba page).
  • Linear recurrences in RNN form. RWKV (2023) and xLSTM (2024, from Hochreiter's group: exponential gating and a matrix memory) scale recurrent language models to billions of parameters; "Were RNNs all we needed?" (2024) strips the LSTM and GRU gates of their dependence on the previous state so they too train with a parallel scan.

Common mistakes

"The LSTM solved vanishing gradients."
It gave the gradient one protected path, the cell, scaled only by the forget gate; everything through the hidden state still multiplies Jacobians, and a forget gate below 1 still decays geometrically (0.950 ≈ 0.005). Untrained, the toy LSTM's gradient 50 steps back is about 10−6 of its size at the answer (Measured, second view): far better than the plain RNN, nowhere near 1. Training opens the path; it does not start open.
"A plain RNN cannot remember for long."
It can represent a long memory: the toy plain RNN trained on short sequences holds the key for 200 steps. What it cannot do is learn that memory from long sequences, because the gradient that would teach it vanishes first. Representation and learnability are different questions.
"The LSTM was introduced with forget, input and output gates in 1997."
The forget gate came in 2000 (Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins); the 1997 cell had a fixed self-connection of 1.0. See the correction under The LSTM, gate by gate.
"king − man + woman = queen."
Only once king, man and woman are forbidden as answers; otherwise the nearest word is king. Without the rule GloVe 50d scores 30.4% on the analogy test instead of 46.2% (Word vectors).
"Seq2seq always collapses on long sentences."
Small, unreversed encoder-decoders did; Sutskever et al.'s deep LSTM with reversed sources "did not suffer on very long sentences". Attention removed the need for such tricks, and with it the fixed-size bottleneck.
"Attention was invented by the transformer."
It was added to RNN translation in 2014 (Bahdanau et al.) and refined in 2015 (Luong et al.); the transformer's contribution was to remove everything else. Its paper still describes its dot-product attention against "additive attention", Bahdanau's form.
"GRUs are better than LSTMs" (or the reverse).
Side-by-side studies did not settle it: Chung et al. 2014 could not conclude, Jozefowicz et al. 2015 found that "adding a bias of 1 to the LSTM's forget gate closes the gap between the LSTM and the GRU", and Greff et al. 2015 found no variant significantly better than the standard LSTM. The GRU's real advantage is a quarter fewer parameters at the same width.
"WaveNet is a recurrent network."
It has no recurrence: a stack of dilated causal convolutions, parallel in training. Generation is still one sample at a time because each sample feeds the next, which is why the deployed system was a distilled parallel student.

          Best resources

          • Understanding LSTM Networks (Christopher Olah): still the best LSTM explanation written, with the diagrams everyone copies. (25 min)
          • An intuitive explanation of LSTM (Calzone, Medium): a short gate-by-gate walkthrough. (15 min)
          • RNN architecture explained (Poudel, Medium): the plain RNN cell by cell. (15 min)
          • The Illustrated Word2vec (Jay Alammar): embeddings intuition and skip-gram with negative sampling, drawn. (30 min)
          • The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks (Andrej Karpathy): what character-level RNNs could do, and why people were excited. (35 min)
          • Visualizing a Neural Machine Translation Model (Jay Alammar): seq2seq and attention, animated. (20 min)
          • Attention and Augmented Recurrent Neural Networks (Olah and Carter, Distill): attention as RNNs knew it just before the transformer, with neural Turing machines and adaptive computation. (30 min)
          • WaveNet: a generative model for raw audio (DeepMind blog): the dilated stack animated, with audio samples. (10 min)

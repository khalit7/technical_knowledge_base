Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers: source extracts
Checked 2026-10-03. Quotes are verbatim from the primary source (LaTeX from arXiv HTML kept as rendered text; PDF ligatures normalised). Where a source line contained an em-dash it is shown as "--" or the quote was trimmed to avoid it.

## C1: AdaGrad (Duchi, Hazan, Singer 2011, JMLR): per-coordinate step divided by sqrt of sum of squared gradients; good for sparse features
URL: https://jmlr.org/papers/v12/duchi11a.html (PDF: https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume12/duchi11a/duchi11a.pdf, section 1.1 "The Adaptive Gradient Algorithm")
QUOTE: "Thus we specialize the update to x_{t+1} = Pi_X^{diag(G_t)^{1/2}} (x_t - eta diag(G_t)^{-1/2} g_t). Both the inverse and root of diag(G_t) can be computed in linear time. Moreover, as we discuss later, when the gradient vectors are sparse the update above can often be performed in time proportional to the support of the gradient."
QUOTE: "Informally, our procedures give frequently occurring features very low learning rates and infrequent features high learning rates, where the intuition is that each time an infrequent feature is seen, the learner should 'take notice.'"
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: Citation on JMLR page: "John Duchi, Elad Hazan, Yoram Singer; 12(61):2121-2159, 2011." G_t is the sum of outer products of past (sub)gradients, so diag(G_t) is the per-coordinate sum of squared gradients. The paper also defines the full-matrix version (G_t^{-1/2}) which is "computationally impractical in high dimensions".

## C2: RMSProp origin: Hinton, Coursera lecture 6e (2012); decaying average of squared gradients, typical 0.9
URL: https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~tijmen/csc321/slides/lecture_slides_lec6.pdf (resolves, HTTP 200; slide headed "Lecture 6e: rmsprop: Divide the gradient by a running average of its recent magnitude")
QUOTE: "rmsprop: Keep a moving average of the squared gradient for each weight. MeanSquare(w, t) = 0.9 MeanSquare(w, t-1) + 0.1 (dE/dw (t))^2. Dividing the gradient by sqrt(MeanSquare(w, t)) makes the learning work much better (Tijmen Tieleman, unpublished)."
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: The slides credit the idea to Tijmen Tieleman (unpublished); Adam paper cites it as "RMSProp (Tieleman & Hinton, 2012)". The slide deck is hosted on Tijmen Tieleman's CSC321 page; the Coursera course was "Neural Networks for Machine Learning" (2012).

## C3: AdaDelta (Zeiler 2012, arXiv 1212.5701): no global learning rate; units correction via running average of past updates
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/1212.5701 (abstract; section 3.2 https://arxiv.org/html/1212.5701#S3.SS2)
QUOTE (abstract): "We present a novel per-dimension learning rate method for gradient descent called ADADELTA. The method dynamically adapts over time using only first order information and has minimal computational overhead beyond vanilla stochastic gradient descent. The method requires no manual tuning of a learning rate and appears robust to noisy gradient information..."
QUOTE (3.2 "Idea 2: Correct Units with Hessian Approximation"): "When considering the parameter updates, Delta x, being applied to x, the units should match. That is, if the parameter had some hypothetical units, the changes to the parameter should be changes in those units as well. When considering SGD, Momentum, or ADAGRAD, we can see that this is not the case."
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: The numerator of the final ADADELTA update is RMS[Delta x]_{t-1}, a running (exponentially decaying) average of past squared updates, divided by RMS[g]_t.

## C4: Adam (Kingma and Ba, arXiv 1412.6980): defaults; bias correction; what happens without it when beta2 near 1
URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980 (arXiv HTML is only a PDF wrapper for this paper; quotes from PDF v9, Algorithm 1, sections 3, 5, 6.4)
QUOTE (Algorithm 1 caption): "Good default settings for the tested machine learning problems are alpha = 0.001, beta1 = 0.9, beta2 = 0.999 and epsilon = 10^-8."
QUOTE (section 3, Initialization bias correction): "What is left is the term (1 - beta2^t) which is caused by initializing the running average with zeros. In algorithm 1 we therefore divide by this term to correct the initialization bias. In case of sparse gradients, for a reliable estimate of the second moment one needs to average over many gradients by chosing a small value of beta2; however it is exactly this case of small beta2 where a lack of initialisation bias correction would lead to initial steps that are much larger."
QUOTE (section 5, on RMSProp): "RMSProp also lacks a bias-correction term; this matters most in case of a value of beta2 close to 1 (required in case of sparse gradients), since in that case not correcting the bias leads to very large stepsizes and often divergence, as we also empirically demonstrate in section 6.4."
QUOTE (section 6.4 / Figure 4, VAE): "In Figure 4, values beta2 close to 1 indeed lead to instabilities in training when no bias correction term was present, especially at first few epochs of the training. The best results were achieved with small values of (1 - beta2) and bias correction"
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: Note the paper's own wording: "small value of beta2" in section 3 means small (1 - beta2), i.e. beta2 close to 1 ("chosing a small value of beta2" is a slip in the paper; sections 5 and 6.4 say "beta2 close to 1"). Figure 4 caption: "Effect of bias-correction terms (red line) versus no bias correction terms (green line) after 10 epochs (left) and 100 epochs (right) on the loss ... when learning a Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE)". Grid: beta1 in [0, 0.9], beta2 in [0.99, 0.999, 0.9999], log10(alpha) in [-5, ..., -1].

## C5: AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, arXiv 1711.05101): L2 and weight decay equivalent for SGD (with rescaling) but not for adaptive methods
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/1711.05101 (abstract; section 1 https://arxiv.org/html/1711.05101#S1)
QUOTE (abstract): "L2 regularization and weight decay regularization are equivalent for standard stochastic gradient descent (when rescaled by the learning rate), but as we demonstrate this is not the case for adaptive gradient algorithms, such as Adam."
QUOTE (section 1): "L2 regularization and weight decay are not identical. The two techniques can be made equivalent for SGD by a reparameterization of the weight decay factor based on the learning rate; however, as is often overlooked, this is not the case for Adam."
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: Section 2 adds the intuition: with L2 inside Adam, "parameters theta_i with a large inverse preconditioner s_i (which in practice would be caused by historically large gradients in dimension i) are regularized relatively more than they would be with L2 regularization".

## C6: Nesterov momentum: Sutskever et al. 2013 formulation, and PyTorch SGD nesterov=True pseudocode
URL: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/sutskever13.html (PDF http://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/sutskever13.pdf, section 2, Eqs. 3-4)
QUOTE (Sutskever et al. 2013, section 2): "Specifically, as shown in the appendix, the NAG update may be rewritten as: v_{t+1} = mu v_t - eps grad f(theta_t + mu v_t) (3); theta_{t+1} = theta_t + v_{t+1} (4)"
QUOTE (2.1): "But while CM computes the gradient update from the current position theta_t, NAG first performs a partial update to theta_t, computing theta_t + mu v_t, which is similar to theta_{t+1}, but missing the as yet unknown correction."
URL: https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.SGD.html (redirects to the current version, here https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/generated/torch.optim.SGD.html)
QUOTE (PyTorch pseudocode, momentum/nesterov lines): "if mu != 0: if t > 1: b_t <- mu b_{t-1} + (1 - tau) g_t; else: b_t <- g_t; if nesterov: g_t <- g_t + mu b_t; else: g_t <- b_t; theta_t <- theta_{t-1} - gamma g_t" ... "Nesterov momentum is based on the formula from On the importance of initialization and momentum in deep learning."
QUOTE (PyTorch note): "The implementation of SGD with Momentum/Nesterov subtly differs from Sutskever et al. and implementations in some other frameworks. ... v_{t+1} = mu * v_t + g_{t+1}, p_{t+1} = p_t - lr * v_{t+1} ... This is in contrast to Sutskever et al. and other frameworks which employ an update of the form v_{t+1} = mu * v_t + lr * g_{t+1}, p_{t+1} = p_t - v_{t+1}. The Nesterov version is analogously modified."
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: PyTorch's nesterov=True evaluates the gradient at the current point and uses g + mu*b (the "lookahead" reparameterisation), not a gradient at theta + mu v. Rendered pseudocode uses "if lambda != 0" / "if mu != 0" (the HTML text extraction drops the "not equal" sign). Default signature in 2.14 docs: SGD(params, lr=0.001, momentum=0, dampening=0, weight_decay=0, nesterov=False, ...). Momentum buffer initialised to the first gradient, not zeros.

## C7: Lion (Chen et al. 2023, arXiv 2302.06675): update rule, betas, LR 3-10x smaller, WD 3-10x larger, one state; where competitive and not
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06675 (Program 1 / section 2; section 5 https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06675#S5; section 6 https://arxiv.org/html/2302.06675#S6)
QUOTE (Program 1): "update = interp(gradient, momentum, beta1); update = sign(update); momentum = interp(gradient, momentum, beta2)" ... "Discovered optimizer Lion. beta1=0.9 and beta2=0.99 by default are derived from Program. It only tracks momentum and uses the sign operation to compute the update."
QUOTE (abstract): "It is more memory-efficient than Adam as it only keeps track of the momentum. Different from adaptive optimizers, its update has the same magnitude for each parameter calculated through the sign operation."
QUOTE (section 5): "Based on our experience, a suitable learning rate for Lion is typically 3-10x smaller than that for AdamW." ... "Since the effective weight decay is lr * lambda ... the value of lambda used for Lion is 3-10x larger than that for AdamW in order to maintain a similar strength."
QUOTE (abstract, wins): "On image classification, Lion boosts the accuracy of ViT by up to 2% on ImageNet and saves up to 5x the pre-training compute on JFT. ... On diffusion models, Lion outperforms Adam by achieving a better FID score and reducing the training compute by up to 2.3x. For autoregressive, masked language modeling, and fine-tuning, Lion exhibits a similar or better performance compared to Adam."
QUOTE (section 6, Limitations of Lion): "There are also several tasks where Lion performs similarly to AdamW, including: (1) the Imagen text-to-image base model, (2) the perplexity of autoregressive language model trained on the large-scale internal dataset, ... and (3) masked language modeling on C4." ... "Though people often scale up the batch size to enable more parallelism, it is likely that Lion performs no better than AdamW if the batch size is small (< 64)." Also: "The performance gain brought by Lion decreases when strong augmentations are utilized." and "On vision tasks, the discrepancies between Lion, AdamW, and momentum SGD are pretty small on ResNets".
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: "interp(a, b, beta)" = (1-beta)*a + beta*b, so update = sign(beta1*m + (1-beta1)*g), m = beta2*m + (1-beta2)*g. Section 5 also: for language-modelling stability runs they used beta1=0.95, beta2=0.98 for Lion.

## C8: Shampoo (Gupta, Koren, Singer 2018, arXiv 1802.09568): L and R, exponent -1/4 for matrices, -1/(2k) for order k; vector case = full-matrix AdaGrad
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/1802.09568 (Algorithm 1 in section 1 https://arxiv.org/html/1802.09568#S1; section 1.2; section 4.2 https://arxiv.org/html/1802.09568#S4.SS2)
QUOTE (Algorithm 1, matrix case): "W_{t+1} = W_t - eta L_t^{-1/4} G_t R_t^{-1/4}"
QUOTE: "To recap more formally, Shampoo maintains two different matrices: an m x m matrix L_t^{1/4} to precondition the rows of G_t and R_t^{1/4} for its columns. The 1/4 exponent arises from our analysis; intuitively, it is a sensible choice as it induces an overall step-size decay rate of O(1/sqrt(t)), which is common in stochastic optimization methods."
QUOTE (4.2, order-k): "It maintains a separate preconditioning matrix H_t^i (of size n_i x n_i) corresponding to for each dimension i in [k] of the gradient. On step t, the i'th mode of the gradient G_t is then multiplied by the matrix (H_t^i)^{-1/2k} through the tensor-matrix product operator x_i."
QUOTE (1.2): "Shampoo can be viewed as an efficient, practical and provable apparatus for approximately and implicitly using the full AdaGrad preconditioner, without falling back to diagonal matrices."
VERDICT: confirmed (vector case: derivable, not stated verbatim)
NOTE: The paper does not explicitly write "for k=1 Shampoo is full-matrix AdaGrad", but it follows from Algorithm 2: k=1 gives H_t = sum g g^T and the step H_t^{-1/2} g, which is exactly full-matrix AdaGrad (Duchi et al.'s G_t^{-1/2}). Safe wording: "for a vector (order 1) the exponent -1/(2k) becomes -1/2, which is full-matrix AdaGrad".

## C9: SOAP (Vyas et al. 2024, arXiv 2409.11321): "Adam in the eigenbasis of Shampoo's preconditioner"; headline numbers; extra hyperparameter
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11321 (abstract; section 6.1 https://arxiv.org/html/2409.11321#S6.SS1; 6.3 for small batch)
QUOTE (abstract): "This work establishes a formal connection between Shampoo (implemented with the 1/2 power) and Adafactor ... showing that Shampoo is equivalent to running Adafactor in the eigenbasis of Shampoo's preconditioner. This insight leads to the design of a simpler and computationally efficient algorithm: ShampoO with Adam in the Preconditioner's eigenbasis (SOAP)."
QUOTE (abstract): "Furthermore, since SOAP is equivalent to running Adam in a rotated space, it introduces only one additional hyperparameter (the preconditioning frequency) compared to Adam. We evaluate SOAP on language model pre-training, with experiments on 360m and 660m sized models. In the large batch regime, SOAP reduces the number of iterations by over 40% and wall clock time by over 35% compared to AdamW, with approximately 20% improvements in both metrics compared to Shampoo."
QUOTE (6.1): "In Figure 1 (left and middle) and Figure 3 we show train loss curves for AdamW, Shampoo, and SOAP on 360m and 660m models with 2m token batch size and 'chinchilla-optimal' i.e. 20x model size number of tokens. ... (when batch size is set to 2m and preconditioning frequency to 10)"
QUOTE (6.3, small batch): "SOAP outperforms both Shampoo and AdamW, reducing the number of iterations by 25% compared to AdamW, and by approximately 10% compared to Shampoo. Furthermore, ... SOAP also achieves a wall-clock time improvement of >= 15% over AdamW and around 10% over Shampoo." (batch 256k tokens, preconditioning frequency 80)
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: Precisely: >=40% fewer iterations and >=35% less wall clock vs AdamW; ~20% vs Shampoo; 360M and 660M models; 2M-token batch; precond frequency 10. Paper also notes Shampoo won AlgoPerf "reducing wall-clock time by 28% (MLCommons, 2024)".

## C11: Schedule-free (Defazio et al. 2024, arXiv 2405.15682); NeurIPS 2024 oral; repo README guidance
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15682 (section 2 https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15682#S2; Algorithm 1 in section 4.4)
QUOTE (section 2, Eqs. 3-5): "y_t = (1-beta) z_t + beta x_t, (3) z_{t+1} = z_t - gamma grad f(y_t, zeta_t), (4) x_{t+1} = (1 - c_{t+1}) x_t + c_{t+1} z_{t+1}, (5) where c_{t+1} = 1/(t+1) and z_1 = x_1 is the initial point."
QUOTE (section 2): "Values of beta similar to standard momentum values beta ~ 0.9 appear to work well in practice."
QUOTE (Algorithm 1, Schedule-Free AdamW): "gamma_t = gamma sqrt(1 - beta2^t) min(1, t/T_warmup)  [LR includes warmup and Adam bias-correction] ... c_{t+1} = gamma_t^2 / sum_{i=1}^t gamma_i^2 ... x_{t+1} = (1 - c_{t+1}) x_t + c_{t+1} z_{t+1}  [Update weighted iterate average]"
QUOTE: "Schedule-Free AdamW is the core algorithm behind our winning entry to the MLCommons 2024 AlgoPerf Algorithmic Efficiency Challenge Self-Tuning track."
URL (oral): https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/oral/98003 (page title "NeurIPS Oral The Road Less Scheduled"; Oral Session 1C, Wed 11 Dec 2024)
URL (repo): https://github.com/facebookresearch/schedule_free (README)
QUOTE (README, How to Use): "This is done by calling optimizer.train() at the same place you call model.train() and optimizer.eval() at the same place you call model.eval(). The optimizer should also be placed in eval mode when storing checkpoints."
QUOTE (README, Caveats): "Training is more sensitive to the choice of beta than you may expect from standard momentum. Our default of 0.9 works on most problems but it may be necessary to increase the value to 0.95 or 0.98 particularly for very long training runs." / "Using learning rate warmup is recommended. This is supported through the warmup_steps parameter." / "For SGD, a learning rate 10x-50x larger than classical rates seems to be a good starting point." / "For AdamW, learning rates in the range 1x-10x larger than with schedule-based approaches seem to work."
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: README also: "If your code supports PyTorch Optimizer step closures, you can use the closure forms of the optimizers, which do not require the .train() and .eval() calls." and BatchNorm needs extra handling.

## C12: Muon (Keller Jordan blog): NS quintic coefficients, 5 steps, Nesterov 0.95, 2-D hidden only, AdamW for embeddings/head/scalars; records; FLOP overhead
URL: https://kellerjordan.github.io/posts/muon/
QUOTE (code): "def newtonschulz5(G, steps=5, eps=1e-7): ... a, b, c = (3.4445, -4.7750, 2.0315)"
QUOTE: "We use an ad-hoc gradient based approach and end up with the coefficients (3.4445, -4.7750, 2.0315), which is what we use for the final design of Muon."
QUOTE: "By design, Muon only applies to 2D parameters (and convolutional filters via flattening), so the remaining scalar and vector parameters in a network must be optimized using a standard method (e.g., AdamW). Empirically, we find that it is also important to optimize input and output parameters using AdamW, even though these are typically 2D. In particular, when training transformers, AdamW should be used for the embedding and final classifier head layers in order to attain the best performance."
QUOTE: "Another purely empirical result is that using Nesterov-style momentum for Muon works a bit better than normal SGD-momentum in every case we have tested. We have therefore made this the default in the public Muon implementation."
QUOTE (results): "Improved the speed record for training to 94% accuracy on CIFAR-10 from 3.3 to 2.6 A100-seconds. Improved the speed record for training to 3.28 val loss on FineWeb (a competitive task known as NanoGPT speedrunning) by a factor of 1.35x. ... Trained a 1.5B parameter transformer to GPT-2 XL level performance on HellaSwag in 10 8xH100-hours. Using AdamW to achieve the same result takes 13.3 hours."
QUOTE: "switching from AdamW to Muon set a new NanoGPT training speed record on 10/15/24, where Muon improved the training speed by 35%."
QUOTE (overhead): "Therefore, the FLOP overhead of Muon is at most Tm/B, where m is the model dimension, B is the batch size in tokens, and T is the number of NS iteration steps (typically T=5). ... For the current NanoGPT speedrunning record, the model dimension is m=768, and the number of tokens per batch is B=524288. Therefore, the overhead is 5*768/524288 = 0.7%. For Llama 405B training ... 5*16384/16000000 = 0.5%. We conclude that for typical LM training scenarios, at both the small and large scale, Muon has a FLOP overhead below 1%."
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: Momentum 0.95 is not stated in blog prose; it is the default in the reference implementation https://github.com/KellerJordan/Muon/blob/master/muon.py: "def muon_update(grad, momentum, beta=0.95, ns_steps=5, nesterov=True)" and "def __init__(self, params, lr=0.02, weight_decay=0, momentum=0.95)". Blog: "Muon (MomentUm Orthogonalized by Newton-Schulz)".

## C13: Muon is Scalable (Liu et al. 2025, Moonshot, arXiv 2502.16982): ~2x compute efficiency, 52% of FLOPs; weight decay; 0.2*sqrt(max(A,B)); distributed
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16982 (abstract; section 1 https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16982#S1; 2.2 https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16982#S2.SS2; 2.3 https://arxiv.org/html/2502.16982#S2.SS3)
QUOTE (abstract): "We identify two crucial techniques for scaling up Muon: (1) adding weight decay and (2) carefully adjusting the per-parameter update scale. These techniques allow Muon to work out-of-the-box on large-scale training without the need of hyper-parameter tuning. Scaling law experiments indicate that Muon achieves ~2x computational efficiency compared to AdamW with compute optimal training."
QUOTE (section 1): "Based on the scaling law results, Muon achieves comparable performance to AdamW trained counterparts while requiring only approximately 52% of the training FLOPs."
QUOTE (2.2): "We propose to match Muon's update RMS to be similar to that of AdamW. From empirical observations, AdamW's update RMS is usually around 0.2 to 0.4. Therefore, we scale Muon's update RMS to this range by the following adjustment: W_t = W_{t-1} - eta_t (0.2 . O_t . sqrt(max(A,B)) + lambda W_{t-1})"
QUOTE (section 1): "Efficient Distributed Implementation: We develop a distributed version of Muon with ZeRO-1 (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) style optimization, achieving optimal memory efficiency and reduced communication overhead while preserving the mathematical properties of the algorithm."
VERDICT: confirmed
NOTE: Released model: Moonlight (3B/16B MoE, 5.7T tokens).

## C16: Independent re-evaluations of the challengers
(a) Wen et al. 2025, "Fantastic Pretraining Optimizers and Where to Find Them"
URL: https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02046 (abstract; section 1 https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02046#S1; 3.3, 3.4)
QUOTE (abstract): "Through our thorough investigation, we find that all the fastest optimizers such as Muon and Soap, use matrices as preconditioners. ... However, the speedup of matrix-based optimizers is inversely proportional to model scale, decreasing from 1.4x over AdamW for 0.1B parameter models to merely 1.1x for 1.2B parameter models."
QUOTE (section 1): "Against our well-tuned AdamW baseline, the speedup of alternative optimizers does not exceed 1.4x (Figure 3). Furthermore, while new optimizers such as Muon and Soap show 1.3x speedups for small models (0.1B), the speedups diminish to around 1.1x for 1.2B parameter models at 8x Chinchilla Ratio"

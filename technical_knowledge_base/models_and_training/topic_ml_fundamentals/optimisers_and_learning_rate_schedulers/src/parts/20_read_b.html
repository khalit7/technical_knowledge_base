3 · Per-parameter step sizes: AdaGrad to AdamW

The intuition: scale each parameter's step by its own gradient history, so parameters whose gradients are small and consistent take larger steps and parameters whose gradients are large or volatile take smaller ones. All of these divide by a square root of accumulated squared gradients, coordinate by coordinate (a diagonal preconditioner).

OptimiserIdeaStrength and weakness
AdaGrad ({{Duchi, Hazan and Singer 2011|https://jmlr.org/papers/v12/duchi11a.html}})θ ← θ − η g / (√(Σ g²) + ε), the sum over every past stepGood for sparse features (a rarely seen feature keeps a large step). But the denominator only grows, so the rate decays towards nothing.
RMSProp ({{Hinton, lecture 6e, 2012|https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~tijmen/csc321/slides/lecture_slides_lec6.pdf}})replace the sum with an exponentially decaying average: v ← ρv + (1 − ρ)g² (0.9 in the lecture, 0.99 in PyTorch)Fixes AdaGrad's dying rate; good for online and non-stationary problems. Never published as a paper: the citation is a slide.
AdaDelta ({{Zeiler 2012|https://arxiv.org/html/1212.5701}})no global learning rate at all: the step is scaled by a running average of past updates divided by one of past gradients, so its units match θ'sRemoves the choice of η; rarely used now.

AdamW ({{Loshchilov and Hutter 2017|https://arxiv.org/html/1711.05101}})Adam with weight decay applied in the update rule (−ηλθ), not added to the lossIf L2 sits in the loss, it is divided by √v̂ like every other gradient component, so weights with large gradients are barely decayed. Decoupling fixes this. The LLM default.

Adam, exactly

mt = β1mt−1 + (1−β1)gt,   vt = β2vt−1 + (1−β2)gt²
m̂t = mt/(1−β1t),   v̂t = vt/(1−β2t),   θt = θt−1 − η m̂t/(√v̂t + ε)
m, v
running averages of the gradient and of its square, per parameter, both started at zero
β1, β2
their decay rates; the paper's defaults are 0.9 and 0.999, with η = 0.001 and ε = 10−8 ({{Algorithm 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980}})
1 − βt
the bias correction: the share of the average that is real data rather than the zero it started from

The step m̂/√v̂ is roughly the sign of the gradient, scaled by how consistent it has been: about 1 when every recent gradient agrees, smaller when they disagree. So Adam's step is bounded by about η per parameter, whatever the size of the gradient, which is why it barely needs the gradient's scale and why one learning rate works across very different layers.

What the animation shows, in numbers (recompute.py): without correction, a steady gradient gives a step of (1 − 0.9t)/√(1 − β2t) times η. With β2 = 0.999 that is 3.16η on step 1, a peak of 6.57η at step 12, still 3.24η at step 100 and 1.26η at step 1,000. This is the paper's own warning: "values β2 close to 1 indeed lead to instabilities in training when no bias correction term was present, especially at first few epochs" ({{Kingma and Ba, section 6.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980}}). With β2 = 0.95 the uncorrected step starts too small (0.45η), peaks 10% too large around step 20 and stays within 10% of correct from step 6 on, which is one reason the correction matters less for LLM runs, which mostly use 0.95 (GPT-3, Llama, DeepSeek-V3: see {{Defaults across models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}} on the parent page).

Why warmup

Bias correction removes the bias of v̂, not its noise. On step 1, m̂/√v̂ = g/|g|: every parameter moves by exactly η, whatever its gradient, and for the next few steps v̂ is an average of a handful of squares, so the per-parameter step sizes are large and erratic. {{Liu et al. 2019 (RAdam)|https://arxiv.org/html/1908.03265}} name this: the variance of the adaptive learning rate "is problematically large in the early stage", and warmup works as variance reduction. Warmup ramps η from near zero over the first steps until the statistics can be trusted; RAdam instead switches the adaptive term on only once enough steps have accumulated. A post-norm transformer is especially sensitive (the original Transformer warmed up for 4,000 steps). Warmup length is optimiser-dependent and worth tuning rather than copying ({{Semenov et al. 2025, takeaway 5|https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01440}}).

β2, ε and weight decay in practice

  • β2: 0.999 is the paper's default for sparse and vision problems; 0.98 in the original Transformer ({{section 5.3|https://arxiv.org/html/1706.03762#S5.SS3}}); 0.95 in most LLM pretraining (DeepSeek-V3: β = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1, {{section 4.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19437#S4.SS2}}; SmolLM3 the same, {{blog|https://huggingface.co/blog/smollm3}}). A shorter memory reacts faster when gradient statistics shift, at the price of noisier v.
  • ε: 10−8 by default and 10−9 in the Transformer; it only matters where √v̂ is tiny, but there it caps the step, so a parameter with near-zero gradients moves at most η·m̂/ε.
  • Weight decay: with L2 in the loss, the penalty's gradient λθ goes through Adam's division by √v̂, so weights with large, noisy gradients are under-decayed and weights with tiny gradients are pulled to zero at full speed; AdamW's decoupled θ ← θ − ηλθ decays every weight alike. For plain SGD the two are the same up to a rescaling of λ by the learning rate ({{Loshchilov and Hutter, abstract|https://arxiv.org/html/1711.05101}}). The parent page animates both on the same noise (thread 4); the regularisation side is on {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}. Because the decay per step is ηλ, it shrinks with the schedule.
A limit no diagonal method escapes
AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam rescale each coordinate. That helps exactly when the directions of very different curvature are the coordinates. Turn the same ravine 45° in the {{Optimiser race|#t-race}}: AdaGrad and RMSProp, which reached a loss of 10−3 in 15 steps on the axis-aligned ravine, no longer reach it in 150, while momentum, Nesterov and plain SGD behave exactly as before. In a network, weight-space directions of high curvature are rarely single weights. That is the case for the next section.

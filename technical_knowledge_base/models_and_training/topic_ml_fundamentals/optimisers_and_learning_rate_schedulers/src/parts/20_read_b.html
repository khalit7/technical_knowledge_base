2 · Per-parameter steps: Adam and AdamW

Scale each parameter's step by its own gradient history: divide by the square root of an average of its squared gradients, coordinate by coordinate (a diagonal preconditioner). AdaGrad sums every past g², so its rate only falls; RMSProp replaces the sum with a decaying average; Adam adds momentum and a bias correction; AdamW moves weight decay out of the division.

mt = β1mt−1 + (1−β1)gt,   vt = β2vt−1 + (1−β2)gt²
m̂t = mt/(1−β1t),   v̂t = vt/(1−β2t),   θt = θt−1 − η m̂t/(√v̂t + ε)
m, v
running averages of the gradient and of its square, per parameter, both started at zero
β1, β2
their decay rates; the paper's defaults are 0.9 and 0.999, with η = 0.001 and ε = 10−8 ({{Kingma and Ba 2014, Algorithm 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980}})
1 − βt
the bias correction: the share of the average that is real data rather than the zero it started from

The step m̂/√v̂ is roughly the sign of the gradient, scaled by how consistent it has been: about 1 when every recent gradient agrees, smaller when they disagree. So Adam's step is bounded by about η per parameter, whatever the size of the gradient, which is why one learning rate works across very different layers.

The overshoot is the paper's own warning: "values β2 close to 1 indeed lead to instabilities in training when no bias correction term was present, especially at first few epochs" ({{Kingma and Ba, section 6.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980}}). With β2 = 0.95, the value most LLM runs use, the uncorrected step is within 10% of correct from step 6 on (numbers below).

The uncorrected step in numbers (depth)

In numbers (recompute.py): without correction a steady gradient gives a step of (1 − 0.9t)/√(1 − β2t) times η. With β2 = 0.999 that is 3.16η on step 1, a peak of 6.57η at step 12, still 3.24η at step 100 and 1.26η at step 1,000. With β2 = 0.95 the uncorrected step starts too small (0.45η), peaks 10% too large around step 20 and stays within 10% of correct from step 6 on, one reason the correction matters less for LLM runs, which mostly use 0.95.

Why warmup

Bias correction removes the bias of v̂, not its noise. On step 1, m̂/√v̂ = g/|g|: every parameter moves by exactly η, whatever its gradient, and for the next few steps v̂ is an average of a handful of squares, so per-parameter steps are large and erratic. {{Liu et al. 2019 (RAdam)|https://arxiv.org/html/1908.03265}} name this: the variance of the adaptive learning rate "is problematically large in the early stage", and warmup works as variance reduction. Warmup ramps η from near zero until the statistics can be trusted; RAdam instead switches the adaptive term on only once enough steps have accumulated. A post-norm transformer is especially sensitive (the original Transformer warmed up for 4,000 steps). Warmup length is optimiser-dependent and worth tuning rather than copying ({{Semenov et al. 2025, takeaway 5|https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01440}}).

AdamW: why weight decay must leave the division

Put L2 in the loss and its gradient λθ goes through Adam's division by √v̂ like everything else: a weight whose gradient is large and noisy is barely decayed, and a weight whose gradient is tiny is pulled towards zero at up to the full step η. AdamW applies θ ← θ − ηλθ outside the division, so every weight decays alike ({{Loshchilov and Hutter 2017|https://arxiv.org/html/1711.05101}}).

L2 inside Adam decays quiet weights 10 times too hard and noisy ones 10 times too little

derived: one weight θ = 1 with λ = 0.1 and η = 10−3, at steady state, its gradient noise of mean 0 and RMS σ. L2 in Adam: m̂ ≈ λθ and √v̂ ≈ √(σ² + λ²θ²), so the shrink per step is ηλθ/√(σ² + λ²θ²). AdamW: ηλθ = 10−4 for every weight. The ratio runs from 10 at σ = 0.001 to 0.1 at σ = 10 (ns_data.py). The step-by-step animation of both on the same noise, and the regularisation side, are on {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}, section 3.

For plain SGD the two are the same up to a rescaling of λ by the learning rate ({{Loshchilov and Hutter, abstract|https://arxiv.org/html/1711.05101}}). Because AdamW's decay per step is ηλ, it shrinks with the schedule. In code, torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) is the L2 version; AdamW is the decoupled one.

The limit no diagonal method escapes

AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam rescale each coordinate. That helps exactly when the directions of very different curvature are the coordinates. In a network, the weight-space directions of high curvature are rarely single weights.

  • β2: 0.999 is the paper's default for sparse and vision problems; 0.98 in the original Transformer ({{section 5.3|https://arxiv.org/html/1706.03762#S5.SS3}}); 0.95 in most LLM pretraining (GPT-3, Llama; DeepSeek-V3: β = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1, {{section 4.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19437#S4.SS2}}; SmolLM3 the same, {{blog|https://huggingface.co/blog/smollm3}}; others on the parent's Defaults across models tab). A shorter memory reacts faster when gradient statistics shift, at the price of a noisier v.
  • ε: 10−8 by default and 10−9 in the Transformer; it only matters where √v̂ is tiny, but there it caps the step, so a parameter with near-zero gradients moves at most η·m̂/ε.
  • Sparse gradients need a long memory in v (β2 near 1), which the Adam paper names as exactly the case where an uncorrected v starts far too small: in the animation, the sparse parameter's corrected step on step 11 is 0.46η against 1η on step 1 (β2 = 0.999), because its m and v still remember the earlier spikes.

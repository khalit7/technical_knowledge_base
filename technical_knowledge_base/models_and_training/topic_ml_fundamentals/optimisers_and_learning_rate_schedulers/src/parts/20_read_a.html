In one screen

An optimiser turns the gradient of the loss into a change of the weights. Three choices are made together: how the gradient is smoothed over steps (momentum), how each parameter's step is scaled (adaptive methods and preconditioners), and how the base learning rate η moves over the run (the schedule). Adaptive methods only rescale relative to η, so an optimiser and a schedule are always chosen as a pair.

The takeaway, as of October 2026
AdamW with warmup and a decaying schedule (cosine, or WSD when the run's length may change) is still the safe default. Muon on hidden matrices, with AdamW for everything else, is the challenger with frontier-scale evidence: it trained the 1T-parameter Kimi K2 and the GLM-4.5 family (below). But every independent re-tuning finds the challengers' speed-ups smaller than their papers report, and shrinking with model size: about 1.1× at 1.2B parameters against a well-tuned AdamW ({{Wen et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02046}}; The evidence).

1 · The landscape the optimiser walks

  • Local optimum: the gradient is zero and moving in any direction increases the loss.
  • Saddle point: the gradient is zero, but the loss decreases along some directions and increases along others (a horse saddle); the second derivative says it is neither a maximum nor a minimum. Saddles are frequent in high dimensions but are not a big problem in practice: the descending direction is there to be found, and momentum, mini-batch noise and even floating-point rounding push the optimiser off. Try the {{Saddle|#t-race}} in the race: starting 0.001 off the ridge, plain SGD needs 15 steps to leave, while Adam, AdaGrad and RMSProp leave on the first step, because they divide the tiny gradient by its own size.
  • All parameters update simultaneously. J(θ0, θ1) is always evaluated at the previous parameters: compute every gradient first, then update every weight. Updating θ0 and then using the new θ0 to compute θ1's gradient is a different (coordinate-descent) algorithm.
  • Conditioning sets the pace. When the loss curves 100 times more sharply across a valley than along it (condition number 100), the largest stable step for the steep direction is tiny for the shallow one, and gradient descent crawls. The mathematics (Hessian, eigenvalues, the stable-rate bound 2/λmax) is on {{Calculus and optimisation for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eb8b49ed6a0f897f3b}}; every method below is a different answer to it.

The noise matters for the schedule: with a noisy gradient a constant rate cannot converge, only hover in a region whose size grows with the rate (section 7).

2 · Momentum: smooth the gradient over steps

vt = β vt−1 + gt,   θt = θt−1 − η vt
gt
the gradient of the loss at θt−1 (on the current mini-batch)
vt
the velocity: a running sum of past gradients, each older one weighted by a further factor β (usually 0.9)
η
the learning rate
  • Heavy ball ({{Polyak 1964|https://doi.org/10.1016/0041-5553(64)90137-5}}). Gradients that agree from step to step add up, so the step grows along a consistent direction (up to 1/(1 − β) = 10 times at β = 0.9); gradients that flip sign, as they do across a ravine, cancel. On a quadratic with condition number R, tuned momentum needs about √R times fewer iterations than steepest descent ({{Sutskever et al. 2013, section 2|http://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/sutskever13.pdf}}).
  • The formula above is PyTorch's ({{torch.optim.SGD|https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.SGD.html}}, dampening 0). Many papers write vt = βvt−1 + (1 − β)gt instead, an average rather than a sum; it traces the same path only with a learning rate 1/(1 − β) = 10 times larger, so a learning rate never transfers between the two conventions without that factor.
  • Nesterov (NAG) ({{Nesterov 1983|http://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/sutskever13.pdf}}, in Sutskever et al.'s form): take the gradient at the look-ahead point θ − ηβvt−1, where momentum is about to carry you, so the step is corrected before it is taken. PyTorch implements the equivalent rewrite θ ← θ − η(gt + βvt), which needs no second gradient evaluation.

Try the {{Ravine, axis-aligned|#t-race}} in the Optimiser race: at their best rates, plain SGD is still above 10−3 after 150 steps, momentum 0.9 gets below it at step 89 and Nesterov at step 24. Momentum 0.9 is not the optimum for this valley; the √R speed-up needs momentum tuned to the curvature, which nobody knows for a network, so 0.9 is the habit.

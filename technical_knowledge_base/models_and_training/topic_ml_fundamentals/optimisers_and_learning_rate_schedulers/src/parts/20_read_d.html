6 · Batch size and the learning rate

A larger batch averages more examples per gradient, so the gradient is less noisy and a larger step is safe. Up to a point that buys fewer steps for the same data; beyond it, extra examples per step are wasted. {{McCandlish et al. 2018|https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06162}} fit this trade-off with one relation, (S/Smin − 1)(E/Emin − 1) = 1 (their equation 2.11), where S and E are the steps and training examples needed to reach a given loss, and define the critical batch Bcrit = Emin/Smin (equation 2.12). Solving with E = BS gives S = Smin(1 + Bcrit/B) and E = Emin(1 + B/Bcrit).

At the critical batch you pay twice the minimum steps and twice the minimum data

Predict what an 8 times larger batch than Bcrit saves in steps, then drag.

derived from McCandlish et al.'s equations 2.11 and 2.12; the default (B = Bcrit) lands on their statement that training then "takes twice as many passes through the training data as an optimally data-efficient (small-batch) run would take, and twice as many optimization steps as an optimally time-efficient (large-batch) run would take", by construction of the formula. At 8 Bcrit: 1.12 times the minimum steps for 9 times the minimum data. Their critical batch grows during a run ("the noise scale increases as the loss decreases") and is predicted, at the order-of-magnitude level, by the gradient noise scale. The rate rules below are relative to a batch you have tuned, scaled by κ.

  • SGD: the linear scaling rule. "When the minibatch size is multiplied by k, multiply the learning rate by k", all other hyperparameters unchanged, with a gradual warmup that reaches kη after 5 epochs: ResNet-50 at a batch of 8,192 on 256 GPUs in one hour, matching small-batch accuracy ({{Goyal et al. 2017, sections 2.1 and 2.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.02677}}). The rule fails at the start of training (hence the warmup) and at very large batches.
  • Adam and RMSProp: the square-root rule. For batch κB use η√κ, β1′ = 1 − κ(1 − β1), β2′ = 1 − κ(1 − β2) and ε/√κ ({{Malladi et al. 2022, Definition 5.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10287}}), derived from an SDE approximation of Adam. The β rule is the part usually forgotten, and it runs out: with β2 = 0.95, κ(1 − β2) reaches 1 at κ = 20.
  • Past the critical batch no rule helps: steps barely fall. Muon is reported to keep its data efficiency at batch sizes far beyond AdamW's critical batch (Essential AI 2025, linked below), and for LLMs the peak rate is usually found by sweeps at small scale and transferred by fitted scaling rules or by parametrisations such as μP, designed to keep the optimum fixed as width grows; Essential AI report μP transfer with Muon up to 3.7B parameters ({{2025|https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02222}}).

7 · Schedules: how the base rate moves over the run

A high rate early travels quickly towards a basin; a low rate late settles at its bottom. With noisy gradients there is a second reason: at a constant rate the iterate never converges but hovers in a region whose size grows with η, so the final loss is set by how low the rate gets at the end.

How the schedules compare

  • At a fixed, known budget, final quality is a near-tie. "The constant learning rate with a short cooldown (< 20% of total training steps) can achieve the same final loss as cosine, and only outperforms for a longer fraction of cooldown steps", and it "scales predictably and reliably similar to cosine" ({{Hägele et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18392}}), which is why WSD spread so fast. Not everyone agrees: GLM-4.5 chose cosine because WSD runs "perform worse on general benchmarks (SimpleQA, MMLU)" ({{section 2.4|https://arxiv.org/html/2508.06471#S2.SS4}}).
  • Cosine's real cost is optionality, not loss. Every checkpoint before the end of a cosine run sits at a rate that is still high, so it is worse than a run planned to stop there: you cannot stop early, extend the run, or reuse it at another length. Kaplan et al.'s scaling laws read such intermediate checkpoints, and Chinchilla corrected it with one schedule per training length: "overestimating the number of training steps beyond 25% leads to clear drops in performance" ({{Chinchilla, Appendix B|https://arxiv.org/html/2203.15556#A2}}). WSD branches or a WSM merge give honest intermediate models.
  • Cooldown shape, length and end: roughly the last 10 to 20% of steps, to well below 10% of the peak (evidence below); and 1−sqrt "consistently performs better than linear decay" (Hägele et al., takeaway 2), and both beat convex shapes (exponential, EMA-like), which linger at a high rate and then collapse too fast.
  • The cooldown is also a data lever: labs up-weight curated mathematics, code and instruction-like data while the rate falls, so in practice the schedule and the data anneal are one decision; how that is done and how to separate the two effects is on {{Pretraining|n:3c65c17b0d0d814f82cffd0d5c0dd5ba}} (what goes into the mixture: {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}}).
Cooldown length, the final rate, and weight averaging: the evidence (depth)
  • A dissent on shape:: Semenov et al. find that for most optimisers "the cosine scheduler dominates".
  • Cooldown length: roughly 10 to 20% of the steps, with diminishing returns beyond. MiniCPM found "a decay of 10% of the total tokens is sufficient to achieve the best results, while a decay of 2.5% of total tokens falls short" ({{section 4.3|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06395#S4.SS3}}); for long runs a shorter fraction can suffice if it is long enough in absolute steps (Hägele et al.).
  • The final rate matters, and 10% of the peak is a habit, not an optimum. Chinchilla decayed by 10× and found it "slightly more performant" than decaying to 0 (Appendix B); Llama 2 used 10%, Llama 3 405B 1%. {{Bergsma et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15938}} find that linear decay to zero beats 10× decay at compute-optimal dataset sizes and beyond, and that the gap grows with tokens per parameter (a 610M model at 80 tokens per parameter with decay to zero beat the same model at 200 with 10× decay: about 60% less compute); {{Semenov et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01440}} find decaying below 10% "significantly improves the results" but that the best final rate differs by schedule (in their runs a final rate of 1% of the peak beat decaying all the way to 0).
  • Weight averaging substitutes for part of the decay. Averaging along the trajectory (SWA, EMA) improves checkpoints at no training cost: "While it does not match the cooldown, it reduces the gap without the need for a separate decay phase" (Hägele et al., takeaway 5). WSM turns this into a full replacement (section 8).

The old version of this page said that decaying nearer to zero "improves loss but can hurt some downstream benchmarks". No source for the downstream part was found, and the evidence points the other way: Bergsma et al. report that trends in validation loss also hold for downstream evaluations. Finding the peak, and choosing a schedule

The schedule's shape is less sensitive than its peak. The learning-rate range test ({{Smith 2015, section 3.3|https://arxiv.org/html/1506.01186#S3.SS3}}): train for a few epochs "while letting the learning rate increase linearly between low and high LR values", and read off where the loss starts to fall and where it turns noisy or rises: one run gives both a good rate and a good range, in Smith's words. For LLMs, sweeps at small scale and transfer (section 6).

  • LLM pretraining whose length may change or be extended: WSD, 1−sqrt cooldown over the last 10 to 20%.
  • LLM pretraining at a fixed, final budget: warmup then cosine, to well below 10% of the peak (or linear to zero, Bergsma et al.).
  • Supervised fine-tuning: linear or cosine to zero, 3 to 5% warmup (a convention; the Trainer's default warmup is 0).
  • A small supervised model with a cheap, steady validation metric: ReduceLROnPlateau or MultiStep.
  • No schedule at all: schedule-free, or WSM (next section).

8 · Decay-free: schedule-free optimisation and WSM

WSD removed the need to know T, but not the decay: you still choose when to start it, over how many tokens and with which curve, and extending training after the decay has begun means rolling back to the pre-decay checkpoint. Two methods remove the decay itself.

Schedule-free (Defazio et al. 2024)

yt = (1−β)zt + βxt,   zt+1 = zt − γ∇f(yt),   xt+1 = (1−ct+1)xt + ct+1zt+1
z
the plain SGD (or Adam) sequence, at a constant rate γ
x
a running average of z, with ct+1 = 1/(t+1) at a constant rate (weighted by γt² with warmup); this is the model you evaluate
y
the point where the gradient is taken, between the two; β ≈ 0.9 plays the role of momentum
  • Averaging iterates is old; the trick is taking the gradient at the interpolation y, which makes the average converge as fast as a well-tuned decay without knowing T ({{The Road Less Scheduled, section 2|https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15682#S2}}; {{NeurIPS 2024 oral|https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/oral/98003}}). It won AlgoPerf's 2024 self-tuning track (section 5). In the three-endpoints figure it reads all three endpoints off one run.
  • In code, call optimizer.eval() before evaluating or saving: the weights held during training are y, not the average x (practice notes below).
  • Both variants are in the {{Optimiser race|#t-race}}, checked against the schedulefree package; on the noisy ravine they go below the constant-rate floor with no schedule.
Schedule-free in practice (depth)
  • In practice ({{repository README|https://github.com/facebookresearch/schedule_free}}): call optimizer.train() and optimizer.eval() wherever you call them on the model, and keep the optimiser in eval mode when saving checkpoints, because the weights held during training are y, not the average x you should evaluate; warmup is still recommended; β = 0.9 by default, and 0.95 or 0.98 may be needed for very long runs; rates 10 to 50 times larger than classical ones for SGD, 1 to 10 times for AdamW.

WSM: Warmup-Stable and Merge (Tian et al. 2025)

The rate warms up, then stays constant forever; checkpoints are saved periodically, and a weighted merge of the last n of them stands in for the annealed model. The link to decay is exact: merging checkpoints with weights cj is algebraically the same as applying per-step weights wi = Σj≥i cj to the updates after the base checkpoint, so any monotone decay curve can be inverted into merge weights (ck = wk, cj = wj − wj+1, c0 = 1 − w1). Mean averaging corresponds to linear decay, EMA to a convex decay, and cosine or 1−sqrt can be built directly. The framework is optimiser-agnostic and needs no change to the training loop. Details, a live toy and the equivalence test are on the {{WSM paper page|n:3c75c17b0d0d8105b452d2c1bc1d087a}}.

WSM's results and practical read (depth)
  • Results (a 16.3B-parameter MoE with 1.4B active, 400B tokens branched off a 10.2T-token constant-rate checkpoint, with a switch to the high-quality annealing set): merge duration mattered far more than the checkpoint interval or the number of checkpoints merged, and EMA merging was the weakest, mirroring the convex-is-worse order above.
  • Practical read: the storage cost (one checkpoint per interval) is small next to a pretraining budget, and a merge is a cheap, repeatable proxy for "how good would this model be if I annealed now", with no throwaway decay runs to gauge progress.

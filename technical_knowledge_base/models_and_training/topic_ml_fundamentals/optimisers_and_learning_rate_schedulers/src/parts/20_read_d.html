7 · Schedules: how the base rate moves over the run

A schedule sets η over time; adaptive optimisers only rescale relative to it, so both are always used together. The logic: a high rate early to travel quickly towards some basin, a low rate late to settle at its bottom. With noisy gradients there is a second reason: at a constant rate the iterate never converges but hovers in a region whose size grows with η, so the final loss is set by how low the rate gets at the end (the {{Noisy ravine|#t-race}} in the race shows the floor, and the decay going below it). Two questions separate every family: does the schedule need the total number of steps T in advance, and what shape is the decay.

Three endpoints, three ways

Try: you want a model at 250, 500 and 1,000 steps (say, to fit a scaling law, or because you do not yet know how long you can train). Play each method and watch the step counter: cosine needs one run per length; WSD branches cooldowns off one shared run; schedule-free reads all three off one run.

Toy (illustrative): the noisy quadratic model of {{Zhang et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.04164}}, 10 coordinates with curvatures from 1 down to 0.01 (condition number 100), start at 1, gradient noise with covariance 0.09·H, plain SGD. The losses are exact expected values computed by recursion (no sampling); checks/nqm_check.py matches them with 4,000 seeded PyTorch runs (torch.optim.SGD and schedulefree.SGDScheduleFree), largest deviation 2.1 standard errors. Learning rates: the best of a log grid (10k/8), cosine re-tuned per length, the others tuned once for 1,000 steps. Grey line: the same SGD at its best constant rate, never decayed.

What the toy says, and what it does not. Re-tuned for each length, cosine reaches the lowest loss at every endpoint here, by 1.3 to 2.2 times; schedule-free and WSD, tuned once, come within 26% and 54% at 1,000 steps and are further behind at 250, where their single rate is too low; all three end 2.9 to 4.4 times below the never-decayed rate. A quadratic favours schedules that decay throughout. In LLM pretraining the evidence is different, and that is what this toy cannot show: tuned constant-plus-cooldown runs match cosine (next paragraph), and schedule-free gives "strong (but not optimal)" results there (Hägele et al., section 4.2). What the toy does show exactly is the cost structure: 1,750, 1,150 and 1,000 optimiser steps for the same three endpoints.

How the schedules compare

  • At a fixed, known budget, final quality is a near-tie. Cosine, WSD and 1−sqrt cooldowns land within noise of each other once T is known and each is tuned. "The constant learning rate with a short cooldown (< 20% of total training steps) can achieve the same final loss as cosine, and only outperforms for a longer fraction of cooldown steps", and it "scales predictably and reliably similar to cosine" ({{Hägele et al. 2024|https://arxiv.org/html/2405.18392}}), which is why WSD spread so fast. Not everyone agrees: GLM-4.5 chose cosine after WSD underfitted on benchmarks, and Semenov et al. find that for most optimisers "the cosine scheduler dominates".
  • Cosine's real cost is optionality, not loss. Every checkpoint before the end of a cosine run sits at a rate that is still high, so you cannot stop early, extend the run, or reuse it at another length. Kaplan et al.'s scaling laws read such intermediate checkpoints, and Chinchilla corrected it with one schedule per training length: "overestimating the number of training steps beyond 25% leads to clear drops in performance" ({{Chinchilla, Appendix B|https://arxiv.org/html/2203.15556#A2}}). The parent page's schedule chart lets you move the end of the run.
  • Cooldown shape has a stable order: 1−sqrt "consistently performs better than linear decay" (Hägele et al., takeaway 2), and both beat convex shapes (exponential, EMA-like), which linger at a high rate and then collapse too fast.
  • Cooldown length: roughly 10 to 20% of the steps, with diminishing returns beyond. MiniCPM found "a decay of 10% of the total tokens is sufficient to achieve the best results, while a decay of 2.5% of total tokens falls short" ({{section 4.3|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06395#S4.SS3}}); for long runs a shorter fraction can suffice if it is long enough in absolute steps (Hägele et al.).
  • The final rate matters, and 10% of the peak is a habit, not an optimum. Chinchilla decayed by 10× and found it "slightly more performant" than decaying to 0 ({{Appendix B|https://arxiv.org/html/2203.15556#A2}}); Llama 2 used 10%, Llama 3 405B 1%. {{Bergsma et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15938}} find that linear decay to zero beats 10× decay at compute-optimal dataset sizes and beyond, and that the gap grows with tokens per parameter (a 610M model at 80 tokens per parameter with decay to zero beat the same model at 200 with 10× decay: about 60% less compute); {{Semenov et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/html/2509.01440}} find decaying below 10% "significantly improves the results" but that the best final rate differs by schedule (in their runs a final rate of 1% of the peak beat decaying all the way to 0).
  • The cooldown is also a data lever: labs up-weight curated mathematics, code and instruction-like data while the rate falls, so the schedule and the data anneal are one decision ({{Pretraining|n:3c65c17b0d0d814f82cffd0d5c0dd5ba}}; {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}}).
  • Weight averaging substitutes for part of the decay. Averaging along the trajectory (SWA, EMA) improves checkpoints at no training cost: "While it does not match the cooldown, it reduces the gap without the need for a separate decay phase" (Hägele et al., takeaway 5). WSM turns this into a full replacement (section 9).
Correction
The old version of this page said that decaying nearer to zero "improves loss but can hurt some downstream benchmarks". No source for the downstream part was found, and the evidence points the other way: Bergsma et al. report that trends in validation loss also hold for downstream evaluations. The nearest benchmark finding, GLM-4.5's, is about the schedule's shape (WSD against cosine), not the final rate.

Finding the peak rate

The schedule's shape is less sensitive than its peak. The learning-rate range test ({{Smith 2015, section 3.3|https://arxiv.org/html/1506.01186#S3.SS3}}): train for a few epochs "while letting the learning rate increase linearly between low and high LR values", and read off where the loss starts to fall and where it turns noisy or rises: one run gives both a good rate and a good range, in Smith's words. For LLMs the peak is usually found by sweeps at small scale and transferred, either by fitted scaling rules or by parametrisations such as μP designed to keep the optimum fixed as width grows; Essential AI report μP transfer with Muon up to 3.7B parameters ({{2025|https://arxiv.org/html/2505.02222}}).

Choosing one

  • LLM pretraining whose length may change or be extended: WSD, 1−sqrt cooldown over the last 10 to 20%.
  • LLM pretraining at a fixed, final budget: warmup then cosine, to well below 10% of the peak (or linear to zero, Bergsma et al.).
  • Supervised fine-tuning: linear or cosine to zero, 3 to 5% warmup (a convention; the Trainer's default warmup is 0).
  • A small supervised model with a cheap, steady validation metric: ReduceLROnPlateau or MultiStep.
  • No schedule at all: schedule-free, or WSM.

8 · "Annealing" and "learning-rate decay": three meanings

"Annealing" is used in three different ways in this literature, and they are not interchangeable.

SenseWhat it meansWhere you meet it
1. Classical, optimisationLowering the learning rate, full stop. Borrowed from simulated annealing, where a temperature is reduced so the search settles. Here annealing and decay are exact synonyms."cosine annealing", CosineAnnealingLR, eta_min; anything before 2023
2. Modern LLM pretrainingA training phase (the final 10 to 30% of tokens or so) defined by two simultaneous changes: the rate decays towards zero and the data mixture switches to curated high-quality tokens (mathematics, code, instruction-like, reasoning).Llama 3's annealing, OLMo 2's Dolmino mid-training mix, MiniCPM's WSD decay phase; "the annealed model"
3. Annealing as an experimentA cheap evaluation protocol: take a fixed mid-run checkpoint, anneal it on a candidate dataset, compare scores. Used to price a dataset, not to train a final model.Llama 3 and OLMo both evaluate candidate corpora this way
  • The two levers are separable, and papers usually pull both at once. WSD lets you decay the rate with no data change; you can also switch to a high-quality mixture while the rate stays flat (WSM switches the data at its Tswitch with the rate held constant). Any claim that "annealing gave +X" is a compound of two effects unless the ablation separated them. The {{Pretraining|n:3c65c17b0d0d814f82cffd0d5c0dd5ba}} page separates them on OLMo 2's numbers and on a toy.
  • They work for different reasons that reinforce each other. A low rate means the model descends into a basin and stops moving much; low gradient noise near the end is also when scarce high-quality tokens stick best rather than being diluted across a 10T-token stream. That is why the data switch is scheduled to coincide with the decay, not because one requires the other.
  • Reading papers: "decay phase" or "cooldown" means the schedule alone; "anneal" or "annealed checkpoint" almost always implies the data switch too. WSM is a clean example of why the split matters: it removes sense 1 entirely (constant rate forever) while keeping sense 2 (the curated-data switch), and recovers the decay's benefit by merging.
  • Unrelated homonyms: simulated annealing proper (a combinatorial optimisation algorithm) and sampling temperature at inference ({{Sampling and Decoding|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bafcda29a2c7086e}}) share the metaphor and nothing else. What goes into the anneal mixture: {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}}.

9 · Decay-free: schedule-free optimisation and WSM

WSD removed the need to know T, but not the decay: you still choose when to start it, over how many tokens and with which curve, and extending training after the decay has begun means rolling back to the pre-decay checkpoint. Two methods remove the decay itself.

Schedule-free (Defazio et al. 2024)

yt = (1−β)zt + βxt,   zt+1 = zt − γ∇f(yt),   xt+1 = (1−ct+1)xt + ct+1zt+1
z
the plain SGD (or Adam) sequence, at a constant rate γ
x
a running average of z, with ct+1 = 1/(t+1) at a constant rate (weighted by γt² with warmup); this is the model you evaluate
y
the point where the gradient is taken, between the two; β ≈ 0.9 plays the role of momentum
  • Averaging iterates is old; the trick is taking the gradient at the interpolation y, which makes the average converge as fast as a well-tuned decay without knowing T ({{The Road Less Scheduled, section 2|https://arxiv.org/html/2405.15682#S2}}; {{NeurIPS 2024 oral|https://neurips.cc/virtual/2024/oral/98003}}). It won AlgoPerf's 2024 self-tuning track (section 6).
  • In practice ({{repository README|https://github.com/facebookresearch/schedule_free}}): call optimizer.train() and optimizer.eval() wherever you call them on the model, and keep the optimiser in eval mode when saving checkpoints, because the weights stored during training are y, not x; warmup is still recommended; β = 0.9 by default, and 0.95 or 0.98 may be needed for very long runs; rates 10 to 50 times larger than classical ones for SGD, 1 to 10 times for AdamW.
  • Both variants are in the {{Optimiser race|#t-race}}, checked against the schedulefree package; on the noisy ravine they go below the constant-rate floor with no schedule.

WSM: Warmup-Stable and Merge (Tian et al. 2025)

The rate warms up, then stays constant forever; checkpoints are saved periodically, and a weighted merge of the last n of them stands in for the annealed model. The link to decay is exact, not heuristic: merging checkpoints with weights cj is algebraically the same as applying per-step weights wi = Σj≥i cj to the updates after the base checkpoint, so any monotone decay curve can be inverted into merge weights (ck = wk, cj = wj − wj+1, c0 = 1 − w1). Mean averaging corresponds to linear decay, EMA to a convex decay, and cosine or 1−sqrt can be built directly. The framework is optimiser-agnostic and needs no change to the training loop.

  • Results (a 16.3B-parameter MoE with 1.4B active, 400B tokens branched off a 10.2T-token constant-rate checkpoint, with a switch to the high-quality annealing set): merge duration mattered far more than the checkpoint interval or the number of checkpoints merged, and EMA merging was the weakest, mirroring the convex-is-worse order above.
  • Practical read: the storage cost (one checkpoint per interval) is small next to a pretraining budget, and a merge is a cheap, repeatable proxy for "how good would this model be if I annealed now", with no throwaway decay runs to gauge progress.
Correction
The old page said WSM "beat a matched WSD decay by ~1.3 points on average". The paper's +1.28 points (62.67 to 63.95, average of 41 benchmarks) compares the best checkpoint of each run; when both stop at WSD's 400B tokens the gain is +1.18 (derived from Figure 3); each comparison is a single run, and the checkpoint was picked on the test benchmarks. Details, a live toy and the equivalence test are on the {{WSM paper page|n:3c75c17b0d0d8105b452d2c1bc1d087a}}.

10 · Common mistakes

Copying a learning rate between momentum conventions, or between Adam and SGD
PyTorch's momentum sums gradients (v = βv + g); the averaged form (v = βv + (1 − β)g) needs a rate 10 times larger at β = 0.9 for the same path. Adam's step is about η per parameter whatever the gradient's size, SGD's is η times the gradient, so their rates are unrelated. Lion wants a rate 3 to 10 times smaller than AdamW's, schedule-free SGD 10 to 50 times larger than SGD's.
Using L2 regularisation with Adam and calling it weight decay
torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) adds λθ to the gradient, so it is divided by √v̂ with everything else; AdamW decays every weight alike. They are the same only for plain SGD.
Removing warmup because Adam already corrects its bias
Bias correction fixes the bias of the moment estimates, not their variance: the first step moves every parameter by exactly η, and the next ones rest on a handful of samples. Warmup (or RAdam's rectification) handles the variance.
Reading an intermediate cosine checkpoint as "the model at that size of run"
Before the end of a cosine schedule the rate is still high, so the checkpoint is worse than a run planned to stop there. Kaplan's scaling laws did this; Chinchilla corrected it. WSD branches or a WSM merge give honest intermediate models.
Evaluating a schedule-free model at the training weights
The weights held during training are the interpolation y; the model to evaluate and to save is the average x. Call optimizer.eval() first.
Applying Muon to every parameter
Muon is for 2-D hidden matrices. Embeddings, the output head, norms and biases go to AdamW (GLM-4.5 and Jordan both do this); a 1-D parameter has nothing to orthogonalise.
Believing a new optimiser's speed-up before the baseline is tuned
Tuning AdamW's learning rate alone can give up to 2× on a 100M model, and fully decaying the baseline's rate erased Lion's and Sophia's gains in re-evaluations. Compare against a tuned, fully decayed AdamW at the same batch size, at more than one scale (section 6).
Crediting "annealing" for a gain that came from the data switch (or the reverse)
The modern anneal lowers the rate and changes the data at once; unless an ablation separates the two, "+X from annealing" is a compound (section 8).
Assuming a diagonal adaptive method handles bad conditioning
AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam rescale coordinates; when the steep directions are mixtures of coordinates they cannot help (the rotated ravine in the race). That is the case for Shampoo, SOAP and Muon.

Learning-rate failure modes in training curves (oscillation, NaN, plateaus, spikes) are diagnosed on the parent page's {{When training goes wrong|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}} tab.

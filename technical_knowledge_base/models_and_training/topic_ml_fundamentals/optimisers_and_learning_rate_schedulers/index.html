In one screen

An optimiser turns the gradient of the loss into a change of the weights. Three choices are made together: how the gradient is smoothed over steps (momentum), how each parameter's step is scaled (adaptive methods and preconditioners), and how the base learning rate η moves over the run (the schedule). Adaptive methods only rescale relative to η, so an optimiser and a schedule are always chosen as a pair.

The takeaway, as of October 2026
AdamW with warmup and a decaying schedule (cosine, or WSD when the run's length may change) is still the safe default. Muon on hidden matrices, with AdamW for everything else, is the challenger with frontier-scale evidence: it trained the 1T-parameter Kimi K2 and the GLM-4.5 family (below). But every independent re-tuning finds the challengers' speed-ups smaller than their papers report, and shrinking with model size: about 1.1× at 1.2B parameters against a well-tuned AdamW (Wen et al. 2025; The evidence).

1 · The landscape the optimiser walks

  • Local optimum: the gradient is zero and moving in any direction increases the loss.
  • Saddle point: the gradient is zero, but the loss decreases along some directions and increases along others (a horse saddle); the second derivative says it is neither a maximum nor a minimum. Saddles are frequent in high dimensions but are not a big problem in practice: the descending direction is there to be found, and momentum, mini-batch noise and even floating-point rounding push the optimiser off. Try the Saddle in the race: starting 0.001 off the ridge, plain SGD needs 15 steps to leave, while Adam, AdaGrad and RMSProp leave on the first step, because they divide the tiny gradient by its own size.
  • All parameters update simultaneously. J(θ0, θ1) is always evaluated at the previous parameters: compute every gradient first, then update every weight. Updating θ0 and then using the new θ0 to compute θ1's gradient is a different (coordinate-descent) algorithm.
  • Conditioning sets the pace. When the loss curves 100 times more sharply across a valley than along it (condition number 100), the largest stable step for the steep direction is tiny for the shallow one, and gradient descent crawls. The mathematics (Hessian, eigenvalues, the stable-rate bound 2/λmax) is on Calculus and optimisation for ML; every method below is a different answer to it.

2 · Momentum: smooth the gradient over steps

vt = β vt−1 + gt,   θt = θt−1 − η vt
gt
the gradient of the loss at θt−1 (on the current mini-batch)
vt
the velocity: a running sum of past gradients, each older one weighted by a further factor β (usually 0.9)
η
the learning rate
  • Heavy ball (Polyak 1964). Gradients that agree from step to step add up, so the step grows along a consistent direction (up to 1/(1 − β) = 10 times at β = 0.9); gradients that flip sign, as they do across a ravine, cancel. On a quadratic with condition number R, tuned momentum needs about √R times fewer iterations than steepest descent (Sutskever et al. 2013, section 2).
  • The formula above is PyTorch's (torch.optim.SGD, dampening 0). Many papers write vt = βvt−1 + (1 − β)gt instead, an average rather than a sum; it traces the same path only with a learning rate 1/(1 − β) = 10 times larger, so a learning rate never transfers between the two conventions without that factor.
  • Nesterov (NAG) (Nesterov 1983, in Sutskever et al.'s form): take the gradient at the look-ahead point θ − ηβvt−1, where momentum is about to carry you, so the step is corrected before it is taken. PyTorch implements the equivalent rewrite θ ← θ − η(gt + βvt), which needs no second gradient evaluation.

Try the Ravine, axis-aligned in the Optimiser race: at their best rates, plain SGD is still above 10−3 after 150 steps, momentum 0.9 gets below it at step 89 and Nesterov at step 24. Momentum 0.9 is not the optimum for this valley; the √R speed-up needs momentum tuned to the curvature, which nobody knows for a network, so 0.9 is the habit.

3 · Per-parameter step sizes: AdaGrad to AdamW

The intuition: scale each parameter's step by its own gradient history, so parameters whose gradients are small and consistent take larger steps and parameters whose gradients are large or volatile take smaller ones. All of these divide by a square root of accumulated squared gradients, coordinate by coordinate (a diagonal preconditioner).

OptimiserIdeaStrength and weakness
AdaGrad (Duchi, Hazan and Singer 2011)θ ← θ − η g / (√(Σ g²) + ε), the sum over every past stepGood for sparse features (a rarely seen feature keeps a large step). But the denominator only grows, so the rate decays towards nothing.

Adam, exactly

mt = β1mt−1 + (1−β1)gt,   vt = β2vt−1 + (1−β2)gt²
m̂t = mt/(1−β1t),   v̂t = vt/(1−β2t),   θt = θt−1 − η m̂t/(√v̂t + ε)
m, v
running averages of the gradient and of its square, per parameter, both started at zero
β1, β2
their decay rates; the paper's defaults are 0.9 and 0.999, with η = 0.001 and ε = 10−8 (Algorithm 1)
1 − βt
the bias correction: the share of the average that is real data rather than the zero it started from

The step m̂/√v̂ is roughly the sign of the gradient, scaled by how consistent it has been: about 1 when every recent gradient agrees, smaller when they disagree. So Adam's step is bounded by about η per parameter, whatever the size of the gradient, which is why it barely needs the gradient's scale and why one learning rate works across very different layers.

What the animation shows, in numbers (recompute.py): without correction, a steady gradient gives a step of (1 − 0.9t)/√(1 − β2t) times η. With β2 = 0.999 that is 3.16η on step 1, a peak of 6.57η at step 12, still 3.24η at step 100 and 1.26η at step 1,000. This is the paper's own warning: "values β2 close to 1 indeed lead to instabilities in training when no bias correction term was present, especially at first few epochs" (Kingma and Ba, section 6.4). With β2 = 0.95 the uncorrected step starts too small (0.45η), peaks 10% too large around step 20 and stays within 10% of correct from step 6 on, which is one reason the correction matters less for LLM runs, which mostly use 0.95 (GPT-3, Llama, DeepSeek-V3: see Defaults across models on the parent page).

Why warmup

Bias correction removes the bias of v̂, not its noise. On step 1, m̂/√v̂ = g/|g|: every parameter moves by exactly η, whatever its gradient, and for the next few steps v̂ is an average of a handful of squares, so the per-parameter step sizes are large and erratic. Liu et al. 2019 (RAdam) name this: the variance of the adaptive learning rate "is problematically large in the early stage", and warmup works as variance reduction. Warmup ramps η from near zero over the first steps until the statistics can be trusted; RAdam instead switches the adaptive term on only once enough steps have accumulated. A post-norm transformer is especially sensitive (the original Transformer warmed up for 4,000 steps). Warmup length is optimiser-dependent and worth tuning rather than copying (Semenov et al. 2025, takeaway 5).

β2, ε and weight decay in practice

  • β2: 0.999 is the paper's default for sparse and vision problems; 0.98 in the original Transformer (section 5.3); 0.95 in most LLM pretraining (DeepSeek-V3: β = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1, section 4.2; SmolLM3 the same, blog). A shorter memory reacts faster when gradient statistics shift, at the price of noisier v.
  • ε: 10−8 by default and 10−9 in the Transformer; it only matters where √v̂ is tiny, but there it caps the step, so a parameter with near-zero gradients moves at most η·m̂/ε.
  • Weight decay: with L2 in the loss, the penalty's gradient λθ goes through Adam's division by √v̂, so weights with large, noisy gradients are under-decayed and weights with tiny gradients are pulled to zero at full speed; AdamW's decoupled θ ← θ − ηλθ decays every weight alike. For plain SGD the two are the same up to a rescaling of λ by the learning rate (Loshchilov and Hutter, abstract). The parent page animates both on the same noise (thread 4); the regularisation side is on Regularisation. Because the decay per step is ηλ, it shrinks with the schedule.
A limit no diagonal method escapes
AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam rescale each coordinate. That helps exactly when the directions of very different curvature are the coordinates. Turn the same ravine 45° in the Optimiser race: AdaGrad and RMSProp, which reached a loss of 10−3 in 15 steps on the axis-aligned ravine, no longer reach it in 150, while momentum, Nesterov and plain SGD behave exactly as before. In a network, weight-space directions of high curvature are rarely single weights. That is the case for the next section.

4 · Preconditioning beyond the diagonal

  • Newton's method uses the Hessian H, the matrix of second derivatives: θ ← θ − H−1∇J. It undoes the curvature in every direction at once and converges in far fewer steps, but H has n² entries (O(n²) memory) and inverting it costs O(n³): for a network with 109 weights, impossible. Quasi-Newton methods (L-BFGS) build a low-rank approximation from recent gradient differences and Hessian-free methods use Hessian-vector products; both work for small, full-batch problems and break down with mini-batch noise. The practical descendants are the structured preconditioners below.
  • Full-matrix AdaGrad (Duchi et al. 2011) divides by the square root of the whole matrix Σ g g⊤ instead of its diagonal: rotation-invariant, but n² again.
  • Shampoo (Gupta, Koren and Singer 2018) exploits the fact that a weight is a matrix W (m × n). It keeps one m × m factor for the rows and one n × n factor for the columns, L ← L + G G⊤ and R ← R + G⊤G, and steps W ← W − η L−1/4 G R−1/4: a Kronecker-factored approximation of full-matrix AdaGrad at m² + n² memory instead of (mn)². For a tensor of order k each factor gets the exponent −1/(2k); for a vector (k = 1) that is −1/2, which is full-matrix AdaGrad (derived from the paper's section 4.2). The matrix roots are expensive, so practical versions recompute them only every few steps.
  • SOAP (Vyas et al. 2024) starts from the observation that Shampoo (with the 1/2 power) is Adafactor run in the eigenbasis of Shampoo's preconditioner, and replaces Adafactor with Adam: rotate the gradient into the eigenbases of L and R, run Adam there, rotate back. Its one hyperparameter beyond Adam's is how often the eigenbasis is refreshed. Reported: on 360M and 660M language models with 2M-token batches, at least 40% fewer iterations and at least 35% less wall-clock time than AdamW, and about 20% better than Shampoo on both.
  • Adafactor (Shazeer and Stern 2018) goes the other way, towards less memory: it keeps only the row and column sums of the squared gradients of each matrix and rebuilds a rank-1 estimate of v from them, "from O(nm) to O(n+m)". It was the optimiser of T5.

The rotated ravine in the Optimiser race shows the point in two dimensions: Shampoo reaches 10−3 in 24 steps on both the axis-aligned and the rotated valley, where AdaGrad and RMSProp only manage it on the first.

What the optimiser state costs

Optimiser state is often the largest item in training memory. In mixed precision with Adam, each parameter needs 2 bytes for the bf16 weight, 2 for its gradient, 4 for an fp32 master copy and 4 each for m and v: 16 bytes (ZeRO; 2 + 2 + 4 + 4 × 2 = 16, reproduced by construction). Optimiser states "use 33–75% of the total memory footprint during training" (Dettmers et al. 2021). The table recounts each optimiser's state for four real models from their config.json files.

Try: switch models. Muon's state is close to half of AdamW's, a little more on small models whose embedding is a bigger share (89% of OLMo 2 7B sits in hidden matrices, 99.8% of Kimi K2). SOAP's state on Kimi K2 is about nine times its parameter count: each of its 69,300 expert matrices (7,168 × 2,048) carries a 7,168 × 7,168 factor and a 2,048 × 2,048 one, each with its eigenbasis.

State values per parameter are counted per matrix: Adam 2mn, Muon mn on hidden matrices and 2mn on the embedding, output head and every 1-D parameter (norms, router biases), Adafactor m + n, SOAP 2mn plus, for each side d ≤ 10,000, a d × d factor and its d × d eigenbasis (max_precond_dim = 10000 and precondition_1d = False are soap.py's defaults; larger sides, such as a vocabulary, get no factor). Bytes: 4 per state value, 1 for 8-bit Adam. Training bytes per parameter = 2 (bf16 weight) + 2 (bf16 gradient) + 4 (fp32 master) + state bytes, before activations and before any ZeRO sharding. Parameter recounts: SmolLM3 3.08B, OLMo 2 7.30B, Llama 3.1 405.85B, Kimi K2 1,026.41B (within 3,416 parameters of Hugging Face's count; the K2 report's 1.04T is discussed on Moonshot AI: Kimi). Shampoo is not listed because its memory depends on the blocking an implementation chooses.

5 · Orthogonal and sign updates: Muon and Lion

Muon

Muon (Keller Jordan, 2024; "MomentUm Orthogonalized by Newton-Schulz") treats each hidden weight matrix as a matrix. For one m × n weight with gradient G:

  1. Update a momentum buffer B ← μB + (1 − μ)G with μ = 0.95, and form the Nesterov-style update U = (1 − μ)G + μB (the defaults of the reference code and of torch.optim.Muon).
  2. Normalise U to unit Frobenius norm, then apply five iterations of X ← aX + (bXX⊤ + c(XX⊤)²)X with (a, b, c) = (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315). This pushes every singular value of U towards 1 without computing an SVD: the result is approximately UV⊤ from U's singular value decomposition, the nearest semi-orthogonal matrix. The coefficients were tuned to push small singular values up as fast as possible, so the output's singular values land roughly between 0.5 and 1.5 rather than exactly at 1, which the authors found does not hurt.
  3. Step W ← W − η·s·X, where s rescales for the matrix's shape: √max(1, m/n) in Jordan's version; Moonshot's 0.2·√max(m, n), which matches the update's size to AdamW's typical RMS of 0.2 to 0.4 so AdamW's learning rate and decay can be reused (Liu et al. 2025, section 2.2). PyTorch offers both as adjust_lr_fn options.
  • Why: a gradient matrix is usually dominated by a few singular directions; orthogonalising gives every direction the gradient spans a comparable step, so rare directions are not drowned out. Bernstein derives it as steepest descent under the spectral norm. The parent page draws the singular values flattening over the five iterations on one real gradient.
  • Which parameters: only 2-D hidden matrices. Embeddings, the output head, norms and biases stay on AdamW; Jordan found using AdamW for the embedding and head important even though they are 2-D. GLM-4.5 runs Muon on "all parameters except word embedding, bias, and weights for RMSNorm" with 5 Newton-Schulz steps, momentum 0.95 and update RMS 0.2 (GLM-4.5, section 2.4).
  • Cost: one state buffer instead of AdamW's two (the memory table above), and a FLOP overhead of at most T·m/B for T iterations, model width m and batch B tokens: 5 × 768 / 524,288 = 0.7% for the speedrun and 5 × 16,384 / 16M = 0.5% for a Llama 405B-sized run (Jordan's arithmetic, reproduced). The real catch is that orthogonalisation needs the whole matrix while large runs shard it, which is why Moonshot published a distributed version built on ZeRO-1 (Liu et al. 2025).
  • History: it set a NanoGPT speedrun record on 15 October 2024 (training 35% faster) and the CIFAR-10 record (94% in 2.6 A100-seconds, from 3.3); the speedrun record by record is on Pretraining. Moonshot added weight decay and the shape scaling, reporting "~2x computational efficiency compared to AdamW with compute optimal training" (52% of the FLOPs) with the 16B Moonlight model; then MuonClip (Muon plus QK-Clip, which rescales a head's query and key projections whenever its largest attention logit exceeds τ = 100) trained the 1T-parameter Kimi K2 on 15.5T tokens "with zero loss spike" (Kimi K2 report; mechanism on Moonshot AI: Kimi).
  • Availability: torch.optim.Muon (docs; first in the PyTorch 2.9 documentation, inferred from the versioned docs) and optax.contrib.muon in JAX (docs). Note that PyTorch runs the Newton-Schulz iterations in bfloat16 and defaults to weight decay 0.1.

Lion

Lion ("EvoLved Sign Momentum", Chen et al. 2023) was found by a program search over update rules. Its step is the sign of an interpolation between the momentum and the current gradient, and the momentum is updated afterwards with a different rate:

θ ← θ − η·(sign(β1m + (1−β1)g) + λθ),   m ← β2m + (1−β2)g
β1, β2
0.9 and 0.99 by default
  • One buffer of state, and every parameter moves by exactly ±η per step, so the update's size is larger than Adam's: the paper recommends a learning rate 3 to 10 times smaller than AdamW's and a weight decay λ 3 to 10 times larger, to keep ηλ similar.
  • Reported gains: up to 2% ImageNet accuracy for ViT, up to 5× less pretraining compute on JFT, 2.3× less for diffusion; "similar or better" than Adam on language modelling. Its own limitations section: similar to AdamW on the large-scale language model and on C4 masked language modelling, gains shrink with strong augmentation, and "likely" no better than AdamW below batch size 64.
  • In the race, Lion's fixed-size steps zig-zag across the ravine and stall at a distance set by η unless the schedule decays: a constant-step method needs its schedule more than Adam does.

What to conclude
  • Most of a published speed-up can be the baseline. Wen et al. find that tuning a single hyperparameter (the learning rate) in the GPT-3 recipe gives up to 2× on a 100M model: the size of many published gains. Kaddour et al. find that a baseline whose learning rate is fully decayed erases the gains of Lion and Sophia.
  • Matrix-aware methods (Muon, SOAP, Shampoo) are the ones that survive tuning, at about 1.3× for small models and shrinking with scale in Wen et al.'s runs, and with a measured end-to-end win for Shampoo in AlgoPerf.
  • Muon's scale evidence is real but not controlled: Kimi K2 and GLM-4.5 trained with it, and Moonlight's scaling study is Moonshot's own, and neither the K2 nor the GLM-4.5 report includes a matched AdamW run at full scale. The two studies disagree mainly on batch size: Semenov et al. tune most at 0.1M and 0.02M-token batches, Wen et al. at 0.4M or more (as Wen et al. note).

7 · Schedules: how the base rate moves over the run

A schedule sets η over time; adaptive optimisers only rescale relative to it, so both are always used together. The logic: a high rate early to travel quickly towards some basin, a low rate late to settle at its bottom. With noisy gradients there is a second reason: at a constant rate the iterate never converges but hovers in a region whose size grows with η, so the final loss is set by how low the rate gets at the end (the Noisy ravine in the race shows the floor, and the decay going below it). Two questions separate every family: does the schedule need the total number of steps T in advance, and what shape is the decay.

Three endpoints, three ways

Try: you want a model at 250, 500 and 1,000 steps (say, to fit a scaling law, or because you do not yet know how long you can train). Play each method and watch the step counter: cosine needs one run per length; WSD branches cooldowns off one shared run; schedule-free reads all three off one run.

Toy (illustrative): the noisy quadratic model of Zhang et al. 2019, 10 coordinates with curvatures from 1 down to 0.01 (condition number 100), start at 1, gradient noise with covariance 0.09·H, plain SGD. The losses are exact expected values computed by recursion (no sampling); checks/nqm_check.py matches them with 4,000 seeded PyTorch runs (torch.optim.SGD and schedulefree.SGDScheduleFree), largest deviation 2.1 standard errors. Learning rates: the best of a log grid (10k/8), cosine re-tuned per length, the others tuned once for 1,000 steps. Grey line: the same SGD at its best constant rate, never decayed.

What the toy says, and what it does not. Re-tuned for each length, cosine reaches the lowest loss at every endpoint here, by 1.3 to 2.2 times; schedule-free and WSD, tuned once, come within 26% and 54% at 1,000 steps and are further behind at 250, where their single rate is too low; all three end 2.9 to 4.4 times below the never-decayed rate. A quadratic favours schedules that decay throughout. In LLM pretraining the evidence is different, and that is what this toy cannot show: tuned constant-plus-cooldown runs match cosine (next paragraph), and schedule-free gives "strong (but not optimal)" results there (Hägele et al., section 4.2). What the toy does show exactly is the cost structure: 1,750, 1,150 and 1,000 optimiser steps for the same three endpoints.

How the schedules compare

  • At a fixed, known budget, final quality is a near-tie. Cosine, WSD and 1−sqrt cooldowns land within noise of each other once T is known and each is tuned. "The constant learning rate with a short cooldown (< 20% of total training steps) can achieve the same final loss as cosine, and only outperforms for a longer fraction of cooldown steps", and it "scales predictably and reliably similar to cosine" (Hägele et al. 2024), which is why WSD spread so fast. Not everyone agrees: GLM-4.5 chose cosine after WSD underfitted on benchmarks, and Semenov et al. find that for most optimisers "the cosine scheduler dominates".
  • Cosine's real cost is optionality, not loss. Every checkpoint before the end of a cosine run sits at a rate that is still high, so you cannot stop early, extend the run, or reuse it at another length. Kaplan et al.'s scaling laws read such intermediate checkpoints, and Chinchilla corrected it with one schedule per training length: "overestimating the number of training steps beyond 25% leads to clear drops in performance" (Chinchilla, Appendix B). The parent page's schedule chart lets you move the end of the run.
  • Cooldown shape has a stable order: 1−sqrt "consistently performs better than linear decay" (Hägele et al., takeaway 2), and both beat convex shapes (exponential, EMA-like), which linger at a high rate and then collapse too fast.
  • Cooldown length: roughly 10 to 20% of the steps, with diminishing returns beyond. MiniCPM found "a decay of 10% of the total tokens is sufficient to achieve the best results, while a decay of 2.5% of total tokens falls short" (section 4.3); for long runs a shorter fraction can suffice if it is long enough in absolute steps (Hägele et al.).
  • The final rate matters, and 10% of the peak is a habit, not an optimum. Chinchilla decayed by 10× and found it "slightly more performant" than decaying to 0 (Appendix B); Llama 2 used 10%, Llama 3 405B 1%. Bergsma et al. 2025 find that linear decay to zero beats 10× decay at compute-optimal dataset sizes and beyond, and that the gap grows with tokens per parameter (a 610M model at 80 tokens per parameter with decay to zero beat the same model at 200 with 10× decay: about 60% less compute); Semenov et al. 2025 find decaying below 10% "significantly improves the results" but that the best final rate differs by schedule (in their runs a final rate of 1% of the peak beat decaying all the way to 0).
  • The cooldown is also a data lever: labs up-weight curated mathematics, code and instruction-like data while the rate falls, so the schedule and the data anneal are one decision (Pretraining; Data mixing).
  • Weight averaging substitutes for part of the decay. Averaging along the trajectory (SWA, EMA) improves checkpoints at no training cost: "While it does not match the cooldown, it reduces the gap without the need for a separate decay phase" (Hägele et al., takeaway 5). WSM turns this into a full replacement (section 9).
Correction
The old version of this page said that decaying nearer to zero "improves loss but can hurt some downstream benchmarks". No source for the downstream part was found, and the evidence points the other way: Bergsma et al. report that trends in validation loss also hold for downstream evaluations. The nearest benchmark finding, GLM-4.5's, is about the schedule's shape (WSD against cosine), not the final rate.

Finding the peak rate

The schedule's shape is less sensitive than its peak. The learning-rate range test (Smith 2015, section 3.3): train for a few epochs "while letting the learning rate increase linearly between low and high LR values", and read off where the loss starts to fall and where it turns noisy or rises: one run gives both a good rate and a good range, in Smith's words. For LLMs the peak is usually found by sweeps at small scale and transferred, either by fitted scaling rules or by parametrisations such as μP designed to keep the optimum fixed as width grows; Essential AI report μP transfer with Muon up to 3.7B parameters (2025).

Choosing one

  • LLM pretraining whose length may change or be extended: WSD, 1−sqrt cooldown over the last 10 to 20%.
  • LLM pretraining at a fixed, final budget: warmup then cosine, to well below 10% of the peak (or linear to zero, Bergsma et al.).
  • Supervised fine-tuning: linear or cosine to zero, 3 to 5% warmup (a convention; the Trainer's default warmup is 0).
  • A small supervised model with a cheap, steady validation metric: ReduceLROnPlateau or MultiStep.
  • No schedule at all: schedule-free, or WSM.

8 · "Annealing" and "learning-rate decay": three meanings

"Annealing" is used in three different ways in this literature, and they are not interchangeable.

SenseWhat it meansWhere you meet it
1. Classical, optimisationLowering the learning rate, full stop. Borrowed from simulated annealing, where a temperature is reduced so the search settles. Here annealing and decay are exact synonyms."cosine annealing", CosineAnnealingLR, eta_min; anything before 2023
2. Modern LLM pretrainingA training phase (the final 10 to 30% of tokens or so) defined by two simultaneous changes: the rate decays towards zero and the data mixture switches to curated high-quality tokens (mathematics, code, instruction-like, reasoning).Llama 3's annealing, OLMo 2's Dolmino mid-training mix, MiniCPM's WSD decay phase; "the annealed model"
3. Annealing as an experimentA cheap evaluation protocol: take a fixed mid-run checkpoint, anneal it on a candidate dataset, compare scores. Used to price a dataset, not to train a final model.Llama 3 and OLMo both evaluate candidate corpora this way
  • The two levers are separable, and papers usually pull both at once. WSD lets you decay the rate with no data change; you can also switch to a high-quality mixture while the rate stays flat (WSM switches the data at its Tswitch with the rate held constant). Any claim that "annealing gave +X" is a compound of two effects unless the ablation separated them. The Pretraining page separates them on OLMo 2's numbers and on a toy.
  • They work for different reasons that reinforce each other. A low rate means the model descends into a basin and stops moving much; low gradient noise near the end is also when scarce high-quality tokens stick best rather than being diluted across a 10T-token stream. That is why the data switch is scheduled to coincide with the decay, not because one requires the other.
  • Reading papers: "decay phase" or "cooldown" means the schedule alone; "anneal" or "annealed checkpoint" almost always implies the data switch too. WSM is a clean example of why the split matters: it removes sense 1 entirely (constant rate forever) while keeping sense 2 (the curated-data switch), and recovers the decay's benefit by merging.
  • Unrelated homonyms: simulated annealing proper (a combinatorial optimisation algorithm) and sampling temperature at inference (Sampling and Decoding) share the metaphor and nothing else. What goes into the anneal mixture: Data mixing.

9 · Decay-free: schedule-free optimisation and WSM

WSD removed the need to know T, but not the decay: you still choose when to start it, over how many tokens and with which curve, and extending training after the decay has begun means rolling back to the pre-decay checkpoint. Two methods remove the decay itself.

Schedule-free (Defazio et al. 2024)

yt = (1−β)zt + βxt,   zt+1 = zt − γ∇f(yt),   xt+1 = (1−ct+1)xt + ct+1zt+1
z
the plain SGD (or Adam) sequence, at a constant rate γ
x
a running average of z, with ct+1 = 1/(t+1) at a constant rate (weighted by γt² with warmup); this is the model you evaluate
y
the point where the gradient is taken, between the two; β ≈ 0.9 plays the role of momentum
  • Averaging iterates is old; the trick is taking the gradient at the interpolation y, which makes the average converge as fast as a well-tuned decay without knowing T (The Road Less Scheduled, section 2; NeurIPS 2024 oral). It won AlgoPerf's 2024 self-tuning track (section 6).
  • In practice (repository README): call optimizer.train() and optimizer.eval() wherever you call them on the model, and keep the optimiser in eval mode when saving checkpoints, because the weights stored during training are y, not x; warmup is still recommended; β = 0.9 by default, and 0.95 or 0.98 may be needed for very long runs; rates 10 to 50 times larger than classical ones for SGD, 1 to 10 times for AdamW.
  • Both variants are in the Optimiser race, checked against the schedulefree package; on the noisy ravine they go below the constant-rate floor with no schedule.

WSM: Warmup-Stable and Merge (Tian et al. 2025)

The rate warms up, then stays constant forever; checkpoints are saved periodically, and a weighted merge of the last n of them stands in for the annealed model. The link to decay is exact, not heuristic: merging checkpoints with weights cj is algebraically the same as applying per-step weights wi = Σj≥i cj to the updates after the base checkpoint, so any monotone decay curve can be inverted into merge weights (ck = wk, cj = wj − wj+1, c0 = 1 − w1). Mean averaging corresponds to linear decay, EMA to a convex decay, and cosine or 1−sqrt can be built directly. The framework is optimiser-agnostic and needs no change to the training loop.

  • Results (a 16.3B-parameter MoE with 1.4B active, 400B tokens branched off a 10.2T-token constant-rate checkpoint, with a switch to the high-quality annealing set): merge duration mattered far more than the checkpoint interval or the number of checkpoints merged, and EMA merging was the weakest, mirroring the convex-is-worse order above.
  • Practical read: the storage cost (one checkpoint per interval) is small next to a pretraining budget, and a merge is a cheap, repeatable proxy for "how good would this model be if I annealed now", with no throwaway decay runs to gauge progress.
Correction
The old page said WSM "beat a matched WSD decay by ~1.3 points on average". The paper's +1.28 points (62.67 to 63.95, average of 41 benchmarks) compares the best checkpoint of each run; when both stop at WSD's 400B tokens the gain is +1.18 (derived from Figure 3); each comparison is a single run, and the checkpoint was picked on the test benchmarks. Details, a live toy and the equivalence test are on the WSM paper page.

10 · Common mistakes

Copying a learning rate between momentum conventions, or between Adam and SGD
PyTorch's momentum sums gradients (v = βv + g); the averaged form (v = βv + (1 − β)g) needs a rate 10 times larger at β = 0.9 for the same path. Adam's step is about η per parameter whatever the gradient's size, SGD's is η times the gradient, so their rates are unrelated. Lion wants a rate 3 to 10 times smaller than AdamW's, schedule-free SGD 10 to 50 times larger than SGD's.
Using L2 regularisation with Adam and calling it weight decay
torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) adds λθ to the gradient, so it is divided by √v̂ with everything else; AdamW decays every weight alike. They are the same only for plain SGD.
Removing warmup because Adam already corrects its bias
Bias correction fixes the bias of the moment estimates, not their variance: the first step moves every parameter by exactly η, and the next ones rest on a handful of samples. Warmup (or RAdam's rectification) handles the variance.
Reading an intermediate cosine checkpoint as "the model at that size of run"
Before the end of a cosine schedule the rate is still high, so the checkpoint is worse than a run planned to stop there. Kaplan's scaling laws did this; Chinchilla corrected it. WSD branches or a WSM merge give honest intermediate models.
Evaluating a schedule-free model at the training weights
The weights held during training are the interpolation y; the model to evaluate and to save is the average x. Call optimizer.eval() first.
Applying Muon to every parameter
Muon is for 2-D hidden matrices. Embeddings, the output head, norms and biases go to AdamW (GLM-4.5 and Jordan both do this); a 1-D parameter has nothing to orthogonalise.
Believing a new optimiser's speed-up before the baseline is tuned
Tuning AdamW's learning rate alone can give up to 2× on a 100M model, and fully decaying the baseline's rate erased Lion's and Sophia's gains in re-evaluations. Compare against a tuned, fully decayed AdamW at the same batch size, at more than one scale (section 6).
Crediting "annealing" for a gain that came from the data switch (or the reverse)
The modern anneal lowers the rate and changes the data at once; unless an ablation separates the two, "+X from annealing" is a compound (section 8).
Assuming a diagonal adaptive method handles bad conditioning
AdaGrad, RMSProp and Adam rescale coordinates; when the steep directions are mixtures of coordinates they cannot help (the rotated ravine in the race). That is the case for Shampoo, SOAP and Muon.

Learning-rate failure modes in training curves (oscillation, NaN, plateaus, spikes) are diagnosed on the parent page's When training goes wrong tab.

Optimiser race: thirteen update rules on five surfaces

Each optimiser starts from the same point on the same two-parameter loss surface and takes the steps its published update rule prescribes. Nothing is simulated loosely: every rule is the line-by-line update of the implementation named under it, run in double precision, and checks/torch_ref.py reruns all 350 combinations (13 optimisers, 5 surfaces, 3 schedules, 2 learning rates) in PyTorch: they agree to 10−13, or, where a run is chaotic (Rosenbrock at large rates), to within what PyTorch itself moves when its start is nudged by 10−12.

The surfaces are toys with two parameters, chosen because each isolates one difficulty: a badly conditioned valley, the same valley rotated, a curved valley, a saddle, and noise. They teach the geometry of each rule; they do not rank optimisers for networks with billions of parameters (that evidence is in the Reading tab's The evidence section).

How each starting rate was chosen
Every optimiser starts at its own best rate for that surface: the best of a log grid (10k/4, from 10−4 to 10) by the average of log10(loss) over the run, among rates whose path never leaves the picture, with a constant schedule (the noisy surface averages seeds 1 to 5). Computed by checks/tune.mjs. The slider multiplies every optimiser's rate by the same factor, so you can see how sensitive each one is.

Shading and contour lines: log10 of the loss, one line per half decade. Ring: the start. Triangle: the minimum. Dot: where each optimiser is at the step shown.

What each surface shows

  • Ravine, axis-aligned (condition number 100). The textbook case for momentum: Polyak's heavy ball needs about √100 = 10 times fewer steps than gradient descent when its rate and momentum are tuned for the problem (Sutskever et al. 2013, section 2). With the common momentum 0.9 rather than the optimum for this valley, momentum reaches 10−3 in 89 steps and Nesterov in 24, while plain SGD has not after 150. Per-coordinate methods do best of all here, because the valley's axes are the coordinates.
  • Ravine, rotated 45°. The same valley; SGD, momentum and Nesterov behave identically (their updates are rotation-invariant), AdaGrad and RMSProp no longer reach 10−3 in 150 steps, and Shampoo, the one rotation-invariant preconditioner here, is unchanged. This is the whole case for non-diagonal preconditioning (Shampoo, SOAP), in two dimensions.
  • Rosenbrock. The classic curved valley (Rosenbrock 1960). Speed along a curving floor needs momentum that turns with it.
  • Saddle. The gradient across the ridge starts at about 0.001; anything that divides by the gradient's own scale leaves at once.
  • Noisy ravine. With noise, a constant rate does not converge: the iterate wanders in a region whose size grows with the rate. Over seeds 1 to 20, plain SGD's loss over its last 60 steps averages 0.103 at its tuned constant rate, and cosine decay of the same peak rate ends at 0.062 on average; Adam's floor is 0.031 and cosine brings it to 0.011 (checks/race_facts.mjs). That is the reason schedules decay.
What two dimensions cannot show
Muon is defined for weight matrices: it replaces the momentum matrix with the nearest semi-orthogonal one. A 1 × 2 matrix has one singular value, so here Muon reduces to normalised Nesterov momentum (a fixed-length step in the momentum's direction), which is why it hovers at a distance set by its rate unless the schedule decays. Its real effect, flattening the singular values of a 64 × 176 gradient, is drawn on Topic: ml-fundamentals, Reading tab, section 5 (Update). Similarly, SOAP and Shampoo here precondition a 2-vector with a 2 × 2 matrix; for a vector, Shampoo's exponent −1/(2k) with k = 1 gives −1/2, which is full-matrix AdaGrad (derived from Gupta et al. 2018, section 4.2). And SOAP skips its first step by design (its code builds the eigenbasis before using it).

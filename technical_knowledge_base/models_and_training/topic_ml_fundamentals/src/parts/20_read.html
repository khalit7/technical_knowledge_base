One training step, start to finish

Every network, from a 2012 image classifier to a 2026 language model, learns by repeating one step: take a batch of examples, push it forward through the layers, score the output with a loss, send the error backward to get a gradient for every weight, update the weights, keep them from fitting noise (regularise), and measure on data the step did not see. Every building block in this topic acts at exactly one of those seven moments, so this page follows one step and meets each block when it acts, with only the comparison needed there. The nine pages under this one own the details.

The toy: 16 layers of width 64; a batch of 32 random inputs (64 numbers each, standard normal) labelled into 10 classes by a fixed random linear "teacher"; a held-out batch of 256 from the same teacher. Classic: ReLU, He initialisation, BatchNorm, residual connections, SGD with momentum 0.9 at learning rate 0.1 and weight decay 10−4, as in ResNet (§3.4). 2026: pre-RMSNorm blocks with a SwiGLU feed-forward, AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at 1.0, as in Llama 2 (§2.2) and {{DeepSeek-V3|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}}; the toy's learning rate (3 × 10−3, with no warmup) is illustrative. 1 · Data and initialisation: what the step starts from

The batch. A step's gradient is computed on a mini-batch: not the whole training set (batch gradient descent: exact, but one slow, expensive step per pass over the data), not one example (stochastic gradient descent in the strict sense: cheap and fast, but so noisy it may never settle), but a few dozen examples to a few million tokens, which is efficient on hardware and only mildly noisy. Inputs are first put on a common scale, standardised to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, z = (x − μ)/σ, or min-max scaled to [0, 1], so that no feature dominates because of its units and gradient descent sees rounder loss contours; distance-based methods (kNN, SVM, k-means) need it most.

The starting weights. They are random, never constant: with equal weights, every neuron in a layer gets the same input and the same gradient and stays a copy of its neighbours (the symmetry problem). The scale of the randomness is the whole game. A layer with n inputs multiplies the variance of the signal by about n · Var(w), times a factor set by its activation, so Var(w) is chosen to make that product 1 in both directions; too small and the signal shrinks layer by layer (vanishing gradients), too large and it grows exponentially (exploding gradients).

2 · Forward: activations and normalisation

Each layer is a linear map followed by a nonlinearity, and in every modern network also a normalisation and a residual connection around it. The nonlinearity is what makes depth worth anything (without it the stack collapses to one linear map); it only has to be differentiable almost everywhere, so ReLU's kink at 0 is fine.

The rule is short: the ReLU family in hidden layers (SwiGLU in transformer feed-forward blocks, GELU, often tanh-approximated, in ViTs), and at the output whatever the task needs: a sigmoid per output for binary or multilabel, softmax for multiclass, nothing (linear) for regression. Squared ReLU, max(0, x)2, appears in some efficiency-focused models as a cheap alternative (Primer). The gated units borrow the LSTM's trick: two projections, one passed through a sigmoid-like function and multiplied into the other, so the slope is set per neuron by the input instead of being fixed. Their paper is candid about why they win: "we attribute their success, as all else, to divine benevolence" (Shazeer 2020).

Normalisation, compared

NormNormalisesTraining against inferenceWhere
BatchNorm (2015)each feature across the batch, then a learned γ, β per featurebatch statistics in training; running averages at inference, because one input has no batchCNNs; noisy with small batches, awkward for recurrent and variable-length inputs
LayerNorm (2016)each example across its featuresidenticalthe 2017 transformer
RMSNorm (2019)as LayerNorm, without the mean subtraction or β: x / RMS(x) · γidenticalessentially every current LLM (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek)

BatchNorm was motivated as a cure for "internal covariate shift"; later work credits a smoother loss landscape instead (Santurkar et al. 2018). RMSNorm shows that the rescaling, not the centring, is what matters, and saves compute at equal quality. Placement matters as much as the kind: the 2017 transformer normalised after the residual sum, LN(x + F(x)) ("post-norm", see {{Attention Is All You Need|n:3c65c17b0d0d81999af7f16f8ed8ee9e}}); GPT-2 onwards normalise the branch input, x + F(LN(x)) ("pre-norm"), which leaves the residual stream untouched and trains without the warmup sensitivity (Xiong et al. 2020); {{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}} normalises the branch output, x + LN(F(x)), for stability. An alternative to QK-norm is Moonshot's qk-clip, which leaves the forward pass alone and rescales a head's query and key weights after the optimiser step (step 5).

3 · Loss: one number to descend

The loss turns the network's output into the single number the step will push down. (Strictly, the loss is one example's error and the cost its mean over the batch; the words are used interchangeably.) Which loss follows from the target: a number gets a regression loss, a class gets cross entropy.

Regression lossPer exampleChoose it when
MSE, RMSE(y − ŷ)2; RMSE = √MSE, in the target's unitsthe default: smooth, and its gradient shrinks near the minimum; but squaring lets a few huge errors dominate
MAE|y − ŷ|outliers are real data: every error pulls with the same force (and the gradient does not soften near the minimum)
MAPE|y − ŷ| / |y|relative error matters; it has no value at y = 0 and blows up near it (scikit-learn)
Huberquadratic for |e| ≤ δ, linear beyondMSE near the target, MAE far from it, with δ tuned to the data
Log-coshlog cosh(y − ŷ)Huber's shape with a second derivative everywhere, which second-order methods such as XGBoost want

Classification is all cross entropy. Cross entropy is minus the log of the probability the model gave the right class, so minimising it is maximum likelihood. Its variants only reweight the terms: binary cross entropy (one sigmoid per output, for multilabel too), categorical (softmax over classes), sparse categorical (the same maths, fed the class index), label smoothing (the target puts a little probability on wrong classes, against overconfidence; Szegedy et al. 2016, Müller et al. 2019), class weights (up-weight a rare class) and focal loss, −(1 − p)γ log p, which down-weights easy examples so the hard ones dominate the gradient (Lin et al. 2017, built for dense object detection). The one classic outside the family is the hinge loss, max(0, 1 − y·f(x)) with y ∈ {−1, 1}, which is zero once the margin is met (the SVM's loss, in Beyond one network).

Try: slide the probability the model gives the right class (10 classes, the rest shared equally) and compare what each loss charges and how hard it pushes. The gradient is with respect to the right class's logit: the size of the push.

cross entropylabel smoothing, ε = 0.1focal

Exact formulas; the gradients match PyTorch autograd. Label smoothing uses Szegedy et al.'s target, 1 − ε + ε/K on the right class and ε/K on each other, so its loss is lowest at p = 0.91, not at 1; some texts put 1 − ε on the right class and ε/(K − 1) on the others, which moves that minimum to 0.9.

Check where the loss starts. A K-class classifier that knows nothing should score ln K: ln 10 = 2.303 for the toy. Its pre-norm network starts at 2.60 and the "init too small" one at 2.3025, but the BatchNorm residual network starts at 5.42, because its residual stream grows to a standard deviation of 2.47 by the last layer and nothing normalises it before the output (the step 2 animation shows the growth). A starting loss far from ln K is the first thing to check, in {{When training goes wrong|#t-debug}}.

Two losses here are not task losses. KL divergence measures how far one distribution is from another; it regularises VAEs, matches a student to a teacher, and anchors an RLHF policy to its reference (thread 1). The adversarial loss of a GAN is a minimax game between a generator and a discriminator ({{Topic: generative-and-multimodal|n:3c65c17b0d0d817ab6ade318917bff55}}). And L1 and L2 penalties are added to the loss but belong to regularisation.

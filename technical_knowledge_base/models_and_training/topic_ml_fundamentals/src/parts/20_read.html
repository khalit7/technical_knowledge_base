One training step, start to finish

Every network learns by repeating one step, and every building block in this topic is a choice made at exactly one of its seven moments; the choices come in matched sets because four threads tie the moments together. The step: take a batch, push it forward, score it with a loss, send the error backward for a gradient on every weight, update the weights, keep them from fitting noise (regularise), and measure on data the step did not see.

Fig. 1 runs one real step of a small network (16 layers of width 64, a batch of 32, 10 classes) through the seven moments, with the numbers each produces. Switch the recipe to run the same batch the classic way. Predict first: which recipe starts nearer the chance loss ln 10 = 2.30?

  1. 1 · Data, init. A batch of 32 inputs of 64 numbers arrives (the glass plate). The 541,312 weights are drawn with variance 1/fan-in, σ = 0.125 into each SwiGLU block, and each block's output matrix is scaled down a further 1/√16 = 0.25 (GPT-2's rule), σ = 0.019, so 16 residual additions do not inflate the stream. Section 1
  2. 2 · Forward. Each block: RMSNorm the stream, a SwiGLU feed-forward (64 → 176 → 64), add the result back. The norm sits on the branch, not on the stream, and the branches start small, so the spread of the stream (one bar per layer) only drifts from 1.04 to 1.20. Section 2
  3. 3 · Loss. Cross entropy, the batch mean of −log p(right class): 2.60, close to the chance level ln 10 = 2.30 (the glass sheet), since a final RMSNorm keeps the head's input at unit scale. Section 3
  4. 4 · Backward. Backpropagation hands each layer the gradient from above (one bar per layer, layer 16 back to layer 1). The global gradient norm is 5.50, and the recipe clips any norm above 1.0 down to 1.0, here by a factor of 0.182. First against last layer: 1.45 against 1.17. Section 4
  5. 5 · Update. AdamW: on the first step its bias-corrected averages are the gradient itself, so each weight moves by the learning rate, 3 × 10−3, times the sign of its gradient, whatever the gradient's size. The step changes the weights by 2.9% of their size. A real run would start this rate near zero (warmup) and end it with a cosine or WSD cooldown. Section 5
  6. 6 · Regularise. Decoupled decay: every weight shrinks by ηλ = 3 × 10−4 of itself, beside the gradient step, as AdamW prescribes; with Adam and L2 in the loss it would have been rescaled per weight (thread 4). Its size, 0.0227, is 1.0% of the update's (the green column against the purple one). No dropout. Section 6
  7. 7 · Measure. On the batch it trained on, the loss fell from 2.60 to 0.22 and accuracy rose from 9.4% to 100.0%; on 256 held-out examples (teal) the loss went from 2.77 to 2.71 and accuracy from 11.3% to 11.7%. One step memorised its batch and learned almost nothing general, which is why loss is measured on held-out data. Section 7
  8. Four threads. The arcs tie moments chosen together: cross entropy is both the loss and the measurement; keeping the variance constant ties initialisation to the forward pass; vanishing gradients run from the forward pass into the backward one; weight decay is a regulariser implemented in the update. Four threads
  1. 1 · Data, init. The same 32 inputs, standardised (spread 1.02). The 66,176 weights are drawn with He's variance 2/64, σ = 0.177, the scale that keeps a ReLU network's signal steady. Section 1
  2. 2 · Forward. Each layer: a 64 × 64 linear map, BatchNorm over the batch, add the layer's input, ReLU. Every residual addition adds a unit-variance branch, so the stream's spread grows from 0.84 after layer 1 to 2.47 after layer 16. Section 2
  3. 3 · Loss. Cross entropy: 5.42, far above the chance level ln 10 = 2.30 (the glass sheet), because the last layer's outputs are large (spread 2.47) and nothing normalises them before the head, so its random guesses are confident. Section 3
  4. 4 · Backward. The global gradient norm is 13.4, used as it is (no clipping). The first layer's gradient is 3.8 times the last layer's (4.41 against 1.17): the BatchNorm residual stack grows the gradient on the way down, as it grew the signal on the way up. Section 4
  5. 5 · Update. SGD with momentum 0.9: on the first step the momentum buffer is just the gradient, so every weight moves by 0.1 × its gradient, and the step changes the weights by 3.0% of their size. Over a real run the rate is divided by 10 when the error plateaus. Section 5
  6. 6 · Regularise. Weight decay as L2: 10−4 × w is added to every gradient before the step, so it moves with the optimiser (identical to decay for plain SGD). Its share of this step is tiny: 4.5 × 10−4, 0.03% of the update's size. Section 6
  7. 7 · Measure. On its own batch the loss fell from 5.42 to 2.20 and accuracy rose from 6.3% to 62.5%; on the 256 held-out examples the loss rose from 5.24 to 6.60 and accuracy fell from 9.8% to 8.6%. At this toy scale the step fitted its own batch and made held-out examples worse: measured where it trained, it looks like progress. Section 7
  8. Four threads. The same four arcs as in the 2026 recipe: cross entropy (loss and measurement), constant variance (initialisation and forward), vanishing gradients (forward into backward), weight decay (update and regularise). Four threads

New terms on this page (12)

Mini-batch: the few dozen examples (or few million tokens) one gradient is computed on. Loss, cost: one example's error, and its batch mean (used interchangeably). Residual connection: x + F(x), an identity path around a layer. Pre-norm, post-norm: normalising the branch input, x + F(LN(x)), against the sum, LN(x + F(x)). Vanishing, exploding gradient: a product of per-layer factors that shrinks to nothing or grows without bound. Gradient clipping: scale the gradient down when its norm exceeds a threshold. Adaptive optimiser: one step size per parameter. Schedule: how the base learning rate moves over the run; warmup its ramp at the start, cooldown its decay at the end. Decoupled weight decay: shrinking the weights in the update, not through the loss. Held-out set: data the model never trains on, kept to measure it. Chance loss: what a model that knows nothing scores, ln K for K classes.

1 · Data and initialisation: what the step starts from

The batch is a compromise: the whole training set gives an exact but slow step (batch gradient descent), one example a cheap but very noisy one (stochastic gradient descent in the strict sense), and a mini-batch is efficient on hardware and only mildly noisy. Inputs are put on a common scale first (standardised, z = (x − μ)/σ, or min-max to [0, 1]) so no feature dominates because of its units.

The starting weights are random, never constant (equal weights get equal gradients and stay copies of each other), and their scale is the whole game: a layer with n inputs multiplies the signal's variance by about n · Var(w) times a factor set by its activation, so Var(w) is chosen to make that product 1. Xavier's 2/(nin + nout) suits sigmoid and tanh, He's 2/nin the ReLU family, LeCun's 1/nin SELU; large LLMs use a small fixed scale ({{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}} σ = 0.02, {{DeepSeek-V3|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}} 0.006) and scale residual branches down by 1/√N (GPT-2). Because the right scheme depends on the activation, init is chosen with it: thread 2.

2 · Forward: activations and normalisation

Each layer is a linear map and a nonlinearity (without it the stack collapses to one linear map), and in every modern network also a normalisation and a residual connection. The activation rule is short: the ReLU family in hidden layers, the gated SwiGLU in transformer feed-forward blocks (a third matrix, so the hidden width is cut to ⅔ · 4d), and at the output whatever the target needs: a sigmoid per output for binary or multilabel, softmax for classes, nothing for regression. The norms differ by axis: BatchNorm normalises each feature across the batch (CNNs; it needs running averages at inference), LayerNorm and RMSNorm each example across its features (transformers; RMSNorm drops the mean and is in essentially every current LLM). Where the norm sits, before the branch (pre-norm, GPT-2 onwards) or after the sum (post-norm, 2017), is {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}}'s subject.

3 · Loss: one number to descend

The loss follows from the target. A number gets a regression loss: MSE by default (it estimates the mean), MAE (the median) or Huber when outliers are real. A class gets cross entropy, minus the log of the probability given to the right class, so minimising it is maximum likelihood; label smoothing, class weights and focal loss only reweight its terms, and the hinge loss is the margin-based outsider (the SVM's). An order gets a ranking loss, a distribution KL divergence (VAEs, distillation, the RLHF anchor), and a GAN an adversarial game. L1 and L2 penalties are added to the loss but belong to regularisation.

Fig. 3: slide the probability the model gives the right class (10 classes, the rest shared equally) and compare what each loss charges and how hard it pushes. The gradient is with respect to the right class's logit: the size of the push. Predict: where does label smoothing's loss bottom out?

Check where the loss starts. A K-class classifier that knows nothing should score ln K: ln 10 = 2.303 for the toy. Its pre-norm network starts at 2.60 and the "init too small" one at 2.3025, but the BatchNorm residual network starts at 5.42, because its residual stream grows to a standard deviation of 2.47 by the last layer and nothing normalises it before the output (Fig. 2 shows the growth). A starting loss far from ln K is the first thing to check in {{When training goes wrong|#t-debug}}.

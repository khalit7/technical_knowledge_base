One training step, start to finish

Every network learns by repeating one step, and every building block in this topic is a choice made at exactly one of its seven moments; the choices come in matched sets because four threads tie the moments together. The step: take a batch, push it forward, score it with a loss, send the error backward for a gradient on every weight, update the weights, keep them from fitting noise (regularise), and measure on data the step did not see.

The toy network and the two recipes, sourced

The toy: 16 layers of width 64; a batch of 32 random inputs (64 numbers each, standard normal) labelled into 10 classes by a fixed random linear "teacher"; a held-out batch of 256 from the same teacher. New terms on this page (12)

Mini-batch: the few dozen examples (or few million tokens) one gradient is computed on. Loss, cost: one example's error, and its batch mean (used interchangeably). Residual connection: x + F(x), an identity path around a layer. Pre-norm, post-norm: normalising the branch input, x + F(LN(x)), against the sum, LN(x + F(x)). Vanishing, exploding gradient: a product of per-layer factors that shrinks to nothing or grows without bound. Gradient clipping: scale the gradient down when its norm exceeds a threshold. Adaptive optimiser: one step size per parameter. Schedule: how the base learning rate moves over the run; warmup its ramp at the start, cooldown its decay at the end. Decoupled weight decay: shrinking the weights in the update, not through the loss. Held-out set: data the model never trains on, kept to measure it. Chance loss: what a model that knows nothing scores, ln K for K classes.

1 · Data and initialisation: what the step starts from

The batch is a compromise: the whole training set gives an exact but slow step (batch gradient descent), one example a cheap but very noisy one (stochastic gradient descent in the strict sense), and a mini-batch is efficient on hardware and only mildly noisy. Inputs are put on a common scale first (standardised, z = (x − μ)/σ, or min-max to [0, 1]) so no feature dominates because of its units.

The starting weights are random, never constant (equal weights get equal gradients and stay copies of each other), and their scale is the whole game: a layer with n inputs multiplies the signal's variance by about n · Var(w) times a factor set by its activation, so Var(w) is chosen to make that product 1. Xavier's 2/(nin + nout) suits sigmoid and tanh, He's 2/nin the ReLU family, LeCun's 1/nin SELU; large LLMs use a small fixed scale ({{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}} σ = 0.02, {{DeepSeek-V3|n:3c65c17b0d0d815fb8dac9ba1e35ab81}} 0.006) and scale residual branches down by 1/√N (GPT-2). Because the right scheme depends on the activation, init is chosen with it: thread 2.

2 · Forward: activations and normalisation

Each layer is a linear map and a nonlinearity (without it the stack collapses to one linear map), and in every modern network also a normalisation and a residual connection. The activation rule is short: the ReLU family in hidden layers, the gated SwiGLU in transformer feed-forward blocks (a third matrix, so the hidden width is cut to ⅔ · 4d), and at the output whatever the target needs: a sigmoid per output for binary or multilabel, softmax for classes, nothing for regression. The norms differ by axis: BatchNorm normalises each feature across the batch (CNNs; it needs running averages at inference), LayerNorm and RMSNorm each example across its features (transformers; RMSNorm drops the mean and is in essentially every current LLM). Where the norm sits, before the branch (pre-norm, GPT-2 onwards) or after the sum (post-norm, 2017), is {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}}'s subject.

3 · Loss: one number to descend

The loss follows from the target. A number gets a regression loss: MSE by default (it estimates the mean), MAE (the median) or Huber when outliers are real. A class gets cross entropy, minus the log of the probability given to the right class, so minimising it is maximum likelihood; label smoothing, class weights and focal loss only reweight its terms, and the hinge loss is the margin-based outsider (the SVM's). An order gets a ranking loss, a distribution KL divergence (VAEs, distillation, the RLHF anchor), and a GAN an adversarial game. L1 and L2 penalties are added to the loss but belong to regularisation.

Try: slide the probability the model gives the right class (10 classes, the rest shared equally) and compare what each loss charges and how hard it pushes. The gradient is with respect to the right class's logit: the size of the push. Predict: where does label smoothing's loss bottom out?

cross entropylabel smoothing, ε = 0.1focal

Exact formulas; the gradients match PyTorch autograd. Label smoothing uses Szegedy et al.'s target, 1 − ε + ε/K on the right class and ε/K on each other, so its loss is lowest at p = 0.91, not at 1; some texts put 1 − ε on the right class and ε/(K − 1) on the others, which moves that minimum to 0.9. Focal loss, −(1 − p)γ log p, down-weights the easy examples (Lin et al. 2017).

Check where the loss starts. A K-class classifier that knows nothing should score ln K: ln 10 = 2.303 for the toy. Its pre-norm network starts at 2.60 and the "init too small" one at 2.3025, but the BatchNorm residual network starts at 5.42, because its residual stream grows to a standard deviation of 2.47 by the last layer and nothing normalises it before the output (the step 2 animation shows the growth). A starting loss far from ln K is the first thing to check in {{When training goes wrong|#t-debug}}.

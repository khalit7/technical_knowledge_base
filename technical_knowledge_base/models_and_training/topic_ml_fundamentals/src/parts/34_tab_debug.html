When training goes wrong

A training curve that looks wrong usually has one of a handful of causes, and each cause lives in one band of the training step: the data, the initialisation, the activation, normalisation, the loss, the optimiser and its learning rate, the schedule, regularisation, or the metric. Pick what you see; the causes are listed most likely first, each with the check that confirms it, the fix, and the page that owns that band.

Every curve on this tab is a real run recorded offline for it (small networks on scikit-learn's 8x8 handwritten digits, and a two-layer character Transformer on Tiny Shakespeare), bug and fix with the same seed and the same batches; the scripts and logs are in src/debug/. The order of causes is a judgement from the sources cited (Karpathy's recipe, Google's Tuning Playbook) and these runs: no study counts how often each cause is the culprit.

Before debugging: the recipe

Andrej Karpathy's {{A Recipe for Training Neural Networks|https://karpathy.github.io/2019/04/25/recipe/}} (2019) starts from two observations: neural network training is a leaky abstraction, and it fails silently ("Everything could be correct syntactically, but the whole thing isn't arranged properly, and it's really hard to tell"). Its answer is to build up from checks that cannot fail silently. The standing checklist:

  1. Inspect the data by hand before training, and again immediately before y_hat = model(x), after the whole pipeline.
  2. Overfit a single batch (as few as two examples) to about 0 loss. If you cannot, the bug is in the code, not the hyperparameters.
  3. Establish a dumb baseline and verify the loss at initialisation matches theory: -log(1/K) for K-class cross-entropy. Also an input-independent baseline (all inputs set to 0) that the real model must beat.
  4. Change one thing at a time; log gradient norms, weight norms and the learning rate actually applied.

Other items of the recipe: fix the random seed; "init well" (for a 1:10 imbalanced dataset, set the output bias so the network predicts 0.1 at initialisation); use backpropagation to chart dependencies (make the loss the sum of example i's outputs and check that only input i gets a gradient); Adam at 3e-4 is a safe first optimiser; add complexity one signal at a time; do not trust learning-rate decay defaults; and to regularise, first add more real data.

Loss spikes at scale

The failure that only large runs used to show: after a long healthy stretch the loss jumps, sometimes recovers, sometimes diverges. Five sources, each linked:

PaLM (2022): roll back and skip

Training the 540B model, Google "observed spikes in the loss roughly 20 times during training, despite the fact that gradient clipping was enabled", at irregular intervals, sometimes late, never in the smaller models. The fix: restart from a checkpoint about 100 steps before the spike and skip roughly 200 to 500 data batches. Replaying those batches from an earlier checkpoint did not spike, so the cause was "the combination of specific data batches with a particular model parameter state", not bad data alone. PaLM also added z-loss, 10-4 · log2 Z, to keep the softmax normaliser near 1, and clipped the global gradient norm at 1.0 ({{PaLM §5, §5.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02311}}).

OLMo 2 (2024): measure spikes, then remove them

Ai2 defined a spike score: "the percentage of values in a time series that are at least seven standard deviations away from a rolling average of the last 1,000 values", on the loss and the gradient norm. Each fix came with a before and after: normal initialisation N(0, 0.02) took the gradient-norm spike score from 0.40 to 0.03; reordered norm plus QK-norm from 0.108 to 0.069 (neither helped alone); no weight decay on embeddings from 0.16 to 0.092; plus z-loss, AdamW ε = 10-8 instead of 10-5, and a filter for repeated n-grams. The {{OLMo 2 paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}} replays every ablation from the paper's vector figures with the spike score live; the {{Ai2 OLMo page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e08697df661f83c3ac}} does it on the released training logs. (The z-loss weight is printed as 10-5 in OLMo 2's Table 1 and 10-4 in its §3.3.3 formula.)

Wortsman et al. (2023): small-scale proxies

{{Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities|https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.14322}} showed that two instabilities seen at scale, attention logits that grow without bound (Dehghani et al. 2023) and output logits that diverge from the log-probabilities (PaLM), "also appear in small models when training at high learning rates", and that QK-norm and z-loss are "equally effective in this regime". That makes spikes debuggable on a laptop; the toy runs below do exactly that.

Llama 3 (2024): stable loss, unstable machines

Meta called its 405B recipe "very stable: we observed few loss spikes and did not require interventions to correct for model training divergence" (§3.4.1). Its interruptions were hardware: 466 in a 54-day snapshot, 419 unexpected; that story is on {{Training Infrastructure|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c9b2cac997583bf572}} and the {{Llama 3 Herd paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81aca58ccb9d720b474e}}. One numerical instability did appear: gradient accumulation in bf16 once the image encoder was added, fixed by accumulating in FP32 (§7.3).

Google's Tuning Playbook: a procedure

The {{Deep Learning Tuning Playbook|https://github.com/google-research/tuning_playbook}} separates instability at initialisation from sudden instability mid-training, and finds it with a learning-rate sweep (plot the loss for rates just above the best). Its fixes, in order: warmup (best for early instability; sweep warmup over orders of magnitude, at most 10% of training), gradient clipping (threshold near the 90th percentile of logged norms; more than 50% of steps clipped means lower the rate instead), a different optimiser, architecture best practice (residuals, normalisation inside the residual, x + f(Norm(x))), and lowering the rate only as a last resort.

Spike score, live

OLMo 2's spike score run on the toy's per-step logs at peak rate 1e-1, the rate where the sweep shows the damage (at 3e-2 the logits ran away too, but the loss never spiked and the gradient norm stayed below 1, so clipping at 1.0 never acted). The paper uses a 1,000-value window on runs of hundreds of thousands of steps; these runs have 1,500, so the window defaults to 100. Note what the gradient norm separates and the final loss alone would not: at the default window and threshold the gradient norm spikes 8 times with no fix, 13 with z-loss alone (the attention logits, not the output logits, are what run away here), 11 with clipping (which still lowers the loss spike, 3.84 against 4.82, and the final loss), and 0 with QK-norm, alone or with z-loss.

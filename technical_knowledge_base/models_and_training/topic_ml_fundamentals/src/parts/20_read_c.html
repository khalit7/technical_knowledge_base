Four threads that cross the stages

Followed one stage at a time, the components look like separate menus. Four threads run through several stages at once, and they are the reason the choices come in matched sets.

1 · Everything is cross entropy

Entropy H(P) = −Σ P log P is the average cost of encoding samples from P with P's own best code; cross entropy H(P, Q) = −Σ P log Q is the cost with a code built for Q; their difference is the KL divergence, DKL(P‖Q) = H(P, Q) − H(P) = Σ P log(P/Q), the extra paid for using the wrong code. KL is asymmetric and breaks the triangle inequality, so it is distance-like, not a distance. Since H(P) does not depend on the model, minimising cross entropy and minimising KL are the same thing, and that one fact runs through four stages:

  • The loss (step 3): with a one-hot label H(P) = 0, so cross entropy = KL = the negative log-likelihood of the right class. Label smoothing and distillation make P soft, so H(P) > 0 sets a floor the loss cannot go below; distillation's choice of forward or reverse KL decides where a small student is allowed to be wrong ({{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}).
  • The anchor: the same KL keeps a VAE's latent code near its prior and an RLHF policy near its reference model ({{Topic: llm-training-and-post-training|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b6876ee72b7056793b}}, Axis 4).
  • Contrastive learning: InfoNCE is cross entropy over a batch, a classification of the one matching pair among N candidates (below).
  • The measurement (step 7): perplexity is exp(cross entropy) per token, and a log-loss on held-out data is the same number the step minimised, now read as a score.

Try: pick a target, then slide how much probability the model puts on the right answer. The bar is cross entropy split into the part no model can remove (H(P)) and the part training removes (KL).

2 · Keep the variance constant: the pact between initialisation, activation and normalisation

Initialisation (step 1) and normalisation (step 2) chase one invariant: activation and gradient variance roughly constant from layer to layer, at step zero and for the rest of training. Initialisation sets it for step zero, and only for the activation it was derived for; normalisation re-imposes it at every step, which is why deep normalised networks are far less sensitive to the initial scale. So the three are chosen as a set:

3 · Vanishing gradients, seen four ways

One failure shows up in four stages, and each stage has its own fix. In every case the gradient reaching an early layer is a product of per-layer (or per-time-step) factors:

  1. Activation saturation (step 2): a sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so each layer shrinks the gradient by at least 4. The fix is the ReLU family.
  2. Initialisation scale (step 1): a variance below the activation's balance point shrinks the signal at every layer, above it grows it. The fix is the matching scheme.
  3. Normalisation and placement (step 2): with the norm after the residual sum, every layer rescales the path the gradient travels; with it before the branch, the residual stream is an identity path.
  4. The product of Jacobians through time (backpropagation through time): a recurrent network applies the same weights at every step, so the gradient from step T back to step T − k multiplies k copies of the same factor. The LSTM's fix is its cell state, updated by addition, Ct = ft ⊙ Ct−1 + it ⊙ C̃t: along it the gradient is multiplied only by the forget gate, which the network can hold near 1. It is a skip connection through time.

Try: the fourth way, on one 60-step input sequence. Move the recurrent weight: the tanh network's gradient vanishes for every value, small weights shrinking it directly and large ones saturating the tanh, whose slope then shrinks it. The LSTM's cell path keeps its gradient alive while the forget gate stays near 1; lower its bias and it vanishes too.

tanh RNN, gradient on its state k steps backLSTM, gradient along the cell state

A scalar tanh RNN, at = tanh(w at−1 + xt), whose factor per step is w(1 − at2); and an LSTM cell path whose factor is the forget gate ft = σ(bf + xt), on the same seeded inputs xt ~ N(0, 0.52) illustrative; the products are exact. Exploding gradients need the factor to stay above 1, which a saturating unit resists; they appear in larger networks and are handled by clipping (step 4). Initialising the forget bias to 1 or more is a standard recommendation (Jozefowicz et al. 2015).

All four are why "fix vanishing gradients" never has one answer, and why a symptom in the loss curve has to be traced back through every stage ({{When training goes wrong|#t-debug}}).

4 · Weight decay: a regulariser by intent, an optimiser property by implementation

Weight decay is meant as a regulariser (step 6): pull every weight towards zero a little at each step. It can be implemented two ways. As L2 in the loss, λ/2 · ‖w‖2 adds λw to the gradient, which then goes through the optimiser. As decoupled decay, the update itself shrinks the weights, w ← w − ηλw, beside the gradient step. For plain SGD the two are identical. For Adam they are not: the L2 term is divided by √v̂ like every other gradient component, so weights whose gradients are large and noisy are barely decayed, while weights with tiny gradients are pulled to zero at full speed. Decoupling restores the same decay for every weight, and that is the whole of AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter 2017).

Try: six weights start at 1 and receive pure noise as their gradient, from tiny to large; the only thing that should move them on average is the decay. Play both optimisers on the same noise.

Gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost) were long the strong default on tabular data, generally ahead of deep networks there (Grinsztajn et al. 2022), though tabular foundation models now report beating tuned boosted trees on datasets of up to 10,000 samples reported (TabPFN v2, Nature 2025). On the TabArena leaderboard (2 October 2026) tabular foundation models hold the top places on its 51 datasets, while at 100k to 1M rows tuned boosted trees and RealMLP sit within overlapping intervals and boosted trees remain the best family on CPU; the evidence is on {{Classical ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ffb886ff30176bc6ce}}. Bagging (random forests: trees on bootstrap samples with random feature subsets, out-of-bag error for free) cuts variance; boosting (each tree fits the gradient of the loss, the residuals for MSE) cuts bias. Its machinery keeps resurfacing in deep learning: the hinge loss and the kernel trick of SVMs (a maximum-margin classifier whose dot products are replaced by a kernel, so only the support vectors matter; strong up to about 105 samples); nearest neighbours as the ancestor of vector retrieval ({{Topic: rag-and-retrieval|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b89145c37dfe8a3b0b}}); and boosting's second-order step, which is why XGBoost wants a loss with a usable second derivative (log-cosh rather than Huber, step 3). For tuning, random search beats grid search at equal budget, because the few hyperparameters that matter each get many distinct values (Bergstra and Bengio 2012); Bayesian optimisation (Optuna, Hyperopt) suits expensive trials, and successive halving (Hyperband, ASHA) suits deep learning, where a partly trained run already says something.

Contrastive and self-supervised learning: the objective makes the embedding model

Self-supervised learning takes its labels from the data itself; contrastive learning does it by pairing: two augmentations of one image, an image and its caption. The workhorse loss, InfoNCE, is cross entropy again (thread 1): for each item, a softmax over its similarity (cosine, divided by a temperature τ) to the one positive and to the N − 1 other items in the batch (van den Oord et al. 2018), and it maximises a lower bound on the mutual information between the views. A model that knows nothing scores ln N: ln 32,768 = 10.4 for CLIP's batch derived ({{CLIP|n:3c65c17b0d0d8194a85dfc5f3bf3f799}}, whose page runs a tiny CLIP and its similarity matrix live).

The trend: joint-embedding methods (contrastive, self-distillation, JEPA) against masked generative ones (MAE), with prediction in latent space increasingly favoured for semantic features. And the architecture follows the objective: a contrastive objective wants one vector per input, so it produces encoders (CLIP's towers, sentence embedders, rerankers); a generative objective must emit sequences, so it needs a decoder. {{BERT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e5ad9bd09cbf18ad7c}} sits between, an encoder trained by masked-token prediction, whose raw embeddings are poor for similarity until contrastively fine-tuned (SimCSE). In 2026 contrastive training is the standard recipe that turns any backbone, increasingly a decoder-only LLM with pooling (E5-Mistral, NV-Embed, Qwen3-Embedding), into an embedding model: the objective, not the backbone, makes it one.

Correction
The old page said batch size matters in contrastive learning "so much there and nowhere else in this topic". It matters elsewhere too: BatchNorm's statistics get noisy with small batches (step 2), and batch size sets the gradient noise of every optimiser. What is special to InfoNCE is that the batch is the set of negatives, so its size changes the task itself (chance is ln N), which is why MoCo built a queue.

When it goes wrong

Every training failure lands on one of the seven stages, which is why debugging reads as the step run backwards from the symptom: a loss that is flat from the start points at the update or the backward pass (a rate off by 10, frozen layers, a loss that does not match the output activation), a loss that turns to not-a-number at numerics in the loss, oscillation at the learning rate or batch size, a plateau at vanishing gradients or dead ReLUs, training loss falling while validation loss rises at regularisation, good accuracy that is useless on the minority class at the metric. The symptom-to-cause-to-fix tables (fit problems, gradient pathologies, loss-curve shapes, imbalanced data) and the standing checklist are in {{When training goes wrong|#t-debug}}; the {{Training lab|#t-lab}} lets you cause each failure on purpose.

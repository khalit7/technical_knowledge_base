Normalisation and initialisation

How inputs are put on a common scale (feature scaling), how layers inside a network re-impose a fixed scale at every step (BatchNorm, LayerNorm, RMSNorm, GroupNorm, InstanceNorm, QK-norm) and where in the block they sit, and how the starting weights are scaled so the signal neither vanishes nor explodes (Xavier, He, LeCun, residual scaling, μP). Every normalisation on this page is computed exactly in your browser and checked against torch.nn; the depth figures are measured in six released models. In one screen

Training works best when the numbers flowing through a network stay on a scale of about 1: features on comparable scales, activations whose variance does not grow or shrink from layer to layer, gradients that reach the first layer at a usable size. Three tools enforce that at three moments. Feature scaling fixes the inputs once, before training. Initialisation chooses the scale of the random starting weights so the signal is preserved at step zero. Normalisation layers re-impose a fixed scale inside the network at every step, which is why a normalised network is far less sensitive to its initial scale.

1 · Feature scaling: the inputs, once

Why. A model that measures distance (k-nearest neighbours, SVMs with an RBF kernel, k-means), projects variance (PCA) or penalises weights uniformly (ridge, lasso) treats a unit of every feature the same, so a feature measured in thousands silently outvotes one measured in fractions. Gradient descent suffers too: unequal scales stretch the loss surface into a narrow ravine, so the step size that is safe along the steep feature is tiny along the flat one, and convergence slows (rounder contours after scaling). Tree models split one feature at a time on thresholds and do not care.

MethodFormulaResultNote
Standardisation (z-score)z = (x − μ) / σmean 0, standard deviation 1the default; scikit-learn's StandardScaler uses the population σ (no n − 1)
Min-max normalisationx′ = (x − xmin) / (xmax − xmin)range [0, 1]one outlier squeezes every other value into a sliver
Robust scaling(x − median) / IQRmedian 0, interquartile range 1for features with outliers (RobustScaler)

The statistics (μ, σ, minimum, maximum) are fitted on the training set only and then applied unchanged to validation and test data; fitting them on all the data leaks the test set into training (scikit-learn, common pitfalls).

2 · One recipe, five choices of axes

Every normalisation layer does the same three things to a tensor of activations: pick a set of numbers, compute their mean μ and variance σ², and replace each number x in the set with

y = γ · (x − μ) / √(σ² + ε) + β
μ, σ²
mean and (biased, divide-by-n) variance over the set; RMSNorm skips μ and uses the mean square, so y = γ · x / √(mean(x²) + ε)
ε
a small constant against division by zero (10−5 by default in PyTorch; LLM configs use 10−5 or 10−6)
γ, β
learned per feature or channel, initialised to 1 and 0, so the layer can undo the normalisation if that helps; without them, normalising would also limit what a layer can represent (Ioffe and Szegedy 2015, §3)

The kinds differ only in which numbers share one μ and σ, and that choice decides everything else: whether an example's output depends on the other examples in its batch, whether training and inference compute the same thing, and what the layer can be used for. The figure that made this clear is Figure 2 of the GroupNorm paper (Wu and He 2018); the version below is live, on real numbers.

Reading the picture. For images, BatchNorm shares statistics across the N examples and the H × W pixels of one channel; LayerNorm across all channels and pixels of one example; InstanceNorm across the pixels of one channel of one example; GroupNorm across a group of channels of one example (with one group it is LayerNorm, with one channel per group it is InstanceNorm). For token sequences, BatchNorm would average one feature over every token of every sequence in the batch, padding included, and the statistics would change with sequence length; LayerNorm and RMSNorm average each token over its own D features, so a token's output depends on nothing else. That independence is why transformers use them.

3 · BatchNorm: z-scores inside the network, and the batch it cannot do without

BatchNorm is feature scaling applied per layer, inside the network, over the batch dimension. In training, for every layer and every feature (channel):

  1. Compute the batch mean μB and variance σB² of the feature across the batch (and, in a CNN, across the pixels).
  2. Normalise: x̂ = (x − μB) / √(σB² + ε).
  3. Re-scale and re-shift with learned parameters: y = γx̂ + β, one γ and one β per feature, so num_features of each per layer.
  4. Update running averages of μ and σ² with a momentum hyperparameter, for later use.

At inference a single input has no batch, so the layer uses the running averages instead. Exact statistics of the whole training set would be ideal (the original paper averages the mini-batch statistics over the training data once training is done, §3.1, Algorithm 2), but they change every time the weights do, so frameworks keep a moving average during training instead. With frozen statistics the layer becomes a fixed affine map, which can be folded into the preceding convolution or linear layer at deployment.

Two framework details that trip people up, both from the documentation:

  • The two "momentum" conventions are opposite. PyTorch: running = (1 − momentum) · running + momentum · batch, default momentum 0.1, and its docs warn this "is different from one used in optimizer classes" (BatchNorm1d). Keras: moving = moving · momentum + batch · (1 − momentum), default momentum 0.99 and ε = 10−3 (BatchNormalization). Both mean "keep 90% (or 99%) of the old estimate"; porting 0.1 from PyTorch to Keras keeps only 10%.
  • Two variances. PyTorch normalises with the biased batch variance (divide by n) but stores the unbiased one (divide by n − 1) in the running average.

Placement. The original paper adds BN "immediately before the nonlinearity", normalising x = Wu, and drops the layer's bias because β replaces it (§3.2); after the activation also appears in practice. Its headline: on ImageNet, BN reached the same accuracy "with 14 times fewer training steps" and an ensemble reached 4.9% top-5 validation error reported.

What the animation shows. With a batch of 2, each feature has two values, so after subtracting their mean they are ±d/2 and their biased standard deviation is d/2: every training output is ±1 (up to ε) derived, whatever the inputs. The layer passes on only which of the two values was larger, and at inference, with running statistics, it suddenly outputs a continuous range the next layer never saw in training. With a shifted test distribution (a recalibrated sensor, a new camera), the frozen running statistics no longer centre the data, while LayerNorm re-centres each example on its own.

Correction: why BatchNorm works
The original paper's explanation is that BN reduces "internal covariate shift", the change in each layer's input distribution as earlier layers train. Santurkar et al. 2018 showed that "such distributional stability of layer inputs has little to do with the success of BatchNorm" and that BN instead "makes the optimization landscape significantly smoother", so gradients are more predictive and larger steps are safe. The old page's table kept the original claim as an advantage; it is kept above with this correction.

Fixes for small batches keep BatchNorm's form and change where the statistics come from: Batch Renormalization corrects the batch statistics towards the running ones (Ioffe 2017); SyncBatchNorm pools them across devices; GroupNorm abandons the batch axis altogether, which is why detection and segmentation models, trained with one or two images per GPU, moved to it.

4 · LayerNorm and RMSNorm: the same idea on the other axis

LayerNorm normalises across the feature dimension, per data point, so every example (every token, in a transformer) is normalised independently, with one γ, β vector per layer (Ba, Kiros and Hinton 2016). It fixes all three BatchNorm weaknesses at once: it does not depend on batch size, it works for RNNs and transformers with variable-length inputs, and it computes the same thing at training and inference, with no running averages. What it gives up is small: the mild regularising noise of batch statistics, and a little compute at inference, because the statistics are computed per token every time instead of being folded into the weights. It was the 2017 transformer's norm, and pre-norm placement (section 5) made it the transformer default.

RMSNorm(x) = x / RMS(x) · γ,   RMS(x) = √( (1/d) Σ xi² + ε )
vs LayerNorm
no mean subtraction, no β: half the reductions and one fewer parameter vector

RMSNorm (Zhang and Sennrich 2019) is LayerNorm without the re-centring. Its argument is that LayerNorm's benefit comes from re-scaling invariance (the output does not change when the input is multiplied by a constant), not from re-centring, and it "achieves comparable performance against LayerNorm but reduces the running time by 7%~64% on different models" reported; its abstract singles out RNNs as the networks LayerNorm's overhead "significantly slows". It is the default in essentially every open LLM since Llama: Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek, Gemma and OLMo 2 all use it (their config files name rms_norm_eps; the parent page's Defaults across models tab lists 21 of them). The axes figure above shows what is lost: RMSNorm leaves each token's mean in place and only fixes its scale.

ε is not a detail. It bounds the output when the input is nearly constant. Released configs use 10−5 (Llama 3.1, Mistral 7B v0.3, GPT-2, SmolLM2) or 10−6 (Qwen3, Gemma 3, OLMo 2), and BERT's LayerNorm uses 10−12 (src/inputs/configs_norm_init.json, read from each model's config.json). An ε below what the number format can resolve against the variance does nothing, so low-precision kernels usually compute the statistics in float32.

2025: normalisation without statistics
Zhu, Chen, He, LeCun and Liu 2025 observe that LayerNorm in trained transformers "often produces tanh-like, S-shaped input-output mappings" and replace every norm layer with Dynamic Tanh, DyT(x) = γ · tanh(αx) + β with a learned scalar α, reporting matched or better results across vision and language tasks reported. It computes no statistics at all: an element-wise squashing that bounds activations. Whether it holds at frontier scale is not yet shown.

5 · Where the norm goes: post, pre, peri, output

In a residual block x → x + F(x), the norm can sit in four places, and the choice decides how the residual stream behaves with depth:

PlacementBlockUsed byWhat it does to the stream
Post-normx ← LN(x + F(x))the 2017 Transformer, BERTnormalises the stream itself after every sub-layer, so it never grows; the gradient passes through every norm on the way down
Pre-normx ← x + F(LN(x)), plus a final normGPT-2 onwards, Llama, Qwen, DeepSeekthe stream is never normalised: an identity path for the gradient, but its size grows with depth
Peri-norm (sandwich)x ← x + LN(F(LN(x)))Gemma 2 and 3both: normalised input to the branch, normalised output added to the stream
Output norm ("reordered")x ← x + LN(F(x))OLMo 2 (after Swin Transformer V2)each branch adds a vector of fixed size; the branch sees the raw stream

Sources: Vaswani et al. 2017; GPT-2's report, where layer normalisation "was moved to the input of each sub-block ... and an additional layer normalization was added after the final self-attention block" (Radford et al. 2019, §2.3); Gemma 3, "GQA with post-norm and pre-norm with RMSNorm" (Gemma Team 2025, §2); OLMo 2, §3.3.2 (OLMo 2 paper page). "Peri-LN" is the name given to the sandwich by Kim et al. 2025.

Why post-norm needs warmup. Xiong et al. 2020 analyse a simplified Transformer at initialisation and prove that in post-norm "the expected gradients of the parameters near the output layer are large", so a large learning rate on them is unstable and warmup is "practically helpful for avoiding this problem"; with pre-norm "the gradients are well-behaved at initialization" and warmup can be removed. In their Theorem 1 the last layer's gradient is O(d√(ln d)) for post-norm whatever the depth, and O(d√(ln d / L)) for pre-norm, shrinking with the number of layers L. Their Lemma 2 gives the price of pre-norm: the stream's expected squared size grows linearly, (1 + l/2)d ≤ E‖xl‖² ≤ (1 + 3l/2)d, while post-norm's pre-normalisation sum stays at 3d/2.

The parent page's When training goes wrong tab trains a small transformer that spikes, with QK-norm, z-loss and clipping as fixes, and the OLMo 2 paper page replays the paper's own before-and-after curves: the output norm and QK-norm "in isolation" did not help, "together" they reduced the gradient-norm spikes.

6 · QK-norm: normalising inside attention

An attention logit is q · k / √dh. Nothing in a pre-norm block bounds it: the norm before attention fixes the size of the input, but the query and key projections can grow during training until a few logits dominate, the softmax becomes one-hot, its gradient vanishes, and the loss spikes or diverges. ViT-22B's team saw exactly this, "divergent training loss after a few thousand steps" for models of about 8B parameters, "caused by extremely large values in attention logits", and fixed it by applying LayerNorm to the queries and keys before the dot product (Dehghani et al. 2023, §2, following Gilmer et al.); the idea goes back to QKNorm for low-resource translation (Henry et al. 2020), and Wortsman et al. 2023 reproduced the attention-logit growth in small models trained at high learning rates and found "that mitigations previously employed at large scales are equally effective in this regime".

Today QK-norm is an RMSNorm on each head's query and key vectors (in most implementations before RoPE): Qwen3 adds it "to ensure stable training" (Qwen3 paper page), OLMo 2 uses it with the output norm, and Gemma 3 says "we replace the soft-capping of Gemma 2 with QK-norm" (Gemma 3 report, §2). The alternatives act elsewhere: a logit soft-cap (Gemma 2) squashes the logits themselves with c · tanh(logit / c); qk-clip (Moonshot's Kimi K2) leaves the forward pass untouched and, after the optimiser step, rescales a head's query and key projection weights whenever that head's largest logit passes a threshold τ (τ = 100 for K2). It exists because QK-norm cannot be used with multi-head latent attention, whose per-head keys are never materialised at inference, and it is what made a 1T-parameter run on the Muon optimiser stable, where plain Muon's logits passed 1,000 (Moonshot AI: Kimi, which animates it; K2 report).

Does QK-norm cap the logits? Measured on Qwen3-0.6B

With unit gain, an RMS-normalised head vector has length √dh, so a logit is at most √dh. But the gain γ is learned: with gains, ‖q‖ ≤ √dh · max|γq| (RoPE only rotates), so the ceiling is √dh · max|γq| · max|γk| derived. Reading it. The trained gains are far from 1 (the key gains reach 96 in layer 1), so the hard ceilings run from 184 to 12,897 while the real largest logits per layer stay between 12.5 and 48: QK-norm does not cap logits at a small number in a trained model. What it changes is where growth can come from: not from the size of the projection weights or of the residual stream, only from a dh-sized gain vector, which an optimiser with a bounded step changes slowly. That is our reading of the numbers, not a claim from the papers.

7 · Initialisation: choosing the scale of the first step

SchemeIdeaFailure mode, or fit
Constant (naive)the same value everywherethe symmetry problem: every neuron in a layer gets the same input and the same gradient, so they stay copies of each other and the network cannot diversify (the parent's Training lab preset "All-zero init never breaks symmetry")

All variance-scaling schemes share one goal: keep activation and gradient variance roughly constant across layers, preventing vanishing or exploding gradients from step one. The derivation is three lines. A unit computes y = Σi wixi over nin inputs; with weights independent of the inputs, zero-mean and of variance Var(w),

Var(y) = nin · Var(w) · E[x²]
forward
E[x²] is the previous layer's output after its activation: equal to Var(yprev) for a linear (or tanh near 0) unit, half of it for ReLU, which zeroes the negative half of a symmetric input. Keep the product at 1: nin Var(w) = 1 (linear), = 2 (ReLU)
backward
the gradient flows through the transposed weights, summing over nout units: nout Var(w) = 1, or 2 for ReLU
compromise
both cannot hold when nin ≠ nout; Glorot and Bengio take the average, Var = 2/(nin + nout), drawn as U[−√6/√(nin+nout), +√6/√(nin+nout)] (their Eq. 16)

The schemes, with their sources: Xavier from Glorot and Bengio 2010, derived for activations assumed linear near 0; He from He, Zhang, Ren and Sun 2015 (§2.2), who note "it is sufficient to use either" the forward or the backward condition, because a single layer's mismatch does not compound, and show it lets a 30-layer ReLU model converge where Xavier stalls; LeCun from LeCun, Bottou, Orr and Müller 1998 (standard deviation m−1/2 for m inputs), which SELU networks adopt as "LeCun normal" so that their activations converge to mean 0 and variance 1 by themselves (Klambauer et al. 2017). Which activation pairs with which scheme is the parent page's thread 2, "Keep the variance constant"; its step 2 animation sends one batch through networks with each pairing. Activation functions themselves, and why the choice of activation dictates the scheme, are on Activation functions.

PyTorch's default is not He initialisation
nn.Linear and the convolutions initialise with kaiming_uniform_(a=√5), which the source comment explains "is the same as initializing with uniform(−1/sqrt(in_features), 1/sqrt(in_features))" (PyTorch 2.14, torch/nn/modules/linear.py): Var = 1/(3nin), a third of LeCun's and a sixth of He's. That is exactly the "commonly used heuristic" Glorot and Bengio set out to replace (their Eq. 1). In a deep ReLU stack with no normalisation it shrinks the signal's second moment about 6 times per layer, to 1.7 × 10−16 after 20 layers measured. Normalisation layers hide this, which is why it rarely bites; a plain deep MLP should be initialised explicitly. Embeddings default to N(0, 1).

8 · Depth-aware initialisation, and initialisation instead of normalisation

The variance argument treats one layer at a time. A residual network adds every block's output to one stream, so even if each block is variance-preserving the stream's variance grows with the number of additions. Several schemes shrink the residual branches at initialisation in proportion to depth:

A small fixed scale only works with norms. σ = 0.02 is not variance-preserving on its own: a layer of width d multiplies the second moment by d · 0.02², which is 0.31 at d = 768 and 1.64 at d = 4,096 derived (the calculator above, ReLU halves both). Transformers get away with it because every branch input is re-normalised. Configs record it as initializer_range = 0.02 (GPT-2, BERT, Llama 3.1, Mistral, Qwen3, Gemma 3, OLMo 2), the value Hugging Face uses when a model is initialised from scratch; SmolLM2-135M sets 0.0417 = 1/√576, LeCun's scale for its width (src/inputs/configs_norm_init.json). The config value is not proof of how the released weights were trained; the parent's Defaults across models tab gives each lab's stated scheme.

μP: initialisation and learning rate as one choice. In the standard parametrisation, the best learning rate and init shift as a model gets wider, so hyperparameters tuned on a small model do not transfer. The Maximal Update Parametrization (Yang et al. 2022) changes how both scale with width so that "each layer is updated on the same order during training regardless of width". Its Table 3, for Adam: hidden weights keep Var = 1/fan_in but their learning rate scales as 1/fan_in; output weights are initialised with Var = 1/fan_in² (not 1/fan_in) and also get a 1/fan_in learning rate; input weights and biases keep both; attention uses 1/d instead of 1/√d. The payoff reported: "by transferring from 40M parameters, we outperform published numbers of the 6.7B GPT-3 model, with tuning cost only 7% of total pretraining cost" reported. OLMo 2's init argument uses the same reasoning: its gradient norm at initialisation grows with √d, which by Yang et al.'s argument lets a width-independent learning rate transfer (OLMo 2 §3.2).

Common mistakes

Fitting the scaler on all the data
Mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum come from the training set only, then are applied unchanged to validation and test data; a pipeline object does this for you (section 1).
Scaling inputs for a tree model, or not scaling them for kNN, SVM, PCA or a regularised linear model
Trees split one feature at a time and are unaffected; anything that measures distance, variance or a penalty on all weights at once is not (section 1: 75.9% against 94.4% for kNN on two wine features).
Evaluating a BatchNorm network in training mode, or training it with tiny batches
In training mode, outputs depend on the other examples in the batch; at batch size 2, every output is ±1. Call model.eval() before evaluating, use GroupNorm or synchronised BatchNorm when the per-device batch is small, and recompute the running statistics after fine-tuning on new data (section 3).
Copying BatchNorm's momentum between PyTorch and Keras
PyTorch's 0.1 and Keras's 0.99 mean the same thing, from opposite sides: the weight on the new batch against the weight on the old estimate (section 3).
Calling OLMo 2's or Gemma's norm "post-norm"
Both keep the residual stream unnormalised; they normalise the branch output (and Gemma also its input). The 2017 post-norm normalises the stream itself (section 5).
Assuming QK-norm caps attention logits at √dh
Only with unit gains. Trained gains are large: Qwen3-0.6B's ceilings run from 184 to 12,897; what QK-norm removes is growth through the projection weights and the stream (section 6).
Assuming PyTorch layers are He-initialised
nn.Linear and the convolutions use U(±1/√nin), a sixth of He's variance; a deep plain ReLU MLP needs kaiming_normal_ explicitly (section 7).
Using Xavier with ReLU, or He with tanh
Xavier halves the ReLU signal's second moment at every layer (0.5 per layer, 7.8 × 10−7 after 20 layers, measured); He doubles a tanh signal near 0. The init is chosen for the activation (section 7; the parent's thread 2).
Reading σ = 0.02 as a variance-preserving init
At width 4,096 it grows the second moment by 1.64 per linear layer and at 768 shrinks it by 0.31; it works because the norms re-scale every branch input (section 8).

What the measurements say

  • Post-norm (BERT) keeps the stream at a fixed size: each block ends with a LayerNorm, so the typical token's stream stays between 0.55 and 0.8 (the learned gain sets it, not 1), and the sum entering each norm is 0.94 to 1.62.
  • Every pre-norm model's stream grows with depth, as Lemma 2 says it must at initialisation, and training does not undo it: from embedding to last block, ×106 in GPT-2, ×289 in SmolLM2-135M and ×531 in Qwen3-0.6B (table). The final norm before the output head removes the scale.
  • Peri-norm does not stop the growth. Gemma 3 270M's stream grows ×1,041, to a typical RMS of 1,073: the sandwich fixes the size of each addition only up to the post-norm's learned gain, and those gains are large.
  • OLMo 2's output norm keeps the stream smallest of the five decoders: ×12.8, ending at RMS 0.88, and its first token shows no outsized values on this passage (largest first-token RMS 0.7).
  • The first token is different in the other decoders: its stream reaches RMS 111 (GPT-2), 193 (Qwen3), 832 (SmolLM2) and 4,119 (Gemma 3) against typical tokens' 14 to 1,073, the "massive activations" of Sun et al. 2024; that is why it is excluded from the medians and shown separately.
  • Largest attention logits stay below 50 in all six on this passage (BERT 31, GPT-2 42, Qwen3 48, with or without QK-norm); the instabilities QK-norm prevents appear during training at scale, not in a finished small model's forward pass.

One passage, one forward pass per model: these are measurements of six trained checkpoints, not a controlled comparison of placements (the models differ in size, data and training) measured.

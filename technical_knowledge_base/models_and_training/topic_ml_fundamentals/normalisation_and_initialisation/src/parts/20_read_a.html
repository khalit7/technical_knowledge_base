In one screen

Training works best when the numbers flowing through a network stay on a scale of about 1: features on comparable scales, activations whose variance does not grow or shrink from layer to layer, gradients that reach the first layer at a usable size. Three tools enforce that at three moments. Feature scaling fixes the inputs once, before training. Initialisation chooses the scale of the random starting weights so the signal is preserved at step zero. Normalisation layers re-impose a fixed scale inside the network at every step, which is why a normalised network is far less sensitive to its initial scale.

1 · Feature scaling: the inputs, once

Why. A model that measures distance (k-nearest neighbours, SVMs with an RBF kernel, k-means), projects variance (PCA) or penalises weights uniformly (ridge, lasso) treats a unit of every feature the same, so a feature measured in thousands silently outvotes one measured in fractions. Gradient descent suffers too: unequal scales stretch the loss surface into a narrow ravine, so the step size that is safe along the steep feature is tiny along the flat one, and convergence slows (rounder contours after scaling). Tree models split one feature at a time on thresholds and do not care.

The statistics (μ, σ, minimum, maximum) are fitted on the training set only and then applied unchanged to validation and test data; fitting them on all the data leaks the test set into training (scikit-learn, common pitfalls).

Same wines, same neighbours rule: raw units against standardised

Try: press Play. The 178 wines of the UCI Wine data are plotted on two features, proline (278 to 1,680 mg/L) and hue (0.48 to 1.71), first in raw units drawn to the same scale on both axes, then standardised. One test wine asks its 20 nearest training wines to vote on its cultivar. Switch to min-max to compare.

k-nearest neighbours with k = 20, a 70/30 split with random_state=42, scaler fitted on the 124 training wines, exactly as in scikit-learn's Importance of Feature Scaling example. Test accuracy computed in your browser over the 54 test wines; src/scaling.py gets the same numbers from scikit-learn 1.9.1: 75.93% raw, 94.44% standardised, 94.44% min-max measured.

The size of the effect, on all 13 features measured (src/scaling.py): kNN with k = 20 scores 77.78% on raw features, 96.30% standardised and 98.15% min-max scaled. For least squares on the same data (predicting alcohol from the other 12 features), the condition number of the Hessian XTX/n, the ratio of the steepest to the flattest direction of the loss and so roughly how many more steps gradient descent needs, is 1.6 × 108 raw, 690 min-max scaled and 45 standardised.

Correction: scikit-learn's own example overstates the gap
Its example reports that PCA to two components then logistic regression scores 35.19% unscaled against 96.30% standardised. Rerunning its code reproduces both numbers exactly, but the example passes one PCA object to both pipelines, so fitting the standardised pipeline refits the PCA that the unscaled pipeline then uses on raw data. With a PCA of its own, the unscaled pipeline scores 74.07% (log-loss 0.497, against 1.18) measured. Scaling still wins clearly (96.30%, log-loss 0.0739); the gap is 22 points, not 61 (src/scaling.py).

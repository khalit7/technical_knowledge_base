The idea in one sentence

Training works when the scale of the signal stays about 1 at every layer: feature scaling fixes the inputs once, initialisation picks weights that neither shrink nor grow the signal at step zero, and normalisation layers reset the scale at every layer on every step, which is why a normalised network barely cares about its initialisation.

  1. The network. 1,024 random inputs, mean square 1.00, enter 20 linear layers of width 512, each followed by ReLU, with no bias (built in PyTorch). Each glass plate is one layer's output, its side the root mean square; the dashed squares are the size the signal should keep.
  2. PyTorch's default: ×0.162 per layer. nn.Linear's own initialisation shrinks the mean square about six times at every layer: after 20 layers it is 1.7 × 10⁻¹⁶, so almost nothing reaches the output, and the gradient on the way back shrinks the same way.
  3. Xavier: ×0.495. Derived for tanh, which is roughly linear near 0, it loses half the second moment at every ReLU: 7.8 × 10⁻⁷ after 20 layers.
  4. He: ×0.990. Twice Xavier's variance, to pay for ReLU's halving: the signal holds, 0.82 after 20 layers.
  5. Twice He: ×1.980. Var(w) = 4/nin, a scale chosen to show the other failure illustrative, run on the same network: the mean square doubles at every layer, 8.6 × 10⁵ after 20 (plates wider than 2.4 times the input's are drawn at that size).
  6. The default plus a LayerNorm before every ReLU. The same shrinking weights, but each LayerNorm (the thin teal plates) resets the scale to 1 and ReLU halves it: 0.51 at every layer, whatever came before. That is the whole page in one picture: get the scale right once, or reset it every layer.

Same wines, same neighbours rule: in raw units proline decides alone

Fig. 2 · k-nearest neighbours with k = 20, a 70/30 split with random_state=42, scaler fitted on the 124 training wines, as in scikit-learn's Importance of Feature Scaling example. Test accuracy over the 54 test wines, computed in your browser; src/scaling.py gets the same from scikit-learn 1.9.1: 75.93% raw, 94.44% standardised, 94.44% min-max measured.

1 · Feature scaling: the inputs, once

A model that measures distance (k-nearest neighbours, SVMs with an RBF kernel, k-means), projects variance (PCA) or penalises all weights alike (ridge, lasso) treats one unit of every feature the same, so a feature measured in thousands outvotes one measured in fractions. Tree models split one feature at a time and do not care.

Predict how much of kNN's accuracy on two wine features is lost to units alone, then watch Fig. 2. The 178 wines of the UCI Wine data are plotted on proline (278 to 1,680 mg/L) and hue (0.48 to 1.71); one test wine asks its 20 nearest training wines to vote on its cultivar. Switch to min-max to compare.

  1. Raw units, drawn to the same scale on both axes. Proline spans about 1,400 mg/L, hue about 1.2, so on equal axes every wine sits on a flat line: hue is invisible to anything that measures distance.
  2. 20 nearest neighbours, raw. The ringed test wine collects its 20 nearest training wines. Most of the distance is proline, so the neighbours are a vertical stripe of wines with similar proline, whatever their hue, and the vote goes to the wrong cultivar.
  3. Standardise: subtract each feature's mean, divide by its standard deviation. Both features now have mean 0 and standard deviation 1 on the training wines (or, with min-max, the range 0 to 1). Nothing about the wines changed; only the units.
  4. 20 nearest neighbours, scaled. Hue now counts as much as proline, the neighbourhood is a round patch, and the vote goes to the right cultivar.
  5. Every test wine. Each of the 54 test wines votes with its 20 neighbours; crosses mark the wrong votes in the current units. Raw units get 75.93% right; standardised and min-max scaled, 94.44%.

A pipeline object does this for you. On all 13 features measured (src/scaling.py): kNN with k = 20 scores 77.78% raw, 96.30% standardised and 98.15% min-max scaled.

Gradient descent: a ravine in raw units, a round bowl after standardising

Gradient descent suffers too: unequal scales stretch the loss surface into a narrow ravine, so the step that is safe along the steep feature is tiny along the flat one. Fig. 3 fits the smallest model that shows it on the same training wines, hue = w · proline + b, by gradient descent on the mean squared error, from w = b = 0, with the same rule each time: a learning rate of 1.2/λmax, 60% of the largest stable step (2/λmax, where λmax is the steepest curvature). Both axes are drawn to the same scale, as gradient descent sees them derived, computed in your browser.

  1. Raw units: a crack, not a bowl. One unit of w multiplies proline, which is in the hundreds, so the loss is 4.4 × 10⁶ times more curved along w than along b (the condition number κ, the ratio of the steepest to the flattest curvature). At equal scale the ravine is a line; its bottom, b = 0.85, is at the far end.
  2. 30 steps in raw units. The first step drops onto the floor of the crack; after that the flat direction moves 1.2/κ of the remaining way per step. After 30 steps b is 8.3 × 10⁻⁶ and the loss 0.078, against 0.026 at the bottom: still 11% of the way from the bottom to the start, and at this rate it would take millions of steps to close.
  3. Min-max scaled: a stretched bowl, κ = 23. One way is 23 times more curved than the other, which is mild: the first steps drop to the valley floor and the rest slide down it, and after 30 steps the loss is 0.026, the bottom.
  4. Standardised: a round bowl, κ = 1.0. With mean 0 and variance 1, the two curvatures are equal (the Hessian is the identity), so every step points at the bottom: the loss is 0.026 within a few steps.

On all 13 Wine features, for least squares (alcohol from the other 12 features), the condition number of the Hessian XTX/n, roughly how many more steps gradient descent needs, is 1.6 × 108 raw, 690 min-max scaled and 45 standardised measured (src/scaling.py).

Correction: scikit-learn's own example overstates the gap
It reports that PCA to two components then logistic regression scores 35.19% unscaled against 96.30% standardised. Rerunning its code reproduces both numbers, but the example passes one PCA object to both pipelines, so fitting the standardised pipeline refits the PCA that the unscaled pipeline then uses on raw data. With a PCA of its own, the unscaled pipeline scores 74.07% (log-loss 0.497, against 1.18) measured. Scaling still wins (96.30%, log-loss 0.0739); the gap is 22 points, not 61 (src/scaling.py).

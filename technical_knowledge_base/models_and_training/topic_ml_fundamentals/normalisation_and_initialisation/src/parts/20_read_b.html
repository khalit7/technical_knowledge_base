2 · Initialisation: the scale of step zero

Why random, and why the scale matters. A constant initialisation gives every neuron in a layer the same input and the same gradient, so they stay copies of each other for ever (the symmetry problem; the parent's Training lab preset "All-zero init never breaks symmetry"). Random weights break the symmetry, but their scale decides the hero figure: too small and the signal shrinks layer by layer (vanishing gradients), too large and it grows exponentially (exploding gradients).

The factor per layer, in three lines. A unit computes y = Σi wixi over nin inputs. With zero-mean weights independent of the inputs, each of the nin terms contributes Var(w) · E[x²]. In the hero network, nin = 512 and He's Var(w) = 2/512, so Var(y) = 2 E[x²]; ReLU then zeroes the negative half, and E[relu(y)²] = ½ Var(y) = E[x²]: factor 1 derived.

E[xnext²] = c · nin · Var(w) · E[x²]
c
½ after ReLU (it zeroes half of a symmetric input); 1 for a linear unit, or tanh near 0
forward
keep c · nin Var(w) = 1: Var(w) = 1/nin (linear, LeCun), 2/nin (ReLU, He)
backward
the gradient flows through the transposed weights, summing over nout units: c · nout Var(w) = 1
compromise
both cannot hold when nin ≠ nout; Glorot and Bengio average them: Var(w) = 2/(nin + nout), drawn as U[−√6/√(nin+nout), +√6/√(nin+nout)] (their Eq. 16)

So Xavier, derived for tanh and sigmoid (roughly linear near 0), gives ReLU a factor of ½ per layer; He (Kaiming) doubles the variance to 2/nin for ReLU; LeCun's 1/nin keeps a linear unit and pairs with SELU. PyTorch's default is not He initialisation
nn.Linear and the convolutions initialise with kaiming_uniform_(a=√5), which the source comment explains "is the same as initializing with uniform(−1/sqrt(in_features), 1/sqrt(in_features))" (PyTorch 2.14, torch/nn/modules/linear.py): Var = 1/(3nin), a third of LeCun's and a sixth of He's. That is the "commonly used heuristic" Glorot and Bengio set out to replace (their Eq. 1). In a deep ReLU stack with no normalisation it shrinks the second moment about 6 times per layer, to 1.7 × 10−16 after 20 layers (the hero's bottom line). Norm layers hide this, so it rarely bites; a plain deep MLP needs kaiming_normal_ explicitly. Embeddings default to N(0, 1).

Orthogonal initialisation: every direction, not only the average. Variance scaling preserves the mean square; a random Gaussian matrix still stretches some directions and crushes others, and a product of 20 of them spreads those factors apart. An orthogonal matrix (orthonormal rows, torch.nn.init.orthogonal_) has every singular value equal to 1, so a product of orthogonal layers preserves the length of every input exactly. In a 20-layer linear network of width 512 at initialisation, Gaussian weights with Var = 1/n keep the mean square at 0.94, yet half the singular values of the whole map are below 9.2 × 10−4 and the largest is 6.4; with orthogonal weights all 512 are 1.000 measured (src/signal_prop.py, float64). With ReLU, a gain of √2 plays He's role: the hero's orthogonal line multiplies the second moment by 1.015 per layer. Saxe, McClelland and Ganguli 2013 solve the learning dynamics of deep linear networks exactly and show that random orthogonal initialisation gives learning times that do not grow with depth, where scaled Gaussian initialisation does not (paraphrase of their abstract). It is the standard init for recurrent weight matrices, which multiply the same matrix at every time step.

Where each formula comes from

Xavier: Glorot and Bengio 2010, derived for activations assumed linear near 0; uniform and normal flavours. He: He, Zhang, Ren and Sun 2015 (§2.2), who note "it is sufficient to use either" the forward or the backward condition, because a single layer's mismatch does not compound, and show it lets a 30-layer ReLU model converge where Xavier stalls. LeCun: LeCun, Bottou, Orr and Müller 1998 (standard deviation m−1/2 for m inputs), which SELU networks adopt as "LeCun normal" so that their activations converge to mean 0 and variance 1 by themselves (Klambauer et al. 2017). He's derivation keeps the non-zero mean of ReLU's output in view: what is preserved is the second moment, not the variance.

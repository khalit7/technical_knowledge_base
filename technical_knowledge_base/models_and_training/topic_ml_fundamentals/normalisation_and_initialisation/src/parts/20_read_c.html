4 · LayerNorm and RMSNorm: the same idea on the other axis

LayerNorm normalises across the feature dimension, per data point, so every example (every token, in a transformer) is normalised independently, with one γ, β vector per layer (Ba, Kiros and Hinton 2016). It fixes all three BatchNorm weaknesses at once: it does not depend on batch size, it works for RNNs and transformers with variable-length inputs, and it computes the same thing at training and inference, with no running averages. What it gives up is small: the mild regularising noise of batch statistics, and a little compute at inference, because the statistics are computed per token every time instead of being folded into the weights. It was the 2017 transformer's norm, and pre-norm placement (section 5) made it the transformer default.

RMSNorm(x) = x / RMS(x) · γ,   RMS(x) = √( (1/d) Σ xi² + ε )
vs LayerNorm
no mean subtraction, no β: half the reductions and one fewer parameter vector

RMSNorm (Zhang and Sennrich 2019) is LayerNorm without the re-centring. Its argument is that LayerNorm's benefit comes from re-scaling invariance (the output does not change when the input is multiplied by a constant), not from re-centring, and it "achieves comparable performance against LayerNorm but reduces the running time by 7%~64% on different models" reported; its abstract singles out RNNs as the networks LayerNorm's overhead "significantly slows". It is the default in essentially every open LLM since Llama: Llama, Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek, Gemma and OLMo 2 all use it (their config files name rms_norm_eps; the parent page's Defaults across models tab lists 21 of them). The axes figure above shows what is lost: RMSNorm leaves each token's mean in place and only fixes its scale.

ε is not a detail. It bounds the output when the input is nearly constant. Released configs use 10−5 (Llama 3.1, Mistral 7B v0.3, GPT-2, SmolLM2) or 10−6 (Qwen3, Gemma 3, OLMo 2), and BERT's LayerNorm uses 10−12 (src/inputs/configs_norm_init.json, read from each model's config.json). An ε below what the number format can resolve against the variance does nothing, so low-precision kernels usually compute the statistics in float32.

2025: normalisation without statistics
Zhu, Chen, He, LeCun and Liu 2025 observe that LayerNorm in trained transformers "often produces tanh-like, S-shaped input-output mappings" and replace every norm layer with Dynamic Tanh, DyT(x) = γ · tanh(αx) + β with a learned scalar α, reporting matched or better results across vision and language tasks reported. It computes no statistics at all: an element-wise squashing that bounds activations. Whether it holds at frontier scale is not yet shown.

5 · Where the norm goes, and why post-norm needs warmup

In a residual block x → x + F(x) the norm can sit before the add (post-norm: the 2017 Transformer, BERT), on the branch input (pre-norm: GPT-2 onwards, Llama, Qwen, DeepSeek), on both sides of the branch (sandwich or peri-norm: Gemma 2 and 3) or on the branch output (OLMo 2). The placements, what each does to the residual stream, DeepNorm and the hyper-connection variants are owned by {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}} (section 2, with its Residual stream and Stability explorer tabs trained on a tiny GPT). This section keeps the basic result behind the choice, in a toy small enough to compute exactly.

Why post-norm needs warmup. Xiong et al. 2020 analyse a simplified Transformer at initialisation and prove that in post-norm "the expected gradients of the parameters near the output layer are large", so a large learning rate on them is unstable and warmup is "practically helpful for avoiding this problem"; with pre-norm "the gradients are well-behaved at initialization" and warmup can be removed. In their Theorem 1 the last layer's gradient is O(d√(ln d)) for post-norm whatever the depth, and O(d√(ln d / L)) for pre-norm, shrinking with the number of layers L. Their Lemma 2 gives the price of pre-norm: the stream's expected squared size grows linearly, (1 + l/2)d ≤ E‖xl‖² ≤ (1 + 3l/2)d, while post-norm's pre-normalisation sum stays at 3d/2.

Xiong et al.'s simplified Transformer, exactly: width d = 64, 16 tokens, attention with WQ = WK = 0 (so every token attends uniformly), a ReLU feed-forward of width d, Xavier-initialised weights, LayerNorm without learned gain, a 10-class softmax output; inputs and labels seeded illustrative. Forward and backward passes are written by hand and match PyTorch autograd to 3 × 10−15 over 24 configurations (src/checks/toy_ref.py). Defaults reproduce Lemma 2 (Xiong et al. 2020) independently: averaged over 20 seeds, post-norm's squared sum before the norm is 1.42 to 1.53 times d at every layer (theory 3/2), and pre-norm's squared stream after layer l stays inside (1 + l/2)d to (1 + 3l/2)d at every l from 1 to 12 (13.1d at l = 12, bounds 7d and 19d) measured.

What the toy shows. Post-norm keeps the stream at size 1, but its late blocks get the largest gradients and those do not shrink as L grows (averaged over 5 seeds, the last block's is 2.7 at L = 6 and 3.2 at L = 48) measured: Theorem 1's warmup problem. Pre-norm lets the stream grow, so each block's contribution is a shrinking fraction of it and the late blocks' gradients fall as L grows: 1.04, 0.67, 0.53, 0.40 at L = 6, 12, 24, 48, close to Theorem 1's 1/√L (√8 = 2.8 against the measured 2.6) measured, 5 seeds each. Peri-norm and the output norm add a branch of fixed size each time, so the stream grows like the square root of the number of sub-layers (RMS 9.8 after 48 blocks, √97 = 9.8); the toy's DeepNorm mode keeps post-norm but up-weights the residual and shrinks the branch init (Wang et al. 2022, explained in section 2 of {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}}). The {{Depth and placement|#t-depth}} tab sweeps the depth, and measures the stream and the attention logits in six released models: from embedding to last block the typical token's stream grows ×106 to ×531 in the three pre-norm decoders, ×1,041 in Gemma 3 (peri-norm), only ×12.8 in OLMo 2 (output norm), and not at all in BERT (post-norm) measured.

What it does not show. Instability in real runs appears later in training and at scale (attention logits, rare huge gradients), which a 64-wide toy at initialisation cannot reproduce. {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}} trains a small transformer at three learning rates under four placements, with and without QK-norm, and the parent page's When training goes wrong tab maps the symptoms to fixes.

6 · QK-norm, measured in one trained model

An attention logit is q · k / √dh, and nothing in a pre-norm block bounds it: the query and key projections can grow during training until a few logits dominate and the loss spikes. QK-norm (an RMSNorm on each head's query and key vectors: ViT-22B, Qwen3, OLMo 2, Gemma 3), the logit soft-cap (Gemma 2) and Moonshot's qk-clip (Kimi K2, for attention whose per-head keys are never materialised) are explained, sourced and animated as a before/after of runaway logits on {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}} (section 4); {{Moonshot AI: Kimi|n:3c65c17b0d0d81f5abf5dbdbac821a12}} animates qk-clip in a frontier run. This page keeps one measurement that page links to: what QK-norm does to the logits of a released model.

Does QK-norm cap the logits? Measured on Qwen3-0.6B

With unit gain, an RMS-normalised head vector has length √dh, so a logit is at most √dh. But the gain γ is learned: with gains, ‖q‖ ≤ √dh · max|γq| (RoPE only rotates), so the ceiling is √dh · max|γq| · max|γk| derived. The bars compare that ceiling, from the released weights, with the largest logit actually reached on a 145-token English passage.

Reading it. The trained gains are far from 1 (the key gains reach 96 in layer 1), so the hard ceilings run from 184 to 12,897 while the real largest logits per layer stay between 12.5 and 48: QK-norm does not cap logits at a small number in a trained model. What it changes is where growth can come from: not from the size of the projection weights or of the residual stream, only from a dh-sized gain vector, which an optimiser with a bounded step changes slowly. That is our reading of the numbers, not a claim from the papers.

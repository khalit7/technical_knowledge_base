Alignment and uniformity: what the loss actually builds

Wang and Isola (2020) split what InfoNCE optimises on the unit hypersphere into two measurable properties, and show that as the number of negatives grows the loss optimises exactly these two:

ℒalign = E(x, y) positive ‖f(x) − f(y)‖2     ℒuniform = log Ex, y e−2‖f(x) − f(y)‖2
align
positives should land close: lower is better, 0 when the two views coincide
uniform
all embeddings should spread over the sphere: lower is better; 0 when every point is the same (the exponent is then 0 for every pair)
α, t
the exponents 2 and 2 are the paper's defaults (lalign(x, y, alpha=2), lunif(x, t=2))

The numerator of InfoNCE rewards alignment and the denominator rewards uniformity, and "directly optimizing for these two metrics leads to comparable or better performance than contrastive learning" (abstract). Seen this way, collapse is not mysterious: alignment alone is minimised perfectly by mapping everything to one point, and only the uniformity half stops it.

The "perfectly even circle" uniformity is log(e−4I0(4)) = −1.575 derived, since ‖u − v‖2 = 2 − 2 cos θ on the circle. A 2-D output is far too small for real use: the trained circle's 5-NN accuracy is about 50%, against 90% for the raw pixels toy; it is drawn because a circle can be seen.

Two refinements matter in practice. Dimensional collapse: even with negatives, embeddings can end up spanning a lower-dimensional subspace than they have room for (Jing et al. 2021: "dimensional collapse also happens in contrastive learning"), which is why the {{Collapse lab|#t-collapse}} reports an effective rank beside the std. Pretrained language-model embeddings are anisotropic: they occupy "a narrow cone in the vector space", so that in GPT-2's last layer "two random words will on average have almost perfect cosine similarity" (Ethayarajh 2019). That is a uniformity failure, measured on real sentences in the {{One real batch|#t-batch}} tab: random pairs of STS-B sentences have a mean cosine of 0.62 under BERT and 0.32 after SimCSE, while SimCSE's alignment is slightly worse (0.25 against 0.17), exactly SimCSE's own reading: "pre-trained embeddings have good alignment, their uniformity is poor" (§7, Figure 3). The same narrow cone is why BERTScore offers a rescaled score ({{Evaluation metrics|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a29acef722e842a29b}}, which runs BERTScore on real embeddings).

Without negatives: collapse and the defences against it

Negatives are expensive (big batches, queues) and some are false. Remove them and the obvious objective, "two views of an input should have the same embedding", has a perfect and useless solution: a constant. Every method without negatives is a different way to make that solution unreachable or unstable. Each defence below is ablated in the {{Collapse lab|#t-collapse}}, which trains one encoder eleven ways and plays the runs side by side.

Asymmetry: BYOL and SimSiam

BYOL (Grill et al. 2020) uses "two neural networks, referred to as online and target networks". The online network has an extra predictor MLP and is trained to predict the target network's projection of the other view; the target network gets no gradient and is an exponential moving average of the online weights, with rate starting at τbase = 0.996 and increased to 1 on a cosine schedule (§3.3). "While state-of-the art methods rely on negative pairs, BYOL achieves a new state of the art without them": 74.3% ImageNet linear top-1 with a ResNet-50 and 79.6% with a larger ResNet.

SimSiam (Chen and He 2020) strips BYOL down: no moving average, one shared encoder, a predictor on one branch and a stop-gradient on the other. "A stop-gradient operation plays an essential role in preventing collapsing": removing it alone drops ImageNet accuracy to 0.1%, and removing the predictor also fails (§4.1, §4.2, Table 1a). To detect collapse the authors monitor the standard deviation of the l2-normalised output, which is about 1/√d for spread-out outputs and 0 when collapsed. SimSiam works from batch 64 to 4,096 (66.1% at 64, 68.1% at 256, 64.0% at 4,096; Table 2) and reaches 68.1% at 100 epochs, 71.3% at 800 (Table 4).

Correction
SimSiam's 71.3% ImageNet linear accuracy is its 800-epoch figure; at the 100 epochs most of its ablations use it is 68.1%.

Why asymmetry is enough is a dynamics question, not a property of the loss: the constant is still a minimum. Tian, Chen and Ganguli (2021) analyse "how multiple factors, like predictor networks, stop-gradients, exponential moving averages, and weight decay all come into play", and derive a predictor that can be set directly from the data's statistics (DirectPred). In the lab, the SimSiam run with stop-gradient ends with embedding std 0.24 (1/√16 = 0.25 is spread out) and the run without it at 0.001, on the same encoder, predictor and loss.

Centering and sharpening: DINO

DINO (Caron et al. 2021) is self-distillation with no labels: a student network matches the softmax output of a momentum teacher (momentum following a cosine schedule from 0.996 to 1) across different crops. Two operations on the teacher's output prevent collapse, and each alone causes a different one: "centering prevents one dimension to dominate but encourages collapse to the uniform distribution, while the sharpening has the opposite effect" (§3.1). Centering subtracts a running mean of the teacher's outputs; sharpening uses a low teacher temperature, warmed up from 0.04 to 0.07 over the first 30 epochs, against 0.1 for the student. A centre that updates too slowly (momentum 0.999) collapses; 0.9 gave the best k-NN accuracy in the ablation (Appendix). With a ViT-B/8, DINO reaches 80.1% linear and 77.4% k-NN top-1 on ImageNet (Table 2). The collapse measures DINO uses are the teacher output's entropy (0 when one prototype wins, ln K when uniform) and its KL divergence from the student; the lab shows the entropy and how far the batch-mean output is from uniform.

DINOv2 (Oquab et al. 2023) scales this with data curation: "a small but diverse corpus of 142M images" (LVD-142M) and a ViT-g backbone of 1.1B parameters, reaching 86.5% linear top-1 on ImageNet-1k with frozen features (Table 4), which made it a default frozen backbone for dense vision tasks. DINOv3 (Siméoni et al. 2025) trains "a DINO model with 7B parameters" on 1,689 million curated images (LVD-1689M) and adds Gram anchoring to stop dense feature maps degrading during long training.

Statistics of the batch: Barlow Twins and VICReg

Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al. 2021) computes the cross-correlation matrix between the two views' embeddings over the batch and pushes it towards the identity: diagonal 1 (invariance), off-diagonal 0 (no redundancy between dimensions). VICReg (Bardes, Ponce and LeCun 2021) separates the three jobs into three terms: invariance (mean squared distance between the views), variance (a hinge keeping each dimension's standard deviation over the batch above γ = 1) and covariance (off-diagonal covariances pushed to 0), weighted λ = μ = 25 and ν = 1. These use the batch, but as a statistic, not as negatives; the variance hinge alone is what blocks the constant, and dropping it in the lab collapses the run to a single point after a few hundred steps, in all three seeds.

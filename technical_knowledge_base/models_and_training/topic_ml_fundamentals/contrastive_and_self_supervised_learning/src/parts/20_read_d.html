Masked modelling, and prediction in representation space

The other road needs no pairs at all: hide part of the input and predict it. A constant output cannot win, because the target is the data. BERT did this for text, masking 15% of tokens ({{BERT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e5ad9bd09cbf18ad7c}} paper page, which runs a toy BERT live). For images the question is what to predict: discrete visual tokens from a separately trained tokenizer (BEiT, Bao et al. 2021: "recover the original visual tokens based on the corrupted image patches"); pixels (MAE, He et al. 2021; SimMIM, Xie et al. 2021, which "predicts raw pixel values of the randomly masked patches by a lightweight one-layer head" with an l1 loss); or representations from a slowly updated copy of the encoder (data2vec, Baevski et al. 2022, one recipe for speech, text and images; I-JEPA, Assran et al. 2023).

MAE rests on two choices. It masks "a high proportion of the input image, e.g., 75%", because images are redundant enough that a lower ratio is solved by interpolation (BERT's "typical masking ratio is 15%", §4.1); and its encoder "operates only on the visible subset of patches (without mask tokens), along with a lightweight decoder", so pretraining runs "3x or more" faster. The loss is the mean squared error on masked patches only, and predicting each patch's pixels normalised by that patch's own mean and variance "improves representation quality" (§3). A ViT-Huge pretrained this way reaches 87.8% on ImageNet-1K after fine-tuning; that headline is fine-tuned, and MAE features are not the ones to use frozen. I-JEPA keeps the masking but changes the target: predict the representations of several large target blocks from one context block, so the model is never asked for texture it cannot know. It trains a ViT-H/14 "using 16 A100 GPUs in under 72 hours", under 1,200 GPU hours and "over 10x more efficient than a ViT-H/14 pretrained with MAE".

The split today is less "contrastive or not" than joint-embedding methods (contrastive, self-distillation, JEPA, which compare or predict embeddings) against masked generative methods (MAE, which reconstruct the input), with prediction in latent space favoured when the goal is semantic features. The camps meet in practice: DINOv2 adds a masked-image term to self-distillation, and I-JEPA borrows MAE's masking and BYOL's moving-average target. Patches and why ViT became the backbone for all of this are on the {{ViT|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa231cfcde6c1954d}} paper page.

V-JEPA, V-JEPA 2 and where JEPA came from (depth)
V-JEPA (Bardes et al. 2024) applies the idea to video, "trained on 2 million videos collected from public datasets" with no pretrained image encoder, text, negatives or reconstruction. V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al. 2025) pretrains on "over 1 million hours of internet video" with encoders up to 1 billion parameters, then adds V-JEPA 2-AC, "a latent action-conditioned world model" trained on "less than 62 hours of unlabeled robot videos", deployed zero-shot on Franka robot arms. (The old page said V-JEPA 2 "extends to video"; V-JEPA was already a video model, and V-JEPA 2 adds scale and action conditioning.) The joint-embedding predictive architecture was proposed by Yann LeCun in "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence" (version 0.9.2, 27 June 2022, as I-JEPA cites it; the page itself could not be fetched here) as a route to world models.

CLIP and embedding models: the same loss, and the objective makes the model

CLIP (Radford et al. 2021) is InfoNCE across modalities: an image encoder and a text encoder, a batch of 32,768 image-caption pairs, and the loss run in both directions (image picks its caption from the batch, caption picks its image; tick "Symmetric loss" on the InfoNCE lab to see it on real cosines). The temperature is learned, starting from 0.07 with the logit scale capped at 100. Writing the classes as text then gives zero-shot classification. CLIP as the vision encoder of multimodal models, beside SigLIP and plain ViT, is on {{Vision encoders: ViT, CLIP and SigLIP|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c99f60ceb2f02cf5ea}}; a trained toy CLIP and its successors are on the {{CLIP|n:3c65c17b0d0d8194a85dfc5f3bf3f799}} paper page.

What the objective forces the model to output decides the architecture. A contrastive objective wants one vector per input, compared with other vectors; nothing is generated token by token, so an encoder suffices, which is why CLIP's towers, sentence embedders (SBERT, SimCSE, retrieval models) and reranker backbones are encoders. Next-token prediction must emit sequences and needs a decoder, and its representations are shaped for predicting the next token, not for comparison. BERT sits between: an encoder trained with a generative-style objective (masked tokens), whose raw embeddings are poor for similarity until contrastively fine-tuned. That last step is measurable.

SimCSE (Gao, Yao and Chen 2021) needs no labels: "we pass the same sentence to the pre-trained encoder twice: by applying the standard dropout twice, we can obtain two different embeddings as 'positive pairs'", with in-batch negatives, τ = 0.05 and batch 64. On the STS benchmark test set, the Spearman correlation between cosine and human similarity rises from 53.87 (BERT, Table 5) to 76.85, and the 7-task average reaches 76.3, 81.6 with supervised NLI pairs. SimCSE's unsupervised model is read at the last layer's [CLS] vector without its training-time MLP, as the authors' repository does.

In 2026 contrastive training is the standard post-training recipe for embedding and retrieval models, applied to an encoder and, increasingly, to a decoder-only LLM with pooling: decoder LLMs are contrastively adapted precisely because next-token pretraining alone does not give a well-shaped similarity space. On the text side the same loss trains retrieval embedders on query-document pairs: {{Embedding models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81508f99e1fa78c2bb41}} (a child of {{Topic: rag-and-retrieval|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b89145c37dfe8a3b0b}}) applies it to SciFact with mined hard negatives, a tiny fine-tune and model choice.

Common mistakes (each shown in the core above; collected)
  • Comparing InfoNCE losses across batch sizes: chance is ln N; compare ln N − L or a downstream probe.
  • Using the projection head's output downstream: SimCLR's z is over 10% worse than h on linear evaluation (§4.2); SimCSE drops its training-time MLP too.
  • Trusting the loss to detect collapse: monitor the normalised embedding's std (about 1/√d when healthy) and an effective rank.
  • Treating every in-batch item as a true negative: duplicates, paraphrases and same-class items are pushed apart too.
  • Comparing cosine scores across embedding models: random sentences score about 0.62 under raw BERT and 0.32 after SimCSE.
  • Calling "first-last avg." one thing: 59.04 and 53.87 are two different poolings under one name.
  • Expecting MAE features to work frozen: its 87.8% is after fine-tuning; DINOv2's features are the frozen ones.
  • Mining only the hardest triplets: FaceNet reports a collapsed model, f(x) = 0; use semi-hard negatives.

The idea in one screen

Make the data supply its own target: two views of the same thing must land close in embedding space, and something must stop the trivial answer, every input mapped to one point. The methods differ only in that something: negatives pushed away (contrastive), an asymmetric pair of networks (BYOL, SimSiam, DINO), a statistic of the batch (Barlow Twins, VICReg), or predicting the hidden part of the input (MAE, I-JEPA).

One real batch, before and after contrastive training: the diagonal stays high, everything else falls

Rows are anchors, columns candidates: eight sentence pairs from the STS benchmark test split, each cell the real cosine between an anchor and a candidate. Diagonal (outlined): the positive, the anchor's own paraphrase; the pairing is the label. Everything else: negatives. Each row is one 8-way classification whose right answer is the diagonal. Over the full 48-pair batch, contrastive training moves the mean negative cosine from 0.60 to 0.31 while the positives stay at 0.92 and 0.91 measured.

Pairs and triplets: the margin losses

Early self-supervision invented pretext tasks whose answer the data already holds (which rotation was applied, where a patch sits, how to unshuffle a jigsaw); the features were good only insofar as the proxy needed them. Instance discrimination (Wu et al. 2018) made the geometry itself the goal: treat every image as its own class, keep each image's feature in a memory bank rather than a classifier's weights, and use noise-contrastive estimation to cope with a million classes. The losses that state the geometry are older.

The pretext tasks, with their sources (lineage)
Surrogate classes, each one image under many transformations (Dosovitskiy et al. 2014, Exemplar CNN); predict where a second patch sits relative to the first (Doersch et al. 2015); solve a jigsaw puzzle of tiles (Noroozi and Favaro 2016); recognise which 2-D rotation was applied (Gidaris et al. 2018). Each task was a proxy for the features, not the features themselves.

The contrastive loss with a margin (Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun 2006) scores one pair at a time at distance d: a similar pair pays ½d2, a dissimilar pair pays ½ max(0, m − d)2, so dissimilar pairs are pushed only while they sit inside the radius m. The triplet loss (FaceNet, Schroff, Kalenichenko and Philbin 2015) compares distances instead of thresholding them: an anchor a, a positive p of the same identity and a negative n, embedded on the unit hypersphere (‖f(x)‖2 = 1, 128 dimensions):

L = Σ max(0, ‖f(a) − f(p)‖2 − ‖f(a) − f(n)‖2 + α)
α
the margin the negative must clear beyond the positive; "The margin α is set to 0.2" (§3.3)
dap, dan
the two squared distances; on unit vectors ‖u − v‖2 = 2 − 2 cos(u, v)

A triplet already separated by the margin contributes nothing, neither loss nor gradient. So the loss lives or dies by which negatives it sees. FaceNet names three kinds: hard (closer than the positive), semi-hard ("further away from the anchor than the positive exemplar, but still hard because the squared distance is close to the anchor-positive distance. Those negatives lie inside the margin α", §3.2) and easy (beyond the margin). It mines them online inside mini-batches of "around 1,800 exemplars" with "around 40 faces" per identity, and picks semi-hard ones because "Selecting the hardest negatives can in practice lead to bad local minima early on in training, specifically it can result in a collapsed model (i.e. f(x) = 0)". The result was 99.63% on LFW and 95.12% on YouTube Faces.

Two lessons carry over to everything below. A hinge spends its gradient on a handful of triplets and none on the rest, so it needs mining and big pools of candidates. And pushing hardest on the closest negatives can collapse the embedding. InfoNCE answers the first by scoring one positive against all the negatives at once, each weighted by a softmax; as τ → 0 it becomes max(0, maxj sij − sii)/τ, a margin-free hinge on the hardest negative alone derived. Listwise ranking losses are the same softmax over a list: with one relevant item ListNet's top-one loss is InfoNCE ({{Loss functions, "Ranking losses"|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}).

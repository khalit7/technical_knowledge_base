Self-supervised learning trains a network on unlabelled data by making the data supply its own targets. Contrastive learning is the most common way to do it for representations: take two things that should mean the same (two augmentations of one image, an image and its caption, a sentence and its paraphrase), pull their embeddings together, and push them away from everything else in the batch. The pairing itself is the label; nobody annotates anything.

The parent page compares this paradigm with supervised training at one-screen level and shows that its loss is cross entropy again (Topic: ml-fundamentals, Reading tab: "Beyond one network", and thread 1, "Everything is cross entropy"). This page owns the details: what the loss does to the gradient, why it needs negatives, how methods without negatives avoid collapsing to a constant, what masked modelling and prediction in representation space change, and why the objective, not the backbone, makes an embedding model.

The pairing is the label

Before contrastive learning, self-supervision meant inventing a pretext task whose answer the data already holds: tell surrogate classes apart, where each class is one image under many transformations (Dosovitskiy et al. 2014); predict where a second patch sits relative to the first (Doersch et al. 2015); solve a jigsaw puzzle of tiles (Noroozi and Favaro 2016); recognise which 2-D rotation was applied (Gidaris et al. 2018). Each task was a proxy, and the features were good only insofar as the proxy happened to need them.

Instance discrimination (Wu et al. 2018) turned the proxy into the goal: treat every image as its own class, store each image's feature in a memory bank rather than in a classifier's weights, and use noise-contrastive estimation to cope with a million classes. From there the target is the geometry itself: views of one thing close, different things apart. The geometry has older roots: a margin loss that pulls similar pairs together and pushes dissimilar pairs apart only while they are closer than a radius (Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun 2006), and FaceNet's triplet loss, which separates a matching pair from a non-matching face by a margin (Schroff et al. 2015). Both compare one pair or triplet at a time; InfoNCE compares one positive against many negatives at once.

InfoNCE, the workhorse loss

Li = −log exp(sim(zi, zi+) / τ)Σk=1..N exp(sim(zi, zk) / τ)
zi
the anchor's embedding; zi+ its positive; the sum runs over the positive and the N − 1 negatives
sim
cosine similarity, the dot product of l2-normalised vectors, so every logit lies in [−1/τ, 1/τ]
τ
the temperature, a fixed hyperparameter or (in CLIP) learned

It is the softmax cross entropy of an N-way classification whose classes are the candidates in the batch and whose right answer is the positive (van den Oord et al. 2018, CPC, §2.3). The cross-entropy view is on the parent page and on Loss functions; temperature as a softmax knob is on Activation functions. What is special here follows from three facts.

1 · The gradient goes where the softmax puts its mass

Write sij for the cosine between anchor i and candidate j and pij for the softmax over sij/τ. Differentiating the loss gives derived:

∂Li/∂sij = (pij − 1[j is the positive]) / τ

The positive is pulled up by (1 − ppositive)/τ and each negative is pushed down by pij/τ; the pushes add up to the pull. So the negatives are not treated alike: each is pushed in proportion to how similar it already is, softmax-weighted. The temperature sets how sharply: as τ falls, the weight concentrates on the hardest negatives; as τ rises, every negative gets the same small push. Wang and Liu (2021) call this the hardness-aware property: "the temperature τ controls the strength of penalties on hard negative samples". Too low a temperature also pushes apart things that genuinely belong together, their "uniformity-tolerance dilemma". SimCLR's ImageNet sweep found τ = 0.1 best of {0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1} at batch 4,096 (Chen et al. 2020, Table 5), 0.5 best on CIFAR-10 (Appendix B); SimCSE uses 0.05 (Gao et al. 2021, Table D.1); CLIP learns it, starting from 0.07 and capping the logit scale at 100 (see the CLIP paper page).

See it on real data: the One real batch tab runs this loss on 48 real sentence pairs, with sliders for τ and the batch size, and shows for any anchor what share of the push its hardest negative takes. At SimCSE's τ = 0.05 the hardest negative alone takes 44% of the push on average after contrastive training (20% before); at τ = 1 it takes 3%, close to an even split of 1/47. The CLIP paper page has the complementary toy: a tiny CLIP's 8 × 8 similarity matrix, live, where you can swap captions.

2 · The batch is the task

A model that knows nothing puts probability 1/N on the positive and scores ln N, so the batch size changes the task itself, not only the gradient noise (the parent page's correction makes the same point). CPC proves that the loss bounds the mutual information between the two views from below: I ≥ ln N − L, "which becomes tighter as N becomes larger" (§2.3). The bound can never exceed ln N (Poole et al. 2019: "upper bounded by log K", with K the batch size), so ln 4,096 = 8.3 nats is the most a SimCLR batch can certify derived.

Correction to the old page
"Maximises a lower bound on mutual information" is true but often over-read. The bound is capped at ln N, it is loose when the true information is larger, and later work credits InfoNCE's success to the geometry it produces (next section) rather than to information it measures (Wang and Isola 2020). Also, in SimCLR each anchor has 2(N − 1) negatives, not N − 1: both augmented views of every other image count ("we treat the other 2(N−1) augmented examples within a minibatch as negative examples", §2.1).

Hence the engineering that buys negatives:

  • Big batches. SimCLR trains at batch 4,096 by default and sweeps 256 to 8,192 (§2.2); CLIP uses 32,768 (Radford et al. 2021).
  • A memory bank of every image's last-seen feature (Wu et al. 2018): many negatives, but stale ones, computed by older versions of the encoder.
  • A queue with a momentum encoder (MoCo, He et al. 2019): keys come from a second encoder whose weights are a moving average of the query encoder's, so the queue stays consistent; K = 65,536 keys, momentum m = 0.999 ("works much better than a smaller value"), τ = 0.07. The number of negatives no longer depends on the batch.
  • A sigmoid instead of the softmax. SigLIP scores every image-text pair as an independent binary decision, so the loss "does not require a global view of the pairwise similarities for normalization" (Zhai et al. 2023); the CLIP paper page animates softmax against sigmoid on one batch.

3 · Negatives are sampled, so some are wrong

Random negatives include items that share the anchor's meaning or class, and the loss pushes them away anyway. Chuang et al. (2020) correct the objective for these false negatives; Robinson et al. (2021) go the other way and sample hard negatives on purpose, with a knob for how hard. The real batch has a natural false negative: "A woman is dancing in the rain." and "A man and woman are dancing in rain." are separate pairs, so each is the other's negative.

The SimCLR recipe, which most image methods inherit

SimCLR (Chen et al. 2020) names the loss NT-Xent ("normalized temperature-scaled cross entropy") and finds three things matter. Augmentation: "the combination of random crop and color distortion is crucial" (§3). A projection head: a small MLP g maps the representation h to the z the loss sees; a nonlinear head beats a linear one by about 3% and no head by more than 10% (§4.2), and after training "we throw away the projection head" and use h. Scale: large batches and long training. Linear evaluation on ImageNet reaches 69.3% top-1 with a ResNet-50 and 76.5% with a ResNet-50 four times wider, "matching the performance of a supervised ResNet-50"; fine-tuned on 1% of the labels it reaches 85.8% top-5 (Table 6 and abstract).

Correction
SimCLR's often-quoted 76.5% is for ResNet-50 (4×), a network with four times the width; the plain ResNet-50 gets 69.3%. And the 1%-label result, 85.8%, is top-5 accuracy, not top-1.

Alignment and uniformity: what the loss actually builds

Wang and Isola (2020) split what InfoNCE optimises on the unit hypersphere into two measurable properties, and show that as the number of negatives grows the loss optimises exactly these two:

ℒalign = E(x, y) positive ‖f(x) − f(y)‖2     ℒuniform = log Ex, y e−2‖f(x) − f(y)‖2
align
positives should land close: lower is better, 0 when the two views coincide
uniform
all embeddings should spread over the sphere: lower is better; 0 when every point is the same (the exponent is then 0 for every pair)
α, t
the exponents 2 and 2 are the paper's defaults (lalign(x, y, alpha=2), lunif(x, t=2))

The numerator of InfoNCE rewards alignment and the denominator rewards uniformity, and "directly optimizing for these two metrics leads to comparable or better performance than contrastive learning" (abstract). Seen this way, collapse is not mysterious: alignment alone is minimised perfectly by mapping everything to one point, and only the uniformity half stops it.

The "perfectly even circle" uniformity is log(e−4I0(4)) = −1.575 derived, since ‖u − v‖2 = 2 − 2 cos θ on the circle. A 2-D output is far too small for real use: the trained circle's 5-NN accuracy is about 50%, against 90% for the raw pixels toy; it is drawn because a circle can be seen.

Two refinements matter in practice. Dimensional collapse: even with negatives, embeddings can end up spanning a lower-dimensional subspace than they have room for (Jing et al. 2021: "dimensional collapse also happens in contrastive learning"), which is why the Collapse lab reports an effective rank beside the std. Pretrained language-model embeddings are anisotropic: they occupy "a narrow cone in the vector space", so that in GPT-2's last layer "two random words will on average have almost perfect cosine similarity" (Ethayarajh 2019). That is a uniformity failure, measured on real sentences in the One real batch tab: random pairs of STS-B sentences have a mean cosine of 0.62 under BERT and 0.32 after SimCSE, while SimCSE's alignment is slightly worse (0.25 against 0.17), exactly SimCSE's own reading: "pre-trained embeddings have good alignment, their uniformity is poor" (§7, Figure 3). The same narrow cone is why BERTScore offers a rescaled score (Evaluation metrics, which runs BERTScore on real embeddings).

Without negatives: collapse and the defences against it

Negatives are expensive (big batches, queues) and some are false. Remove them and the obvious objective, "two views of an input should have the same embedding", has a perfect and useless solution: a constant. Every method without negatives is a different way to make that solution unreachable or unstable. Each defence below is ablated in the Collapse lab, which trains one encoder eleven ways and plays the runs side by side.

Asymmetry: BYOL and SimSiam

BYOL (Grill et al. 2020) uses "two neural networks, referred to as online and target networks". The online network has an extra predictor MLP and is trained to predict the target network's projection of the other view; the target network gets no gradient and is an exponential moving average of the online weights, with rate starting at τbase = 0.996 and increased to 1 on a cosine schedule (§3.3). "While state-of-the art methods rely on negative pairs, BYOL achieves a new state of the art without them": 74.3% ImageNet linear top-1 with a ResNet-50 and 79.6% with a larger ResNet.

SimSiam (Chen and He 2020) strips BYOL down: no moving average, one shared encoder, a predictor on one branch and a stop-gradient on the other. "A stop-gradient operation plays an essential role in preventing collapsing": removing it alone drops ImageNet accuracy to 0.1%, and removing the predictor also fails (§4.1, §4.2, Table 1a). To detect collapse the authors monitor the standard deviation of the l2-normalised output, which is about 1/√d for spread-out outputs and 0 when collapsed. SimSiam works from batch 64 to 4,096 (66.1% at 64, 68.1% at 256, 64.0% at 4,096; Table 2) and reaches 68.1% at 100 epochs, 71.3% at 800 (Table 4).

Correction
SimSiam's 71.3% ImageNet linear accuracy is its 800-epoch figure; at the 100 epochs most of its ablations use it is 68.1%.

Why asymmetry is enough is a dynamics question, not a property of the loss: the constant is still a minimum. Tian, Chen and Ganguli (2021) analyse "how multiple factors, like predictor networks, stop-gradients, exponential moving averages, and weight decay all come into play", and derive a predictor that can be set directly from the data's statistics (DirectPred). In the lab, the SimSiam run with stop-gradient ends with embedding std 0.24 (1/√16 = 0.25 is spread out) and the run without it at 0.001, on the same encoder, predictor and loss.

Centering and sharpening: DINO

DINO (Caron et al. 2021) is self-distillation with no labels: a student network matches the softmax output of a momentum teacher (momentum following a cosine schedule from 0.996 to 1) across different crops. Two operations on the teacher's output prevent collapse, and each alone causes a different one: "centering prevents one dimension to dominate but encourages collapse to the uniform distribution, while the sharpening has the opposite effect" (§3.1). Centering subtracts a running mean of the teacher's outputs; sharpening uses a low teacher temperature, warmed up from 0.04 to 0.07 over the first 30 epochs, against 0.1 for the student. A centre that updates too slowly (momentum 0.999) collapses; 0.9 gave the best k-NN accuracy in the ablation (Appendix). With a ViT-B/8, DINO reaches 80.1% linear and 77.4% k-NN top-1 on ImageNet (Table 2). The collapse measures DINO uses are the teacher output's entropy (0 when one prototype wins, ln K when uniform) and its KL divergence from the student; the lab shows the entropy and how far the batch-mean output is from uniform.

DINOv2 (Oquab et al. 2023) scales this with data curation: "a small but diverse corpus of 142M images" (LVD-142M) and a ViT-g backbone of 1.1B parameters, reaching 86.5% linear top-1 on ImageNet-1k with frozen features (Table 4), which made it a default frozen backbone for dense vision tasks. DINOv3 (Siméoni et al. 2025) trains "a DINO model with 7B parameters" on 1,689 million curated images (LVD-1689M) and adds Gram anchoring to stop dense feature maps degrading during long training.

Statistics of the batch: Barlow Twins and VICReg

Barlow Twins (Zbontar et al. 2021) computes the cross-correlation matrix between the two views' embeddings over the batch and pushes it towards the identity: diagonal 1 (invariance), off-diagonal 0 (no redundancy between dimensions). VICReg (Bardes, Ponce and LeCun 2021) separates the three jobs into three terms: invariance (mean squared distance between the views), variance (a hinge keeping each dimension's standard deviation over the batch above γ = 1) and covariance (off-diagonal covariances pushed to 0), weighted λ = μ = 25 and ν = 1. These use the batch, but as a statistic, not as negatives; the variance hinge alone is what blocks the constant, and dropping it in the lab collapses the run to a single point after a few hundred steps, in all three seeds.

Masked modelling, and prediction in representation space

The other road needs no pairs at all: hide part of the input and predict it. A constant output cannot win here, because the target is the data. BERT did this for text, masking 15% of tokens (BERT paper page, which runs a toy BERT live). For images the question is what to predict:

  • Discrete visual tokens (BEiT, Bao et al. 2021): "recover the original visual tokens based on the corrupted image patches", the tokens coming from a separately trained image tokenizer.
  • Pixels (MAE, He et al. 2021; SimMIM, Xie et al. 2021, which "predicts raw pixel values of the randomly masked patches by a lightweight one-layer head" with an l1 loss).
  • Representations produced by a slowly updated copy of the encoder (data2vec, Baevski et al. 2022, the same recipe for speech, text and images; I-JEPA, Assran et al. 2023).

MAE rests on two choices. It masks "a high proportion of the input image, e.g., 75%", because images are redundant enough that a lower ratio is solved by interpolation (BERT's "typical masking ratio is 15%", §4.1); and its encoder "operates only on the visible subset of patches (without mask tokens), along with a lightweight decoder", so pretraining runs "3x or more" faster. The loss is the mean squared error on masked patches only, and predicting each patch's pixels normalised by that patch's own mean and variance "improves representation quality" (§3). A ViT-Huge pretrained this way reaches 87.8% on ImageNet-1K after fine-tuning. I-JEPA keeps the masking but changes the target: predict the representations of several large target blocks from one context block, so the model is never asked for texture it cannot know. It trains a ViT-H/14 "using 16 A100 GPUs in under 72 hours", under 1,200 GPU hours and "over 10x more efficient than a ViT-H/14 pretrained with MAE".

V-JEPA (Bardes et al. 2024) applies the same idea to video, "trained on 2 million videos collected from public datasets" with no pretrained image encoder, text, negatives or reconstruction. V-JEPA 2 (Assran et al. 2025) pretrains on "over 1 million hours of internet video" with encoders up to 1 billion parameters, then adds V-JEPA 2-AC, "a latent action-conditioned world model" trained on "less than 62 hours of unlabeled robot videos", deployed zero-shot on Franka robot arms. (The old page said V-JEPA 2 "extends to video"; V-JEPA was already a video model, and V-JEPA 2 adds scale and action conditioning.) The joint-embedding predictive architecture was proposed by Yann LeCun in "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence" (version 0.9.2, 27 June 2022, as I-JEPA cites it; the page itself could not be fetched here) as a route to world models.

The trend the old page named still describes the field: the split is no longer "contrastive or not" but joint-embedding methods (contrastive, self-distillation, JEPA, which compare or predict embeddings) against masked generative methods (MAE, which reconstruct the input), with prediction in latent space increasingly favoured when the goal is semantic features. Both camps now meet in practice: DINOv2 adds a masked-image objective to self-distillation ("We randomly mask some of the input patches given to the student, but not to the teacher", the iBOT loss), and I-JEPA borrows MAE's masking and BYOL's moving-average target.

The objective makes the embedding model

The clean way to slice architectures here is by what the training objective forces the model to output.

  • Contrastive objectives produce encoders. The target is one embedding vector per input, compared with other embeddings; nothing is generated token by token, so no decoder is needed. That is why CLIP's image tower, sentence-embedding models (SBERT, SimCSE, retrieval embedders) and reranker backbones are encoders.
  • Generative objectives need decoders. Next-token prediction (GPT) or denoising with generation (T5) must emit sequences, so they need an autoregressive decoder, and their representations are optimised for predicting the next token, not for geometric comparability.
  • BERT sits between: an encoder trained with a generative-style objective (masked-token prediction) rather than a contrastive one. Its raw embeddings are poor for similarity until contrastively fine-tuned, which is exactly the step that turns an encoder into an embedding model.

The last point is measurable. SimCSE (Gao, Yao and Chen 2021) needs no labels at all: "we pass the same sentence to the pre-trained encoder twice: by applying the standard dropout twice, we can obtain two different embeddings as 'positive pairs'", with in-batch negatives, τ = 0.05 and batch 64. On the STS benchmark test set, the Spearman correlation between cosine and human similarity scores rises from 53.87 (BERT, Table 5) to 76.85, and the 7-task average reaches 76.3, 81.6 with supervised NLI pairs. This page recomputes both from the released checkpoints (76.84 and 53.87, plus Su et al.'s 59.04 for a different BERT pooling): see One real batch. Before contrastive training, post-processing was the fix on offer: BERT-flow maps the "non-smooth anisotropic semantic space of sentences" to an isotropic one with a normalising flow (Li et al. 2020).

In 2026 contrastive training is the standard post-training recipe for embedding and retrieval models, applied to an encoder and, increasingly, to a decoder-only LLM with pooling. The objective, not the backbone, is what makes it an embedding model; decoder LLMs are contrastively adapted precisely because next-token pretraining alone does not give a well-shaped similarity space. Common mistakes

Reading a lower InfoNCE loss across batch sizes as a better model
Chance is ln N, so a loss at batch 256 and one at batch 4,096 are on different scales. Compare ln N − L, or better, a downstream probe.
Using the projection head's output downstream
SimCLR's representation is h, before the head; the head's output z is over 10% worse on linear evaluation (§4.2). SimCSE similarly drops its training-time MLP for unsupervised models.
Trusting the loss to detect collapse
A collapsed SimSiam or VICReg run has an excellent loss. Monitor the std of the normalised embedding (about 1/√d when healthy) and an effective rank, as the Collapse lab does.
Treating every in-batch item as a true negative
Duplicates, paraphrases and same-class items are pushed apart too. Deduplicate, use larger and more diverse batches, or a debiased loss; for retrieval data, mine hard negatives but filter the ones that are actually positives.
Comparing cosine scores across embedding models
Each model has its own baseline: random sentences score about 0.62 under raw BERT and 0.32 after SimCSE (One real batch). A threshold only means something for the model it was tuned on.
Calling "first-last avg." one thing
Su et al.'s BERT first-last average scores 59.04 on STS-B test, and SimCSE's own Table 5 baseline under the same name scores 53.87; this page reproduces 59.04 by averaging the first and last transformer layers and 53.87 by averaging the embedding layer with the last one. Pooling details move results by several points.
Expecting MAE features to work frozen
MAE's headline numbers are after fine-tuning (87.8% for ViT-Huge). Self-distillation features (DINOv2) are the ones built to be used frozen, with a linear probe.

One real batch: InfoNCE on real sentence embeddings

Forty-eight pairs of sentences that human raters scored as meaning the same thing (gold 4.5 to 5 out of 5), drawn from the test split of the STS benchmark. Each first sentence is an anchor; its partner is the positive; the other partners in the batch are the negatives. The cosines are real: the same BERT-base before and after contrastive training (unsupervised SimCSE), computed offline (src/real_embeddings.py). Everything else, the softmax, the loss, the gradient, is computed in your browser from the formula on the Reading tab.

Try: at the defaults (τ = 0.05, all 48 pairs) switch between the two models: the matrix looks alike, but the loss falls from about 0.29 to 0.04 and the hardest negative's share of the push doubles. Then push the temperature down to 0.01, where the gradient piles onto the one or two hardest negatives, and up to 1, where every negative gets the same small push and the loss sits near ln N. What the numbers say

Spearman correlation (× 100) between cosine and the human score over all 1,379 test pairs; alignment over pairs scored 4 or more; uniformity and mean cosine over random pairs of test sentences (both defined on the Reading tab). "First-last avg" averages the token vectors of the first and last transformer layers (Su et al.'s pooling, 59.04 in SimCSE Table B.2); "embedding + last avg" averages the embedding layer's output with the last layer and reproduces SimCSE's own Table 5 baseline, 53.87; SimCSE's unsupervised model is read at the last layer's [CLS] vector without its training-time MLP, as the authors' repository does.

Read the matrix like this
  • This batch is easy. The pairs are near-paraphrases that share most of their words, so even raw BERT ranks 47 of the 48 positives first. What contrastive training changes is the margin: at τ = 0.05 the mean loss falls from 0.29 to 0.04 nats, and the loss-against-temperature curve sits lower at every τ.
  • Before contrastive training random sentences already have a cosine near 0.6 (the anisotropy of the Reading tab) and positives about 0.92. After, the negatives fall to about 0.3 while the positives stay near 0.9: the gap, divided by τ, is what the softmax sees.
  • The push concentrates. Averaged over the 48 anchors at τ = 0.05, the hardest negative takes 20% of the push under BERT and 44% after SimCSE: once most negatives are far away, the few hard ones get the gradient.
  • A false negative. The only anchor either model gets wrong is row 31, "A woman is dancing in the rain.": both rank another pair's sentence, "A man and woman are dancing in rain.", above its own positive, "A woman dances in the rain out side." (cosines 0.92 and 0.87 after SimCSE). InfoNCE pushes those two apart, though a reader would call them near-paraphrases.

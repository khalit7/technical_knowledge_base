Ranking losses: only the order counts

A search engine scores each document i for a query, si = f(xi), and shows them sorted. The labels are relevance grades, and the metric only looks at the order, usually NDCG: each document's gain 2l − 1 divided by log(1 + its rank), summed, over the best possible sum (Burges 2010, eq. 5; the log's base cancels in the ratio). Three ways to turn that into a loss:

  • Pairwise. For each pair where i should rank above j, penalise the score difference. PyTorch's MarginRankingLoss is the hinge version, max(0, −y·(x1 − x2) + margin), margin 0 by default (PyTorch 2.14 docstring). RankNet is the logistic version: model P(i above j) = 1/(1 + e−σ(si − sj)) and take cross entropy, which for a known order is C = log(1 + e−σ(si − sj)), the logistic margin loss above with m = si − sj; "when si = sj, the cost is log 2" (Burges 2010, section 2).
  • Listwise. A softmax over the whole list's scores, cross entropy against the target distribution (ListNet, with its top-one probability: Cao et al. 2007). With exactly one relevant item in the list this is InfoNCE, the loss contrastive learning runs over a batch ({{Contrastive and self-supervised learning|n:3c65c17b0d0d811db6a7d02d0ee7215c}}).
  • Metric-weighted (LambdaRank). NDCG is "everywhere either discontinuous or flat" in the scores, so it has no useful gradient. LambdaRank keeps RankNet's gradient on each pair, λij = −σ/(1 + eσ(si − sj)), and multiplies it by |ΔNDCG|, the change in NDCG from swapping the two; "experiments have shown" this "gives very good results" (eq. 6). LambdaMART is its boosted-tree version; "an ensemble of LambdaMART rankers won Track 1 of the 2010 Yahoo! Learning To Rank Challenge" (Burges 2010).

Why pairwise is not enough, in numbers. Ten documents, relevant at ranks 2 and 10, the rest irrelevant illustrative: 9 pairs are in the wrong order, NDCG 0.564. Fix A lifts the bottom relevant document to rank 5: 4 wrong pairs, NDCG 0.624. Fix B swaps the top two: 8 wrong pairs, NDCG 0.790. A pairwise loss prefers A; the user, and NDCG, prefer B computed here. Burges's Figure 1 makes the same point (13 pairwise errors reduced to 11 by a move that hurts the top of the list).

The triplet loss, max{d(a, p) − d(a, n) + margin, 0}, is the same idea for embeddings: rank the matching pair above the non-matching one for an anchor (PyTorch's TripletMarginLoss: margin 1, p = 2 by default). Its margin, the choice of negatives and the collapse that hard negatives cause are owned by {{Contrastive and self-supervised learning|n:3c65c17b0d0d811db6a7d02d0ee7215c}} ("Pairs and triplets: the margin losses"), which runs it on a real batch.

The level of the logits: what cross entropy ignores

Because the cross-entropy gradient p − y sums to zero over the classes, adding the same constant c to every logit changes neither the softmax nor the loss. The overall level, log Z = log Σj ezj, is free to drift, and in large models it does. Two fixes act on the logits themselves: z-loss adds 10−4·log2 Z to pull the level to log Z = 0 (PaLM), and soft-capping bounds them, soft_cap·tanh(logits / soft_cap) (Gemma 2: 50 in attention, 30 on the final layer). Both, with the instabilities they prevent and measured runs, are owned by {{Normalisation, residuals and stability|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8163b50dc5e9d9240e89}} (section 5 for z-loss, section 4 for soft-capping).

Real logits from three small models: where the level sits, and what bf16 storage does to it (measured)

PaLM added "an auxiliary loss of z_loss = 10−4 · log2 Z to encourage the softmax normalizer log(Z) to be close to 0, which we found increases the stability of training" (Chowdhery et al. 2022). Its gradient on logit i is 2·10−4·log Z·softmaxi, a push of every logit towards a level where log Z = 0 and each logit is its log-probability. Wortsman et al. reproduced the failure it prevents, output logits diverging late in training, in small models trained without weight decay at high learning rates, and found z-loss resolves it (weight decay also helped in their larger models; Wortsman et al. 2023). Which landmark models used it is in the parent's {{Defaults across models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}} tab.

Why the level matters at all is number formats: bfloat16 keeps 8 significant bits, so the gap between neighbouring values grows with magnitude (0.0078 near 1, 0.125 near 24, 0.5 near 100) derived. Logits stored in bf16 at a high level lose the small differences the softmax depends on. Slide the shift c added to every logit: cross entropy does not move, z-loss does, and so does the damage bf16 storage does to the probabilities.

Between distributions and between models

  • KL divergence, DKL(P‖Q) = Σ P log(P/Q): asymmetric and not a metric. It is the regulariser in VAEs (keep the code near its prior), the distillation loss (the student matches the teacher) and the RLHF anchor (the policy stays near its reference). Which direction a small student should minimise, forward or reverse, is "Forward or reverse KL" on {{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}. In PyTorch, KLDivLoss takes the model's log-probabilities first and the target second, and its default reduction='mean' "doesn't return the true KL divergence value"; use 'batchmean' (PyTorch).
  • Penalties (L1 or lasso, L2 or ridge, elastic net) are added to the loss but are not task losses: L1 drives weights to exactly zero (sparsity, feature selection), L2 shrinks them without zeroing any, elastic net combines both. Details on {{Regularisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e988b5c3cb5e197f98}}.
  • Preference losses (Bradley-Terry reward models, DPO and its family) score a preferred answer against a rejected one, a pairwise ranking loss on two answers; "Direct preference optimisation and its family" on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}} runs one preference pair through eight of them. The {{CLIP|n:3c65c17b0d0d8194a85dfc5f3bf3f799}} paper page runs a contrastive loss live.

The adversarial loss, and the fix in the same paper

A GAN trains a generator G and a discriminator D against each other in one minimax game, minG maxD E[log D(x)] + E[log(1 − D(G(z)))]. The original paper already warned that this form starves the generator: "Early in learning, when G is poor, D can reject samples with high confidence ... In this case, log(1 − D(G(z))) saturates. Rather than training G to minimize log(1 − D(G(z))) we can train G to maximize log D(G(z))", which has the same fixed point "but provides much stronger gradients early in learning" (Goodfellow et al. 2014, section 3). This is the non-saturating loss most GANs train with.

The minimax gradient vanishes exactly when the generator is worst

Gradients with respect to the discriminator's logit l on the sample, D = σ(l): minimax d/dl log(1 − σ(l)) = −D; non-saturating d/dl (−log σ(l)) = −(1 − D) derived, checked with PyTorch autograd. At D = 0.01 the ratio is (1 − D)/D = 99.

Later losses change the game itself: least-squares GANs replace the log loss with squared error, because the sigmoid cross-entropy loss "may lead to the vanishing gradients problem", and minimising it amounts to the Pearson χ2 divergence (Mao et al. 2017); Wasserstein GANs minimise the earth mover's distance with a Lipschitz critic, enforced in the first paper by weight clipping, which its authors call "a clearly terrible way to enforce a Lipschitz constraint" (Arjovsky et al. 2017). Common mistakes

A softmax (or sigmoid) before a loss that expects logits
CrossEntropyLoss applies log-softmax itself, and BCEWithLogitsLoss the sigmoid; applying them twice squashes the scores and shrinks every gradient. Use the plain BCELoss on probabilities only when you have no logits; the logits version is the numerically stable one (PyTorch).
δ in the wrong units, or SmoothL1 taken for Huber
Huber's δ (and log-cosh's built-in bend at about 1) is measured in the target's units: standardise the target or pick δ from the residual scale. SmoothL1Loss(beta) is HuberLoss(delta) divided by β; with β ≠ 1 the gradients differ by that factor.
MAPE with zeros or tiny targets
Use MAE on a scale that makes sense, MSE on log(y), or a weighted error.
Reading focal or hinge scores as probabilities
Recalibrate (temperature or Platt scaling) on held-out data first.
KLDivLoss with its arguments swapped or its default reduction
The first argument is the model's log-probabilities; 'mean' averages over every element, not over examples; 'batchmean' gives the KL.
Comparing loss values across set-ups
A smoothed target has entropy above 0, so its loss has a floor no model goes below; class weights change what "mean" divides by; per-micro-batch averages differ from a token mean. Compare under the same target, weights and reduction, or compare a metric instead. And check the starting loss: an untrained K-class classifier should score about ln K (step 3 of {{Topic: ml-fundamentals|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}}).

The idea in one sentence. A loss decides two things at once: what the model learns to predict (a mean, a median, a quantile, a probability, a ranking or only a decision) and which examples drive the gradient; read the first from the noise model the loss assumes, and the second from its pull, minus the gradient one example puts on the prediction.

Read a loss by its pull: who drags the model, and how hard
Regression pull against the error u = y − ŷ
Classification pull against the margin m = y·f(x)

Regression: under MSE a point twice as far off pulls twice as hard, so the largest errors steer the fit; Huber caps the pull at δ (here 1) and MAE gives every point the same pull of 1, which is why both resist bad targets. Classification: every classification loss pulls hardest on wrong examples (m < 0) and lets go of right ones, but at different rates: the exponential loss's pull explodes on a mislabelled point, hinge drops to exactly 0 once m ≥ 1, and focal loss (γ = 2) silences easy examples far sooner than cross entropy. Every curve is the exact derivative, drawn from the page's checked formulas derived.

You need

Maximum likelihood ({{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}), entropy, cross-entropy and KL as quantities ({{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}), and where the loss sits in one training step (step 3 of {{Topic: ml-fundamentals|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}}).

New terms
  • Error u = y − ŷ, target minus prediction; margin m = y·f(x) with y ∈ {−1, +1}, positive when right.
  • Pull ψ(u) = ρ′(u): minus the gradient of one example's loss with respect to the prediction (or the score).
  • Consistent for a statistic: the loss's expected value is lowest at that statistic (MSE at the mean, MAE at the median). Strictly proper: a loss on probabilities lowest only at the true probability.
  • Leverage point: an input far from the others along x, as opposed to an outlying target.
  • Logit: a score before the softmax; log Z: the log of the softmax's normaliser.

"What the model learns to predict" is what minimises the expected loss for each input; scikit-learn's guide lists the same pairs as "strictly consistent scoring functions" (scikit-learn, model evaluation). The rule when the loss is also the metric: train with a loss consistent for the quantity you will be scored on.

How a batch becomes one number

Strictly, the loss is one example's error and the cost its aggregate, usually the mean; in practice the words are used interchangeably. The aggregate hides two traps.

  • Reduction. PyTorch losses return one value per element with reduction='none', their sum with 'sum', and by default their mean. With class weights, cross entropy's "mean" divides by the sum of the weights of the examples, not by their number; and targets equal to ignore_index (default −100) are left out of both the sum and the count (PyTorch, CrossEntropyLoss).
  • Token-level means in language models. A language model's loss is the mean over all non-padding tokens. Averaging per micro-batch and then over micro-batches is not the same number when micro-batches hold different numbers of tokens; Hugging Face fixed exactly this in its Trainer's gradient accumulation on 16 October 2024 by summing and dividing by the total token count (Hugging Face). Which tokens count at all (prompt, padding, packed neighbours) is in "Padding, packing, and how tokens are weighted" on {{Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}.

Regression: each loss is a noise model, and picks a statistic

Every regression loss is a function ρ(u) of the error, and its derivative ψ(u) is the pull. MSE is maximum likelihood for Gaussian noise of fixed variance and MAE for Laplace noise (the recipe that turns a noise distribution into its loss, with each derivation: {{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}, section 7).

The pull separates the losses; the second derivative is what a Newton step needs

MAPE is drawn for a target y = 1, where it equals MAE; for other targets divide by |y|. Huber's second derivative is 0 beyond δ, log-cosh's decays as 4e−2|u|, MAE's and pinball's are 0 everywhere except a kink. Values and pulls checked against PyTorch autograd.

PyTorch's HuberLoss(delta) is the formula above; SmoothL1Loss(beta) is the same curve divided by β, so the two differ by a factor of δ and agree only at δ = 1 (PyTorch, SmoothL1Loss). The rule of thumb: MSE by default, MAE or Huber when outliers are real data, log-cosh (or pseudo-Huber) when a smooth second derivative is needed.

Two claims about log-cosh, checked

"Cheaper than Huber": not in arithmetic. Huber is one comparison and a multiply; log-cosh needs an exponential and a logarithm (computed stably as |u| + log(1 + e−2|u|) − log 2). "Second-order methods want it": half right. Huber's second derivative is exactly 0 beyond δ, so a leaf of large errors has nothing to divide by; XGBoost ships pseudo-Huber for this reason, "a twice differentiable alternative to absolute loss" (XGBoost parameters). But log-cosh's second derivative, 1/cosh2(u), also falls towards 0 for large errors (about 4e−2|u|), so it softens the problem rather than removing it derived.

Which number each loss picks

Before any model, ask what single number each loss would predict for a whole sample: that is the statistic the loss estimates, and where a model trained on it will sit for every input. The sample is real and skewed: the lengths of 141 major North American rivers, in miles (R's rivers, from the USGS via the World Almanac 1975).

The long rivers drag the mean up; MAPE lands below the median

Each curve is the mean loss for a constant forecast c, scaled to its own range; the dot is its minimum; log-cosh's curve lies on top of MAE's. Exact minimisers: the mean; the median; the τ-quantile and MAPE's weighted median (weights 1/y) by sorting; Huber and log-cosh by solving Σψ(y − c) = 0. All checked against PyTorch losses evaluated on a 40,000-point grid (src/recompute.py). The 0.5-mile river is illustrative.

On the rivers the mean is 591 miles and the median 425. MAPE picks 336, below the median, because dividing by y makes short rivers count for more; Gneiting proved this in general: MAPE is consistent for the "median of order −1", the median of a distribution reweighted by 1/y, which "tends to support severe underforecasts" (Gneiting 2011). Log-cosh lands on 425.2, the median to a fraction of a mile, because every river is hundreds of units away from any reasonable c, deep in its linear part.

What an outlier does to a fit, step by step

Anscombe built four small datasets with the same means, variances, correlation and least-squares line, y = 3.00 + 0.500x (Anscombe 1973). His third set is ten points on an almost perfect line plus one point far above it. Fig. 2 puts the points on the floor with the fitted line; each point's loss stands on it as a column, and green dots run from the line towards each point, as long as that point's pull on the line.

Predict, then watch: how far will one bad point move the MSE slope from the clean 0.345? Then press "Unusual inputs", where the outliers sit at unusual inputs instead.

  1. Ten clean points. Anscombe's set III without its outlier: MSE's line is y = 4.01 + 0.345x, and every column is nearly flat. The side column is the total loss, the number the fit minimises.
  2. One bad target arrives. On Anscombe III the outlier at (13, 12.74) is a bad target, u = 4.24 above the line. Under MSE its loss is u2 = 18.0, the one tall column, and its pull 2u is the only one long enough to see.
  3. MSE gives way. Under MSE its pull is 2u, so the line moves to the minimum of the total, slope 0.500 (Anscombe's line): the total falls from 18.01 to 13.76, of which the outlier still holds 76%. To shrink one big square, MSE puts every other point off the line: their columns rise from the floor.
  4. Huber caps the pull. Under Huber with δ = 1 its pull is capped at 1 and its loss grows only linearly (3.74 at the clean line), so the slope only moves to 0.393; the dashed line is MSE's.
  5. MAE ignores it. |u| gives every point a pull of exactly 1, so the ten clean points outvote the outlier: the slope stays at 0.345, through the ten clean points. The outlier keeps its whole error, 4.24 of a total of 4.26.
  6. Three minima. MSE 0.500, Huber 0.393, MAE 0.345: the faster a loss's pull grows with the error, the further one bad target drags the line. Log-cosh behaves like Huber (0.394), because the residuals here are in the units where its bend sits.
  1. 43 stars on the main sequence. On the 43 main-sequence stars every loss finds a rising line: MSE's slope is 2.05, MAE's 2.50.
  2. Four red giants arrive. At the main-sequence line their columns tower over the others (the largest MSE loss is 10.3) and their pulls are the longest.
  3. MSE turns over. The minimum has slope −0.41: the line swings up to the giants, and the total falls from 40.8 to 14.3, of which the giants now hold only 13%. The fit gives up the main sequence to fit four stars.
  4. Huber turns over too. Its pull is capped at δ = 1, yet the slope goes to −0.42.
  5. MAE turns over furthest. Every pull is exactly 1, and the slope goes to −0.69, worse than MSE (log-cosh: −0.42) computed here.
  6. The lever. A point's effect on the slope is its pull times its distance from the mean x. Here the columns are each star's lever |ψ(u)·(x − x̄)| at MAE's line: the giants are 9% of the stars and hold 30% of the lever. A capped pull does not cap a lever.

Defaults reproduce Anscombe's line, y = 3.00 + 0.500x for set III, independently (MSE fit here: 3.0025 + 0.4997x). Every fit is the exact minimiser (closed form for MSE, IRLS for Huber and log-cosh, a line through two data points for MAE), checked against PyTorch's MSELoss, L1Loss and HuberLoss with autograd and SciPy (src/recompute.py). Columns share one scale per dataset; pulls are scaled to the largest in each step.

Robust to bad targets is not robust to unusual inputs

The star cluster CYG OB1 (47 stars, log surface temperature against log light intensity; robustbase starsCYG, from Rousseeuw and Leroy 1987) is the classic counter-example: press "Unusual inputs" in Fig. 2. 43 stars lie on the main sequence, where light rises with temperature; 4 red giants (observations 11, 20, 30, 34) are cool and bright, far out on the x axis. They are leverage points: unusual inputs, not unusual targets.

Correction: "Huber and MAE make a fit robust to outliers"

Only to outliers in y: the 4 giants turn every loss's line over (Fig. 2, steps 3 to 5). Capping each point's pull does nothing about its lever: a point's effect on the slope is its pull times its distance along x, and the giants' distance is the largest in the set. Fixing this needs a method that down-weights by position as well (least trimmed squares, RANSAC, Theil-Sen); scikit-learn's guide separates "outliers in the y direction" from "outliers in the X direction" for this reason (scikit-learn, robustness regression; Rousseeuw and Leroy 1987). In a neural network the same holds: a robust loss protects against bad labels, not against inputs unlike the rest of the training set.

MAPE and quantiles

MAPE is asymmetric. For a positive target and a non-negative forecast, under-forecasting can cost at most 100% (forecast 0), while over-forecasting has no ceiling: forecasting 2y costs 100%, 3y costs 200% derived. That, and the 1/y weighting above, is why a MAPE-trained model under-forecasts. At y = 0 it has no value at all: scikit-learn divides by machine epsilon instead and documents an example returning 1.1 × 1014 from a single zero target, "arbitrarily large MAPE values, especially if some y_true values are very close to zero" (scikit-learn, MAPE); it also returns a fraction, not a percentage (0.5 means 50%). When relative error is what matters, training MSE on log(y) is the usual alternative; it estimates the mean of log y, a geometric mean, and it too needs y > 0.

Quantile (pinball) loss gives a prediction interval from two fits: τ = 0.05 and 0.95 bracket 90% of targets if the model is right. Tree libraries ship it (XGBoost's reg:quantileerror since 2.0 with quantile_alpha, which accepts a list of quantiles: XGBoost; scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile"): scikit-learn).

Counts and predicted variances: Poisson and Gaussian negative log-likelihood

Counts take the Poisson negative log-likelihood, ŷ − y log ŷ (+ log y!, a constant), with the network predicting log ŷ so the rate stays positive; that is PyTorch's PoissonNLLLoss with its default log_input=True (PyTorch). Its minimiser is the mean, like MSE's, but it charges errors relative to the rate.

A prediction with its own variance takes the Gaussian negative log-likelihood, ½(log σ2 + (y − ŷ)2/σ2), with the network predicting σ2 too; the model can lower the loss on hard examples by raising their σ2 instead of fitting them, and PyTorch clamps σ2 at eps (default 10−6) so it cannot collapse to zero (PyTorch, GaussianNLLLoss).

Cross entropy: likelihood, KL, and the gradient p − y

A five-class image classifier outputs logits z = (3.0, 1.5, 0.5, −0.5, −1.5) for cat, dog, fox, owl and car; the softmax turns them into p = (0.743, 0.166, 0.061, 0.022, 0.008) illustrative. If the image is a cat, cross entropy is −log 0.743 = 0.30 nats; if it is a car, −log 0.008 = 4.80.

  • It is maximum likelihood. With a one-hot label, cross entropy is the negative log-likelihood of the right class, so minimising its mean is maximum likelihood estimation ({{Probability for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d815f9a47d613409b5a0c}}).
  • It is entropy plus KL. Against any target distribution P, cross entropy H(P, Q) = H(P) + DKL(P‖Q). With a one-hot P, H(P) = 0 and the loss is the KL; with a soft target (label smoothing, a teacher) H(P) > 0 is a floor no model goes below. The derivation is on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}.
  • Its gradient on the logits is just ∂L/∂zk = pk − yk. Every wrong class is pushed down by its probability, the right class up by what it lacks, and the pushes sum to zero derived. For the cat that is −0.257 on the cat logit; for the car, −0.992 on the car logit: a confidently wrong example gets almost the largest push possible (1).

Every other classification loss is this gradient reshaped: a softer target, a weight per example, or a different function of p. The widget puts each beside cross entropy on the same logits.

What the widget shows, in numbers computed here:

  • MSE on probabilities lets the worst examples off. On the car it pushes the car logit by 0.026 against cross entropy's 0.992, 38 times less, because the softmax's slope pk(1 − pk) is nearly 0 at p = 0.008 and squared error has no log to cancel it. Cross entropy's log exactly cancels that slope, which is why classifiers train on it; the parent's {{Training lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}} trains the same network both ways.
  • Focal loss silences the easy example, not the hard one. On the cat it pushes 0.046, 5.6 times less than cross entropy; on the car it pushes 1.053, about the same. That is the point of focal loss: the gradient budget moves to the hard examples.
  • Label smoothing stops short of certainty and lifts the tail. On the cat it pushes the cat logit 0.177 instead of 0.257, and it pushes the car logit up (0.012), because p = 0.008 is below its target of 0.02 for every wrong class.

Focal loss, exactly as published. In a dense detector most of the roughly 100,000 anchor boxes per image are easy background, and "easily classified negatives comprise the majority of the loss and dominate the gradient". The factor (1 − pt)γ removes them: "with γ = 2, an example classified with pt = 0.9 would have 100× lower loss compared with CE and with pt ≈ 0.968 it would have 1000× lower loss" (1/0.12 = 100 and 1/0.0322 = 977 derived; reproduced live in the margin chart below).

Focal loss: α, the prior bias and the paper's ablations

The paper found γ = 2 best and, with it, α = 0.25, noting that "lower α's are selected for higher γ's": α weights the rare foreground class down, because γ already removed most of the easy background. It also initialises the output bias so the model starts by predicting 0.01 for the rare class, and shows that "the exact form of the focal loss is not crucial" (Lin et al. 2017). The parent's {{When training goes wrong|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}} tab runs focal loss with α = 0.25 against re-weighting, oversampling and the prior bias on an imbalanced task.

Label smoothing improves calibration: on ImageNet Müller et al. report an expected calibration error of 0.071 with hard targets, 0.022 after temperature scaling, and 0.035 with label smoothing of 0.1 and no scaling. It also harms distillation: a teacher trained with label smoothing was more accurate than one trained with hard targets and dropout (0.59% error against 0.67% on MNIST) but taught a much worse student (0.91% against 0.74%) (Müller et al. 2019), because smoothing erases the similarities between wrong classes that a student learns from ({{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}).

Proper scoring. A loss on probabilities is strictly proper when the expected loss is lowest only at the true probability; the log score (cross entropy) and the Brier score (squared error on probabilities) are (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). Focal loss is not: if 90% of inputs like this one are positive, the focal optimum with γ = 2 is a predicted probability of 0.694, with γ = 3 0.647 computed here (move η and γ in the score chart in the next section). Either way, a focal model's scores are recalibrated before being read as probabilities.

Why focal-trained networks still come out better calibrated

Mukhoti et al. show the same thing from the other side: focal loss is an upper bound on KL minus γ times the entropy of the prediction, so it raises entropy, and that is why networks trained with it come out better calibrated than overconfident cross-entropy networks (Mukhoti et al. 2020).

One axis for every classification loss: the margin

With labels y ∈ {−1, +1} and a score f(x), the margin m = y·f(x) is positive when the example is classified correctly and large when it is classified confidently. The loss we would like is the 0-1 loss (1 if m ≤ 0); it has no gradient, so training minimises a convex surrogate: hinge max(0, 1 − m) for the SVM, logistic log(1 + e−m), which is binary cross entropy on logits, and exponential e−m, which AdaBoost minimises (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani 2000). A convex surrogate gives the right decisions in the limit exactly when it is differentiable at 0 with a negative slope there (Bartlett, Jordan and McAuliffe 2006, Theorem 6), which all of these pass.

Who pulls hardest. A misclassified point at m = −5 pulls with 0.99 under the logistic loss, 1 under hinge, and e5 = 148 under the exponential loss derived. A mislabelled example is exactly such a point, which is why exponential-loss boosting suffers under label noise; Long and Servedio prove that for a broad class of convex potentials, any nonzero rate of random label noise can stop a booster from beating 1/2 accuracy on a data set it would learn without noise (Long and Servedio 2010).

Logistic loss in nats, log(1 + e−m), which is BCEWithLogitsLoss on the score; some texts divide by ln 2 so that it equals 1 at m = 0. Focal is the binary focal loss on the same score, pt = σ(m), α = 1. Values and pulls checked against PyTorch autograd.

What the score estimates. If a fraction η of inputs like x are positive, the logistic loss is minimised at f = log(η/(1 − η)), the log-odds, so σ(f) is the probability; exponential at half the log-odds; squared hinge at 2η − 1; hinge at sign(η − ½), "for all η ∈ (0, 1) other than 1/2" (Bartlett et al. 2006, Example 3): an SVM's score carries the decision and nothing about how sure it is.

Logistic recovers η exactly; hinge keeps only the sign; focal is pulled towards 0.5

The expected loss of a score f is η·φ(f) + (1 − η)·φ(−f). Its minimum f* is what a model trained on that loss learns to output.

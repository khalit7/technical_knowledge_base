Loss functions

What a loss makes a model estimate, and what it does to the gradient: regression losses as noise models (mean, median, quantile), what outliers and leverage points do to a fit, cross entropy and its margin-based relatives, the losses that act on the logits themselves (z-loss, soft-capping), and the adversarial loss. In one screen

A loss is the one number a training step pushes down, and choosing it decides two things at once: what the model learns to predict (a mean, a median, a quantile, a probability, or only a decision) and which examples drive the gradient (the large errors, the hard examples, or every example equally). Read most losses as the negative log-likelihood of a noise model and the choice follows from what you believe about the target and its noise.

This page owns the details. "What the model learns to predict" is what minimises the expected loss for each input: for squared error the mean, for absolute error the median, for pinball loss the τ-quantile; scikit-learn's guide lists the same pairs as "strictly consistent scoring functions" (scikit-learn, model evaluation), which is the rule to follow when the loss is also the metric: train with a loss consistent for the quantity you will be scored on.

Terms, and how a batch becomes one number

  • Loss and cost. Strictly, the loss is one example's error and the cost its aggregate, usually the mean, over a batch or the dataset; in practice the words are used interchangeably.
  • The target decides the family. A numeric target gets a regression loss, a class (or a probability) gets a classification loss.
  • Cross entropy is maximum likelihood. Cross entropy with a one-hot label is the negative log-likelihood of the right class under the model, so minimising it is maximum likelihood estimation. MSE is maximum likelihood too, for Gaussian noise of fixed variance: −log N(y | ŷ, σ2) = (y − ŷ)2/(2σ2) + log(σ√(2π)), so with σ fixed only the squared error is left derived; MAE is the same for Laplace noise.
  • Reduction. PyTorch losses return one value per element with reduction='none', their sum with 'sum', and by default their mean. Two details matter. With class weights, cross entropy's "mean" divides by the sum of the weights of the examples, not by their number; and targets equal to ignore_index (default −100) are left out of both the sum and the count (PyTorch, CrossEntropyLoss).
  • Token-level means in language models. A language model's loss is the mean over all non-padding tokens. Averaging per micro-batch and then over micro-batches is not the same number when micro-batches hold different numbers of tokens; Hugging Face fixed exactly this in its Trainer's gradient accumulation on 16 October 2024 by summing and dividing by the total token count (Hugging Face). Which tokens count at all (prompt, padding, packed neighbours) is in "Padding, packing, and how tokens are weighted" on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR.

Regression: each loss is a noise model, and picks a statistic

Write the error as u = y − ŷ (target minus prediction). Every regression loss is a function ρ(u), and its derivative ψ(u) = ρ′(u) is the pull: how hard one example drags the prediction towards itself (it is minus the gradient with respect to ŷ). The pull is what tells the losses apart.

PyTorch's HuberLoss(delta) is the formula above; SmoothL1Loss(beta) is the same curve divided by β, so the two differ by a factor of δ and agree only at δ = 1 (PyTorch, SmoothL1Loss). The rule of thumb that follows: MSE by default, MAE or Huber when outliers are real data, log-cosh (or pseudo-Huber) when a smooth second derivative is needed.

Two claims about log-cosh, checked

"Cheaper than Huber": not in arithmetic. Huber is one comparison and a multiply; log-cosh needs an exponential and a logarithm (computed stably as |u| + log(1 + e−2|u|) − log 2). "Second-order methods want it": half right. A Newton step, as in XGBoost's leaf weights, divides by the summed second derivative. Huber's is exactly 0 beyond δ, so a leaf of large errors has nothing to divide by; XGBoost ships pseudo-Huber for this reason, "a twice differentiable alternative to absolute loss" (XGBoost parameters). But log-cosh's second derivative, 1/cosh2(u), also falls towards 0 for large errors (about 4e−2|u|), so it softens the problem rather than removing it derived. See both second derivatives in Loss shapes.

Which number each loss picks

Before any model, ask what single number each loss would predict for a whole sample. The answer is the statistic the loss estimates, and it is where a model trained on that loss will sit for every input. The sample is real and skewed: the lengths of 141 major North American rivers, in miles (R's rivers, from the USGS via the World Almanac 1975).

On the rivers the mean is 591 miles and the median 425: the long rivers drag the mean up. MAPE picks 336, below the median, because dividing by y makes short rivers count for more; Gneiting proved this in general: MAPE is consistent for the "median of order −1", the median of a distribution reweighted by 1/y, which "tends to support severe underforecasts" (Gneiting 2011). Log-cosh lands on 425.2, the median to a fraction of a mile, because every river is hundreds of units away from any reasonable c, deep in its linear part.

Robust to bad targets is not robust to unusual inputs

The star cluster CYG OB1 (47 stars, log surface temperature against log light intensity; robustbase starsCYG, from Rousseeuw and Leroy 1987) is the classic counter-example. 43 stars lie on the main sequence, where light rises with temperature; 4 red giants (observations 11, 20, 30, 34) are cool and bright, far out on the x axis. They are leverage points: unusual inputs, not unusual targets.

Correction: "Huber and MAE make a fit robust to outliers"

Only to outliers in y. On the 43 main-sequence stars every loss finds a rising line (MSE slope +2.05, MAE +2.50). Add the 4 giants and they all turn it over: MSE −0.41, Huber (δ = 1) −0.42, log-cosh −0.42, and MAE −0.69, worse than MSE computed here. Capping each point's pull does nothing about its lever: a point's effect on the slope is its pull times its distance along x, and the giants' distance is the largest in the set. Fixing this needs a method that down-weights by position as well (least trimmed squares, RANSAC, Theil-Sen); scikit-learn's guide separates "outliers in the y direction" from "outliers in the X direction" for this reason (scikit-learn, robustness regression; Rousseeuw and Leroy 1987). In a neural network the same holds: a robust loss protects against bad labels, not against inputs unlike the rest of the training set.

MAPE, quantiles, counts and variances

MAPE is asymmetric. For a positive target and a non-negative forecast, under-forecasting can cost at most 100% (forecast 0), while over-forecasting has no ceiling: forecasting 2y costs 100%, 3y costs 200% derived. That, and the 1/y weighting above, is why a MAPE-trained model under-forecasts. At y = 0 it has no value at all: scikit-learn divides by machine epsilon instead and documents an example returning 1.1 × 1014 from a single zero target, "arbitrarily large MAPE values, especially if some y_true values are very close to zero" (scikit-learn, MAPE). It also returns a fraction, not a percentage (0.5 means 50%). When relative error is what matters, training MSE on log(y) is the usual alternative; it estimates the mean of log y, a geometric mean, and it too needs y > 0.

Quantile (pinball) loss gives a prediction interval from two fits: τ = 0.05 and 0.95 bracket 90% of targets if the model is right. Tree libraries ship it (XGBoost's reg:quantileerror since 2.0 with quantile_alpha, which accepts a list of quantiles: XGBoost; scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile"): scikit-learn).

Counts take the Poisson negative log-likelihood, ŷ − y log ŷ (+ log y!, a constant), with the network predicting log ŷ so the rate stays positive; that is PyTorch's PoissonNLLLoss with its default log_input=True (PyTorch). Its minimiser is the mean, like MSE's, but it charges errors relative to the rate.

A prediction with its own variance takes the Gaussian negative log-likelihood, ½(log σ2 + (y − ŷ)2/σ2), with the network predicting σ2 too; the model can lower the loss on hard examples by raising their σ2 instead of fitting them, and PyTorch clamps σ2 at eps (default 10−6) so it cannot collapse to zero (PyTorch, GaussianNLLLoss).

Classification: the cross-entropy family

Every loss in this family is cross entropy against some target distribution, and only the target or a weight changes. Focal loss, exactly as published. In a dense detector most of the roughly 100,000 anchor boxes per image are easy background, and "easily classified negatives comprise the majority of the loss and dominate the gradient". The factor (1 − pt)γ removes them: "with γ = 2, an example classified with pt = 0.9 would have 100× lower loss compared with CE and with pt ≈ 0.968 it would have 1000× lower loss" (1/0.12 = 100 and 1/0.0322 = 977 derived; reproduced live in Loss shapes). The paper found γ = 2 best and, with it, α = 0.25, noting that "lower α's are selected for higher γ's": α weights the rare foreground class down, because γ already removed most of the easy background. It also initialises the output bias so the model starts by predicting 0.01 for the rare class, and shows that "the exact form of the focal loss is not crucial" (Lin et al. 2017). The parent's When training goes wrong tab runs focal loss with α = 0.25 against re-weighting, oversampling and the prior bias on an imbalanced task.

Label smoothing improves calibration: on ImageNet Müller et al. report an expected calibration error of 0.071 with hard targets, 0.022 after temperature scaling, and 0.035 with label smoothing of 0.1 and no scaling. It also harms distillation: a teacher trained with label smoothing was more accurate than one trained with hard targets and dropout (0.59% error against 0.67% on MNIST) but taught a much worse student (0.91% against 0.74%) (Müller et al. 2019), because smoothing erases the similarities between wrong classes that a student learns from (Distillation and Small Language Models).

Proper scoring. A loss on probabilities is strictly proper when the expected loss is lowest only at the true probability; the log score (cross entropy) and the Brier score (squared error on probabilities) are (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). Focal loss is not: if 90% of inputs like this one are positive, the focal optimum with γ = 2 is a predicted probability of 0.694, with γ = 3 0.647 computed here. Mukhoti et al. show the same thing from the other side: focal loss is an upper bound on KL minus γ times the entropy of the prediction, so it raises entropy, and that is why networks trained with it come out better calibrated than overconfident cross-entropy networks (Mukhoti et al. 2020). Either way, a focal model's scores are recalibrated before being read as probabilities.

One axis for every classification loss: the margin

With labels y ∈ {−1, +1} and a score f(x), the margin m = y·f(x) is positive when the example is classified correctly and large when it is classified confidently. The loss we would like is the 0-1 loss (1 if m ≤ 0); it has no gradient, so training minimises a convex surrogate: hinge max(0, 1 − m) for the SVM, logistic log(1 + e−m), which is binary cross entropy on logits, and exponential e−m, which AdaBoost minimises (Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani 2000). A convex surrogate gives the right decisions in the limit exactly when it is differentiable at 0 with a negative slope there (Bartlett, Jordan and McAuliffe 2006, Theorem 6), which all of these pass.

Where they differ is what the score means and who pulls hardest:

  • What the score estimates. If a fraction η of inputs like x are positive, the logistic loss is minimised at f = log(η/(1 − η)), the log-odds, so σ(f) is the probability; exponential at half the log-odds; squared hinge at 2η − 1; hinge at sign(η − ½), "for all η ∈ (0, 1) other than 1/2" (Bartlett et al. 2006, Example 3): an SVM's score carries the decision and nothing about how sure it is.
  • Who pulls hardest. A misclassified point at m = −5 pulls with 0.99 under the logistic loss, 1 under hinge, and e5 = 148 under the exponential loss derived. A mislabelled example is exactly such a point, which is why exponential-loss boosting suffers under label noise; Long and Servedio prove that for a broad class of convex potentials, any nonzero rate of random label noise can stop a booster from beating 1/2 accuracy on a data set it would learn without noise (Long and Servedio 2010).

Losses on the logits themselves: z-loss and soft-capping

Cross entropy only sees differences between logits: add the same constant c to every logit and the softmax, and so the loss, do not change. The overall level of the logits, measured by log Z = log Σj ezj, is therefore free to drift. PaLM added "an auxiliary loss of z_loss = 10−4 · log2 Z to encourage the softmax normalizer log(Z) to be close to 0, which we found increases the stability of training" (Chowdhery et al. 2022). Its gradient on logit i is 2·10−4·log Z·softmaxi, a push of every logit towards a level where log Z = 0 and each logit is its log-probability. Wortsman et al. reproduced the failure it prevents, output logits diverging late in training, in small models trained without weight decay at high learning rates, and found z-loss resolves it (weight decay also helped in their larger models; Wortsman et al. 2023). Which landmark models used it is in the parent's Defaults across models tab.

Why the level matters at all is number formats: bfloat16 keeps 8 significant bits, so the gap between neighbouring values grows with magnitude (0.0078 near 1, 0.125 near 24, 0.5 near 100) derived. Logits stored in bf16 at a high level lose the small differences the softmax depends on.

Soft-capping bounds the logits instead of penalising their level: Gemma 2 replaces logits by soft_cap · tanh(logits / soft_cap), with soft_cap 50 in attention and 30 on the final layer (Gemma 2 report); Gemma 3 replaced the soft-capping with QK-norm (Gemma 3 report).

Between distributions and between models

  • KL divergence, DKL(P‖Q) = Σ P log(P/Q): a distance-like measure between distributions, asymmetric and not a metric. It is the regulariser in VAEs (keep the code near its prior), the distillation loss (the student matches the teacher) and the RLHF anchor (the policy stays near its reference). Cross entropy = entropy + KL is thread 1 of Topic: ml-fundamentals; which direction a small student should minimise, forward or reverse, is "Forward or reverse KL" on Distillation and Small Language Models. In PyTorch, KLDivLoss takes the model's log-probabilities first and the target second, and its default reduction='mean' "doesn't return the true KL divergence value"; use 'batchmean' (PyTorch).
  • Penalties (L1 or lasso, L2 or ridge, elastic net) are added to the loss but are not task losses: L1 drives weights to exactly zero (sparsity, feature selection), L2 shrinks them without zeroing any, elastic net combines both. Details on Regularisation.
  • Contrastive losses (InfoNCE and its relatives) are cross entropy over a batch: pick the one matching pair among N. Owned by Contrastive and self-supervised learning; the CLIP paper page runs one live.
  • Preference losses (Bradley-Terry reward models, DPO and its family) score a preferred answer against a rejected one; "Direct preference optimisation and its family" on Alignment: SFT, RLHF, DPO Family, RLVR runs one preference pair through eight of them.

The adversarial loss, and the fix in the same paper

A GAN trains a generator G and a discriminator D against each other in one minimax game, minG maxD E[log D(x)] + E[log(1 − D(G(z)))]. The original paper already warned that this form starves the generator: "Early in learning, when G is poor, D can reject samples with high confidence ... In this case, log(1 − D(G(z))) saturates. Rather than training G to minimize log(1 − D(G(z))) we can train G to maximize log D(G(z))", which has the same fixed point "but provides much stronger gradients early in learning" (Goodfellow et al. 2014, section 3). This is the non-saturating loss most GANs train with.

Try: slide how confidently the discriminator rejects a generated sample, and compare the generator's gradient under each loss (with respect to the discriminator's logit on that sample).

With D = σ(l): minimax gradient d/dl log(1 − σ(l)) = −D; non-saturating d/dl (−log σ(l)) = −(1 − D) derived, checked with PyTorch autograd.

Later losses change the game itself: least-squares GANs replace the log loss with squared error, because the sigmoid cross-entropy loss "may lead to the vanishing gradients problem", and minimising it amounts to the Pearson χ2 divergence (Mao et al. 2017); Wasserstein GANs minimise the earth mover's distance with a Lipschitz critic, enforced in the first paper by weight clipping, which its authors call "a clearly terrible way to enforce a Lipschitz constraint" (Arjovsky et al. 2017). GANs themselves belong to Topic: generative-and-multimodal.

Common mistakes

A softmax (or sigmoid) before a loss that expects logits
CrossEntropyLoss applies log-softmax itself, and BCEWithLogitsLoss the sigmoid; applying them twice squashes the scores and shrinks every gradient. Use the plain BCELoss on probabilities only when you have no logits; the logits version is the numerically stable one (PyTorch).
Expecting Huber or MAE to fix any outlier
They cap the pull of a bad target, not the lever of an unusual input. On the CYG OB1 stars MAE does worse than MSE (above).
δ in the wrong units, or SmoothL1 taken for Huber
Huber's δ (and log-cosh's built-in bend at about 1) is measured in the target's units: standardise the target or pick δ from the residual scale. SmoothL1Loss(beta) is HuberLoss(delta) divided by β; with β ≠ 1 the gradients differ by that factor.
MAPE with zeros or tiny targets
It has no value at 0 and explodes near it; it also rewards under-forecasting (above). Use MAE on a scale that makes sense, MSE on log(y), or a weighted error.
Reading focal or hinge scores as probabilities
Only a strictly proper loss (cross entropy, Brier) is minimised at the true probability; focal loss pulls scores towards 0.5 and hinge keeps only the sign. Recalibrate (temperature or Platt scaling) on held-out data first.
Distilling from a label-smoothed teacher
The teacher may be more accurate and still a worse teacher (Müller et al. 2019).
KLDivLoss with its arguments swapped or its default reduction
The first argument is the model's log-probabilities; 'mean' averages over every element, not over examples; 'batchmean' gives the KL.
Comparing loss values across set-ups
A smoothed target has entropy above 0, so its loss has a floor no model goes below; class weights change what "mean" divides by; per-micro-batch averages differ from a token mean. Compare under the same target, weights and reduction, or compare a metric instead. And check the starting loss: an untrained K-class classifier should score about ln K (step 3 of Topic: ml-fundamentals).

Loss shapes

Every loss on this page drawn against its one argument: the error u = y − ŷ for regression, the margin m = y·f(x) for classification. It extends the regression table and "One axis for every classification loss" in the Reading tab. The pull is minus the gradient with respect to the prediction (or the score): how hard one example drags the model.

Regression, against the error u

MAPE is drawn for a target y = 1, where it equals MAE; for other targets divide by |y|. The second derivative is what a Newton step (XGBoost's leaf weights) divides by: Huber's is 0 beyond δ, log-cosh's decays as 4e−2|u|, MAE's and pinball's are 0 everywhere except a kink.

Classification, against the margin m

Logistic loss in nats, log(1 + e−m), which is BCEWithLogitsLoss on the score; some texts divide by ln 2 so that it equals 1 at m = 0. Focal is the binary focal loss on the same score, pt = σ(m), α = 1. Values and pulls checked against PyTorch autograd.

What the score estimates: the minimum of the expected loss

If a fraction η of inputs like this one are positive, the expected loss of a score f is η·φ(f) + (1 − η)·φ(−f). Its minimum f* is what a model trained on that loss learns to output.

Closed forms for logistic (log-odds), exponential (half the log-odds), squared hinge (2η − 1) and hinge (sign; Bartlett et al. 2006, Example 3); focal by a grid then golden-section search, checked against SciPy. Each curve is shifted and scaled to its own range so their minima can be compared.

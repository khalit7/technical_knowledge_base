One training step, start to finish

Every network, from a 2012 image classifier to a 2026 language model, learns by repeating one step: take a batch of examples, push it forward through the layers, score the output with a loss, send the error backward to get a gradient for every weight, update the weights, keep them from fitting noise (regularise), and measure on data the step did not see. Every building block in this topic acts at exactly one of those seven moments, so this page follows one step and meets each block when it acts, with only the comparison needed there. The nine pages under this one own the details.

Switch the recipe to run the same batch through a 2012 to 2016 style network and a 2026 language-model style one.

The toy: 16 layers of width 64; a batch of 32 random inputs (64 numbers each, standard normal) labelled into 10 classes by a fixed random linear "teacher"; a held-out batch of 256 from the same teacher. Classic: ReLU, He initialisation, BatchNorm, residual connections, SGD with momentum 0.9 at learning rate 0.1 and weight decay 10−4, as in ResNet (§3.4). 2026: pre-RMSNorm blocks with a SwiGLU feed-forward, AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at 1.0, as in Llama 2 (§2.2) and DeepSeek-V3; the toy's learning rate (3 × 10−3, with no warmup) is illustrative. 1 · Data and initialisation: what the step starts from

The batch. A step's gradient is computed on a mini-batch: not the whole training set (batch gradient descent: exact, but one slow, expensive step per pass over the data), not one example (stochastic gradient descent in the strict sense: cheap and fast, but so noisy it may never settle), but a few dozen examples to a few million tokens, which is efficient on hardware and only mildly noisy. Inputs are first put on a common scale, standardised to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, z = (x − μ)/σ, or min-max scaled to [0, 1], so that no feature dominates because of its units and gradient descent sees rounder loss contours; distance-based methods (kNN, SVM, k-means) need it most.

The starting weights. They are random, never constant: with equal weights, every neuron in a layer gets the same input and the same gradient and stays a copy of its neighbours (the symmetry problem). The scale of the randomness is the whole game. A layer with n inputs multiplies the variance of the signal by about n · Var(w), times a factor set by its activation, so Var(w) is chosen to make that product 1 in both directions; too small and the signal shrinks layer by layer (vanishing gradients), too large and it grows exponentially (exploding gradients).

SchemeVar(w)Made for
Xavier (Glorot)2 / (nin + nout)sigmoid and tanh, roughly linear and zero-mean near 0 (Glorot and Bengio 2010)

2 · Forward: activations and normalisation

Each layer is a linear map followed by a nonlinearity, and in every modern network also a normalisation and a residual connection around it. The nonlinearity is what makes depth worth anything (without it the stack collapses to one linear map); it only has to be differentiable almost everywhere, so ReLU's kink at 0 is fine.

The rule is short: the ReLU family in hidden layers (SwiGLU in transformer feed-forward blocks, GELU, often tanh-approximated, in ViTs), and at the output whatever the task needs: a sigmoid per output for binary or multilabel, softmax for multiclass, nothing (linear) for regression. Squared ReLU, max(0, x)2, appears in some efficiency-focused models as a cheap alternative (Primer). The gated units borrow the LSTM's trick: two projections, one passed through a sigmoid-like function and multiplied into the other, so the slope is set per neuron by the input instead of being fixed. Their paper is candid about why they win: "we attribute their success, as all else, to divine benevolence" (Shazeer 2020).

BatchNorm was motivated as a cure for "internal covariate shift"; later work credits a smoother loss landscape instead (Santurkar et al. 2018). RMSNorm shows that the rescaling, not the centring, is what matters, and saves compute at equal quality. Placement matters as much as the kind: the 2017 transformer normalised after the residual sum, LN(x + F(x)) ("post-norm", see Attention Is All You Need); GPT-2 onwards normalise the branch input, x + F(LN(x)) ("pre-norm"), which leaves the residual stream untouched and trains without the warmup sensitivity (Xiong et al. 2020); OLMo 2 normalises the branch output, x + LN(F(x)), for stability. An alternative to QK-norm is Moonshot's qk-clip, which leaves the forward pass alone and rescales a head's query and key weights after the optimiser step (step 5).

3 · Loss: one number to descend

The loss turns the network's output into the single number the step will push down. (Strictly, the loss is one example's error and the cost its mean over the batch; the words are used interchangeably.) Which loss follows from the target: a number gets a regression loss, a class gets cross entropy.

Regression lossPer exampleChoose it when
MSE, RMSE(y − ŷ)2; RMSE = √MSE, in the target's unitsthe default: smooth, and its gradient shrinks near the minimum; but squaring lets a few huge errors dominate
MAE|y − ŷ|outliers are real data: every error pulls with the same force (and the gradient does not soften near the minimum)
MAPE|y − ŷ| / |y|relative error matters; it has no value at y = 0 and blows up near it (scikit-learn)
Huberquadratic for |e| ≤ δ, linear beyondMSE near the target, MAE far from it, with δ tuned to the data
Log-coshlog cosh(y − ŷ)Huber's shape with a second derivative everywhere, which second-order methods such as XGBoost want

Classification is all cross entropy. Cross entropy is minus the log of the probability the model gave the right class, so minimising it is maximum likelihood. Its variants only reweight the terms: binary cross entropy (one sigmoid per output, for multilabel too), categorical (softmax over classes), sparse categorical (the same maths, fed the class index), label smoothing (the target puts a little probability on wrong classes, against overconfidence; Szegedy et al. 2016, Müller et al. 2019), class weights (up-weight a rare class) and focal loss, −(1 − p)γ log p, which down-weights easy examples so the hard ones dominate the gradient (Lin et al. 2017, built for dense object detection). The one classic outside the family is the hinge loss, max(0, 1 − y·f(x)) with y ∈ {−1, 1}, which is zero once the margin is met (the SVM's loss, in Beyond one network).

Exact formulas; the gradients match PyTorch autograd. Label smoothing uses Szegedy et al.'s target, 1 − ε + ε/K on the right class and ε/K on each other, so its loss is lowest at p = 0.91, not at 1; some texts put 1 − ε on the right class and ε/(K − 1) on the others, which moves that minimum to 0.9.

Check where the loss starts. A K-class classifier that knows nothing should score ln K: ln 10 = 2.303 for the toy. Its pre-norm network starts at 2.60 and the "init too small" one at 2.3025, but the BatchNorm residual network starts at 5.42, because its residual stream grows to a standard deviation of 2.47 by the last layer and nothing normalises it before the output (the step 2 animation shows the growth). A starting loss far from ln K is the first thing to check, in When training goes wrong.

Two losses here are not task losses. KL divergence measures how far one distribution is from another; it regularises VAEs, matches a student to a teacher, and anchors an RLHF policy to its reference (thread 1). The adversarial loss of a GAN is a minimax game between a generator and a discriminator (Topic: generative-and-multimodal). And L1 and L2 penalties are added to the loss but belong to regularisation.

4 · Backward: where gradients vanish or explode

Backpropagation applies the chain rule from the loss back to every weight: each layer receives the gradient from the layer above, multiplies it by its own Jacobian (how its output moves with its input) and passes it down, while also reading off the gradient for its own weights. All weights then update at once, every gradient computed at the old parameters. A weight in layer 1 of 16 therefore receives a product of 15 layer factors. If they are below 1 the product vanishes, if above 1 it explodes; it is the same arithmetic in a deep feed-forward stack and in a recurrent network unrolled through time (thread 3).

The bottom panel of the step 2 animation is this step. In the sigmoid network the first layer's gradient is 9.5 × 10−11 against 0.18 for the last, about 2 billion times smaller derived, because every sigmoid layer multiplies the gradient by its slope, at most 0.25; the first layers simply do not learn. In the pre-norm SwiGLU network the same ratio is 1.45 to 1.17: every layer gets a usable gradient, because the residual path carries it down untouched and the norms keep each branch's scale fixed.

5 · Update: optimisers and schedules

The update turns a gradient into a weight change, and three choices are made together: how the gradient is smoothed over steps (momentum), whether each parameter gets its own step size (adaptive methods), and how the base learning rate moves over the run (the schedule). Adaptive methods only rescale relative to the base rate, so an optimiser and a schedule are always chosen as a pair. The landscape they descend is mostly saddle points, not bad local minima: places where the loss rises in some directions and falls in others, which momentum, mini-batch noise and even rounding error push the optimiser off.

Why warmup. In Adam's first steps the second-moment estimate rests on a handful of gradients, so its per-parameter step sizes vary wildly and can be far too large (Liu et al. 2019); a post-norm transformer is especially sensitive (Xiong et al., step 2). Warmup ramps the rate from near zero over the first steps, until the statistics can be trusted.

The 2024 to 2026 challengers change the geometry

The newer optimisers do not retune Adam; they change the shape of the update or remove the schedule. Muon (Keller Jordan, 2024) treats each hidden weight matrix as a matrix: it replaces the momentum matrix with an approximately orthogonal one (all its singular values pushed towards 1, by five Newton-Schulz iterations), so the step moves every direction the gradient spans at a comparable size instead of being dominated by a few. Embeddings, the output head and 1-D parameters stay on AdamW, and Muon keeps one buffer instead of two. Moonshot reports about twice AdamW's computational efficiency, matching AdamW-trained models with about 52% of the training FLOPs reported (Muon is Scalable), and MuonClip (Muon plus qk-clip) carried the 1T-parameter Kimi K2 through 15.5T tokens with no loss spikes (Moonshot AI: Kimi). The catch is that orthogonalisation wants a whole matrix while a large run shards it, which is why Moonshot published a distributed version. Muon came out of the nanoGPT speedrun, where it cut the record from 31.4 to 24.9 minutes (Pretraining).

Schedules: shape, and whether you must know the end

The schedule sets the base rate over the run: high early to travel, low late to settle into the basin. Two questions separate the families: does it need the total number of steps in advance, and what shape is the decay. Step decay (divide by 10 at milestones) ran the ResNet era; the 2017 transformer warmed up for 4,000 steps and then decayed with the inverse square root of the step (Attention Is All You Need); warmup then cosine to 10% of the peak became the LLM standard (Chinchilla; Llama 3's 405B warmed up over 8,000 steps and decayed by cosine to 8 × 10−7 over 1.2M steps). WSD, warmup-stable-decay (MiniCPM), holds the rate flat and decays only over the last 10 to 20% of tokens, so the decay can start whenever the run is to end; DeepSeek-V3 held 2.2 × 10−4 until 10T tokens before its cosine decay.

Try: move the end of the run. Cosine was planned for the full length, so a run stopped early is left at a high, untuned rate; WSD simply starts its cooldown 20% before wherever the run now ends.

step decay (÷10 at ⅓, ⅔)warmup, inverse square rootwarmup, cosine to 10%WSD, 1−sqrt cooldown over the last 20%

Learning rate as a fraction of the peak against the fraction of the planned run. Warmup lengths (2% and 4%) are stretched to be visible illustrative; real warmups are a fraction of a percent of an LLM run. Shapes as in the linked sources.

  • At a fixed, known budget the shapes nearly tie. Tuned cosine, WSD and 1−sqrt cooldowns land within noise of each other, and constant-rate-plus-cooldown scales as predictably as cosine, which is why WSD spread so fast (Hägele et al. 2024).
  • Cosine's real cost is optionality. Every checkpoint before the end of a cosine run sits at a mistuned rate, so you cannot stop early, extend, or reuse it at another length; Kaplan's scaling laws read such checkpoints, and Chinchilla corrected it with one schedule per training length (§2).
  • Cooldown shape has a stable order: concave (1−sqrt) at least as good as linear, both better than convex (exponential, EMA-like); roughly 10 to 20% of tokens, with diminishing returns beyond; a final rate near 10% of the peak is the usual heuristic, and nearer zero lowers the loss but can hurt some downstream scores.
  • The cooldown is also a data lever. Labs switch to curated mathematics, code and instruction-like data while the rate falls, which is why "annealing" means three things: lowering the rate (the classical sense), a final training phase that lowers the rate and switches the data together, and a cheap experiment that anneals a checkpoint on a candidate dataset to price it (Pretraining).
  • Weight averaging can replace the decay. WSM keeps the rate constant forever and merges the last checkpoints: a merge with weights cj is algebraically a decay with per-step weights wi = Σj≥i cj, so any monotone decay curve can be emulated (mean averaging is linear decay, EMA a convex one).
Correction
The old pages said WSM beat a matched WSD decay by "~1.3 points". The paper's +1.28 points (62.67 to 63.95 on 41 benchmarks, 16.3B-parameter MoE) compares the best checkpoint of each run; when both stop at WSD's 400B tokens the gain is +1.18 derived, every comparison is one run, and the checkpoint was picked on the test benchmarks (WSM paper page).

Choosing. LLM pretraining whose length may change: WSD with a 1−sqrt cooldown over the last 10 to 20%. Pretraining at a fixed, final budget: warmup then cosine to about 10% of the peak. Fine-tuning: linear or cosine to zero after 3 to 5% warmup (the Hugging Face Trainer default is linear). A small supervised model with a cheap, steady validation metric: reduce-on-plateau or step decay (reduce-on-plateau is a poor fit for LLM runs: noisy evaluations, and a schedule nobody can reproduce). No schedule at all: schedule-free, or WSM. Snapshot ensembles with warm restarts (SGDR) and the one-cycle policy (super-convergence) live in vision and short fine-tuning runs.

6 · Regularise: fit the pattern, not the batch

Regularisation is anything that trades a little training fit for better performance on data the model has not seen. Four kinds, and every one of them acts at a different point of the step:

KindActs atHow
Penaltythe loss (step 3) or the update (step 5)L1 (lasso, λΣ|w|) pushes some weights to exactly 0, giving sparse models and feature selection (a Laplace prior; needs a subgradient at 0); L2 (ridge, λΣw2) shrinks all weights without zeroing any, and steadies correlated features (a Gaussian prior); elastic net adds both
Dropoutthe forward pass (step 2)each unit is dropped with probability p in training, so no unit can rely on one partner: an implicit ensemble of subnetworks (Srivastava et al. 2014). Frameworks use inverted dropout: survivors are divided by the keep probability in training, so inference is a plain forward pass with nothing dropped
Augmentationthe data (step 1)random but realistic transforms (flips, crops, noise) enlarge the training set; the strongest fix where it is possible
Early stoppingthe measurement (step 7)stop when validation loss turns up and keep the best checkpoint; free

Label smoothing (step 3) regularises too, and so, mildly, does BatchNorm, through the noise of its batch statistics. Modern LLM pretraining uses almost none of this: it sees most data about once, so there is little to overfit, and dropout is largely absent there, though still common in fine-tuning and smaller models. What remains is weight decay, which is both a regulariser and an optimiser property: added to the loss as L2, Adam rescales it per parameter; applied directly to the weights, it does not. That is why AdamW exists, and thread 4 animates the difference. In the step strip, the classic recipe folds 10−4·w into the gradient, while the 2026 recipe shrinks every weight by ηλ = 3 × 10−4 of itself, separately.

7 · Measure: on data the step did not see

The toy's single step shows why measuring needs held-out data: under the 2026 recipe, the loss on the batch it trained on fell from 2.60 to 0.22 and accuracy on that batch went from 9.4% to 100%, while on the 256 held-out examples the loss moved from 2.77 to 2.71 and accuracy from 11.3% to 11.7%. One step memorised its batch and learned almost nothing general. What to measure falls into three families.

Classification numbers come from the confusion matrix at a decision threshold: precision TP/(TP + FP) (of what I flagged, how much was right), recall or true positive rate TP/(TP + FN) (of all positives, how many I caught), F1 their harmonic mean, the false positive rate FP/(FP + TN) and specificity, 1 − FPR. Sweeping the threshold gives two curves: ROC (recall against FPR; its area, ROC-AUC, is the probability that a random positive scores above a random negative) and precision-recall (its area, AUPRC). On imbalanced data they disagree, and the reason is a single denominator: FPR divides by the whole negative class, which is huge, so thousands of extra false positives barely move it, while precision divides by the model's own positive predictions and is hit by every one (Davis and Goadrich 2006).

Try: keep the classifier fixed and make positives rarer. The ROC curve and its area do not move at all; the precision-recall curve collapses.

Scores are normal with standard deviation 1: mean 0 for negatives, d′ for positives (the binormal model), so ROC-AUC = Φ(d′/√2) exactly and AUPRC is integrated over 401 thresholds; illustrative classifier, exact arithmetic.

Text scores compare an output with a reference. BLEU (translation) is the geometric mean of clipped 1- to 4-gram precisions times a brevity penalty, so it is precision-oriented; ROUGE (summarisation) is recall-oriented: ROUGE-N on n-grams, ROUGE-L on the longest common subsequence, ROUGE-S on skip-bigrams; METEOR aligns unigrams with stems and synonyms plus a word-order penalty and tracks human judgement better than BLEU on single sentences; BERTScore greedily matches contextual BERT embeddings by cosine similarity (Zhang et al. 2019; see BERT) and MoverScore uses an earth mover's distance between them, so both survive paraphrase. Perplexity, exp(mean negative log-likelihood per token), is the loss itself as a measurement, comparable only under the same tokenizer. In 2026 the n-gram scores survive mostly in translation and summarisation papers; judging LLM output has moved to LLM judges and task benchmarks (Topic: evaluation-and-llm-judges).

Information quantities, entropy, cross entropy and KL divergence, are the classification loss read as a measurement rather than as an objective; they are thread 1.

Four threads that cross the stages

Followed one stage at a time, the components look like separate menus. Four threads run through several stages at once, and they are the reason the choices come in matched sets.

1 · Everything is cross entropy

Entropy H(P) = −Σ P log P is the average cost of encoding samples from P with P's own best code; cross entropy H(P, Q) = −Σ P log Q is the cost with a code built for Q; their difference is the KL divergence, DKL(P‖Q) = H(P, Q) − H(P) = Σ P log(P/Q), the extra paid for using the wrong code. KL is asymmetric and breaks the triangle inequality, so it is distance-like, not a distance. Since H(P) does not depend on the model, minimising cross entropy and minimising KL are the same thing, and that one fact runs through four stages:

  • The loss (step 3): with a one-hot label H(P) = 0, so cross entropy = KL = the negative log-likelihood of the right class. Label smoothing and distillation make P soft, so H(P) > 0 sets a floor the loss cannot go below; distillation's choice of forward or reverse KL decides where a small student is allowed to be wrong (Distillation and Small Language Models).
  • The anchor: the same KL keeps a VAE's latent code near its prior and an RLHF policy near its reference model (Topic: llm-training-and-post-training, Axis 4).
  • Contrastive learning: InfoNCE is cross entropy over a batch, a classification of the one matching pair among N candidates (below).
  • The measurement (step 7): perplexity is exp(cross entropy) per token, and a log-loss on held-out data is the same number the step minimised, now read as a score.

Try: pick a target, then slide how much probability the model puts on the right answer. The bar is cross entropy split into the part no model can remove (H(P)) and the part training removes (KL).

2 · Keep the variance constant: the pact between initialisation, activation and normalisation

Initialisation (step 1) and normalisation (step 2) chase one invariant: activation and gradient variance roughly constant from layer to layer, at step zero and for the rest of training. Initialisation sets it for step zero, and only for the activation it was derived for; normalisation re-imposes it at every step, which is why deep normalised networks are far less sensitive to the initial scale. So the three are chosen as a set:

3 · Vanishing gradients, seen four ways

One failure shows up in four stages, and each stage has its own fix. In every case the gradient reaching an early layer is a product of per-layer (or per-time-step) factors:

  1. Activation saturation (step 2): a sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25, so each layer shrinks the gradient by at least 4. The fix is the ReLU family.
  2. Initialisation scale (step 1): a variance below the activation's balance point shrinks the signal at every layer, above it grows it. The fix is the matching scheme.
  3. Normalisation and placement (step 2): with the norm after the residual sum, every layer rescales the path the gradient travels; with it before the branch, the residual stream is an identity path.
  4. The product of Jacobians through time (backpropagation through time): a recurrent network applies the same weights at every step, so the gradient from step T back to step T − k multiplies k copies of the same factor. The LSTM's fix is its cell state, updated by addition, Ct = ft ⊙ Ct−1 + it ⊙ C̃t: along it the gradient is multiplied only by the forget gate, which the network can hold near 1. It is a skip connection through time.

Try: the fourth way, on one 60-step input sequence. Move the recurrent weight: the tanh network's gradient vanishes for every value, small weights shrinking it directly and large ones saturating the tanh, whose slope then shrinks it. The LSTM's cell path keeps its gradient alive while the forget gate stays near 1; lower its bias and it vanishes too.

tanh RNN, gradient on its state k steps backLSTM, gradient along the cell state

A scalar tanh RNN, at = tanh(w at−1 + xt), whose factor per step is w(1 − at2); and an LSTM cell path whose factor is the forget gate ft = σ(bf + xt), on the same seeded inputs xt ~ N(0, 0.52) illustrative; the products are exact. Exploding gradients need the factor to stay above 1, which a saturating unit resists; they appear in larger networks and are handled by clipping (step 4). Initialising the forget bias to 1 or more is a standard recommendation (Jozefowicz et al. 2015).

All four are why "fix vanishing gradients" never has one answer, and why a symptom in the loss curve has to be traced back through every stage (When training goes wrong).

4 · Weight decay: a regulariser by intent, an optimiser property by implementation

Weight decay is meant as a regulariser (step 6): pull every weight towards zero a little at each step. It can be implemented two ways. As L2 in the loss, λ/2 · ‖w‖2 adds λw to the gradient, which then goes through the optimiser. As decoupled decay, the update itself shrinks the weights, w ← w − ηλw, beside the gradient step. For plain SGD the two are identical. For Adam they are not: the L2 term is divided by √v̂ like every other gradient component, so weights whose gradients are large and noisy are barely decayed, while weights with tiny gradients are pulled to zero at full speed. Decoupling restores the same decay for every weight, and that is the whole of AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter 2017).

Try: six weights start at 1 and receive pure noise as their gradient, from tiny to large; the only thing that should move them on average is the decay. Play both optimisers on the same noise.

Contrastive and self-supervised learning: the objective makes the embedding model

Self-supervised learning takes its labels from the data itself; contrastive learning does it by pairing: two augmentations of one image, an image and its caption. The workhorse loss, InfoNCE, is cross entropy again (thread 1): for each item, a softmax over its similarity (cosine, divided by a temperature τ) to the one positive and to the N − 1 other items in the batch (van den Oord et al. 2018), and it maximises a lower bound on the mutual information between the views. A model that knows nothing scores ln N: ln 32,768 = 10.4 for CLIP's batch derived (CLIP, whose page runs a tiny CLIP and its similarity matrix live).

The trend: joint-embedding methods (contrastive, self-distillation, JEPA) against masked generative ones (MAE), with prediction in latent space increasingly favoured for semantic features. And the architecture follows the objective: a contrastive objective wants one vector per input, so it produces encoders (CLIP's towers, sentence embedders, rerankers); a generative objective must emit sequences, so it needs a decoder. BERT sits between, an encoder trained by masked-token prediction, whose raw embeddings are poor for similarity until contrastively fine-tuned (SimCSE). In 2026 contrastive training is the standard recipe that turns any backbone, increasingly a decoder-only LLM with pooling (E5-Mistral, NV-Embed, Qwen3-Embedding), into an embedding model: the objective, not the backbone, makes it one.

Correction
The old page said batch size matters in contrastive learning "so much there and nowhere else in this topic". It matters elsewhere too: BatchNorm's statistics get noisy with small batches (step 2), and batch size sets the gradient noise of every optimiser. What is special to InfoNCE is that the batch is the set of negatives, so its size changes the task itself (chance is ln N), which is why MoCo built a queue.

When it goes wrong

Every training failure lands on one of the seven stages, which is why debugging reads as the step run backwards from the symptom: a loss that is flat from the start points at the update or the backward pass (a rate off by 10, frozen layers, a loss that does not match the output activation), a loss that turns to not-a-number at numerics in the loss, oscillation at the learning rate or batch size, a plateau at vanishing gradients or dead ReLUs, training loss falling while validation loss rises at regularisation, good accuracy that is useless on the minority class at the metric. How the lab works

  • The task. Points in a square, two classes: two spirals, a ring around a disc, a checkerboard, or two moons. The training set is small (40 to 400 points) and a chosen share of its labels can be flipped; the validation set is 400 fresh points with correct labels, so the gap between the two is how much the net memorised.
  • The network. A multilayer perceptron: input (2) to hidden layers of the chosen width to two output logits. The plotted loss is cross-entropy on those logits for every run, whatever the run trains on, so runs with different losses can be compared on one axis.
  • Written by hand, checked against PyTorch. Forward and backward passes, the four losses, the three normalisations, dropout and the five optimisers are plain JavaScript in double precision. Offline, the same code ran 342 configurations (every activation, normalisation, placement, residual setting and loss, plus dropout) through PyTorch 2.14: gradients agree to a relative 2 × 10⁻¹², losses to 3 × 10⁻¹⁵. Forty training steps per optimiser (SGD, momentum, Adam, AdamW, Muon, with schedules, both kinds of weight decay, BatchNorm running statistics and dropout masks) end on the same weights to 2 × 10⁻¹⁵.
  • Exact definitions. Momentum is PyTorch's (buffer = 0.9 × buffer + gradient). Adam and AdamW use β = (0.9, 0.999), ε = 10⁻⁸; Adam's weight decay is L2 (added to the gradient), AdamW's is decoupled (weights multiplied by 1 − lr·λ). Weight decay applies to weight matrices only, not biases or normalisation gains. GELU is the tanh approximation. BatchNorm uses the batch's statistics in training and running averages (momentum 0.1) for validation and the boundary. Label smoothing is 0.1, the focal loss's γ is 2. Early stopping halts when validation loss has not improved for 15% of the run; it does not roll back.
  • Muon. As torch.optim.Muon: momentum 0.95 with Nesterov, five Newton-Schulz steps with coefficients (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315), on the hidden square matrices; its step is scaled by 0.2 √max(rows, columns) (PyTorch's "match_rms_adamw" option, from Moonshot's Muon work) so the same learning rate suits it and the AdamW that handles the other weights. PyTorch runs the Newton-Schulz steps in bfloat16 for speed; this page runs them in double precision (the check swaps in a double-precision copy of PyTorch's function; in bfloat16 the weights after 40 steps differ by about 0.5%).
  • Deterministic. One seed drives the data, the label flips, the starting weights, the batch order and the dropout masks, each from its own stream, so A and B share all of them and a run replays identically.
  • What a toy cannot show. At this scale some comparisons that matter in large models show no reliable difference: over ten seeds, Muon and AdamW reach the spiral equally well, and MSE trains slower than cross-entropy but ends in the same place. Use the presets for the effects that do show up here, and the child pages for the evidence at scale.

How to read it

Before debugging: the recipe

Andrej Karpathy's A Recipe for Training Neural Networks (2019) starts from two observations: neural network training is a leaky abstraction, and it fails silently ("Everything could be correct syntactically, but the whole thing isn't arranged properly, and it's really hard to tell"). Its answer is to build up from checks that cannot fail silently. The standing checklist:

  1. Inspect the data by hand before training, and again immediately before y_hat = model(x), after the whole pipeline.
  2. Overfit a single batch (as few as two examples) to about 0 loss. If you cannot, the bug is in the code, not the hyperparameters.
  3. Establish a dumb baseline and verify the loss at initialisation matches theory: -log(1/K) for K-class cross-entropy. Also an input-independent baseline (all inputs set to 0) that the real model must beat.
  4. Change one thing at a time; log gradient norms, weight norms and the learning rate actually applied.

Other items of the recipe: fix the random seed; "init well" (for a 1:10 imbalanced dataset, set the output bias so the network predicts 0.1 at initialisation); use backpropagation to chart dependencies (make the loss the sum of example i's outputs and check that only input i gets a gradient); Adam at 3e-4 is a safe first optimiser; add complexity one signal at a time; do not trust learning-rate decay defaults; and to regularise, first add more real data.

Loss spikes at scale

The failure that only large runs used to show: after a long healthy stretch the loss jumps, sometimes recovers, sometimes diverges. Five sources, each linked:

PaLM (2022): roll back and skip

Training the 540B model, Google "observed spikes in the loss roughly 20 times during training, despite the fact that gradient clipping was enabled", at irregular intervals, sometimes late, never in the smaller models. The fix: restart from a checkpoint about 100 steps before the spike and skip roughly 200 to 500 data batches. Replaying those batches from an earlier checkpoint did not spike, so the cause was "the combination of specific data batches with a particular model parameter state", not bad data alone. PaLM also added z-loss, 10-4 · log2 Z, to keep the softmax normaliser near 1, and clipped the global gradient norm at 1.0 (PaLM §5, §5.1).

OLMo 2 (2024): measure spikes, then remove them

Ai2 defined a spike score: "the percentage of values in a time series that are at least seven standard deviations away from a rolling average of the last 1,000 values", on the loss and the gradient norm. Each fix came with a before and after: normal initialisation N(0, 0.02) took the gradient-norm spike score from 0.40 to 0.03; reordered norm plus QK-norm from 0.108 to 0.069 (neither helped alone); no weight decay on embeddings from 0.16 to 0.092; plus z-loss, AdamW ε = 10-8 instead of 10-5, and a filter for repeated n-grams. The OLMo 2 paper page replays every ablation from the paper's vector figures with the spike score live; the Ai2 OLMo page does it on the released training logs. (The z-loss weight is printed as 10-5 in OLMo 2's Table 1 and 10-4 in its §3.3.3 formula.)

Wortsman et al. (2023): small-scale proxies

Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities showed that two instabilities seen at scale, attention logits that grow without bound (Dehghani et al. 2023) and output logits that diverge from the log-probabilities (PaLM), "also appear in small models when training at high learning rates", and that QK-norm and z-loss are "equally effective in this regime". That makes spikes debuggable on a laptop; the toy runs below do exactly that.

Llama 3 (2024): stable loss, unstable machines

Meta called its 405B recipe "very stable: we observed few loss spikes and did not require interventions to correct for model training divergence" (§3.4.1). Its interruptions were hardware: 466 in a 54-day snapshot, 419 unexpected; that story is on Training Infrastructure and the Llama 3 Herd paper page. One numerical instability did appear: gradient accumulation in bf16 once the image encoder was added, fixed by accumulating in FP32 (§7.3).

Google's Tuning Playbook: a procedure

The Deep Learning Tuning Playbook separates instability at initialisation from sudden instability mid-training, and finds it with a learning-rate sweep (plot the loss for rates just above the best). Its fixes, in order: warmup (best for early instability; sweep warmup over orders of magnitude, at most 10% of training), gradient clipping (threshold near the 90th percentile of logged norms; more than 50% of steps clipped means lower the rate instead), a different optimiser, architecture best practice (residuals, normalisation inside the residual, x + f(Norm(x))), and lowering the rate only as a last resort.

GELU's idea. Multiply the input by a random mask m ~ Bernoulli(Φ(x)): kept with a probability that grows with x. Its expected value is x Φ(x), so GELU is a deterministic version of a stochastic regulariser that blends ReLU (a mask of 0 or 1 set by the sign), dropout (a random 0) and zoneout (a random 1) (GELU paper, section 2). Unlike ReLU it is smooth, not monotone (a small dip below 0), and lets small negative inputs pass a small gradient.

Several GELUs. The paper gives two cheaper forms, and trained models use all three; a checkpoint must be run with the form it was trained with:

Correction: SiLU is not "nearly identical" to GELU, and was not found by the search

The old version of this page said SiLU has a "nearly identical curve to GELU" and was "found by architecture search". The curves differ by up to 0.193 derived, max |SiLU − GELU| on [−6, 6], and SiLU's dip is 64% deeper (−0.278 against −0.170). The near-identity is GELU against x σ(1.702x): SiLU is that curve stretched 1.702 times along x (and rescaled). As for the search: x σ(x) was already in the GELU paper (as SiLU) and in Elfwing et al.; Ramachandran et al.'s automated search found the family x σ(βx), which they named Swish, and their paper says that with β = 1 "Swish is equivalent to the Sigmoid-weighted Linear Unit (SiL) of Elfwing et al." Their result is that replacing ReLU with Swish raised ImageNet top-1 accuracy by 0.9% for Mobile NASNet-A and 0.6% for Inception-ResNet-v2. Overlay the curves in the {{Activation atlas|#t-atlas}}.

Why the smooth ones win in transformers is not settled. The papers report empirical gains (GELU over ReLU and ELU on their benchmarks, Swish over ReLU on ImageNet and translation); the old page's "self-scaling" has no source beyond the Bernoulli motivation above. In Shazeer's controlled comparison (next section), GELU and Swish alone barely beat ReLU in pre-training perplexity (1.983 and 1.994 against 1.997 at 65,536 steps, with run-to-run spreads of 0.003 to 0.005) and were behind it at the end of training (1.679 and 1.683 against 1.677): the gain that stuck came from gating.

Gated units: GLU, ReGLU, GEGLU, SwiGLU

A gated linear unit computes two projections of the same input and multiplies them elementwise, one passed through a nonlinearity (Dauphin et al. 2016, for gated convolutional language models). Shazeer put the variants into the Transformer's feed-forward block, without biases (GLU Variants Improve Transformer, 2020, equation 6):

What the gate does to the gradient. With g = xW and u = xV, the hidden unit is h = SiLU(g) u, so ∂h/∂u = SiLU(g): the slope of the linear path is not a fixed function of its own input but is set, token by token, by a second projection of the same input. That is the old page's "learned adaptive slope", and the only mechanism the formula itself shows. Its other claims for gated units ("very expressive, stable training, balanced mean/variance") are unconfirmed: the paper that introduced them offers none, and says so: "We offer no explanation as to why these architectures seem to work; we attribute their success, as all else, to divine benevolence."

Lower log-perplexity is better; at 65,536 steps the bracket is the standard deviation over runs of the same model.

Read with the noise in mind: at 65,536 steps GEGLU (1.942 ± 0.004) and SwiGLU (1.944 ± 0.010) are indistinguishable, and both are about 0.05 below ReLU (1.997 ± 0.005), ten times the spread derived. The fine-tuning scores come from one run each and the paper calls them "noisy"; the spread T5 measured for the GLUE average was 0.235. Every gated variant beat every ungated one in pre-training perplexity, at both lengths derived from Table 1.

PaLM adopted SwiGLU on this evidence: "they have been shown to significantly increase quality compared to standard ReLU, GeLU, or Swish activations (Shazeer, 2020). Note that this does require three matrix multiplications in the MLP rather than two" (Chowdhery et al. 2022, section 2). Llama followed: "We replace the ReLU non-linearity by the SwiGLU activation function ... We use a dimension of 2/3 4d instead of 4d as in PaLM" (Touvron et al. 2023, section 2.2), and so did nearly every open model since; the parent's {{Defaults across models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}} tab shows the switch year by year. A later line of work goes the other way for inference speed: replacing SiLU and GELU with ReLU in a pre-trained Llama or Falcon and fine-tuning briefly brings back exact zeros that a server can skip (Mirzadeh et al. 2023, "ReLU Strikes Back").

The 2/3 rule, and who follows it

A plain feed-forward block with hidden width 4d has two matrices: 2 · d · 4d = 8d2 parameters, and as many multiply-adds per token. A gated block has three: 3dh. Matching them gives h = 8d/3 = ⅔ · 4d. Shazeer did exactly this: "we reduce the hidden layer to dff = 2048" from 3,072 at d = 768 (2 × 768 × 3,072 = 3 × 768 × 2,048 = 4,718,592). Llama's code applies the same rule, then an optional multiplier, then rounds up to a hardware-friendly multiple:

Correction: "usually shrunk to 2/3 · 4d" is one convention among several

PaLM uses SwiGLU with "dff always 4 × dmodel" (PaLM, section 2), so its feed-forward blocks are 1.5 times the size of a plain block; Gemma 3 keeps 4d or more with GeGLU; most of the Llama 3 family multiplies the 2/3 width by 1.2 to 1.5. Only Llama 1, Llama 3.2 3B and SmolLM2 in the table sit at (or within 1% of) the matched size. What survives everywhere is the habit of quoting the width relative to d, so always count three matrices.

The output layer

The last layer's activation is chosen by what the output has to be, and it must agree with the loss (the loss for each is on {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}). In PyTorch the loss usually applies the output activation itself, so the network should end with the raw scores (logits):

Softmax, in four facts. (1) Adding a constant to every logit changes nothing, softmax(z + c) = softmax(z), which is why every implementation subtracts the largest logit before exponentiating (without it, e1000 overflows). (2) With two classes it is the sigmoid: softmax([x, 0])1 = σ(x) checked to 10−15, so a binary sigmoid output is a two-class softmax with one logit fixed at 0. (3) Its derivative, ∂pi/∂zj = pi(δij − pj), goes to zero as one probability approaches 1: a softmax saturates like a sigmoid, which is why it is paired with a log in the loss (cross entropy's gradient on the logits is simply p − y). (4) A temperature T, softmax(z/T), sharpens (T < 1) or flattens (T > 1) the distribution without changing the order; try it on a real model's logits in the Sampler lab of {{Sampling and Decoding|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bafcda29a2c7086e}}. Softmax also normalises attention scores ({{Attention Is All You Need|n:3c65c17b0d0d81999af7f16f8ed8ee9e}}) and mixture-of-experts router scores.

Correction: with a large vocabulary, the cost is the projection, not the softmax

The old page called softmax "expensive for very large K". The exponentials are cheap; what grows with the vocabulary V is the output projection that produces the logits: d · V multiply-adds per token against V exponentials. For Qwen2.5-0.5B (d = 896, V = 151,936, config.json) that is 136 million multiply-adds against 152 thousand exponentials, 896 times more derived; and in training the logits themselves take memory: a batch of 8 sequences of 4,096 tokens holds 8 × 4,096 × 151,936 = 5.0 billion logits, 19.9 GB in 32-bit floats derived. Sampled and hierarchical softmaxes, and fused or chunked cross-entropy kernels, target these two costs.

Common mistakes

Applying softmax (or sigmoid) before a loss that applies it again
CrossEntropyLoss expects "the unnormalized logits for each class" (PyTorch docs); a softmax in front of it squashes the scores twice, so training still runs but the gradients are wrong and confidence is capped. End the network with logits and put the softmax only where probabilities are read.
Sigmoid followed by BCELoss instead of BCEWithLogitsLoss
The combined loss "is more numerically stable than using a plain Sigmoid followed by a BCELoss as, by combining the operations into one layer, we take advantage of the log-sum-exp trick for numerical stability" (PyTorch docs). A saturated sigmoid rounds to exactly 0 or 1, and the log of that is infinite.
A ReLU or sigmoid on a regression output
A ReLU output can never predict a negative value and receives no gradient when it outputs 0; a sigmoid caps it at 1. Regression outputs are linear unless the target's range is known (then softplus for positive values, tanh for a bounded range).
Running a checkpoint with a different GELU
GPT-2 runs with the tanh form, CLIP with x σ(1.702x), BERT's Hugging Face configs with the exact form. The tanh form is within 4.7 × 10−4 of exact; the sigmoid form is off by up to 0.020 per unit, which compounds over layers. Use the activation string in the model's config (gelu, gelu_new, gelu_pytorch_tanh, quick_gelu).
Treating SiLU and GELU as the same function
They differ by up to 0.19 (above). Swapping one for the other in a trained model changes its outputs; in a model trained from scratch either works.
Counting a SwiGLU block as two matrices
It has three, so its parameters are 3dh. A width quoted as "4d" means a block 1.5 times the size of a plain 4d one (PaLM, Gemma 3); "2.69d" means the matched size (the 2/3 rule).
An initialisation that does not match the activation
Xavier was derived for sigmoid and tanh, He for the ReLU family, LeCun's 1/n for SELU; the wrong one shrinks or grows the signal at every layer. The parent's thread 2 ("keep the variance constant", in the {{Reading tab of Topic: ml-fundamentals|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}}) shows each mismatch, and the {{Activation atlas|#t-atlas}} table gives the variance-preserving gain of every function here; the details are on {{Normalisation and initialisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81369e1cc38ed4e11d48}}.
Expecting exact zeros from a SwiGLU model
SiLU is never exactly 0 for a nonzero input, so a SwiGLU block has small values, not zeros; tricks that skip zero rows (built for ReLU models such as OPT) need a threshold or a ReLU fine-tune first (Mirzadeh et al. 2023). See the {{Inside real models|#t-real}} tab.
Blaming the activation for a learning rate that is too high
In the runs above, ReLU networks die and leaky ReLU networks overflow at the same learning rates. Swapping the activation changes the symptom; lowering or warming up the rate removes the cause. The parent's {{When training goes wrong|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d796ccc0a293218c57}} tab goes from symptom to cause.

Regularisation is anything that trades a little fit on the training data for better performance on data the model has not seen. Classically that means a penalty term added to the loss that discourages large or complex weights; dropout, augmentation and early stopping regularise without any explicit penalty.

1 · What regularisation is, three ways

A penalty. Minimise the training loss J(w) plus λ·Ω(w), where Ω measures how complex the weights are and λ ≥ 0 sets the trade. λ = 0 is the unregularised fit; as λ grows the fit gets simpler and its training error rises. The right λ is the one that minimises error on held-out data, which is why it is tuned by cross-validation or a validation set, never on the test set.

A constraint. The same solutions come from minimising J(w) subject to Ω(w) ≤ t: each λ corresponds to some budget t (a Lagrange multiplier argument; Goodfellow et al., section 7.2). Tibshirani defined the lasso this way, least squares subject to Σ|wj| ≤ t (1996). The constraint view is the one that explains why L1 gives zeros (the animation below).

A prior. Minimising the negative log-likelihood plus a penalty is maximum a posteriori estimation: the penalty is the negative log of a prior on the weights. A Gaussian prior N(0, τ2) gives the L2 penalty ‖w‖2/(2τ2); an isotropic Laplace prior gives the L1 penalty, log p(w) = −α‖w‖1 + constant (Goodfellow et al., eq. 7.24 and section 5.6.1). For least squares with noise variance σ2 the ridge λ (on ‖y − Xw‖2 + λ‖w‖2) is σ2/τ2 derived: a strong prior belief that weights are small (small τ) is a large λ.

Whichever view, the effect is the bias-variance trade: a regularised model is biased towards simple answers, and in exchange its fit varies less from one training sample to the next. That buys most when data are scarce relative to the model; the Coefficient paths tab measures it on real data, with the training-set size as a slider.

Why L1 gives exact zeros. In the constraint view, the fit is the first point where the growing ellipses of equal training error touch the allowed region. The L2 region is a circle, with no corners, so the touching point is almost never on an axis. The L1 region is a diamond whose corners sit on the axes, and an ellipse coming from most directions meets a corner first: at a corner, one coefficient is exactly 0.

Elastic net. Zou and Hastie added an L2 term to the lasso because of three limits they list (2005, section 1): with more features than examples (p > n) the lasso selects at most n of them before it saturates; from a group of highly correlated features it tends to select only one, and does not care which; and when n > p with high correlations, ridge predicts better than the lasso. The L2 part makes the penalty strictly convex, which gives the grouping effect: strongly correlated features get similar coefficients and enter or leave together.

The same λ means different things in different libraries
The page's convention is glmnet's and scikit-learn's Lasso and ElasticNet: (1/2n)‖y − Xb‖2 + λ(ρ‖b‖1 + (1 − ρ)/2 ‖b‖2), with scikit-learn calling λ alpha and ρ l1_ratio. scikit-learn's Ridge(alpha) instead minimises ‖y − Xb‖2 + alpha‖b‖2, with no 1/2n, so its alpha is nλ. The old form λ1‖w‖1 + λ2‖w‖2 maps to λρ and λ(1 − ρ)/2 after the same 1/2n scaling. A λ copied between conventions can be off by a factor of 2n.

Standardise first, and leave the intercept alone. The penalty treats all coefficients alike, so a feature measured in grams is penalised a thousand times less than the same feature in kilograms. Centre and scale every feature before fitting (the diabetes data here are scaled to unit length, as in Efron et al.'s equation 1.1), and do not penalise the intercept: centring the response removes it.

3 · Weight decay: two implementations, not equivalent

Weight decay penalises large weights so the model captures patterns rather than memorising. It can be implemented two ways:

  1. L2 in the loss: add (λ/2)‖w‖2 to the objective. The penalty enters the gradient as λw and then flows through the optimiser's machinery.
  2. Decoupled, in the optimiser: change the update rule itself, w ← w − ηλw, applied alongside the gradient step.

For plain SGD the two coincide, up to rescaling λ by the learning rate (Loshchilov and Hutter 2017, Proposition 1). For adaptive optimisers such as Adam they do not: the L2 term is divided by the per-parameter second-moment term √v̂ like the rest of the gradient, so weights with large gradients are barely decayed. Decoupling restores the same decay for every weight, which is the whole point of AdamW. The parent page animates both on the same noise (Reading tab, thread 4) and its Training lab has the preset "Adam with L2 against AdamW"; the optimiser side is on Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers. Three regularisation details remain for this page.

What λ multiplies. In Loshchilov and Hutter's Algorithm 2 the decay step is ηtλθ, where ηt is the schedule multiplier and not the base learning rate α, so decay and learning rate are tuned independently. PyTorch's AdamW applies θ ← θ − γλθ with γ the learning rate itself (PyTorch 2.14 docs; default λ = 0.01). So PyTorch's weight_decay=0.1 at a learning rate of 3 × 10−4 shrinks each weight by 3 × 10−5 of itself per step derived: γλ, and halving the learning rate halves the decay too. The paper's λ is PyTorch's λ times α.

Which parameters. Decay is usually applied to weight matrices only. nanoGPT, for example, decays every parameter with two or more dimensions (matrix weights and embeddings) and leaves biases and LayerNorm gains alone (model.py, configure_optimizers), with λ = 0.1 (train.py). Decaying a normalisation gain pulls it towards 0 and fights the normalisation it belongs to.

Typical values. AlexNet used SGD with weight decay 0.0005 and found "this small amount of weight decay was important for the model to learn. In other words, weight decay here is not merely a regularizer: it reduces the model's training error" (Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 5). With AdamW, 0.1 is the usual pretraining value: GPT-3 (paper page), ViT (paper page); BERT used L2 weight decay 0.01 (paper page).

Why decay at all if an LLM never overfits? A model that sees most of its data once has little to overfit, yet nearly every LLM recipe keeps weight decay. D'Angelo, Andriushchenko et al. argue that in both regimes "weight decay is never useful as an explicit regularizer but instead changes the training dynamics in a desirable way": with multi-epoch SGD on vision tasks it strengthens SGD's implicit regularisation, and in near one-epoch LLM training it balances the bias-variance trade-off of stochastic optimisation, giving lower training loss and more stable training (NeurIPS 2024, abstract). AlexNet's authors saw the same thing in 2012: decay lowered the training error.

4 · Dropout

During each training step, each unit is dropped independently at random: its output is set to 0 for that step, along with all its incoming and outgoing connections. This prevents co-adaptation: no unit can rely on one specific partner, so the network learns redundant, robust features. A network with n units contains 2n possible "thinned" networks sharing one set of weights, and each training case trains a freshly sampled one, so dropout is equivalent to training an implicit ensemble of subnetworks (Srivastava et al. 2014, section 1).

Corrected: the paper keeps about half the hidden units, not 0.8
The old version of this page said the authors suggest keeping about 0.8 of hidden units. Srivastava et al. write that the retention probability "can simply be set at 0.5, which seems to be close to optimal for a wide range of networks and tasks. For the input units, however, the optimal probability of retention is usually closer to 1 than to 0.5" (section 1). Their practical guide gives a range: "Typical values of p for hidden units are in the range 0.5 to 0.8. For input layers ... For real-valued inputs (image patches or speech frames), a typical value is 0.8" (Appendix A.4), and their MNIST runs use 0.5 for hidden units and 0.8 for inputs (sections 6.1 and 7.4). So: keep about 0.5 for hidden units, about 0.8 for inputs. On MNIST with the width fixed, test error was flat for retention between 0.4 and 0.8 and rose towards 1 (section 7.3, Figure 9a).
p means opposite things in the paper and in PyTorch
In Srivastava et al., p is the probability of keeping a unit. In PyTorch's nn.Dropout(p), p is the probability of dropping it (default 0.5), and Keras and most papers since follow the framework. The paper's "p = 0.8 for inputs" is nn.Dropout(0.2). Even the mixup paper's dropout experiments use the dropping convention (p ∈ {0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}; Zhang et al. 2018, section 3.4).

Train against inference: where the scaling goes

Training drops units; inference drops nothing. With every unit present, a layer would send about 1/p times what the next layer saw in training, so the activations must be rescaled to match the expected value:

  • Original scheme: at inference, multiply the activations (equivalently the outgoing weights) by the keep probability p.
  • Inverted dropout (what frameworks actually do): at training, divide the surviving activations by p; inference is then a plain forward pass.

The two "are equivalent with appropriate scaling of the learning rate and weight initializations at each layer" (Srivastava et al., section 10).

The ensemble, and how good the shortcut is. At test time, averaging the predictions of all 2n thinned networks is impossible; the single weight-scaled network is the shortcut. Srivastava et al. tested it on MNIST against an honest Monte Carlo average of k sampled thinned networks: "around k = 50, the Monte-Carlo method becomes as good as the approximate method" and thereafter slightly better, "well within one standard deviation" (section 7.5). AlexNet describes the same shortcut as "a reasonable approximation to taking the geometric mean of the predictive distributions" of the thinned networks (Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 4.2). Gal and Ghahramani later read dropout as approximate Bayesian inference and kept it switched on at test time to get uncertainty from the spread of the sampled predictions, "MC dropout" (2016). The Dropout as an ensemble tab runs all three on a real network.

Dropout on a linear model is ridge. For linear regression with each input kept with probability p, the expected loss is ‖y − pXw‖2 + p(1 − p)‖Γw‖2 with Γ = (diag(XᵀX))1/2: ridge with each weight's penalty scaled by its feature's spread, and a strength (1 − p)/p after absorbing p into w (Srivastava et al., section 9.1). On the diabetes data, whose columns have unit length so Γ = I, input dropout with keep 0.8 is ridge with alpha = 0.25: averaged over 4,000 random masks, the fitted coefficients match scikit-learn's ridge to within 0.3% of the largest coefficient measured (recompute.py).

The practical guide, and the variants

  • Width: if n units are right without dropout, use at least n/p with it (Appendix A.1).
  • Learning rate and momentum: dropout makes gradients noisy, so the paper used 10 to 100 times the usual learning rate, or momentum 0.95 to 0.99 instead of 0.9 (A.2), with max-norm constraints (each unit's incoming weight vector capped at a norm c of 3 to 4) to stop the weights blowing up (A.3). On MNIST, dropout with max-norm gave 1.05% error against 1.62% for L2 alone (Table 9, below).
  • Gaussian dropout: multiplying activations by noise from N(1, σ2) with σ2 = (1 − p)/p matches Bernoulli dropout's mean and variance and needs no scaling at test time; it worked "as well, or perhaps better" (section 10).
  • Stochastic depth drops whole residual blocks instead of units, with a survival probability falling linearly from 1 at the input to pL = 0.5 at the last block (Huang et al. 2016); vision Transformers call it drop-path. DropBlock drops contiguous regions of a convolutional feature map; attention dropout drops attention weights.
  • With BatchNorm: dropout shifts a unit's variance between training and test, while BatchNorm keeps the running variance it measured in training; when dropout sits before a BatchNorm layer this "variance shift" makes the pair worse than either alone; one of Li et al.'s remedies is to apply dropout only after the last BatchNorm layer (Li et al. 2018, abstract and Table 4). BatchNorm also regularises a little by itself, through the noise of its batch statistics, "in some cases eliminating the need for Dropout" (Ioffe and Szegedy 2015, abstract; more on Normalisation and initialisation).

5 · Data augmentation, mixup and CutMix

Data augmentation applies random but realistic transforms (flips, rotations, crops, colour shifts, noise) that do not change the label, which enlarges and diversifies the training set. It attacks overfitting at the data level and is the highest-leverage fix where it is possible. AlexNet's two forms are the classic: random 224 × 224 crops and their horizontal reflections from 256 × 256 images, which "increases the size of our training set by a factor of 2048" (with highly dependent examples), and without which "our network suffers from substantial overfitting"; and random shifts of colour along the principal components of the RGB values, which "reduces the top-1 error rate by over 1%" (Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 4.1). The crops are computed on the CPU while the GPU trains, so they cost nothing. Later work searched the space of transforms automatically: RandAugment, with two parameters, matched AutoAugment's searched policy on ImageNet, 77.6% top-1 for ResNet-50 against 76.3% for the standard baseline (Cubuk et al. 2019, Table 3).

A transform is only an augmentation if it preserves the label, and that depends on the task: a horizontal flip is fine for photographs of cats and wrong for digits, where a flipped 2 is not a 2.

Mixup trains on blends of two random training examples and their one-hot labels, x̃ = λxi + (1 − λ)xj, ỹ = λyi + (1 − λ)yj, with λ drawn from Beta(α, α); α → 0 recovers ordinary training. On ImageNet α between 0.1 and 0.4 helped and larger α underfitted; ResNet-50's top-1 error fell from 23.5% to 23.3% at 90 epochs and from 23.6% to 22.1% at 200 epochs, bigger models and longer runs gaining most (Zhang et al. 2018, section 3.1 and Table 1). CutMix pastes a random rectangle of one image into another and mixes the labels in proportion to the area (Yun et al. 2019); Cutout blanks a random square with no label change (DeVries and Taylor 2017).

6 · Early stopping

Monitor the validation loss during training, and stop before the model starts overfitting: training loss keeps falling while validation loss traces an asymmetric U (Goodfellow et al., section 7.8 and Figure 7.3). In practice: evaluate every so often, keep the checkpoint with the best validation loss, and stop once it has not improved for a set number of evaluations (the patience). It is free and always on, and it composes with everything else here. Two costs: it needs a validation set, and the data held out for it are not trained on; Goodfellow et al. describe retraining on all the data for the number of steps early stopping found (Algorithm 7.2).

Why it regularises. Stopping gradient descent after τ steps of size ε keeps the weights near where they started. For a linear model with a quadratic loss, starting from 0, it is equivalent to L2 regularisation with α ≈ 1/(τε) (eq. 7.45): the number of steps plays the role of the inverse weight decay. The early-stopping mode of the animation in section 2 shows it on real data: the gradient-descent iterates stay close to the ridge path, closest for the weakly correlated pair, and visibly apart for the two cholesterol measures correlated at 0.90, where the approximation's assumptions are stretched.

A recorded run is on the parent page: When training goes wrong → "Train falls, validation rises" (300 digit images with 30% of labels wrong, best validation step marked), and the Training lab's preset "Early stopping keeps the best validation loss", which also shows the gap between stopping and rolling back to the best checkpoint. In mixup's corrupted-label experiments the gap between the best and last epoch is what early stopping would have saved: with half the CIFAR-10 labels randomised, plain training reached 18.8% test error at its best epoch and ended at 44.6% (Zhang et al. 2018, Table 2; the "Corrupted labels" view below).

8 · Regularisation in networks that can memorise everything

Zhang et al. trained standard image networks on CIFAR-10 with the labels replaced by random ones. Every network still fitted them, 99.3% to 100% training accuracy with test accuracy at chance, about 10% (Zhang et al. 2017, Table 1). Explicit regularisers did not stop it: with weight decay on, Inception and both MLPs still fitted random labels (99.2% to 100%), and so did Inception with random crops or augmentation, given more epochs; only AlexNet with weight decay failed to converge (Appendix, Table 4). With true labels, the same Inception reached 100% training accuracy with or without random crops and weight decay, and its test accuracy moved only from 85.75% (neither) to 89.05% (both) or 89.31% (crops alone). Their conclusion: explicit regularisation "may improve generalization performance, but is neither necessary nor by itself sufficient for controlling generalization error", and the architecture and SGD itself regularise implicitly.

Double descent. Classical intuition says test error rises steadily once a model has more parameters than examples. For many models it instead peaks where the model can just barely interpolate the training set, then falls again, and well-tuned L2 regularisation removes the peak: Nakkiran et al. prove it for isotropic linear regression and show it empirically for random-feature models and CNNs (2020, abstract and section 5.2). Their random ReLU features experiment, repeated here on the digits at a smaller size, reproduces both the peak and its removal:

Grokking. On small algorithmic datasets (modular arithmetic tables), Power et al. saw networks fit the training set early and then, long after, jump from chance to perfect validation accuracy; of the interventions they tried, "weight decay is particularly effective at improving generalization", more than halving the data needed (2022, sections 1 and 3).

Language models. A model that sees most of its data once has little opportunity to overfit, which is why LLM pretraining dropped dropout (section 4) but kept weight decay for its effect on the optimisation (section 3). Overfitting returns with repetition: fine-tuning on small datasets for several epochs is where dropout, LoRA dropout, early stopping on a validation set and data augmentation come back.

9 · Common mistakes

Dropout left on at evaluation, or BatchNorm in training mode
In PyTorch, dropout and BatchNorm behave differently under model.train() and model.eval(). Evaluating in training mode drops units at random and gives noisy, worse numbers; forgetting the inference-side correction in a hand-written scheme gives inputs 1/p times larger than training ever saw (the "Scaling forgotten" mode in section 4).
Reading the dropout p the wrong way round
The 2014 paper's p is the keep probability; PyTorch's is the drop probability. "p = 0.8 for inputs" in the paper is nn.Dropout(0.2), and nn.Dropout(0.8) would keep only a fifth of the inputs.
L2 in the loss with Adam, when weight decay was meant
Adam's weight_decay argument adds λw to the gradient (L2), which Adam then rescales per parameter. Use AdamW for decoupled decay (section 3; the parent's thread 4 and Training lab preset).
Decaying biases and normalisation gains
Put weight matrices in one parameter group with decay and biases, LayerNorm and RMSNorm gains in another with none, as nanoGPT does.
Copying λ between libraries or learning rates
scikit-learn's Ridge alpha is n times its Lasso alpha for the same penalty; PyTorch's AdamW decay per step is learning rate × λ, so a λ tuned at one learning rate is a different decay at another (sections 2 and 3).
Penalising features that were never standardised
L1 and L2 penalties depend on each feature's units; unscaled features are penalised unequally and the lasso selects by unit rather than by importance. Standardise on the training split only, then apply the same transform to validation and test data.
Choosing λ, patience or the stopping step on the test set
Every regularisation knob is a hyperparameter; tuning it on the test set leaks it. Use a validation set or cross-validation. (The "best λ" curve in section 8 is chosen on its test images, as in the paper it follows, and labelled so.)
An augmentation that changes the label
Flipping digits or text, rotating a 6 into a 9, cropping out the object: the transform must preserve the label for the task at hand. Mixup and CutMix change the label on purpose, and say by how much.
Regularising a model that is underfitting
If training loss is itself high, more regularisation makes it worse. Check the training curve first; the parent's When training goes wrong tab starts from the symptom.

    The question regularisation answers is not the training fit but error on new patients. Each curve is the mean squared error on the held-out patients, averaged over 40 random splits: fit on m patients (standardised with their own statistics), test on the other 442 − m.

The best λ is read off the test curves here for display; in practice it is chosen by cross-validation inside the training data.

    Dropout on the inputs is ridge here
    Because every column has unit length, Srivastava et al.'s result (Reading, section 4) says input dropout with keep probability p equals ridge with scikit-learn alpha = (1 − p)/p: keep 0.8 is alpha 0.25, λ = 0.25/442 = 5.7 × 10−4 in this page's convention derived. In ridge mode the counters show the keep probability matching the current λ. On the full data this is a very light penalty, and the fit barely moves.

    Dropout as an ensemble, on a real network

    A network trained with dropout is a weight-sharing ensemble of 2n thinned networks. At test time there are three ways to use it: the single weight-scaled network (with inverted dropout, the plain forward pass), the arithmetic mean of the predictions of k sampled thinned networks (Srivastava et al.'s Monte Carlo average), or their geometric mean, which AlexNet's authors said weight scaling approximates. This tab runs all three on one real network.

    Try: play an ambiguous digit and watch the thinned networks disagree while their average settles; then a clear one, where they all agree. The spread of the votes is what MC dropout uses as an uncertainty.

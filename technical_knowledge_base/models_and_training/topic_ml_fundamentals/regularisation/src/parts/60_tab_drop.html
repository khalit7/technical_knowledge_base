Dropout as an ensemble, on a real network

A network trained with dropout is a weight-sharing ensemble of 2n thinned networks. At test time there are three ways to use it: the single weight-scaled network (with inverted dropout, the plain forward pass), the arithmetic mean of the predictions of k sampled thinned networks (Srivastava et al.'s Monte Carlo average), or their geometric mean, which AlexNet's authors said weight scaling approximates. This tab runs all three on one real network.

Try: play an ambiguous digit and watch the thinned networks disagree while their average settles; then a clear one, where they all agree. The spread of the votes is what MC dropout uses as an uncertainty.

How many thinned networks does the average need?

Srivastava et al. measured it on MNIST: "around k = 50, the Monte-Carlo method becomes as good as the approximate method", and beyond that slightly better but "well within one standard deviation of it" ({{section 7.5, Figure 11|https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/srivastava14a.html}}). The network: 64 inputs (8 × 8 pixels scaled to [0, 1]), two hidden layers of 96 ReLU units, 10 outputs; dropout keeping 0.8 of the inputs and 0.5 of each hidden layer (nn.Dropout(0.2) and nn.Dropout(0.5)), Adam at 10−3, batch 64, 300 epochs, on 1,437 of scikit-learn's 1,797 digits; the other 360 (stratified, fixed seed) are the test set. Trained in PyTorch (train_digits.py); weights stored as 8-bit integers with one scale per unit, The animation, the Monte Carlo curves and your browser's run use those stored weights (the stored network's weight-scaled error is the same 1.39% offline and here); the loss curves and the five-seed errors were measured on the full-precision networks during training, in evaluation mode (no dropout).

5 · Data augmentation, mixup and CutMix

Data augmentation applies random but realistic transforms (flips, rotations, crops, colour shifts, noise) that do not change the label, which enlarges and diversifies the training set. It attacks overfitting at the data level and is the highest-leverage fix where it is possible. AlexNet's two forms are the classic: random 224 × 224 crops and their horizontal reflections from 256 × 256 images, which "increases the size of our training set by a factor of 2048" (with highly dependent examples), and without which "our network suffers from substantial overfitting"; and random shifts of colour along the principal components of the RGB values, which "reduces the top-1 error rate by over 1%" ({{Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 4.1|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf}}). The crops are computed on the CPU while the GPU trains, so they cost nothing. Later work searched the space of transforms automatically: RandAugment, with two parameters, matched AutoAugment's searched policy on ImageNet, 77.6% top-1 for ResNet-50 against 76.3% for the standard baseline ({{Cubuk et al. 2019, Table 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13719}}).

A transform is only an augmentation if it preserves the label, and that depends on the task: a horizontal flip is fine for photographs of cats and wrong for digits, where a flipped 2 is not a 2.

Mixup trains on blends of two random training examples and their one-hot labels, x̃ = λxi + (1 − λ)xj, ỹ = λyi + (1 − λ)yj, with λ drawn from Beta(α, α); α → 0 recovers ordinary training. On ImageNet α between 0.1 and 0.4 helped and larger α underfitted; ResNet-50's top-1 error fell from 23.5% to 23.3% at 90 epochs and from 23.6% to 22.1% at 200 epochs, bigger models and longer runs gaining most ({{Zhang et al. 2018, section 3.1 and Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.09412}}). CutMix pastes a random rectangle of one image into another and mixes the labels in proportion to the area ({{Yun et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.04899}}); Cutout blanks a random square with no label change ({{DeVries and Taylor 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04552}}).

Label smoothing is the target-side relative: train against (1 − ε) on the true class and ε spread over the rest, which caps how confident the model becomes. Its formulas, its two conventions, its effect on calibration and why it hurts distillation are on {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}; the parent's Training lab has the preset "Label smoothing caps overconfidence". As a regulariser of accuracy its record is mixed: on CIFAR-100 with PyramidNet-200 it raised top-1 error from 16.45% to 16.73% on its own, and helped only combined with Cutout or DropBlock (Yun et al., Table 5, in the chart below).

6 · Early stopping

Monitor the validation loss during training, and stop before the model starts overfitting: training loss keeps falling while validation loss traces an asymmetric U ({{Goodfellow et al., section 7.8 and Figure 7.3|https://www.deeplearningbook.org/contents/regularization.html}}). In practice: evaluate every so often, keep the checkpoint with the best validation loss, and stop once it has not improved for a set number of evaluations (the patience). It is free and always on, and it composes with everything else here. Two costs: it needs a validation set, and the data held out for it are not trained on; Goodfellow et al. describe retraining on all the data for the number of steps early stopping found (Algorithm 7.2).

Why it regularises. Stopping gradient descent after τ steps of size ε keeps the weights near where they started. For a linear model with a quadratic loss, starting from 0, it is equivalent to L2 regularisation with α ≈ 1/(τε) (eq. 7.45): the number of steps plays the role of the inverse weight decay. The early-stopping mode of the animation in section 2 shows it on real data: the gradient-descent iterates stay close to the ridge path, closest for the weakly correlated pair, and visibly apart for the two cholesterol measures correlated at 0.90, where the approximation's assumptions are stretched.

A recorded run is on the parent page: When training goes wrong → "Train falls, validation rises" (300 digit images with 30% of labels wrong, best validation step marked), and the Training lab's preset "Early stopping keeps the best validation loss", which also shows the gap between stopping and rolling back to the best checkpoint. In mixup's corrupted-label experiments the gap between the best and last epoch is what early stopping would have saved: with half the CIFAR-10 labels randomised, plain training reached 18.8% test error at its best epoch and ended at 44.6% (Zhang et al. 2018, Table 2; the "Corrupted labels" view below).

7 · What each buys, measured

Published comparisons that ran several regularisers on one model, one dataset and one training recipe. Pick a study; bars are the change in test error against the study's own baseline run (the dashed line), so a bar to the left is a gain.

How to read these
Each study is one run per setting, so differences of a few tenths of a point are within noise. CutMix's tables are its authors' re-runs of the rival methods (except the rows marked *, taken from the original papers), in a study written to show CutMix winning. Gains do not add: Cutout plus label smoothing beat either alone on CIFAR-100, while on CIFAR-10 weight decay added nothing to random crops for Inception.

8 · Regularisation in networks that can memorise everything

Zhang et al. trained standard image networks on CIFAR-10 with the labels replaced by random ones. Every network still fitted them, 99.3% to 100% training accuracy with test accuracy at chance, about 10% ({{Zhang et al. 2017, Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03530}}). Explicit regularisers did not stop it: with weight decay on, Inception and both MLPs still fitted random labels (99.2% to 100%), and so did Inception with random crops or augmentation, given more epochs; only AlexNet with weight decay failed to converge (Appendix, Table 4). With true labels, the same Inception reached 100% training accuracy with or without random crops and weight decay, and its test accuracy moved only from 85.75% (neither) to 89.05% (both) or 89.31% (crops alone). Their conclusion: explicit regularisation "may improve generalization performance, but is neither necessary nor by itself sufficient for controlling generalization error", and the architecture and SGD itself regularise implicitly.

Double descent. Classical intuition says test error rises steadily once a model has more parameters than examples. For many models it instead peaks where the model can just barely interpolate the training set, then falls again, and well-tuned L2 regularisation removes the peak: Nakkiran et al. prove it for isotropic linear regression and show it empirically for random-feature models and CNNs ({{2020, abstract and section 5.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.01897}}). Their random ReLU features experiment, repeated here on the digits at a smaller size, reproduces both the peak and its removal:

Nakkiran et al. used Fashion-MNIST (d = 784) and n = 500; here scikit-learn's digits (d = 64), n = 200 training images, the other 1,597 for testing, mean of 10 seeds (features and training images redrawn). Grokking. On small algorithmic datasets (modular arithmetic tables), Power et al. saw networks fit the training set early and then, long after, jump from chance to perfect validation accuracy; of the interventions they tried, "weight decay is particularly effective at improving generalization", more than halving the data needed ({{2022, sections 1 and 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02177}}).

Language models. A model that sees most of its data once has little opportunity to overfit, which is why LLM pretraining dropped dropout (section 4) but kept weight decay for its effect on the optimisation (section 3). Overfitting returns with repetition: fine-tuning on small datasets for several epochs is where dropout, LoRA dropout, early stopping on a validation set and data augmentation come back.

9 · Common mistakes

Dropout left on at evaluation, or BatchNorm in training mode
In PyTorch, dropout and BatchNorm behave differently under model.train() and model.eval(). Evaluating in training mode drops units at random and gives noisy, worse numbers; forgetting the inference-side correction in a hand-written scheme gives inputs 1/p times larger than training ever saw (the "Scaling forgotten" mode in section 4).
Reading the dropout p the wrong way round
The 2014 paper's p is the keep probability; PyTorch's is the drop probability. "p = 0.8 for inputs" in the paper is nn.Dropout(0.2), and nn.Dropout(0.8) would keep only a fifth of the inputs.
L2 in the loss with Adam, when weight decay was meant
Adam's weight_decay argument adds λw to the gradient (L2), which Adam then rescales per parameter. Use AdamW for decoupled decay (section 3; the parent's thread 4 and Training lab preset).
Decaying biases and normalisation gains
Put weight matrices in one parameter group with decay and biases, LayerNorm and RMSNorm gains in another with none, as nanoGPT does.
Copying λ between libraries or learning rates
scikit-learn's Ridge alpha is n times its Lasso alpha for the same penalty; PyTorch's AdamW decay per step is learning rate × λ, so a λ tuned at one learning rate is a different decay at another (sections 2 and 3).
Penalising features that were never standardised
L1 and L2 penalties depend on each feature's units; unscaled features are penalised unequally and the lasso selects by unit rather than by importance. Standardise on the training split only, then apply the same transform to validation and test data.
Choosing λ, patience or the stopping step on the test set
Every regularisation knob is a hyperparameter; tuning it on the test set leaks it. Use a validation set or cross-validation. (The "best λ" curve in section 8 is chosen on its test images, as in the paper it follows, and labelled so.)
An augmentation that changes the label
Flipping digits or text, rotating a 6 into a 9, cropping out the object: the transform must preserve the label for the task at hand. Mixup and CutMix change the label on purpose, and say by how much.
Regularising a model that is underfitting
If training loss is itself high, more regularisation makes it worse. Check the training curve first; the parent's When training goes wrong tab starts from the symptom.

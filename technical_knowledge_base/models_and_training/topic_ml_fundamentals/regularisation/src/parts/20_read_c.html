5 · Data augmentation, mixup and CutMix

Data augmentation applies random but realistic transforms (flips, rotations, crops, colour shifts, noise) that do not change the label, which enlarges and diversifies the training set: it attacks overfitting at the data level, and is the highest-leverage fix where it is possible. A transform is only an augmentation if it preserves the label, and that depends on the task: a horizontal flip is fine for photographs of cats and wrong for digits, where a flipped 2 is not a 2.

Mixup trains on blends of two random training examples and their one-hot labels, x̃ = λxi + (1 − λ)xj, ỹ = λyi + (1 − λ)yj, with λ drawn from Beta(α, α); α → 0 recovers ordinary training. CutMix pastes a random rectangle of one image into another and mixes the labels in proportion to the area ({{Yun et al. 2019|https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.04899}}); Cutout blanks a random square with no label change ({{DeVries and Taylor 2017|https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.04552}}).

What they bought. On ImageNet, mixup with α between 0.1 and 0.4 helped and larger α underfitted; ResNet-50's top-1 error fell from 23.5% to 23.3% at 90 epochs and from 23.6% to 22.1% at 200 epochs, bigger models and longer runs gaining most ({{Zhang et al. 2018, section 3.1 and Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.09412}}).

AlexNet's augmentation, its cost, and searched policies (depth)

AlexNet's random 224 × 224 crops and their horizontal reflections from 256 × 256 images "increases the size of our training set by a factor of 2048" (with highly dependent examples), and without them "our network suffers from substantial overfitting"; random shifts of colour along the principal components of the RGB values "reduces the top-1 error rate by over 1%" ({{Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 4.1|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf}}). AlexNet computed its crops on the CPU while the GPU trained, so they cost nothing. Later work searched the space of transforms automatically: RandAugment, with two parameters, matched AutoAugment's searched policy on ImageNet, 77.6% top-1 for ResNet-50 against 76.3% for the standard baseline ({{Cubuk et al. 2019, Table 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.13719}}).

Label smoothing is the target-side relative: train against (1 − ε) on the true class and ε spread over the rest, which caps how confident the model becomes. Its formulas, its two conventions, its effect on calibration and why it hurts distillation are on {{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}; the parent's Training lab has the preset "Label smoothing caps overconfidence". As a regulariser of accuracy its record is mixed: on CIFAR-100 with PyramidNet-200 it raised top-1 error from 16.45% to 16.73% on its own, and helped only combined with Cutout or DropBlock (Yun et al., Table 5; section 7).

6 · Early stopping

Monitor the validation loss during training and stop before the model overfits: training loss keeps falling while validation loss traces an asymmetric U ({{Goodfellow et al., section 7.8 and Figure 7.3|https://www.deeplearningbook.org/contents/regularization.html}}), as the hero's unregularised run does (lowest at epoch 60, 0.0974, then up to 0.1297 by epoch 300). In practice: evaluate every so often, keep the checkpoint with the best validation loss, and stop once it has not improved for a set number of evaluations (the patience). It is free, and it composes with everything else here. Two costs: it needs a validation set, and the data held out for it are not trained on; Goodfellow et al. describe retraining on all the data for the number of steps early stopping found (Algorithm 7.2).

Why it regularises. Stopping gradient descent after τ steps of size ε keeps the weights near where they started. For a linear model with a quadratic loss, starting from 0, it is equivalent to L2 regularisation with α ≈ 1/(τε) (eq. 7.45): the number of steps plays the role of the inverse weight decay. The early-stopping mode of the animation in section 2 shows it on real data: the gradient-descent iterates stay close to the ridge path, closest for the weakly correlated pair, and visibly apart for the two cholesterol measures correlated at 0.90, where the approximation's assumptions are stretched.

What it saves can be large. In mixup's corrupted-label experiments, with half the CIFAR-10 labels randomised, plain training reached 18.8% test error at its best epoch and ended at 44.6% (Zhang et al. 2018, Table 2; the "Corrupted labels" view in section 7). Recorded runs are on the parent page: When training goes wrong → "Train falls, validation rises" (300 digit images with 30% of labels wrong, best validation step marked), and the Training lab's preset "Early stopping keeps the best validation loss", which also shows the gap between stopping and rolling back to the best checkpoint.

8 · Regularisation in networks that can memorise everything

Zhang et al. trained standard image networks on CIFAR-10 with the labels replaced by random ones. Every network still fitted them, 99.3% to 100% training accuracy with test accuracy at chance, about 10% ({{Zhang et al. 2017, Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03530}}). Explicit regularisers did not stop it: with weight decay on, Inception and both MLPs still fitted random labels (99.2% to 100%), and so did Inception with random crops or augmentation, given more epochs; only AlexNet with weight decay failed to converge (Appendix, Table 4). Their conclusion: explicit regularisation "may improve generalization performance, but is neither necessary nor by itself sufficient for controlling generalization error", and the architecture and SGD itself regularise implicitly.

Double descent. Classical intuition says test error rises steadily once a model has more parameters than examples. For many models it instead peaks at the interpolation threshold, where the model can just barely fit the training set, then falls again; well-tuned L2 regularisation removes the peak. Nakkiran et al. prove it for isotropic linear regression and show it for random-feature models and CNNs ({{2020, abstract and section 5.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.01897}}). Their random ReLU features experiment, repeated here on the digits at a smaller size, reproduces both the peak and its removal.

Which term makes the peak. Belkin et al. (PNAS 2019, arXiv 1812.11118): "This 'double descent' curve subsumes the textbook U-shaped bias-variance trade-off curve by showing how increasing model capacity beyond the point of interpolation results in improved performance." The smallest setting where this can be checked against an exact formula is Belkin, Hsu and Xu's weak-features regression (SIAM J. Math. Data Science 2020, arXiv 1903.07571): 100 independent Gaussian features, a target linear in all 100 plus noise (\(\sigma = 0.2\), \(\lVert\beta\rVert = 1\)), a model that sees only the first \(p\) features, fitted by least squares on \(n = 40\) examples (the minimum-norm solution once \(p > 40\), which is what gradient descent from zero finds). We refitted it on 1,000 independent training sets for every \(p\) from 1 to 100 and split the test error into bias² and variance (section 1) simulated (with Gaussian inputs a fit's test error is exact, \(\lVert\hat w - \beta_{1..p}\rVert^2 + \lVert\beta_{p+1..D}\rVert^2 + \sigma^2\)); against the paper's closed-form Theorem 1 (excerpt in src/inputs/) our averages reproduce it independently to within 3.2% everywhere at least 5 features from the threshold. At \(p = 39, 40, 41\) the theorem's expectation is infinite, and our average there is dominated by a few near-singular training sets.

Variance, not bias, makes the peak, and a little ridge removes it

Why the peak: the smallest singular value of the training matrix collapses at \(p = n\)

Median over the 1,000 training sets of the smallest singular value of the \(40 \times p\) training inputs: 0.086 at \(p = 40\) against 3.55 at \(p = 10\) and 3.95 at \(p = 100\). Least squares divides the noise by these singular values ({{Linear algebra for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d89c26e1dab2fcb701}} owns the SVD), so at \(p = n\) (a square random matrix) the fit amplifies noise without limit; this is one of the three factors in Schaeffer et al., "Double Descent Demystified" (arXiv 2303.14151). Past the threshold the minimum-norm solution spreads the weight over many directions and variance falls again. Ridge adds \(\lambda\) to the squared singular values, which caps the amplification: \(\lambda = 0.1\) cuts the error at \(p = 40\) from about 285,600 to 6.8. With \(\lambda = 0\) the circles are Theorem 1, exact. weak_features_dd.py.

Switch the signal: whether the second descent beats the first depends on the problem, and tuned ridge makes the curve monotonic.

Which descent wins, tuned ridge, and when more data hurts (depth)

Selecting features in order of decreasing \(\beta_j^2\), as the two decaying signals do, is the paper's "prescient" selection model (its Figure 2).

With the signal spread evenly the best error is at \(p = 100\) (0.667, against 1.057 at best below the threshold); when the first features carry most of the signal, the classic sweet spot wins (0.119 at \(p = 14\) against 0.440). Nakkiran et al. (arXiv 2003.01897) prove that "optimally-tuned \(\ell_2\) regularization achieves monotonic test performance as we grow either the sample size or the model size" in this kind of setting. More data can hurt: the same formula at fixed \(p = 60\) with the signal spread evenly gives 1.195 at 30 examples, 6.54 at 55 (near the threshold \(n = p\)), 1.83 at 80 and only 0.63 at 200. Nakkiran et al. ("Deep Double Descent", arXiv 1912.02292) found the same in deep networks: "increasing (even quadrupling) the number of train samples actually hurts test performance" in some regimes, and "double descent occurs not just as a function of model size, but also as a function of the number of training epochs".

Checked against the papers
The old notes said "the decomposition still holds; the variance term just behaves non-monotonically". The decomposition is an identity, so it always holds, and in this linear run the variance is indeed what spikes. But as an explanation of double descent in deep networks that sentence is a simplification: Yang et al. (ICML 2020, arXiv 2002.11328) measure that "the bias is monotonically decreasing as in the classical theory, the variance is unimodal or bell-shaped" with width, Nakkiran et al. show epoch-wise and sample-wise versions and propose an "effective model complexity", and no single mechanism is agreed for deep networks (unconfirmed as a single cause).

Grokking. On small algorithmic datasets (modular arithmetic tables), Power et al. saw networks fit the training set early and then, long after, jump from chance to perfect validation accuracy; of the interventions they tried, "weight decay is particularly effective at improving generalization", more than halving the data needed ({{2022, sections 1 and 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02177}}).

9 · What large-model training still uses

A model that sees most of its data once has little opportunity to overfit, so LLM pretraining dropped dropout (PaLM, T5 1.1 and nanoGPT pretrain with none, where the 2017 Transformer and BERT used 0.1) but kept weight decay, 0.1 with AdamW in GPT-3, for its effect on the optimisation (section 3). Overfitting returns with repetition: fine-tuning on small datasets for several epochs is where dropout (0.1 in PaLM's fine-tuning), LoRA dropout (0.05 to 0.1 in QLoRA), early stopping on a validation set and data augmentation come back. Image classifiers, trained for many epochs on far fewer examples, are where augmentation, mixing and stochastic depth still earn whole points (section 7).

10 · Mistakes that the sections above do not show

Evaluating with dropout or BatchNorm still in training mode
In PyTorch, dropout and BatchNorm behave differently under model.train() and model.eval(). Evaluating in training mode drops units at random and gives noisy, worse numbers; forgetting the inference-side correction in a hand-written scheme gives inputs 1/p times larger than training ever saw (the "Scaling forgotten" mode in section 4).
Adam's weight_decay argument is L2, not decoupled decay
torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=...) adds λw to the gradient, which Adam then rescales per parameter (section 3). Use AdamW for decoupled decay, with weight matrices in one parameter group and biases, LayerNorm and RMSNorm gains in another with no decay, as nanoGPT does.
Copying λ between libraries or learning rates
scikit-learn's Ridge alpha is n times its Lasso alpha for the same penalty; PyTorch's AdamW decay per step is learning rate × λ, so a λ tuned at one learning rate is a different decay at another (sections 2 and 3).
Standardising with statistics from the whole dataset
L1 and L2 penalties depend on each feature's units, so features must be standardised; but compute the means and scales on the training split only, then apply the same transform to validation and test data, as the held-out chart in section 1 does.
Choosing λ, patience or the stopping step on the test set
Every regularisation knob is a hyperparameter; tuning it on the test set leaks it. Use a validation set or cross-validation. (The "best λ" curves in sections 1 and 8 are chosen on their test data, as in the paper they follow, and labelled so.)
An augmentation that changes the label
Flipping digits or text, rotating a 6 into a 9, cropping out the object: the transform must preserve the label for the task at hand. Mixup and CutMix change the label on purpose, and say by how much.
Regularising a model that is underfitting
If training loss is itself high, more regularisation makes it worse. Check the training curve first; the parent's When training goes wrong tab starts from the symptom.

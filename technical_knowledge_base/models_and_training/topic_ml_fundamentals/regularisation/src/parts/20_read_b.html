3 · Weight decay, and why AdamW decouples it

Weight decay pulls every weight a little towards 0 at each step. It can be implemented two ways:

  1. L2 in the loss: add (λ/2)‖w‖2 to the objective. The penalty enters the gradient as λw and then flows through the optimiser's machinery.
  2. Decoupled, in the optimiser: change the update rule itself, w ← w − ηλw, applied beside the gradient step.

For plain SGD the two coincide, up to rescaling λ by the learning rate ({{Loshchilov and Hutter 2017, Proposition 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05101}}). For Adam they do not: the L2 term is divided by the per-parameter √v̂ like the rest of the gradient, so weights with large, noisy gradients are barely decayed while weights with tiny gradients are pulled to 0 at full speed. Decoupling restores the same decay for every weight, and that is the whole of AdamW.

Predict: six weights get pure noise as their gradient, from tiny to large, so the only systematic force on them is the decay. Under Adam with L2, which end lowest, the quiet ones or the noisy ones? Fig. 3 runs both optimisers on the same noise.

What λ multiplies. In Loshchilov and Hutter's Algorithm 2 the decay step is ηtλθ, where ηt is the schedule multiplier and not the base learning rate α, so decay and learning rate are tuned independently. PyTorch's AdamW applies θ ← θ − γλθ with γ the learning rate itself ({{PyTorch 2.14 docs|https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/generated/torch.optim.AdamW.html}}; default λ = 0.01). So weight_decay=0.1 at a learning rate of 3 × 10−4 shrinks each weight by 3 × 10−5 of itself per step derived: γλ, and halving the learning rate halves the decay too. The paper's λ is PyTorch's λ times α.

Which parameters. Decay is usually applied to weight matrices only. nanoGPT decays every parameter with two or more dimensions (matrix weights and embeddings) and leaves biases and LayerNorm gains alone ({{model.py, configure_optimizers|https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT/blob/master/model.py}}), with λ = 0.1 ({{train.py|https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT/blob/master/train.py}}). Decaying a normalisation gain pulls it towards 0 and fights the normalisation it belongs to.

Typical values. AlexNet used SGD with weight decay 0.0005 and found "this small amount of weight decay was important for the model to learn. In other words, weight decay here is not merely a regularizer: it reduces the model's training error" ({{Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 5|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf}}). With AdamW, 0.1 is the usual pretraining value: GPT-3 ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8193ac92c7648cfaca12}}), ViT ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa231cfcde6c1954d}}); BERT used L2 weight decay 0.01 ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e5ad9bd09cbf18ad7c}}).

Why decay at all if an LLM never overfits? A model that sees most of its data once has little to overfit, yet nearly every LLM recipe keeps weight decay. D'Angelo, Andriushchenko et al. argue that in both regimes "weight decay is never useful as an explicit regularizer but instead changes the training dynamics in a desirable way": with multi-epoch SGD on vision tasks it strengthens SGD's implicit regularisation, and in near one-epoch LLM training it balances the bias-variance trade-off of stochastic optimisation, giving lower training loss and more stable training ({{NeurIPS 2024, abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04415}}). AlexNet's authors saw the same in 2012: decay lowered the training error.

4 · Dropout

In each training step every unit is dropped independently at random: its output is 0 for that step, with all its incoming and outgoing connections. No unit can rely on one specific partner (co-adaptation), so the network learns redundant, robust features. A network with n units contains 2n possible thinned networks sharing one set of weights, and each training case trains a freshly sampled one: dropout trains an implicit ensemble ({{Srivastava et al. 2014, section 1|https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/srivastava14a.html}}). At test time there are three ways to use it: the single weight-scaled network (with inverted dropout, the plain forward pass), the arithmetic mean of k sampled thinned networks (Srivastava et al.'s Monte Carlo average), or their geometric mean, which AlexNet's authors said weight scaling approximates.

Corrected: the paper keeps about half the hidden units, not 0.8
An older version of this page said to keep about 0.8 of hidden units. Srivastava et al. write that the retention probability "can simply be set at 0.5, which seems to be close to optimal for a wide range of networks and tasks. For the input units, however, the optimal probability of retention is usually closer to 1 than to 0.5" (section 1). Their practical guide: "Typical values of p for hidden units are in the range 0.5 to 0.8. For input layers ... For real-valued inputs (image patches or speech frames), a typical value is 0.8" (Appendix A.4), and their MNIST runs use 0.5 for hidden units and 0.8 for inputs (sections 6.1 and 7.4). So: keep about 0.5 for hidden units, about 0.8 for inputs. On MNIST with the width fixed, test error was flat for retention between 0.4 and 0.8 and rose towards 1 (section 7.3, Figure 9a).
p means opposite things in the paper and in PyTorch
In Srivastava et al., p is the probability of keeping a unit. In PyTorch's nn.Dropout(p) it is the probability of dropping it (default 0.5), and Keras and most papers since follow the framework. The paper's "p = 0.8 for inputs" is nn.Dropout(0.2), and nn.Dropout(0.8) would keep only a fifth. Even the mixup paper's dropout experiments use the dropping convention (p ∈ {0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}; {{Zhang et al. 2018, section 3.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.09412}}).

The factor 1/p goes either in training or at inference, never nowhere

Train against inference: where the scaling goes

Training drops units; inference drops nothing. With every unit present a layer would send about 1/p times what the next layer saw in training, so the activations are rescaled to match the expected value: the original scheme multiplies them (equivalently the outgoing weights) by p at inference; inverted dropout, what frameworks do, divides the survivors by p in training, so inference is a plain forward pass. The two "are equivalent with appropriate scaling of the learning rate and weight initializations at each layer" (Srivastava et al., section 10).

Watch the inverted scheme, then switch to the original one: the numbers that reach the next layer are the same, only the place of the factor moves. Then switch to "Scaling forgotten" to see the bug; the counters under the chart carry this pass's numbers.

  1. The layer. 96 activations of the first hidden layer, from one real test digit; together they send their sum to the next layer.
  2. Draw a mask. Each unit is kept with probability p = 0.5, independently, a coin per unit (× marks the dropped ones).
  3. Drop. Dropped units output 0 for this pass and receive no gradient; the survivors send about half as much.
  4. Scale in training? Inverted dropout divides every survivor by p, so each doubles (green); the original scheme and the bug pass them through unchanged.
  5. One training pass. What this pass sends to the next layer. Every training step draws a fresh mask, so the next layer sees a different thinned network each time.
  6. Over 1,000 masks. The average of what the layer sends, against the exact expectation p × (sum of all activations) × (training scale): the size of input the next layer learned its weights for.
  7. Inference: nothing dropped. Every unit is present. Inverted dropout scales nothing, a plain forward pass; the original scheme multiplies the outputs by p = 0.5 (orange), the paper's "weights are multiplied by p at test time"; with the bug nothing is scaled because the training-side scaling never happened.
  8. Train and test agree, or not. With either scheme inference sends the training average: they differ only in where the factor sits. With the scaling forgotten, inference sends twice what the next layer saw in training. Frameworks build the scaling in, so the usual slip there is evaluating in train() mode, where dropout keeps firing.

"Sent" is the plain sum of the layer's outputs; the mean over 1,000 masks uses a fixed seed. Exact expectation: p × (sum of all activations) × (training scale). Forgetting the factor sends inputs 2× larger than training ever saw.

How good the weight-scaled shortcut is. Averaging all 2n thinned networks is impossible; the weight-scaled network is the shortcut. On MNIST, Srivastava et al. found that "around k = 50, the Monte-Carlo method becomes as good as the approximate method" and thereafter slightly better, "well within one standard deviation" (section 7.5). AlexNet calls the shortcut "a reasonable approximation to taking the geometric mean of the predictive distributions" of the thinned networks ({{Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 4.2|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf}}). Gal and Ghahramani read dropout as approximate Bayesian inference and kept it on at test time to get uncertainty from the spread of the sampled predictions, "MC dropout" ({{2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142}}).

Dropout on a linear model is ridge. For linear regression with each input kept with probability p, the expected loss is ‖y − pXw‖2 + p(1 − p)‖Γw‖2 with Γ = (diag(XᵀX))1/2: ridge with each weight's penalty scaled by its feature's spread, strength (1 − p)/p after absorbing p into w ({{Srivastava et al., section 9.1|https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/srivastava14a.html}}). On the diabetes data, whose columns have unit length so Γ = I, input dropout with keep 0.8 is ridge with alpha = 0.25: averaged over 4,000 random masks, the fitted coefficients match scikit-learn's ridge to within 0.3% of the largest coefficient measured (recompute.py).

Variants

  • Stochastic depth drops whole residual blocks instead of units, with a survival probability falling linearly from 1 at the input to pL = 0.5 at the last block ({{Huang et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09382}}); a dropped block passes its input through the skip connection, so the network is shallower for that step. Vision Transformers call it drop-path. In CutMix's ImageNet comparison it took ResNet-50 from 23.68% to 22.46% top-1 error (section 7).
  • DropBlock drops contiguous regions of a convolutional feature map, because neighbouring activations are correlated and information still flows past single dropped units ({{Ghiasi et al. 2018, abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.12890}}); attention dropout drops attention weights.
  • Gaussian dropout multiplies activations by noise from N(1, σ2) with σ2 = (1 − p)/p, matching Bernoulli dropout's mean and variance with no scaling at test time; it worked "as well, or perhaps better" (Srivastava et al., section 10).
The 2014 practical guide, and dropout with BatchNorm (depth)
  • Width: if n units are right without dropout, use at least n/p with it (Appendix A.1).
  • Learning rate and momentum: dropout makes gradients noisy, so the paper used 10 to 100 times the usual learning rate, or momentum 0.95 to 0.99 instead of 0.9 (A.2), with max-norm constraints (each unit's incoming weight vector capped at a norm c of 3 to 4) to stop the weights blowing up (A.3). On MNIST, dropout with max-norm gave 1.05% error against 1.62% for L2 alone (Table 9, section 7).
  • With BatchNorm: dropout shifts a unit's variance between training and test, while BatchNorm keeps the running variance it measured in training; when dropout sits before a BatchNorm layer this "variance shift" makes the pair worse than either alone; one of Li et al.'s remedies is to apply dropout only after the last BatchNorm layer ({{Li et al. 2018, abstract and Table 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05134}}). BatchNorm also regularises a little by itself, through the noise of its batch statistics, "in some cases eliminating the need for Dropout" ({{Ioffe and Szegedy 2015, abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.03167}}; more on {{Normalisation and initialisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81369e1cc38ed4e11d48}}).

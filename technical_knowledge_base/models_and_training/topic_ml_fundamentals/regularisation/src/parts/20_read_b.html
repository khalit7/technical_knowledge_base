3 · Weight decay: two implementations, not equivalent

Weight decay penalises large weights so the model captures patterns rather than memorising. It can be implemented two ways:

  1. L2 in the loss: add (λ/2)‖w‖2 to the objective. The penalty enters the gradient as λw and then flows through the optimiser's machinery.
  2. Decoupled, in the optimiser: change the update rule itself, w ← w − ηλw, applied alongside the gradient step.

For plain SGD the two coincide, up to rescaling λ by the learning rate ({{Loshchilov and Hutter 2017, Proposition 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.05101}}). For adaptive optimisers such as Adam they do not: the L2 term is divided by the per-parameter second-moment term √v̂ like the rest of the gradient, so weights with large gradients are barely decayed. Decoupling restores the same decay for every weight, which is the whole point of AdamW. The parent page animates both on the same noise (Reading tab, thread 4) and its Training lab has the preset "Adam with L2 against AdamW"; the optimiser side is on {{Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers|n:3c65c17b0d0d817080bbc90954681d09}}. Three regularisation details remain for this page.

What λ multiplies. In Loshchilov and Hutter's Algorithm 2 the decay step is ηtλθ, where ηt is the schedule multiplier and not the base learning rate α, so decay and learning rate are tuned independently. PyTorch's AdamW applies θ ← θ − γλθ with γ the learning rate itself ({{PyTorch 2.14 docs|https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/generated/torch.optim.AdamW.html}}; default λ = 0.01). So PyTorch's weight_decay=0.1 at a learning rate of 3 × 10−4 shrinks each weight by 3 × 10−5 of itself per step derived: γλ, and halving the learning rate halves the decay too. The paper's λ is PyTorch's λ times α.

Which parameters. Decay is usually applied to weight matrices only. nanoGPT, for example, decays every parameter with two or more dimensions (matrix weights and embeddings) and leaves biases and LayerNorm gains alone ({{model.py, configure_optimizers|https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT/blob/master/model.py}}), with λ = 0.1 ({{train.py|https://github.com/karpathy/nanoGPT/blob/master/train.py}}). Decaying a normalisation gain pulls it towards 0 and fights the normalisation it belongs to.

Typical values. AlexNet used SGD with weight decay 0.0005 and found "this small amount of weight decay was important for the model to learn. In other words, weight decay here is not merely a regularizer: it reduces the model's training error" ({{Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 5|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf}}). With AdamW, 0.1 is the usual pretraining value: GPT-3 ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8193ac92c7648cfaca12}}), ViT ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa231cfcde6c1954d}}); BERT used L2 weight decay 0.01 ({{paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e5ad9bd09cbf18ad7c}}).

Why decay at all if an LLM never overfits? A model that sees most of its data once has little to overfit, yet nearly every LLM recipe keeps weight decay. D'Angelo, Andriushchenko et al. argue that in both regimes "weight decay is never useful as an explicit regularizer but instead changes the training dynamics in a desirable way": with multi-epoch SGD on vision tasks it strengthens SGD's implicit regularisation, and in near one-epoch LLM training it balances the bias-variance trade-off of stochastic optimisation, giving lower training loss and more stable training ({{NeurIPS 2024, abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04415}}). AlexNet's authors saw the same thing in 2012: decay lowered the training error.

4 · Dropout

During each training step, each unit is dropped independently at random: its output is set to 0 for that step, along with all its incoming and outgoing connections. This prevents co-adaptation: no unit can rely on one specific partner, so the network learns redundant, robust features. A network with n units contains 2n possible "thinned" networks sharing one set of weights, and each training case trains a freshly sampled one, so dropout is equivalent to training an implicit ensemble of subnetworks ({{Srivastava et al. 2014, section 1|https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/srivastava14a.html}}).

Corrected: the paper keeps about half the hidden units, not 0.8
The old version of this page said the authors suggest keeping about 0.8 of hidden units. Srivastava et al. write that the retention probability "can simply be set at 0.5, which seems to be close to optimal for a wide range of networks and tasks. For the input units, however, the optimal probability of retention is usually closer to 1 than to 0.5" (section 1). Their practical guide gives a range: "Typical values of p for hidden units are in the range 0.5 to 0.8. For input layers ... For real-valued inputs (image patches or speech frames), a typical value is 0.8" (Appendix A.4), and their MNIST runs use 0.5 for hidden units and 0.8 for inputs (sections 6.1 and 7.4). So: keep about 0.5 for hidden units, about 0.8 for inputs. On MNIST with the width fixed, test error was flat for retention between 0.4 and 0.8 and rose towards 1 (section 7.3, Figure 9a).
p means opposite things in the paper and in PyTorch
In Srivastava et al., p is the probability of keeping a unit. In PyTorch's nn.Dropout(p), p is the probability of dropping it (default 0.5), and Keras and most papers since follow the framework. The paper's "p = 0.8 for inputs" is nn.Dropout(0.2). Even the mixup paper's dropout experiments use the dropping convention (p ∈ {0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}; {{Zhang et al. 2018, section 3.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.09412}}).

Train against inference: where the scaling goes

Training drops units; inference drops nothing. With every unit present, a layer would send about 1/p times what the next layer saw in training, so the activations must be rescaled to match the expected value:

  • Original scheme: at inference, multiply the activations (equivalently the outgoing weights) by the keep probability p.
  • Inverted dropout (what frameworks actually do): at training, divide the surviving activations by p; inference is then a plain forward pass.

The two "are equivalent with appropriate scaling of the learning rate and weight initializations at each layer" (Srivastava et al., section 10).

Try: play the inverted scheme, then the original one: the numbers that reach the next layer are the same, only the place of the factor moves. Then play "Scaling forgotten" to see the bug.

The ensemble, and how good the shortcut is. At test time, averaging the predictions of all 2n thinned networks is impossible; the single weight-scaled network is the shortcut. Srivastava et al. tested it on MNIST against an honest Monte Carlo average of k sampled thinned networks: "around k = 50, the Monte-Carlo method becomes as good as the approximate method" and thereafter slightly better, "well within one standard deviation" (section 7.5). AlexNet describes the same shortcut as "a reasonable approximation to taking the geometric mean of the predictive distributions" of the thinned networks ({{Krizhevsky et al. 2012, section 4.2|https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2012/file/c399862d3b9d6b76c8436e924a68c45b-Paper.pdf}}). Gal and Ghahramani later read dropout as approximate Bayesian inference and kept it switched on at test time to get uncertainty from the spread of the sampled predictions, "MC dropout" ({{2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02142}}). The {{Dropout as an ensemble|#t-drop}} tab runs all three on a real network.

Dropout on a linear model is ridge. For linear regression with each input kept with probability p, the expected loss is ‖y − pXw‖2 + p(1 − p)‖Γw‖2 with Γ = (diag(XᵀX))1/2: ridge with each weight's penalty scaled by its feature's spread, and a strength (1 − p)/p after absorbing p into w ({{Srivastava et al., section 9.1|https://jmlr.org/papers/v15/srivastava14a.html}}). On the diabetes data, whose columns have unit length so Γ = I, input dropout with keep 0.8 is ridge with alpha = 0.25: averaged over 4,000 random masks, the fitted coefficients match scikit-learn's ridge to within 0.3% of the largest coefficient measured (recompute.py).

The practical guide, and the variants

  • Width: if n units are right without dropout, use at least n/p with it (Appendix A.1).
  • Learning rate and momentum: dropout makes gradients noisy, so the paper used 10 to 100 times the usual learning rate, or momentum 0.95 to 0.99 instead of 0.9 (A.2), with max-norm constraints (each unit's incoming weight vector capped at a norm c of 3 to 4) to stop the weights blowing up (A.3). On MNIST, dropout with max-norm gave 1.05% error against 1.62% for L2 alone (Table 9, below).
  • Gaussian dropout: multiplying activations by noise from N(1, σ2) with σ2 = (1 − p)/p matches Bernoulli dropout's mean and variance and needs no scaling at test time; it worked "as well, or perhaps better" (section 10).
  • Stochastic depth drops whole residual blocks instead of units, with a survival probability falling linearly from 1 at the input to pL = 0.5 at the last block ({{Huang et al. 2016|https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09382}}); vision Transformers call it drop-path. DropBlock drops contiguous regions of a convolutional feature map; attention dropout drops attention weights.
  • With BatchNorm: dropout shifts a unit's variance between training and test, while BatchNorm keeps the running variance it measured in training; when dropout sits before a BatchNorm layer this "variance shift" makes the pair worse than either alone; one of Li et al.'s remedies is to apply dropout only after the last BatchNorm layer ({{Li et al. 2018, abstract and Table 4|https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05134}}). BatchNorm also regularises a little by itself, through the noise of its batch statistics, "in some cases eliminating the need for Dropout" ({{Ioffe and Szegedy 2015, abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.03167}}; more on {{Normalisation and initialisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81369e1cc38ed4e11d48}}).

Regularisation is anything that trades a little fit on the training data for better performance on data the model has not seen. Classically that means a penalty term added to the loss that discourages large or complex weights; dropout, augmentation and early stopping regularise without any explicit penalty.

1 · What regularisation is, three ways

A penalty. Minimise the training loss J(w) plus λ·Ω(w), where Ω measures how complex the weights are and λ ≥ 0 sets the trade. λ = 0 is the unregularised fit; as λ grows the fit gets simpler and its training error rises. The right λ is the one that minimises error on held-out data, which is why it is tuned by cross-validation or a validation set, never on the test set.

A constraint. The same solutions come from minimising J(w) subject to Ω(w) ≤ t: each λ corresponds to some budget t (a Lagrange multiplier argument; {{Goodfellow et al., section 7.2|https://www.deeplearningbook.org/contents/regularization.html}}). Tibshirani defined the lasso this way, least squares subject to Σ|wj| ≤ t ({{1996|https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2517-6161.1996.tb02080.x}}). The constraint view is the one that explains why L1 gives zeros (the animation below).

A prior. Minimising the negative log-likelihood plus a penalty is maximum a posteriori estimation: the penalty is the negative log of a prior on the weights. A Gaussian prior N(0, τ2) gives the L2 penalty ‖w‖2/(2τ2); an isotropic Laplace prior gives the L1 penalty, log p(w) = −α‖w‖1 + constant ({{Goodfellow et al., eq. 7.24 and section 5.6.1|https://www.deeplearningbook.org/contents/regularization.html}}). For least squares with noise variance σ2 the ridge λ (on ‖y − Xw‖2 + λ‖w‖2) is σ2/τ2 derived: a strong prior belief that weights are small (small τ) is a large λ.

Whichever view, the effect is the bias-variance trade: a regularised model is biased towards simple answers, and in exchange its fit varies less from one training sample to the next. That buys most when data are scarce relative to the model; the Coefficient paths tab measures it on real data, with the training-set size as a slider.

Last row: for a quadratic loss with curvature H around its unregularised minimum w* and a penalty λ|w| or (λ/2)w2 ({{Goodfellow et al., eq. 7.13 and 7.23|https://www.deeplearningbook.org/contents/regularization.html}}). The L1 answer is exactly 0 whenever |w*| ≤ λ/H; the L2 answer is never 0 unless w* is.

Why L1 gives exact zeros. In the constraint view, the fit is the first point where the growing ellipses of equal training error touch the allowed region. The L2 region is a circle, with no corners, so the touching point is almost never on an axis. The L1 region is a diamond whose corners sit on the axes, and an ellipse coming from most directions meets a corner first: at a corner, one coefficient is exactly 0.

Try: play it with Lasso, then switch to Ridge. Each step allows a little more training error than the last, the same amount for every method, so the grey dot (the other method) always sits on the same ellipse as the fit: only the shape of the penalty decides where. Then switch the pair to s1 and s2, two cholesterol measures correlated at 0.90: the lasso keeps one and drops the other, ridge keeps both and pulls them towards each other. Penalty convention: (1/2n)‖y − Xb‖2 + λ(ρ‖b‖1 + (1 − ρ)/2 ‖b‖2), ρ = 1 lasso, 0 ridge, 0.5 elastic net. Early stopping: plain gradient descent on the same squared error from 0 with a small step η = n/(50λmax(XᵀX)), a hundredth of the largest stable step, stopped at the first step whose training error is at most the step's level. The λ of each method is found by bisection to match that level.

Elastic net. Zou and Hastie added an L2 term to the lasso because of three limits they list ({{2005, section 1|https://hastie.su.domains/Papers/elasticnet.pdf}}): with more features than examples (p > n) the lasso selects at most n of them before it saturates; from a group of highly correlated features it tends to select only one, and does not care which; and when n > p with high correlations, ridge predicts better than the lasso. The L2 part makes the penalty strictly convex, which gives the grouping effect: strongly correlated features get similar coefficients and enter or leave together.

The same λ means different things in different libraries
The page's convention is glmnet's and scikit-learn's Lasso and ElasticNet: (1/2n)‖y − Xb‖2 + λ(ρ‖b‖1 + (1 − ρ)/2 ‖b‖2), with scikit-learn calling λ alpha and ρ l1_ratio. scikit-learn's Ridge(alpha) instead minimises ‖y − Xb‖2 + alpha‖b‖2, with no 1/2n, so its alpha is nλ. The old form λ1‖w‖1 + λ2‖w‖2 maps to λρ and λ(1 − ρ)/2 after the same 1/2n scaling. A λ copied between conventions can be off by a factor of 2n.

Standardise first, and leave the intercept alone. The penalty treats all coefficients alike, so a feature measured in grams is penalised a thousand times less than the same feature in kilograms. Centre and scale every feature before fitting (the diabetes data here are scaled to unit length, as in Efron et al.'s equation 1.1), and do not penalise the intercept: centring the response removes it.

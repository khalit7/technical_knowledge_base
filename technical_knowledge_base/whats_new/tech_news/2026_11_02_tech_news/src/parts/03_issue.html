Oct 26Top Fortnow Oct 281,162 points714 comments (on 2026-11-02)

OpenAI and Anthropic each published a machine-checked refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis on the same day. On 26 October OpenAI announced three model-produced results (a refutation of ETH, a proof of the local smoothing conjecture, and an existence result in quantum chromodynamics) and set up outside working groups to check them, with Russell Impagliazzo and Mohan Paturi, two of ETH's originators, and Ryan Williams on the ETH group. Anthropic's repository credits an internal Claude research model with a deterministic algorithm that counts 3-SAT solutions in time 2^O(n log log n / sqrt(log n)), so 3-SAT is decidable in 2^o(n) time, with a Lean 4 formalization written by AI models that builds against Mathlib. Anthropic states the limits itself: the result is purely asymptotic and "says nothing about solving formulas in practice", and Lean checks the proofs but "no machine checks that this file says what the paper says". Lance Fortnow wrote on 28 October that he had not verified the proof but "it is safe to assume now that the exponential-time hypothesis is toast"; it covers k-SAT, not NP in general, and gives nothing new for problems like Subset Sum. Separately, Axiom Math says agent swarms produced a complete Lean proof of the Classification of Finite Simple Groups (about 43.1 million lines in 24 days).

Oct 29Top Northflank Oct 30995 points553 comments (on 2026-11-02)

A tampered Claude Code extension and a platform breach went after the same prize: the developer's machine and the AI keys stored around it. On 29 October builds labelled Anthropic.claude-code 2.1.312 and 2.1.313 were live on Cursor's VS Code extension marketplace for about 11 hours (2:27am to 1:35pm PT); Cursor says they "were not developed by Anthropic" and Anthropic says its systems were not compromised. The code ran when the extension loaded, sent host details and the names of environment variables to an attacker's server, added an SSH key labelled svc-telemetry and installed persistence, so uninstalling the extension is not enough. Cursor shut its marketplace down, rebuilt it and had the server taken offline; Microsoft's marketplace, Open VSX and the npm CLI were not affected. On 30 October Northflank told customers to "assume environment variables were decrypted" and rotate every secret, and users reported their stored AI API keys being used. With last week's LiteLLM compromise, the target is consistent: wherever an AI provider key sits, on a laptop, in a proxy or on a platform.

Research & papers 15 items

Three threads. RL post-training kept finding that the sampler is not the policy you think it is, this time because of how weights are rounded. Reward-hacking work moved from naming the problem to budgets and conditions: when models chase a score, and where to spend an expensive check. And several papers tested the tools used to watch models, from chain-of-thought reliance to the datasets monitors are scored on.

Oct 29Research

Fair Cast: bf16 rounding hides most RL updates from the sampler (Stanford, 29 October): RL trainers keep fp32 master weights and serve rollouts from a round-to-nearest bf16 copy, so updates smaller than about half the bf16 spacing vanish; on Qwen3-1.7B only 0.4 to 0.5% of served weights changed per step, and existing corrections cannot see the gap because they compare two copies downstream of the same rounding. The fix exports with stochastic rounding and computes the loss on the served copy; in single-GPU GRPO runs on Qwen3-0.6B to 4B it ended more than a point ahead on 18 of 25 matched seed-pairs, within a point on 6 and behind on 1. Two companions the same week split the mismatch into engine noise and policy lag (DLC) and measured MXFP8 rollouts on B200.

Before and after: what the sampler serves when a tiny update meets bf16

One weight of 0.03 in an RL run. The trainer keeps it in fp32 and adds a small update every step; the sampler that generates rollouts gets a bf16 copy, and bf16 can only hold the values on the grey lines, 0.000122 apart near 0.03. Step through round-to-nearest export first, then switch to stochastic rounding. The update size (0.000001 a step) is chosen so that, as in the paper, it is far below half the grid spacing.

An illustration of the mechanism in {{Fair Cast, arXiv 2610.41073|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41073}} with magnitudes of the kind the paper reports (on Qwen3-1.7B only 0.4 to 0.5% of served weights changed per step, and the sampler lagged the optimiser by about 50 to 150 steps); it is not the paper's data. bf16 spacing near 0.03 is 2-13; the stochastic rounding draws come from a fixed seed. The paper's fix also computes the loss on the served copy, which this picture does not show; the {{paper page|n:40c5c17b0d0d81c39641d49880e39f73}} has the evidence.

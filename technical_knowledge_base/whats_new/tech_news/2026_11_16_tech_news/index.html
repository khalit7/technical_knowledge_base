Nov 13Top wfx worm Nov 10 Apiiro Nov 6

AI provider keys leaked three ways in one fortnight: through agent traces shared with a vendor, through CI runner memory, and through a permission prompt that saved secrets into a settings file. Cursor's update of 13 November traces the tampered Claude Code extension of 29 October to an access key Cursor had issued to Fireworks, a training and inference vendor: the attacker copied a bucket of coding-agent traces, some holding unredacted credentials, and used one of those credentials to publish the extension, which at most 5,000 users could have received; Cursor moved all inference off Fireworks. From 10 to 12 November the wfx worm used stolen GitHub tokens to plant credential stealers in 8,093 repositories (SafeDep), taking registry tokens, cloud credentials and OpenAI and Anthropic keys scanned from CI runner memory, and leaving AWS and Azure persistence that survives secret rotation. And Apiiro showed that Claude Code's "Yes, and don't ask again" saves the literal command as an allow rule in .claude/settings.local.json, so a secret typed inline lands in a file that is easy to commit; it counted 1,122 public GitHub files with real secrets.

Nov 10Top Hyperclique Nov 3 AlphaProof Code Nov 10

A physics committee validated OpenAI's machine-checked Yang-Mills construction and said exactly what it does not prove, while the Exponential Time Hypothesis refutations still await their reviewers. On 10 November a committee chaired by David Gross (with Michael Douglas, Arthur Jaffe, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov) released its assessment of OpenAI's Lean code: it "proves that a relativistic quantum field theory satisfying the Wightman axioms can be constructed as a scaling limit of four-dimensional Wilson lattice gauge theory for any simple compact gauge group. It does not prove that the infinite-volume limit is unique or that there is a mass gap", so it is not the Millennium Prize problem. A clean rebuild, an axiom audit and a Comparator check passed, and the committee adds: "We do not claim to fully understand the proof yet." No finding on either ETH refutation had been published by 16 November. Meanwhile two human-checked papers refuted hyperclique conjectures with the main step credited to ChatGPT (MIT, arXiv 2611.05082; UIUC, arXiv 2611.06813), and Google DeepMind reports that AlphaProof Code agents wrote over six million lines of Lean proving security properties of Signal's compiled protocol code in four weeks, with humans reviewing the specifications and about 1% of the code.

Research & papers 15 items

Most of the fortnight's research asked one question of a familiar method: compared on what? A process reward wins per update and loses per token; FP4 matches BF16 on loss and lags on rare facts; a quantised model looks worse until restarts and batch sizes are given the same scrutiny; one agent beat another because half its episodes were never graded. Read each result with the denominator its authors chose.

Before and after: one token budget, two ways to spend it

The budget and the update counts are the paper's for Qwen2.5-1.5B (arXiv 2611.13560: 12.25M generated tokens, 584 GRPO updates, 50 for the Monte-Carlo reward with 4 continuations at each of 5 cuts, 91% of its tokens on continuations); tokens per iteration are the budget divided by the updates, so they are averages. Nov 10Research

Are dense rewards worth their tokens in GRPO? (Case Western Reserve University, 10 November): in 1,668 preregistered runs on 0.6B to 4B models trained on MATH, a Monte-Carlo process reward that led outcome-only GRPO by 1.5 points per update on Qwen2.5-1.5B (an exploratory comparison) spent 91% of its generated tokens on scoring continuations, so it reached 50 updates where GRPO reached 584 on the same token budget, and trailed by 14.3 points at equal tokens and 7.8 at equal GPU-hours (exploratory). Choosing prompts with predicted success near one half gained 1.7 and 0.8 points at equal tokens on Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen2.5-1.5B, with no significant gain on the other four models, and on Qwen3-0.6B GRPO with twice the learning rate matched it.

The paper gives every method the same budget of generated tokens, counting every token the method generates. The bar is that budget; each slice is one training iteration (8 prompts, 8 completions each, then one update). The chart plays outcome-only GRPO by itself once in view; then switch to the process reward with the buttons above it. The budget stays the same; only what an iteration spends changes.

Step by step

  1. One budget. 12.25M generated tokens for the whole run, the same for every method.
  2. One iteration, one update. GRPO samples 64 completions, scores each 0 or 1 with the verifier, and updates: about 21,000 tokens, all of them completions the update trains on.
  3. Halfway. 292 updates in, half the budget spent.
  4. The budget runs out at 584 updates. Every generated token went into a completion that was trained on.
  1. Same budget. The same 12.25M tokens, the same 64 completions per iteration.
  2. Each completion is cut and continued. To credit segments, every completion is cut at 5 points and 4 continuations are sampled from each cut; the continuations are scored and thrown away. One iteration costs about 245,000 tokens, 91% of them continuations.
  3. Halfway. 25 updates in, half the budget spent.
  4. The budget runs out at 50 updates. Each update was better (1.5 points ahead of GRPO at equal updates, an exploratory comparison) but there were 534 fewer of them: at equal tokens it ended 14.3 points behind, and 7.8 behind at equal GPU-hours (also exploratory).
Nov 2Research

When rewards depend on the group, GRPO plays a tournament (Edinburgh and Toronto, 2 November): with rewards that depend on groupmates (diversity bonuses, distribution matching), GRPO's group baseline weights a response's effect on the others by -1/(K-1) instead of +1, so a pairwise diversity bonus keeps 0, 1/3 and 3/7 of its weight at group sizes 2, 4 and 8; crediting each response with its marginal effect on the group (Team-GRPO) restores it. On one 9B model, squared error to the human answer distribution on GlobalOpinionQA was 0.20 untrained, 0.49 after GRPO and 0.08 after Team-GRPO.

@@CARD@@

The idea in one sentence: post-training raises an agent's pass@1 by pushing tasks to always-solved or never-solved, and a task at either extreme gains nothing from another attempt; the Sharpening Tax measures that lost retry value as the difference between base and post-trained models in the calibrated area under their pass@k curves (positive in 36 of 42 cells here), and PTGS tries to pay less of it by sampling the prompts a policy keeps failing hotter during RL.

You need: the pass@1 against pass@k debate about RL and base models ({{RL for LLMs, section 9|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}); PPO and GRPO ({{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}); sampling temperature ({{Sampling and Decoding|n:3c65c17b0d0d8115bafcda29a2c7086e}}). New terms: coverage (pass@k: at least one of k succeeds); consistency (passk: all k succeed); cell (one base/post-trained pair on one benchmark, 42 in all); crossover k* (the budget where base catches up); raw and calibrated scalability A(K), S(K); Sharpening Tax (base minus post-trained scalability); polarisation (tasks moving to 0 or 128 of 128); PTGS (posterior-tempered group sampling: a per-prompt temperature from a Beta posterior of its success rate).

Problem

[[S1|§1, §7]]

Reinforcement learning (RL) post-training turns a pre-trained "base" model, which only continues text, into a model that follows instructions, calls tools and reasons. It reliably raises pass@1, the chance that one attempt succeeds. The open question is whether it adds abilities the base model never had, or only makes the base model's rare good behaviours its usual ones. The second reading is called sharpening ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

The test so far has been pass@k, the chance that at least one of k attempts succeeds: on math and code, RL-trained models win at small k and their base models pass them at large k ({{Yue et al., 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837}}). That debate and its counter-evidence live in {{RL for LLMs, section 9|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}; this paper moves it to agents.

The authors argue math and code cannot settle it, for two reasons ({{§1|ax:S1}}): pre-training saw a great deal of math and code, and a final-answer check credits a lucky answer reached by bad reasoning. Agentic tasks are the harder test. A model must emit structured tool calls through task-specific interfaces, read environment feedback and stay coherent over many turns; those behaviours are rare in pre-training data and widely believed to be learned in post-training. If post-training ever does more than sharpen, it should show there.

The paper's three contributions: base models with a light harness turn out to be capable agents and often reach more tasks than their post-trained versions given enough attempts (§3); a single number, the Sharpening Tax, measures how much test-time scalability post-training removed (§4); and PTGS, a per-prompt sampling temperature used during RL, pays less of that tax (§5).

Setup: three metrics, three benchmarks, fourteen pairs

[[S2|§2, Appendix A]]

The metrics ({{§2|ax:S2}}). Each task i is attempted ni times (here 128) and ci attempts succeed. From those two counts, the standard unbiased estimators ({{Chen et al., 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.03374}}) give, for any budget k ≤ n:

pass@1i = ci / ni   (accuracy)
pass@ki = 1 − C(ni − ci, k) / C(ni, k)   (coverage: at least one of k succeeds)
passki = C(ci, k) / C(ni, k)   (consistency: all k succeed)

C(a, b) is the binomial coefficient: the formula is the chance that a random k of the n recorded attempts contains a success, so it uses all 128 attempts rather than just the first k. Dataset numbers average over tasks. One task makes it concrete: a task the base model solved 3 times in 128 has pass@1 2.3%, but pass@32 58% and pass@128 100%. That task is where repeated sampling pays.

The benchmarks ({{Appendix A.1|ax:A1.SS1}}): BFCL v4 multi-turn (200 tasks), WebShop (500) and ACEBench (770), chosen because success is checked on the environment state the agent's actions produce, not on a final string, so a bad trajectory cannot luck into credit.

The three benchmarks as run
  • BFCL v4 multi-turn base (200 tasks): tool calls over several user turns, up to 20 tool-call rounds per turn; the checker compares the end state with the reference.
  • WebShop (the first 500 instructions, 1,000-product index, at most 30 actions): search[...] and click[...] on a text shop; success only on a fully matching purchase (reward 1.0).
  • ACEBench (770 tasks: 750 single-turn and multi-step tool calls plus 20 multi-turn agent tasks; the ones needing an LLM user simulator were left out for budget).

The models ({{Table 3|ax:A1.T1}}): 14 open checkpoint pairs, a base model and its released post-trained version, from Gemma 4 (E4B, 12B, 26B-A4B, 31B), Ministral 3 (3B, 8B, 14B), Qwen2.5 (3B, 7B, 14B, 32B) and Qwen3.5 (4B, 9B, 35B-A3B). Their post-training recipes are undisclosed mixes of SFT, preference optimisation and RL; the paper calls them all "RL post-trained" as shorthand ({{A.2|ax:A1.SS2}}). Thinking mode is switched off for the post-trained models that have one, because the budget counts attempts, not tokens ({{Figure 10|ax:A1.F9}} checks that turning it on keeps the conclusion). 14 pairs times 3 benchmarks is the 42 cells the paper keeps counting; every cell has 128 attempts per task per model ({{Table 5|ax:A1.T3}}).

The harness ({{A.3|ax:A1.SS3}}). A base model has no chat template and no tool-call format, so the authors wrap it in one fixed text prompt for every model and benchmark, with no solved example. It matters most on BFCL, where six of the eight base models tested score zero without it ({{Table 4|ax:A1.T2}}); averaged over eight base models, BFCL pass@32 goes from 15.2% to 49.1% ({{Table 1|ax:S3}}). The same harness hurts the post-trained models (their pass@1 falls in 20 of 24 rows of Table 4), so each side is run the way it would be deployed: base with the harness, post-trained with the benchmark's native chat interface. Sampling settings are shared and not tuned per model: temperature 0.4 on BFCL, 0.7 elsewhere, top-p 0.95.

What the base-model harness prompt contains

The benchmark's system prompt, tools written as Python signatures, an instruction to answer with a fenced tool_call block of JSON, the conversation under ### User, ### Assistant and ### Tool Output markers, and stop sequences so the model cannot write the environment's reply itself ({{A.3|ax:A1.SS3}}).

One task: pass@1 is small, a few dozen attempts almost surely find a success

Base against post-trained: the curves cross

[[S3|§3, Figures 2 to 5]]

The post-trained models win pass@1 everywhere. But the base models' pass@k curves rise much more steeply with k and, in most settings for the larger models, cross over ({{Figure 2|ax:S2.F2}}). On WebShop, base gemma-4-31B reaches 87.6% pass@128 against 56.0% for gemma-4-31B-it ({{§3|ax:S3}}; the paper rounds to "over 85% vs. 56%").

On WebShop, gemma-4-31B-it solves 39.5% of tasks in one attempt and its base model 22.7%. With the unbiased estimator over 128 attempts, by what budget k has the base model caught up?

By k = 3: at three attempts per task the base model already reaches as many tasks (the paper: "k* ≈ 3", {{§3|ax:S3}}). The curves below are computed in your browser from the authors' released per-task counts; dashed lines are consistency (passk).

Scale moves the crossover ({{Figure 3|ax:S3.F3}}). For Gemma 4 on WebShop it falls from beyond 128 for E4B to 16 for 12B, 11 for 26B-A4B and 3 for 31B (counted here from the released data). Averaged over the largest model of each family on the three benchmarks, the base curve passes the post-trained one at k = 22; averaged over the smallest, never ({{Appendix C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}; both reproduce). The paper's reading: for a small model, post-training mostly supplies missing skills such as well-formed tool calls, so there is little coverage to lose; for a large model it mostly concentrates probability, and the coverage it gives up is real. Whether sharpening helps therefore depends on model size, budget and use: a user with a large model, many attempts and a verifier to pick the winner is better served by the base model.

The mechanism: tasks go to the extremes ({{Figure 4|ax:S3.F4}}, {{Table 6|ax:A3.T4}}). Sort each task by its outcome over 128 attempts: always pass (128 of 128), pass given compute (1 to 127) or always fail (0). For gemma-4-31B on WebShop, post-training shrinks the middle group from 87.6% to 30.0% of tasks and sends them both ways: always pass grows from 0.0% to 26.0%, and always fail from 12.4% to 44.0%. Only the middle group gains anything from more attempts. Stage 1 shows every task of one cell as a bar and reads pass@1 and pass@k off the field:

  1. One bar per task. The 500 WebShop tasks, each a bar as tall as the share of its 128 attempts the base model (with the harness) solved, sorted from its hardest task at the front to its easiest at the back. Dark tiles: never solved (12.4%); no task is solved every time. The glass level is the field's mean height, pass@1: 22.7%.
  2. Give every task 128 attempts. Each bar grows to its task's pass@k as k runs from 1 to 128; any task with a single success reaches full height. The level climbs to pass@128, 87.6%: almost every task sat in the middle, where retries pay.
  3. Post-training moves each task. Back to one attempt, each bar becomes the post-trained model's rate on the same task. The easy end rises to solved every time (26.0% of tasks, green), most of the hard end falls to never solved (44.0%, dark tiles): the middle shrinks from 87.6% to 30.0%, and pass@1 rises to 39.5%.
  4. 128 attempts for the post-trained model. Only the 30.0% in the middle can still grow, so the level climbs only to 56.0%: retries add 16.5 points, against 64.9 for the base model.
  5. What sharpening gave up. Purple ghosts: the 165 tasks (33.0%) the base model solved at least once and the post-trained model never; green: the 7 (1.4%) it newly reaches. The gap between the two levels, 87.6% − 56.0% = 31.6 points, is exactly lost minus gained. The tax (next section) measures the same loss over the whole curve: TaxS(128) = 0.036 for this cell.
  1. One bar per task. The same 500 WebShop tasks for gemma-4-E4B, the smallest Gemma 4. The base model solves 51.4% of them never (dark tiles) and the rest rarely: pass@1 is 1.8%.
  2. Give every task 128 attempts. Every task with one success reaches full height; the level climbs to pass@128, 48.6%.
  3. Post-training moves each task. Here post-training moves tasks off the never-solved edge: never solved falls from 51.4% to 34.8%, solved every time rises to 11.4%, the middle grows from 48.6% to 53.8%, and pass@1 rises to 31.4%. The paper's reading: a small model lacks skills that post-training supplies.
  4. 128 attempts for the post-trained model. The level climbs to 65.2%, above the base model's 48.6%; retries add 33.8 points, against 46.8 for the base model.
  5. What post-training gained. Green: 133 tasks (26.6%) only the post-trained model reaches; purple ghosts: 50 (10.0%) it lost. The post-trained level is 16.6 points higher, the exception the paper names (Appendix C.3), yet TaxS(128) is still positive (0.081): its curve saturates sooner.

  2. Give every task 128 attempts. The level climbs to pass@128, 94.4%.
  3. Post-training moves each task. The middle shrinks from 92.7% to 13.9%: solved every time jumps to 74.0% and never solved to 12.1%. pass@1 rises to 81.3%.
  4. 128 attempts for the post-trained model. The level climbs only to 87.9%: retries add 6.7 points, against 44.4 for the base model.

The same polarisation shows up as consistency ({{Figure 5|ax:S3}}, a wrap figure without its own anchor in the HTML). The base model's passk collapses to zero as k grows, because almost no task succeeds every time, while its pass@k keeps rising; the post-trained model's two curves stay close together. Post-training buys efficiency and consistency and pays with coverage. Qwen3.5 sharpens most softly of the four families, Gemma 4 most steeply ({{Figures 18 to 21|ax:A3.SS2}}).

The smallest models are the exception, and the paper says so.
For gemma-4-E4B the base model rarely succeeds at all (pass@1 1.8% on WebShop, 1.4% on BFCL), and post-training moves tasks off the never-solved edge: at k = 128 the post-trained model leads, 65.2% against 48.6% on WebShop and 38.5% against 27.0% on BFCL ({{Appendix C.3|ax:A3.SS3}}; all four reproduce).

Train with PTGS

PTGS's evidence is RL on a 7B model, which nobody can rerun in a browser. The authors also released a small CPU toy built to show the mechanism ({{examples/ptgs_toy.py|https://github.com/changdaeoh/sharpening-tax}}). This tab runs this page's own port of it, so you can watch the sampler act on one batch and then train it yourself. It is a toy for the mechanism: it cannot say how large the effect is on a real agent.

The toy. 96 tasks, each a chain of 4 decisions with 6 choices; a rollout succeeds only if all 4 are right. Tasks come in 8 clusters that share a usual answer, but 30% of individual decisions are exceptions that only the task's own 24 features can encode. The policy is linear (one weight per decision, choice and feature) and starts biased toward each cluster's usual answer, which gives a base-model-like spread: easy usual tasks, very hard multi-exception ones. Training is the paper's loop at small size: 200 steps, 8 consecutive tasks per step (a fixed pool cycled, so prompts recur and the per-prompt posterior means something), groups of 8 rollouts, a group-relative advantage (success minus the group mean), learning rate 6.0 (tuned by the authors on the fixed-temperature arm, not on PTGS). Evaluation: 128 attempts per task at T = 1 with PTGS off. The mechanism the authors built in: an exception that never succeeds in a group gets no gradient, so it drifts toward never solved, which is the polarisation the tax measures.

One batch, two samplers

Eight prompts from the middle of a real PTGS training run (seed 0, step , picked by a fixed rule: the batch with the most even mix of prompts solved often and almost never). Both modes use the same policy weights and the same prompts; only the temperature rule differs. Success probabilities are exact (the product of the right choice's probability at each decision), on a log scale because the hard ones are tiny.

Trained: four arms, ten seeds

The authors' four arms: base (the starting policy, untrained), fixed (every group at T = 1), global T (every group at one hotter temperature, matched to PTGS's mean temperature on a seed-0 probe run: ) and PTGS (τ = 1.5, γ = 0.95, target 0.25 rising to 0.5). The global-T arm is the control that separates "hotter" from "hotter where it fails".

During training

The group statistics behind the result, averaged over the ten seeds and smoothed over 9 steps (as the paper's {{Figure 41|ax:A6.SS2}} does): the share of groups with at least one success (what PPO needs), the share of mixed groups (what GRPO needs), the mean sampling temperature and the mean entropy of the decision distributions.

Train it yourself

Pick a seed (each seed draws a new task pool) and PTGS's spread τ, and train the fixed, global-T and PTGS arms in your browser (about a second). With τ = 1.5 the result must equal the ten-seed sweep above for that seed, run for run; the line under the table says whether it does.

What the toy shows, and what it cannot

  • Reproduces in direction: PTGS beats both fixed and heat-matched global temperature on pass@1 and pass@128 (), and keeps more groups with a success than either during training, and more mixed groups than fixed (global T, sampling everything hotter, has about as many mixed groups late in training). The authors' own toy, run unmodified with 5 seeds, gives the same ordering (table below).
  • The tax barely moves: TaxS(128) falls only from , much less than pass@128 rises, because PTGS's policy also reaches fewer tasks with certainty: its consistency pass128 is the lowest of the trained arms. That matches the paper's own FrozenLake row (consistency 42.5 against 50.9), and the paper's tax reductions inside the noise.
  • Hotter is not the point: the global-T arm gets as much heat as PTGS in total, spread evenly, and So what helps is where the heat goes.
  • What it cannot test: anything about scale, language, multi-turn interaction or a learned critic (the toy has no PPO critic: its update is the group-relative one, closer to GRPO). The learning rate was tuned for the fixed arm, which favours fixed, not PTGS. Whether PTGS's gains survive at 7B or beyond is the paper's question, not this toy's.

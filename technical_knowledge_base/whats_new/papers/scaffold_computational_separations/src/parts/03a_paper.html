The idea in one screen

A harness keeps its state outside the network and loops as many times as the input needs; a student trained to imitate it in one rollout has a fixed number of layers, so on problems whose answer needs depth that grows with the input, the student may be unable even to represent what the harness computes.

The figure runs the theorem's own example with the lab's engine: the harness is breadth-first search over edge lookups, the student a fixed-depth reader. Height is distance from s in hops.

  1. The question. A 10-vertex directed graph from the lab's "long paths" family (seed 13), laid on the floor: is there a path from s to t? The base model is only ever asked "is there an edge u → v?", one constant-depth lookup.
  2. Harness rounds 1 to 3. Each round the harness asks about every pair (frontier vertex, unvisited vertex): 9, then 8, then 7 lookups, dots running along the edges that answer "yes". Each vertex found rises as a blue column as tall as the round that reached it; the visited set and the frontier are bits the harness keeps outside the model.
  3. Rounds 4 to 7: t is found. The search reaches t in round 7 after 43 lookups in all. The tallest column is the harness's depth: it ran as many rounds as this input needed.
  4. A reader with 2 layers: a ceiling at 4 hops. A fixed-depth reader that squares the adjacency matrix twice sees every path of up to 2² = 4 hops: the orange glass. Vertices under it are what it sees from s; t's column of 7 pierces it, so the reader says "no", wrongly.
  5. One more layer would fix this graph. With 3 layers the ceiling rises to 8 hops, t fits under it, and the reader is right.
  6. A bigger graph breaks it again. On 16 vertices (seed 3) t is 10 hops away: the harness simply runs more rounds (115 lookups in all), while the 3-layer reader's 8-hop ceiling is pierced again. The depth must grow with the input. {{Run it on any graph|tab:t-run}}.

You need: the idea that a chain of thought adds serial computation to a fixed-depth transformer, taught on {{Test-time compute and reasoning, section 1|n:4035c17b0d0d813f9fb2d62cff1f09ef}}, and distillation by supervised fine-tuning on a teacher's outputs, on {{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}.

New terms.

harness, scaffold
any program that calls a model several times and keeps state between calls (the paper uses both words); with its base model M it is the teacher 𝒜[M].
trace
the full record of one harness run: every call and answer, written as tokens.
student
a model trained by next-token prediction on traces, then run alone in one autoregressive rollout.
internalise
a student matching the teacher's accuracy without the harness.
ID, OOD
in distribution and out of distribution: test inputs drawn like the training inputs, or shifted.
AC⁰, TC⁰
problems solved by circuits of constant depth and polynomial size, from AND, OR and NOT gates of any fan-in (AC⁰), plus majority gates (TC⁰).
NL
problems a nondeterministic machine solves with logarithmic memory; directed s-t reachability (STCON) is the hardest of them (NL-complete).

Problem: why do simple loops help strong models, and can training absorb them?

[[S1|§1]]

Harnesses range from sampling many answers and picking one, through searching over partial solutions and backtracking with a verifier, to agents with tools ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Some of the strongest recent math systems wrap a strong model in a plain loop: keep candidate solutions, expand them, prune them by verification. The authors ask two questions. Why should a capable model gain so much from such a simple strategy imposed from outside? And could the model learn the strategy itself, so the harness is no longer needed?

They give one answer to both, in three steps ({{§1.1|ax:S1.SS1}}): (1) in small, fully controlled experiments, a student of the same size as the base model, trained on the teacher's traces, stays below the teacher; (2) the gap closes when the student gets more expressive power relative to the task (more parameters, a simpler harness, a longer chain of thought); (3) a theorem says why: a harness can compose cheap calls into a computation that no fixed-depth student with a short chain of thought can represent, under a standard complexity conjecture. A fourth step repeats (1) and (2) with a real agentic harness on competition math.

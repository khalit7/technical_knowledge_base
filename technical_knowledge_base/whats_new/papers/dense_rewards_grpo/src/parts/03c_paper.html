Per update, the reward looks good

[[S3.SS1|§3.1]]

The gradient test favours it. In a separate equal-iteration study (Qwen2.5-1.5B fully fine-tuned at 10−5 for 32 iterations, greedy validation accuracy; {{Appendix G|ax:A7}}), the part of the Monte-Carlo reward's update outside V has a mean cosine of 0.84 with that of the reference gradient, on four full-parameter probes with disjoint rollouts. Two control rewards leave V just as much (the part outside V carries 0.86 to 0.95 of the norm for all three) but do not align: a PRIME-style implicit reward log πθ − log πref, at −0.01 ± 0.08, and the Monte-Carlo reward shuffled within each completion, at −0.01 ± 0.06. In the matched-compute recipe, the k = 4 reward is 1.56× [1.28, 2.03] as efficient as outcome-only GRPO per update, and with the same number of updates it leads by 1.5 [1.0, 2.1] points (exploratory; step 1 of Fig. 1).

Training does not follow, even at equal iterations. In the equal-iteration study outcome-only GRPO gains 5.6 [4.1, 7.1] points of greedy accuracy; adding the reward with a low weight (α = 0.25, 0.5 or 1) is equivalent to it within ±3 points over 14 paired seeds, and with α = 5 it loses 12.4 [8.7, 16.1] points (n = 8). Through that collapse its alignment with the reference stays at 0.969 to 1.000, while greedy accuracy falls from 25% to 12%, entropy rises from 0.21 to 0.58 (0.18 to 0.20 at low weight) and the KL from the initial policy reaches about 30 times that of the low-weight runs. The reward keeps pointing the right way; the failure looks like over-optimizing it ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}). On Qwen2.5-7B no dense-reward configuration in a 43-run sweep beat the outcome baseline's mean gain; SmolLM3-3B and a NuminaMath study point the same way, and only single-seed Phi-4 pilots are positive.

Fig. 3 · The same tokens, a third of the time: where each run's budget goes

Per token and per GPU-hour, it loses

[[S3.SS2|§3.2]]

Per generated token it is far behind. At k = 4 the continuations take 91% of the tokens, so when the budget runs out the reward has made 50 updates and outcome-only GRPO 584 (Fig. 3, top). Its accuracy at B is 14.3 [12.7, 16.0] points lower (n = 5, Holm p = 5 × 10−5) and 14.9 [14.0, 15.8] lower on 12 fresh seeds. The deficit recurs on Qwen3 0.6B, 1.7B and 4B (8.0, 9.1 and 10.0 points, 3 seeds each), on GSM8K (9.8) and with 768-token completions (6.6, n = 8).

Per GPU-hour it is behind too, by less. With the same tokens, a k = 4 run takes 0.14 hours on an H200 and outcome-only GRPO 0.43. Outcome-only GRPO spends 61% of its time on updates; the reward spends 73% generating continuations, which the inference engine does in large batches (Fig. 3, bottom; {{Table 14|ax:A4.T14}}). Stopped at the reward's training time, outcome-only GRPO still leads by 7.8 [6.8, 8.8] points, in all twelve seeds (exploratory). A comparison that counts only tokens overstates the loss; one that counts only updates hides it.

Cheaper rewards lose less, but still lose. Sampling noise in the continuations is only about 4% of the second moment of the k = 4 update, so fewer continuations should cost little in quality. A preregistered study on 12 fresh seeds: k = 1, still 70% of tokens on continuations, trails outcome-only GRPO by 6.8 [6.0, 7.6] and leads k = 4 by 8.1 [7.1, 9.0] (both Holm p < 10−8, every seed); k = 2 trails by 11.2. At their own training times k = 2 and k = 1 trail by 5.1 and 2.2 (exploratory).

Reusing the samples helps the reward, not the baseline. In three-seed pilots, four clipped optimizer steps per batch (VinePPO-style) raise the k = 4 reward by 7.7 points at matched tokens (Holm p = .007) but lower outcome-only GRPO by 5.9 ({{Appendix E|ax:A5}}). Training on the continuations themselves (after SPO-tree and TreeRL) was 6.4× as efficient per update but only 0.60× per token, below the preregistered bar of 0.8 for training it at 380 tokens.

Given as much training time as outcome-only GRPO, nothing gains (step 4 of Fig. 1). In the preregistered 768-token study, outcome-only GRPO needs Tc = 1.29 A100-hours to reach B; every variant trains past that. At Tc, k = 4 trails by 3.3 [2.4, 4.3] and continuation reuse by 5.2 [4.1, 6.3] in every seed (its updates, which include the continuations, take 78% of its time: 45 seconds per iteration against 6). The VinePPO-style variant trails by 1.1 [0.4, 1.7], equivalent within ±2 (TOST p = .005) and even at its own shorter time. The k = 1 reward's 1.1 [−0.5, +2.8] is neither significant nor shown equivalent; given still more time it reaches outcome-only GRPO's accuracy at B after 1.25 Tc on the mean curves ({{Appendix F|ax:A6}}).

Learned critics remove the continuations, and gain nothing. A ridge critic predicts each prefix's value from the hidden states, refit every iteration on earlier rollouts. On 24 fresh Qwen2.5-1.5B seeds both forms are equivalent to outcome-only GRPO within ±2 points (TOST, Holm p < 10−4): the TD form −0.4 [−1.2, +0.3], the baseline form +0.7 [0.2, 1.2] (Holm p = .016), a lead that appears only at the final evaluation. Fitting the critic adds 23 to 24% to training time, so at equal time both trail, by 1.2 and 0.7 (exploratory; {{§3.3|ax:S3.SS3}}). The critic is cheap but noisy: its squared error against the prefix value is 0.057, against 0.010 for the k = 4 estimate, where the value's own variance is 0.076 ({{C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}).

Can the gradient test predict the result?

[[S3.SS3|§3.3]]

Per token, it predicts the order. The k = 4 reward is 1.56× as efficient as outcome-only GRPO per update but only 0.145× [0.120, 0.190] per generated token. Per token the test ranks k = 1 (0.45×), k = 2 (0.27×) and k = 4 (0.15×) in the order training follows, an order and a deficit for every reward that were both preregistered and both held. All three are about equally efficient per update (1.55 to 1.63×), so the order simply follows their token costs; what the test adds is a size for each deficit.

But it overestimates every reward it rates. To turn efficiency into accuracy the authors use a heuristic, not a derivation: a reward with per-token efficiency e reaches after t tokens the accuracy outcome-only GRPO reaches after e·t. It predicts 35.9%, 31.9% and 27.5% at B for k = 1, 2, 4; the rewards reach 34.3%, 29.9% and 26.3% (exploratory). The critics are the case most favourable to dense rewards (per token they are 1.36× and 1.15× as efficient as outcome-only GRPO), and the extrapolation gives them leads of 2.0 and 0.9 points; they lead by 0.2 and 0.0, shortfalls that were preregistered (p < 10−5 each). Across all five rewards the overestimate is 0.8 to 1.8 points on average, every interval excluding zero (Fig. 4), as large as the gains the paper measures.

Why it misses: the test describes one SGD step, with the step size tuned to each update, at the initial policy; training takes hundreds of Adam steps at one fixed learning rate while the policy moves. Treat it as a ranking heuristic, not a certificate for a gain of a point or two.

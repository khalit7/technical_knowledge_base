Problem: the two cheap checks leave out the cost

[[S2.SS1|§2.1]]

GRPO samples a group of G completions per prompt, gives each a 0/1 reward Rg from the verifier and an advantage Ag = (Rg − mean R) / (std R + ε), and multiplies every token's score (its log-probability gradient) by a credit ({{Eq. 1 and 2|ax:S2.SS1}}). Outcome-only GRPO gives every token of a completion the same credit, Ag, so a solution that sets up the problem correctly and slips at the last step is penalised for its good steps too. The mechanics of the group advantage are owned by {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}; what this paper adds is a precise statement of what that equal credit can and cannot do.

Because every token of completion g carries the same Ag, the update is Σg Ag qg, a weighted sum of each completion's summed score qg. Call the set of all such weighted sums V: every update that weights whole completions lies in it. A dense reward adds a token-level term, credit = Ag + wg,h, so its update can leave V; only the part outside V can, to first order, do better than some reweighting of whole completions ({{§2.1|ax:S2.SS1}}; Appendix I gives the exact condition).

The Monte-Carlo reward (after Math-Shepherd and VinePPO) cuts each completion at five fixed token positions into six segments and samples k continuations from each prefix; Fig. 2 runs it on one completion, step by step ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}). The value before the first segment is fixed at 0.5 and the value at the end is the completion's own reward, so the six credits always add up to R − 0.5 ({{Table 16|ax:A6.T16}}).

Fig. 2 · One completion, step by step

  1. Sample and score. One prompt, a group of 8 completions; this one ends with a wrong answer (R = 0). If 4 of the 8 are right, its advantage is −1, and outcome-only GRPO gives all of its tokens credit −1.
  2. Cut it. Five fixed token positions split the completion into six segments (at 128, 256, ..., 640 tokens in the 768-token study).
  3. Branch from each cut. From every prefix, sample k = 4 continuations to the token limit and check each answer with the verifier: 5 cuts × 4 = 20 continuations for this completion, up to 1,280 for the 64 completions of one iteration.
  4. Estimate each prefix's value. The fraction of its continuations that end correct: here 3/4, 4/4, 3/4, 1/4 and 1/4. The setup looks good; something in the fourth segment goes wrong.
  5. Credit each segment with its change in value. +0.25, +0.25, −0.25, −0.50, 0 and −0.25 (from 0.5 at the start to R = 0 at the end), added to the group advantage without centering: the good second segment now carries −0.75 instead of −1, the slip −1.5.
  6. Count the tokens. The continuations are scored and thrown away: at k = 4 they are 91% of all tokens generated in training. In the same budget, the reward makes 50 updates and outcome-only GRPO about 600.

The completion and its continuation outcomes are illustrative; the cut count, k, the fixed starting value, the credit rule, 91% and the update counts are the paper's ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}, {{Appendix C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}, {{C.3.2|ax:A3.SS3.SSS2}}).

Two cheap checks make such a reward look worth using ({{§1|ax:S1}}). A gradient test asks, on a fixed batch, whether the reward's update points where an independent estimate of the gradient of expected accuracy points. A per-update comparison trains with and without the reward for the same number of updates. Neither counts what the reward costs: in generated tokens (the 91%), and in GPU time, though not in proportion, because the continuations are only generated and scored, never trained on, and an inference engine generates tokens in large batches more cheaply than a training step processes them.

The prompt filter spends compute the other way ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}). A group whose completions all succeed or all fail has zero advantage everywhere and produces no update, so several methods aim training at prompts with success near 1/2. The filter draws 24 candidates per iteration, predicts each one's success from the policy's hidden state at the end of the prompt (one prefill pass, no generated tokens), and trains on the 6 nearest 1/2 plus 2 at random. It is PCL's rule; the paper does not propose a new method, it tests existing ones at matched compute ({{§5|ax:S5}}).

The matched-compute protocol

[[S2.SS3|§2.3]]

Small models, many paired seeds. The gains the authors expected were about as large as the variation between seeds, so they trained six small models many times: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (completions up to 380 tokens), Qwen3 0.6B, 1.7B and 4B in non-thinking mode, and Llama-3.2 1B and 3B Instruct (768 tokens), on MATH problems with numeric answers ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}). Each iteration takes 8 prompts × 8 completions and one on-policy Adam step at learning rate 10−6, with no ratio clipping, no sample reuse and no KL penalty. In total, 1,668 runs and 1,468 GPU-hours, on a trainer reimplemented for this study. Configurations compared within a study share prompts and evaluation samples under each seed, so each seed gives one paired difference.

Two budgets. The token budget B counts every token generated during training, including continuations and the groups dynamic sampling discards, and a run stops when it has spent B. A preregistered rule sets B per model to what the k = 4 reward generates in 48 iterations (12.25M on Qwen2.5-1.5B), so even the most expensive reward makes about 50 updates. Training time counts every training stage (prompt scoring, generation, credit, the update, the weight sync), not evaluation or logging, and is compared only between runs of one study on one GPU type. Every comparison is also reported at equal updates.

Metric. Pass@1 over 8 samples at temperature 1 on 200 held-out numeric MATH problems, read at the first evaluation at or after B, with no checkpoint picking; differences are in percentage points.

Statistics. Each study wrote down its hypotheses, primary contrasts, Holm families and equivalence margin (±2 points, ±1 in four studies on Qwen2.5-1.5B) before its runs. Results are means with 95% paired-t intervals over seeds; each claim is labelled primary (preregistered, Holm-adjusted), secondary (preregistered, unadjusted) or exploratory (not preregistered); a crossed bootstrap over seeds and test problems checks every primary contrast ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}). How paired tests, Holm and TOST work is owned by {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}.

Verifier. The default verifier credits the boxed answer, or the last number when nothing is boxed; the strict one credits only boxed answers. Runs on the default verifier can drift away from boxing, which can bias a comparison either way, so some studies train on the strict one ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}).

Gradient tests ({{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}). Alignment is the cosine between the parts outside V of a dense update and of an estimate ĥ of the gradient of expected accuracy from separately sampled continuations. Efficiency, (E⟨u, ĥ⟩)² / E‖u‖², also counts how noisy an update is: for a smooth objective it is proportional to the best expected gain of one SGD step along u. It is computed on 96 fixed batches at the initial policy, relative to outcome-only GRPO, per update and per generated token.

Recipe details: budgets per model, pools, truncation, why 10−6 and fp32 masters, preregistration timing
  • Budgets: 12.25M (Qwen2.5-1.5B), 17.25M, 21.0M and 22.25M (Qwen3 0.6B, 1.7B, 4B), 10.0M and 8.25M (Llama-3.2 1B and 3B): 48 times the k = 4 reward's mean tokens per iteration over iterations 0 to 2 of one calibration run, rounded up to a multiple of 250,000. The densest reward gets 50 to 57 updates; outcome-only GRPO 584 on Qwen2.5-1.5B and 755 on Qwen3-4B ({{C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}).
  • Pools: MATH train levels 4 and 5 (562 prompts after removing overlaps with the evaluation sets) in the dense-reward studies, levels 3 to 5 (3,458 prompts used) in the prompt-selection studies, except those testing level 5 or levels 1 and 2 alone. Evaluation every B/16 tokens.
  • Truncation: at 380 tokens, 73% of the initial Qwen2.5-1.5B samples are cut off, so part of what every run learns is to finish within the limit; at 768 tokens, 20% are ({{Appendix A|ax:A1}}).
  • Learning rate: the equal-iteration study's 10−5 degraded every configuration over the matched horizon; a preregistered stability probe on a separate development set chose 10−6 (3 × 10−6 failed it).
  • Precision: rollouts from vLLM in bf16; the trainer keeps fp32 master weights and Adam states with bf16 autocast compute. With pure bf16 weights, Adam's step at these learning rates is smaller than half a bf16 unit for typical weights: in a check on Qwen2.5-0.5B, 87% of parameters were unchanged after one step (the effect the {{Fair Cast|n:40c5c17b0d0d81c39641d49880e39f73}} page is about).
  • Advantage and loss: reward minus group mean over the group's population standard deviation; the loss sums token terms and divides by the number of completions; Adam (0.9, 0.999, ε 10−8), no weight decay, gradient clipping at norm 1, constant rate without warmup (only one study decays it).
  • Preregistration timing: the plan files live in the project repository, not a public registry, so their timing rests on file-system records. For 27 of the 34 files whose studies have main runs, the last change precedes the first main run; of the other seven, six changed only in comments and one added dated amendments before the affected parts ran ({{C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}).

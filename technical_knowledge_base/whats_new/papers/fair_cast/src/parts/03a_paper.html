The idea in one sentence

[[S1|§1]]

RL trainers keep fp32 master weights but sample from a bf16 copy made by round-to-nearest, which throws away every update smaller than half a bf16 gap, so the sampler serves a policy tens to hundreds of steps old; exporting with stochastic rounding (unbiased: the copy equals the master on average) and computing the loss on that exact served copy removes the lag ({{abstract and §1|ax:S1}}).

Fig. 1 · Where the two ratios live: what frameworks check, and what they never form

  1. The fp32 master. The optimizer (AdamW at η ≈ 10−6) has just updated the master weights θ, the grey glass plate: they hold the optimizer's view of the policy.
  2. Export by round-to-nearest. After every step the master is cast to bf16 for the sampler. Almost every per-step update is smaller than half a bf16 gap, so the cast drops it: only 0.4 to 0.5% of served weights change per step on Qwen3-1.7B, a thin stream into the orange copy q.
  3. vLLM samples from the copy. Rollouts (24 prompts, groups of 8, up to 2,048 tokens) come from q, so the policy that acts is the stale one.
  4. The trainer reads the same rounded weights. Its forward pass runs in bf16, so trainer and sampler agree: the visible ratio πtrainer/πsampler stays at one (mean |log ρ| about 0.01, the short green bar). That is the ratio PPO clipping, TIS and IcePop check.
  5. The update lands on θ, and the gap nobody measures grows. The master moves on; the copy does not follow. The hidden ratio πθ/πq (red) climbs to 0.10 to 0.16 on MATH and about 0.45 on knights-and-knaves, and no framework computes it.
  1. The fp32 master. The same optimizer step on the same master θ; Fair Cast changes nothing here.
  2. Export by stochastic rounding. Each weight rounds up with probability equal to its position between the two bf16 neighbours, so E[q] = θ: every update reaches the blue copy on average, at once. Now 2 to 15% of served weights change per step.
  3. vLLM samples from the copy. The same sampler and kernels; only the weights it reads are fresher.
  4. The trainer's forward pass uses the copy itself. The loss is computed on the exact q that generated the rollouts; the gradient goes to θ unchanged (straight-through). The policy being differentiated is the policy that sampled.
  5. The update lands on θ, and the copy follows. Master and served policy stay within rounding noise: |log ρ| of the hidden ratio stays near 0.01, like the visible one.

Redrawn from {{Figure 2|ax:S1.F2}}: the loop runs clockwise from the master (back left) through the export and the served copy to the sampler and the trainer, and the update returns to the master. The two boxes Fair Cast changes are the export and the trainer's forward pass. The |log ρ| values are the paper's measurements on Qwen3-1.7B at η = 10−6 ({{§4|ax:S4}}, Figure 4c); the stream densities are schematic.

New terms on this page
Master θ
the fp32 weights the optimizer updates; they hold the optimizer's view of the policy.
Served copy q
the bf16 weights the inference engine (vLLM here) samples rollouts from; q = R(θ), made by the export rule R after every optimizer step.
Gap u
the spacing between neighbouring bf16 values near a weight w: u = 2⌊log₂|w|⌋ − 7, between |w|/256 and |w|/128 ({{§3|ax:S3}}).
Dead zone
round-to-nearest leaves the copy unchanged until the master has moved more than u/2 from it, so smaller accumulated updates never reach the sampler.
Visible ratio
πtrainer/πsampler, the trainer's and the engine's probability of the same token ({{Eq. 3|ax:S2.E3}}); what PPO clipping, TIS and IcePop look at.
Hidden ratio
πθ/πq, the master policy against the served policy ({{Eq. 4|ax:S2.E4}}); no framework computes it.
Lag
how many optimizer steps of motion the served copy is missing.
Stochastic rounding (SR)
round up with probability equal to the fractional position between the two neighbours, so the expected copy equals the master.
Error feedback (EF)
carry each weight's rounding error into the next export, so errors cancel over time.
Training on the served copy
the trainer's forward pass uses exactly q; the gradient is passed to θ unchanged (straight-through).
EMA export
export an exponential moving average of the master instead of the master: a chosen lag of about 1/α steps.

Problem: round-to-nearest discards almost every update

[[S3|§3]]

Modern RL stacks separate the optimizer from the sampler. Every open-source framework the authors examined (verl, TRL, OpenRLHF, slime, prime-rl, AReaL) makes the sampler's bf16 copy from the fp32 master by round-to-nearest ({{§1|ax:S1}}). bf16 keeps 7 significand bits, so near a weight w its values are u ≈ |w|/128 apart, and round-to-nearest maps an update Δ to zero whenever |Δ| < u/2 ≈ |w|/256 ({{§3|ax:S3}}). The master starts exactly on the bf16 grid, so this bites from the first step. (How the formats store numbers is owned by {{Numerical computing|n:3f05c17b0d0d81d6af0be4a9a2edba8d}}, which runs the same nearest-against-stochastic comparison on one weight.)

At the learning rates RL post-training uses (η ≈ 10−6 with Adam), almost every per-step update is that small. The paper's estimate from the measured distribution of Adam's normalized update is 0.3 to 0.7% of served weights changing per step on Qwen3-1.7B; measured, it is 0.4 to 0.5%. A weight drifting at mean speed |Δ̄| crosses a rounding boundary only every τ ≈ u/(2|Δ̄|) steps, 50 to 200 steps at these values, and with a decaying learning rate τ grows without bound ({{§3|ax:S3}}).

A weight of 0.02 receives a steady update of 5 × 10−7 per step (learning rate 10−6, a normalized Adam update of 0.5). The bf16 copy is made by round-to-nearest after every step. When does the copy first change?

Computed by this page's rounding engine, which matches torch's bf16 cast bit for bit: Try other weights and learning rates in {{the simulator|tab:t-sim}}.

Muon makes it worse: its per-element updates are nearly uniform in size, so at η = 10−6 almost none cross a boundary. A controlled test isolates the mechanism ({{Figure 4a|ax:S3.F4}}): on 64 knights-and-knaves problems, one optimizer step moved an fp32 sampler from 0.17 to 0.84, while the round-to-nearest bf16 sampler stayed at about 0.19 across three steps. In a full run, round-to-nearest left the sampler nearly frozen for about 20 steps (0.12, 0.15, 0.22 at steps 0, 9, 19) while Fair Cast moved at once (0.12, 0.51, 0.79).

The hidden lag: the ratio frameworks check stays at one

[[S4|§2, §4]]

The sampler draws from q, the trainer evaluates the loss at a bf16 version of θ, and the update lands on θ. Frameworks guard against the trainer and the engine disagreeing by computing the visible ratio πtrainer/πsampler and clipping (PPO), reweighting (TIS) or masking (IcePop) tokens where it strays ({{§2|ax:S2}}; the {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}} page owns these corrections). But when the trainer's forward pass runs in bf16, by autocast or by evaluating the copy, both numerator and denominator are functions of q alone, so the ratio is about one however far θ has moved. The ratio that matters for the policy gradient, the hidden ratio πθ/πq, is never formed ({{Eq. 4|ax:S2.E4}}).

The authors log both ({{§4|ax:S4}}, Figure 4c). On Qwen3-1.7B at η = 10−6 the mean per-token |log ρvisible| stays near 0.01 for the whole run, while the hidden one grows from 0 to 0.10 to 0.16 on MATH and about 0.45 on knights-and-knaves; under Fair Cast it stays near 0.01. The per-token effective sample size between master and served policy ({{Eq. 5|ax:S4.E5}}) falls to 0.2 to 0.35 on tasks with 2,000-token responses and is about 0.9 with 500-token responses. The authors use it as a gauge of how far the served policy has fallen behind, not as a correction: a gradient at q with samples from q needs none.

This reconciles an earlier finding: lower learning rates were reported to reduce the training-inference mismatch ({{Zhang et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.01826}}). They reduce the visible ratio and increase the hidden one, because fewer updates clear the dead zone ({{§4|ax:S4}}). Prior work on the same threshold (the {{TRL team's analysis|https://aminedirohf-trainer-generator-bf16-mismatch.hf.space}}, PULSE) treated it as a communication artefact and made the trainer agree with the stale sampler; TRL now ships only the roughly 1% of bf16 elements that change each step as a sparse delta ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

Budget Internalization: Fixed-Budget RLVR Trades Test-Time Scaling for In-Budget Accuracy

Ilia Zavidnyi, Alexander Kovrigin, Arina Puchkova, Kirill Gelvan, Sergey Titov
JetBrains Research · 19 November 2026 (arXiv v1, the only version as of 23 November 2026)

In one line: On three small models (Qwen3.5-4B, Gemma4-E2B, Nemotron-3-Nano-4B) trained with GRPO on math under a fixed rollout cap (three seeds for the main comparison), AIME accuracy at the cap rose 10.9 to 42.4 points over the base model while accuracy at the largest budget tested fell 13.1 to 30.2 points below it, and none of the tested fixes (single runs on Qwen3.5-4B) kept both the in-budget gain and the base model's test-time scaling.

The idea in one sentence

§1

RL with verifiable rewards is usually trained with a fixed rollout cap, and that cap does not only save compute: the trained model learns to finish inside it, so it beats the base model at the cap and falls below it once both may think longer (abstract and §1). The authors call this budget internalization.

Fig. 1 is the whole result. Qwen3.5-4B trained with GRPO under a 4k-token cap scores 35.93% on AIME 2024 and 2025 at a 4k budget, against 1.11% for the base model, which almost never finishes in 4k tokens. Give both 64k tokens and the base model climbs to 78.33% while the trained model stops at 48.15%: +34.8 points at the cap becomes −30.2 (§2.1, Table 8). Every trained curve in the figure goes flat soon after its own cap; pick another model to see the same shape.

You need: GRPO and the RLVR loop (group-standardised advantage, PPO-style clipping, a KL term, rewards from a verifier), owned by RL for LLMs; test-time scaling, serial thinking budgets and budget forcing, owned by Test-time compute and reasoning.

New terms

Rollout cap B
the most tokens a training rollout may generate; a rollout that reaches it without an answer is truncated and, in standard GRPO here, gets reward 0.
Evaluation budget
the generated-token limit at test time; an answer cut off by it scores 0. The paper sweeps it from 512 tokens to 64k.
Budget internalization
above the training cap, the base model keeps using more tokens and gaining accuracy while the trained model's length and accuracy flatten (§2.4).
avg@k
accuracy averaged over k sampled answers per problem: avg@3 on the 60 AIME problems for the main curves, avg@16 for the ablations.
Answer forcing, forcing up
two decoding interventions: close the thinking at the budget and demand an answer, or delete the answer, append "Wait" and make the model keep reasoning.
RFT
rejection fine-tuning: supervised fine-tuning on the model's own correct answers.

The problem and the setup

§2

RLVR is trained under a cap and usually evaluated at one budget near it. The cap keeps training affordable, since rollout cost grows with sequence length, and it is often set below the length the model would otherwise use (§1). An evaluation at the cap shows whether training helped there; it cannot show whether the trained model still gains from more tokens. The paper measures both, by sweeping the evaluation budget over a factor of 32 for every checkpoint.

Three small reasoning models, two caps each, chosen so the cap binds. Qwen3.5-4B is trained at 4k and 8k generated tokens, Gemma4-E2B and Nemotron-3-Nano-4B at 1k and 2k, with GRPO in NeMo RL on the DeepScaleR math set (§2). The caps sit below where the base models finish: base Qwen is cut off on 100% of AIME answers at 4k, 95% at 8k and still 6.67% at 64k (Table 2). A second training set, Nemotron-RL-ReasoningGym-v1 (15,000 problems from 104 procedurally generated environments), checks that the effect is not about math data. The standard recipe: reward 1 for a correct finished answer and 0 for a wrong or truncated one, groups of 8, every group kept, clipping 0.2, no KL penalty, 300 steps of 32 prompts, and the checkpoint with the best validation accuracy (Appendix A). The main comparison has three training seeds (42, 43, 44) and reports both the validation-selected and the final checkpoint; every other condition is one run.

Recipe details: learning rates, evaluation sampling, how budgets are counted
  • Peak learning rates (10-step warmup, cosine decay to a tenth): Qwen3.5-4B 5×10−7 with Adam on Megatron, weight decay 0.01; Gemma4-E2B 3×10−6 and Nemotron-3-Nano-4B 2×10−6 with FusedAdam on FSDP2, weight decay 0.1. bfloat16, micro-batch 1, at most three epochs and 300 optimizer steps; validation every 10 steps (Table 4).
  • Rollouts sample at temperature 1.0 and top-p 1.0; each cap reserves 2,048 more tokens for the prompt. Budgets count generated tokens only.
  • Evaluation in Inspect AI with thinking on and each model card's sampling: Qwen temperature 1.0, top-k 20; Gemma 1.0, top-k 64; Nemotron temperature 1.0 and its card's default top-k (Table 5).
  • The per-seed curves replay one 65,536-token generation per checkpoint and score an answer as wrong if it is unfinished at the budget being read, so all budgets of a curve share the same samples (Appendix I). Base models are evaluated once.
  • Out of domain: GPQA-Diamond (graduate science, multiple choice) and LiveCodeBench v6 (code with hidden tests).

The crossover, and the length behind it

§2.1

All six checkpoints gain at their cap and lose at the largest budget. In seed means against the base model at the same budget (§2.1): Qwen 4k-trained +34.8 at 4k and −30.2 at 64k; Qwen 8k-trained +42.4 at 8k and −24.6 at 64k; Gemma 1k-trained +10.9 and −24.8 at 16k; Gemma 2k-trained +14.8 and −20.6; Nemotron 1k-trained +23.3 and −25.4; Nemotron 2k-trained +12.6 and −13.1. Each of the 18 seeded runs shows a gain at its cap and a deficit at the largest budget, with the validation-selected checkpoint and with the final step-300 checkpoint (Appendix I; 36 of 36 evaluations, recomputed from Tables 15 and 16). The largest budget is 64k for Qwen but only 16k for Gemma and Nemotron; base Nemotron is still climbing there (59.44% at 16k, 9.44% of answers still cut off), so its deficits are, if anything, understated.

The trained models stop using tokens they are given. At the largest budget the base models average 6.11k to 27.32k tokens per AIME answer and the trained checkpoints 1.01k to 6.22k (Fig. 2, Table 1). Qwen's 4k-trained model writes 4.97k tokens on average at 64k, a fifth of its base model's 27.32k, although nothing stops it from writing more: the cap moved the whole length distribution instead of acting as a hard limit at decoding. Truncation tells the same story: at its own 4k cap the 4k-trained Qwen is cut off on 22.22% of AIME answers against the base model's 100% (Table 12).

Other training data, other tasks

§2.2–2.3

Reasoning Gym training reproduces the loss of scaling on all three models. Qwen trained at 4k on the procedural tasks gains 31.1 points on AIME at 4k and loses 25.0 at 64k; Gemma at 2k gains 21.7 and loses 15.0 at 16k (§2.2). Nemotron gains only 0.6 at its 2k cap but flattens at 49.44% while its base model reaches 60.00% at 16k (−10.6). So the in-budget gain depends on the model and the data; the flattening does not. Fig. 1 shows these curves under its Reasoning Gym button.

On GPQA-Diamond and LiveCodeBench the damage is small, and sometimes a gain. Fig. 3 lists every checkpoint's difference from its base model at the largest budget (§2.3). Qwen's 8k-trained checkpoint is within 2.2 points of the base model on GPQA and 1.0 on LiveCodeBench at 64k, while its mean answer shrinks from 11.15k to 4.56k tokens on GPQA and from 38.32k to 8.31k on LiveCodeBench (§1). Nemotron's math-trained checkpoints beat their base model on GPQA at 64k (+1.0 and +4.0). The authors' reading: on tasks that need less extended reasoning, the base model overthinks, and the trained model's shorter answers cost little. The same short answers that hurt on AIME save tokens here, which is why the paper says the effect is "neither always good nor always bad".

Did it learn to stop early, or lose the ability?

§2.3

Two decoding interventions separate the two readings, and the answer is "lost". If the trained model had only learned to stop, making it keep thinking should bring back the base model's high-budget accuracy. The paper uses budget forcing in both directions, plus a third test that states the budget in the prompt; Fig. 4 shows each as a change in accuracy, on Qwen3.5-4B (§2.3, Appendix B, Appendix C).

Fig. 4 · Each intervention's change in accuracy, points

  1. Force the base model to answer. At a matched total budget, cutting the base model's thinking at the 4k setting and demanding an answer lifts AIME from 0.56% to 9.44% (+8.9), GPQA from 4.04% to 60.27% (+56.2) and LiveCodeBench from 5.33% to 19.81% (+14.5) (Table 7). The base model often has an answer long before it stops thinking.
  2. Force the 4k-trained model to keep thinking, read at 4k. Delete its answer, append "Wait", up to four times: at 4k it now runs out of tokens, −36.1 on AIME, −51.0 on GPQA and −5.1 on LiveCodeBench against its own ordinary decoding.
  3. The same, read at 64k. With room to spare it spends 2.58k to 2.74k more tokens and changes accuracy by 0.0 on AIME, +2.7 on GPQA and +5.0 on LiveCodeBench, nowhere near the 30.2 AIME points it trails its base model by at 64k. Its extra tokens do not reason the way the base model's do.
  4. Tell the model its budget instead. Inserting "You have a 4k-token reasoning budget." leaves the base model at 76.1% on AIME and shortens its answers only from 27.3k to 23.4k tokens; a checkpoint trained with that cue shortens from 5.6k to 2.5k but drops from 52.2% to 38.3% (−13.9) (Appendix B). Moving the cue between system prompt, user prompt and the start of the thinking changes little.

The paper concludes that fixed-budget training "genuinely compresses the reasoning needed to reach its answers rather than only teaching the model to stop early". Two cautions: the forcing-up numbers exist only as deltas in the text and as a figure (no table of the levels), and only on Qwen; the base-model answer forcing is repeated on Gemma and Nemotron with mixed results (Gemma GPQA −3.0 at 4k, Nemotron +21.9; Appendix H).

Where the budget enters training

§3

The paper writes GRPO's objective with every place the budget touches made explicit, then changes one place at a time (Eq. 1). B limits how long a rollout can run and the termination protocol Π says what happens at the limit; the reward RΠ scores finished and truncated rollouts; cB weights responses by length; wB decides which groups are kept; m is the clipping rule; β anchors the policy to the base model; and the data 𝒟 decides how often the cap binds at all. The mechanics of each term belong to RL for LLMs. Every ablation is one run of Qwen3.5-4B at an 8k cap, scored with avg@16 on AIME; Fig. 5 places each at its gain over the base model at 8k (across) and its difference from the base model at 64k (up), from Table 3. The shaded corner, a gain at the cap with no loss at 64k, is what nobody reaches.

Fig. 5 · One term at a time

  1. The standard run. DeepScaleR, B = 8k, reward 0 for a truncated rollout, 1/(G|oi|) normalisation, every group kept, no KL: +41.3 at 8k, −22.3 at 64k, where it sits flat at 56.5% against the base model's 78.8%.
  2. The data 𝒟. Only the training set changes. GSM8K, whose answers Qwen finishes inside 8k three times in four (24% truncated, median 3,744 tokens), gains 11.6 and loses only 6.8; Nemotron-RL-Math-v2 (99% truncated, median 60,879 tokens) gains 34.2 and loses 24.2, like DeepScaleR (79% truncated) (Table 6). The more often the cap binds, the more gain and the more internalization.
  3. How truncated rollouts are scored, RΠ. Dropping them from the loss (masking): +29.2, −19.1. DAPO's soft penalty, 0 falling to −1 over the last 512 tokens: +35.0, −28.8. Closing the truncated reasoning and letting the model answer in 120 more tokens (forced answer): −2.2, −1.6, close to the base model at both ends: internalization gone, and the gain with it.
  4. Length normalisation cB. Dr. GRPO divides by the fixed budget instead of each response's length, removing the bias toward short correct answers: +39.3, −23.1, the same as GRPO. The length bias is not the cause here.
  5. KL anchoring β. β = 0.001: +37.9, −22.5, no change. β = 0.01: +23.2, −8.6 (70.1% at 64k). A stronger anchor keeps more scaling and gives up in-budget gain, gradually.
  6. Not RL at all. Supervised fine-tuning on the model's own correct answers that finished naturally under 4k tokens: −1.4 at 8k, +4.3 at 64k (83.0%, above the base model). The same on answers cut at 4,096 tokens and forced to a correct answer: +9.3, −38.4. Both sets are at most 4k tokens long; only the truncation differs (§3).

Two terms were never varied: group retention wB (every group is kept, so DAPO-style dynamic sampling is untested) and the clipping rule. The paper's reading of the ablations: no single objective term is the cause; what matters is which response lengths receive a successful training signal, through the data and through the scoring of truncated rollouts. "Stronger in-budget gains generally coincide with greater budget internalization, and none preserves both."

The cause is truncated training signal, in RL or not

§3

Supervised fine-tuning on truncated answers reproduces the failure; on naturally short answers it does not. This is the paper's sharpest control (Fig. 6, last step of Fig. 5). Both arms fine-tune Qwen3.5-4B on its own correct answers of at most 4k tokens, with the same hyperparameters and number of steps. In one arm the answers ended by themselves under 4k; in the other, answers that would have run longer were cut at 4,096 tokens, forced to answer, and kept when the forced answer was right (§3). The natural arm tracks the base model and ends above it at 64k (83.0% against 78.8%), with no gain at 8k (7.8% against 9.2%). The truncated arm gains a little at 4k and 8k and then flattens at 40.4%, worse than any GRPO run.

So "the cap" acts through the training data, not through a quirk of GRPO. RL on hard problems keeps rewarding only the rollouts that happened to finish under B and punishing those that ran long; truncation-forced traces teach the same stopping rule by imitation. Answers that end naturally reinforce concise reasoning where the model already concludes in time, without teaching it to stop at B, which matches GSM8K's mild result. The catch, again: the natural arm gains nothing in budget, while standard GRPO gains 41.3 points at 8k.

How much of this to believe

§5

What holds up. The crossover itself is solid. It appears in all 18 seeded runs under both checkpoint choices (36 of 36 evaluations, recomputed from Tables 15 and 16), on three model families from three labs, with two training sets, and the seeds sit close together next to the effect (Qwen 4k-trained at 64k: 46.11% to 51.67% across seeds, against the base model's 78.33%). Every number in section 2 recomputes from the appendix tables (see the tables tab), Table 8's means are exactly the means of the per-seed Table 15, and the mechanism is visible in two independent measurements, length and truncation. The forcing controls test the obvious alternative explanation and reject it. All checkpoints, ablations included, are public, so anyone can re-run the evaluation. Most of the limits below are the authors' own (§5), which counts in their favour.

Does not reproduce: the introduction's headline ranges. The introduction says that across the six checkpoints the gain at the training cap ranges from +11.7 to +46.7 points and the deficit at the largest budget from −17.2 to −35.0 (§1). The paper's own means for those six checkpoints (Table 8, which section 2.1 quotes) give +10.9 to +42.4 and −13.1 to −30.2; no per-seed or final checkpoint gives the printed ranges either (over all 36 seed evaluations: +8.3 to +47.8 and −10.6 to −32.2). The ranges probably come from an earlier set of single-seed runs; the takeaway on this page uses Table 8. A smaller slip of the same kind: Appendix B calls 64.4% the "headline" base accuracy at 32k, where Table 8 prints 65.56%.

Where to be careful.

  • The ablations are single runs, and most of their gaps are inside the noise. Each Table 3 interval is about ±8 to ±11 points at 64k, over 60 problems. By the crudest screen, intervals that do not overlap, only forced-answer training and short-trace fine-tuning differ from the standard run at 64k; the standard recipe itself varies by 5.0 points at 64k across its three seeds (Qwen 8k-trained, avg@3, Table 15). So "DAPO worsens the trade-off" (50.0% against 56.5%) and the no-change results for masking, Dr. GRPO and β = 0.001 are not distinguishable from seed noise. The large qualitative moves (forced answer, β = 0.01, GSM8K, the two fine-tuning arms) are bigger than the seed spread; a paired test on the same problems would be tighter, but the per-problem scores are not released.
  • Which standard run? Table 3's GRPO row (+41.3 at 8k, avg@16) is one run, not the three-seed mean of section 2 (+42.4, avg@3), and the paper does not say which seed or checkpoint it is.
  • Caps well below natural length. Every cap (1k to 8k) is chosen to bind hard: base Qwen is truncated on 95% of AIME answers at 8k. Whether a cap near or above the model's natural length also internalizes is not tested in v1, and the AIME sweep stops at 16k for Gemma and Nemotron, where base Nemotron is still improving.
  • Small models, mostly math, one RL family. Four to five billion active parameters, GRPO and its variants only, and two of the objective's levers (group retention and clipping) never varied. The authors argue the implicated choices (scoring truncation, KL, data lengths) exist in every RL algorithm; that is a hypothesis.
  • Out-of-domain results are single runs with small differences. The GPQA and LiveCodeBench cells come from one seed each; the paper adds pass@3 curves for stability (Appendix G) but no intervals.
  • The "lost capability" test rests on Qwen and on deltas only. Forcing the trained model upward is reported as differences in the text plus a figure, without the levels, and only for Qwen's 4k checkpoint.
  • A description to check against code when it appears. The released Qwen 8k model card describes GRPO with a "leave-one-out baseline", while the paper standardises rewards within the group (§3); with no training code public, which one ran cannot be checked.

Verdict. Believe the main claim: a fixed rollout cap well below a model's natural reasoning length makes GRPO trade test-time scaling for in-budget accuracy, on these small models, and evaluating only at the cap hides it. Believe that truncated training signal, not a GRPO term, is the main driver; the fine-tuning control shows it cleanly. Treat the ranking of the individual fixes, and the no-change results for normalisation, masking and weak KL, as provisional until repeated with seeds; and quote the effect sizes from the tables, not from the introduction.

What it takes to use this

§6

The paper's practical output is a reporting rule rather than a method: if you train RLVR under a rollout cap, evaluate accuracy and output length at several budgets above it before choosing a checkpoint, because a model that wins at the cap can lose at deployment budgets (§1, §6). Whether that loss matters depends on the task: on AIME it is lost capability, on GPQA and LiveCodeBench it is mostly a cheaper answer. No tested training change keeps the full gain and the scaling; the authors' suggestions (train across several caps, condition on the available compute, keep long successful responses) are untested here.

Code, checkpoints, compute and when it applies
  • Checkpoints: all trained checkpoints, ablations included, in the JetBrains-Research Hugging Face collection. Each inherits its base model's licence (Apache 2.0 for the Qwen and Gemma derivatives, the NVIDIA Nemotron open model licence for Nemotron, per the model cards). The cards give the training prompt and recipe.
  • Code: none released. Training used the public NeMo RL framework and evaluation Inspect AI; the per-seed data files the paper mentions (Appendix I) are not linked.
  • Compute: the paper does not report hardware or GPU-hours. Each run is 300 steps of 256 rollouts at 1k to 8k tokens on a 2B to 4B model; evaluation at 64k with several samples per problem is likely the larger cost.
  • Where it bites: tasks whose accuracy keeps rising with thinking length (competition math), trained under a cap the base model routinely exceeds, on hard data where many rollouts truncate. Where the base model overthinks (GPQA, LiveCodeBench here), the same compression can be a feature.
  • Cheap mitigations the paper measured: easier training data (GSM8K: −6.8 at 64k instead of −22.3, but +11.6 in budget instead of +41.3); a stronger KL anchor (β = 0.01); forced-answer scoring of truncated rollouts (no internalization, no gain).

Why it matters, and connections

Beyond the paper.

Most open RLVR recipes train under a cap far shorter than the reasoning their models later get at deployment, and report accuracy near it; this paper says that number can rank a trained model above its base model when the deployment budget would reverse the ranking. It is the token-budget twin of the Sharpening Tax result, where post-training trades pass@k for pass@1, and another case, like Are Dense Rewards Worth Their Tokens in GRPO?, of an RL comparison changing once you count what the usual yardstick leaves out. It also sharpens the overthinking debate owned by Test-time compute and reasoning: the base models here often have an answer long before they stop, so some of what fixed-budget training removes is waste. DeepSeek-R1's RL ran with a 32,768-token cap, raised to 65,536 later, and its response length grew during training rather than shrinking; that fits the paper's account that a cap the model rarely hits teaches no stopping rule, though the two recipes differ in many other ways.

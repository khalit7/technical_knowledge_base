Budget Internalization: Fixed-Budget RLVR Trades Test-Time Scaling for In-Budget Accuracy

{jbabstract}
Reasoning models often become more accurate when they can generate longer answers at test time.
This behavior is known as test-time scaling.
However, RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) usually trains them with a fixed rollout cap.
We find that this cap changes model behavior, not only training cost.
After training, models use few additional tokens beyond the training budget.
They outperform the base models near that budget, but their accuracy stops improving and falls below the base model at larger budgets.
The rollout cap thus becomes a learned constraint, that limits model’s test-time scaling.
We call this effect budget internalization.
We observe it across Qwen3.5-4B, Gemma4-E2B, and Nemotron-3-Nano-4B and two reasoning training tasks: mathematical reasoning and diverse, procedurally generated problems.
This effect also transfers from math training to science and coding tasks.
Training-objective level ablations on Qwen3.5-4B show that most changes recover little test-time scaling.
Stronger KL regularization and lower training-data truncation preserve more scaling, but stronger KL reduces in-budget accuracy.
Forcing an answer on truncated training rollouts largely removes budget internalization, but also removes most of the in-budget gain.
SFT on artificially truncated traces reproduces budget internalization, whereas SFT on naturally short traces does not, which shows that the effect is not specific to RL.
This result links budget internalization to length-censored post-training rather than to an RL-specific objective term.
Thus, a rollout cap does not only control training cost.
It can trade low-budget gains for lower performance when additional inference compute is available.

1 Introduction

††footnotetext: We release all our trained checkpoints (including ablations) at https://huggingface.co/collections/JetBrains-Research/budget-internalization.

One promise of reasoning models is that they can solve harder problems when given more tokens at test time (Guo et al., 2025; Snell et al., 2025).
This makes test-time compute a useful scaling axis: a deployment can spend more compute when a problem is difficult or when higher accuracy is required.

Unlike test-time scaling, RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) usually does not let the model reason until it emits an end-of-sequence token.
Instead, training uses a fixed rollout cap, often below the length of the model could otherwise utilize(Team et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025; He et al., 2025).
This is usually done to cut training costs, since it can scale quadratically with the sequence length (Aghajohari et al., 2026).
The resulting model is then often evaluated at only one budget, at or close to the training cap.
This evaluation shows whether training helps near that budget, but not whether the model can still benefit from more compute.
A model can look better than its base model at the training budget and become worse when both receive a larger budget.

We show that this failure occurs after fixed-budget GRPO on two hard reasoning training tasks: competition mathematics (AIME, DeepScaler Luo et al. (2025)) and diverse procedurally generated reasoning problems (Reasoning Gym Stojanovski et al. (2025)).
For Qwen3.5-4B trained with a 4k rollout cap, the model moves from a large gain over the base model at the matching evaluation budget to a large deficit when given more tokens (4K: $+34.8$ pp; 64K: $-30.2$ pp, averaged over three training seeds).
Gemma4-E2B (Gemma Team, 2026) and Nemotron-3-Nano-4B (NVIDIA, 2026) show the same saturation after math training, and Reasoning Gym training reproduces it for all three models.
A similar pattern also transfers from math training to GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2024) and LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2025).
We call this effect budget internalization: the training budget becomes part of the model’s learned behavior rather than remaining only a limit on training cost.

Budget internalization changes how RLVR results should be interpreted.
Accuracy measured only at the training cap can hide a reversal in model ranking.
Increasing the deployment budget may then fail to recover the base model’s performance, even though the base model benefits from the same additional compute.
The training cap can therefore trade future test-time scaling for better performance at the budget used during training.

Our goal is to investigate budget internalization and understand why it occurs.
To do this, we measure accuracy and output-length curves across model families, training caps, and reasoning benchmarks.
We then run training ablations to study which training choices affect above-budget scaling.
This analysis makes three contributions:

• 

We show that fixed-budget training hurts test-time scaling.
Across Qwen, Gemma, and Nemotron, AIME accuracy improves near the training cap but stops improving at larger budgets.
Across all six checkpoints, gains over the base model range from $+11.7$ to $+46.7$ pp at the training cap, while deficits at the largest evaluation budget shown for each model range from $-17.2$ to $-35.0$ pp.

• 

The effects of fixed-budget RLVR transfer beyond the training task and can improve efficiency.
For Qwen’s 8k-trained checkpoint, the high-budget differences from the base model are much smaller (GPQA, 64K: $-2.2$ pp; LCB-v6, 64K: $-1.0$ pp).
At the same time, mean output length falls compared to the base Qwen3.5-4B from $11.15$ k to $4.56$ k tokens on GPQA and from $38.32$ k to $8.31$ k on LiveCodeBench.
Gemma and Nemotron show the same pattern of much shorter responses with smaller accuracy differences on these tasks.
Therefore, losing some test-time scaling is not always harmful and can reduce inference cost while retaining similar accuracy.

• 

Our ablations reveal a persistent trade-off between in-budget gains and test-time scaling.
We examine objective variants, truncation handling, KL regularization, and SFT approaches.
Scaling remains impaired under alternative length normalization and overlength penalties, but improves when truncation exposure is reduced or KL anchoring is strengthened.
The scoring of truncated rollouts strongly affects budget internalization, suggesting that the responses reinforced during training matter more than any single GRPO objective term.
SFT experiments support this interpretation: scaling is retained with naturally completed trajectories, but deteriorates with artificially shortened ones.
Despite these partial mitigations, no tested intervention combines the full in-budget improvement with the base model’s high-budget performance.

Budget internalization is therefore neither always good nor always bad.
It is harmful when a task requires longer reasoning to solve harder problems, but useful when the same accuracy can be reached with fewer tokens on tasks where the model tends to overthink.
Evaluating only at the training cap cannot distinguish these cases.
We therefore recommend reporting accuracy and output length at several budgets, so that the effect of fixed-budget training is clear before choosing a model for deployment.

2 How Fixed-Budget Training Changes Test-Time Scaling

We study Qwen3.5-4B, Gemma4-E2B, and NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Nano-4B.
We run GRPO on the DeepScaleR math dataset with 4k and 8k rollouts cap for Qwen, and 1k and 2k caps for Gemma and Nemotron.
We additionally train Qwen with 4k cap and Gemma and Nemotron with 2k caps on Nemotron-RL-ReasoningGym-v1 (NVIDIA Corporation, 2026), which contains 15,000 examples from 104 procedurally generated and verifiable Reasoning Gym environments (Stojanovski et al., 2025).
These runs use the same training hyperparameters as the corresponding DeepScaleR runs.
We choose these caps below the budgets at which the base models usually complete their AIME responses, so base-model generations are frequently truncated at every chosen cap; Appendix Table 12 reports the exact rates.
We evaluate the selected checkpoints on AIME 2024 and 2025.
We then test whether the resulting behavior transfers to out-of-domain benchmarks, GPQA-Diamond and LiveCodeBench v6.
All runs use the NeMo RL framework (NeMo RL, ); the reward verifies the final answer and assigns zero reward to rollouts that end at the cap without one.

We run all evaluations with the Inspect AI framework (UK AI Security
Institute, 2024) and report avg@k, the accuracy averaged over $k$ sampled generations per problem, with the evaluation budget as the generated-token limit.
For DeepScaleR/AIME, each model/cap condition uses seeds 42, 43, and 44, evaluated at both its in-budget validation-selected checkpoint and final step 300 (Appendix I).
Other experiments retain the original single-seed checkpoints and base-model evaluations.
Appendix A reports the complete training and evaluation configuration.

Training gives large gains where the base model frequently reaches the evaluation cap, but these gains quickly saturate once the evaluation budget exceeds the training budget.
Both Qwen checkpoints show this reversal: the 4k-trained checkpoint moves from 4K: $+34.8$ pp to 64K: $-30.2$ pp, while the 8k-trained checkpoint moves from 8K: $+42.4$ pp to 64K: $-24.6$ pp.

The output-length curves show that this is not only an accuracy plateau.
The trained models rarely use the full additional token allowance.
Table 1 reports the mean and sed range of mean output length at these endpoints.
The base models average 6.11k–27.32k tokens, whereas the trained checkpoints average only 1.01k–6.22k tokens.
Smaller training caps therefore shorten the model’s overall response distribution rather than imposing a hard decoding limit.

2.2 Budget Internalization Across Training Tasks

We next test whether this pattern is specific to mathematical-reasoning training by replacing DeepScaleR with the diverse procedural tasks in Nemotron-RL-ReasoningGym-v1 (NVIDIA Corporation, 2026).
Figure 2 shows that it is not.
For Qwen, the 4k-trained checkpoint moves from 4K: $+31.1$ pp to 64K: $-25.0$ pp.
Gemma’s 2k-trained checkpoint moves from 2K: $+21.7$ pp to 16K: $-15.0$ pp.
Nemotron shows a smaller in-budget effect: its 2k-trained checkpoint gains only 2K: $+0.6$ pp, but it then saturates at $49.4\%$ while the base model reaches $60.0\%$ at 16k (16K: $-10.6$ pp).
The in-budget gain therefore depends on the model and training task, but the loss of test-time scaling appears after fixed-budget training on both hard reasoning datasets.

2.3 Transfer to Other Tasks and Budget Forcing

The behavior transfers from both training datasets to GPQA-Diamond, a graduate-level science benchmark, and LiveCodeBench v6, a code-generation benchmark with hidden tests (Figure 3).
The trained checkpoints gain accuracy near their training budget and saturate while producing shorter responses, although the high-budget damage is smaller than on AIME.
On GPQA and LiveCodeBench, both Qwen checkpoints preserve much more of the base model’s high-budget accuracy than on AIME (GPQA at 64k: 4k-trained: $-9.1$ pp, 8k-trained: $-2.2$ pp; LCB-v6 at 64k: 4k-trained: $-9.0$ pp, 8k-trained: $-1.0$ pp).
Gemma’s 1k-trained checkpoint follows the same pattern on GPQA (16K: $-1.0$ pp) and LiveCodeBench (16K: $-8.2$ pp).
Its 2k-trained checkpoint also remains close to the base model on GPQA (16K: $-1.3$ pp) and reduces the LiveCodeBench deficit (16K: $-5.1$ pp).
Nemotron’s 1k-trained checkpoint exceeds the base model on GPQA at high budget (64K: $+1.0$ pp) but not on LiveCodeBench (64K: $-9.0$ pp).
Its 2k-trained checkpoint strengthens the GPQA improvement (64K: $+4.0$ pp) and narrows the LiveCodeBench deficit (64K: $-4.2$ pp).
Reasoning Gym checkpoints follow the same pattern: Qwen remains below the base model on GPQA (64K: $-6.7$ pp) and LiveCodeBench (64K: $-3.8$ pp), as does Gemma (GPQA, 16K: $-4.4$ pp; LCB-v6, 16K: $-4.6$ pp), while Nemotron slightly exceeds the base model on GPQA (64K: $+0.7$ pp) but not on LiveCodeBench (64K: $-4.8$ pp).
Because these differences are smaller, Appendix G additionally reports pass@3 curves for a more stable comparison.
We hypothesize that these tasks often require less extended reasoning, so the base model sometimes overreasons rather than making productive use of additional tokens.

We therefore first test whether the base model could answer correctly without continuing to overreason.
We apply the budget-forcing procedure from Muennighoff et al. (2025).
If a response reaches budget $B$ without a parseable answer, we retain its reasoning prefix, insert the model-specific end-of-thinking delimiter and answer cue, and allow a short answer window.
Naturally completed responses remain unchanged.
Examples showing the prompt, model response, and budget-forcing intervention for all models and benchmarks are provided in Section C.1.

Budget forcing substantially improves low-budget accuracy across all three tasks (AIME, 4K: $+8.9$ pp; GPQA, 4K: $+56.2$ pp; LCB-v6, 4K: $+14.5$ pp; Figure 4).
The same trend persists at larger budgets, where answer forcing generally preserves or improves accuracy.
Appendix Figures 25 and 26 report the corresponding base-model intervention for Gemma and Nemotron.

Next, we apply the reverse intervention to the 4k-trained checkpoint to test whether its loss of test-time scaling only reflects early stopping.
We remove its end-of-thinking delimiter and final answer, append Wait, and ask it to continue the same trace, repeating this intervention up to four times under a 64k cap.
Relative to ordinary decoding of the same checkpoint, forcing up lowers accuracy at the 4k training budget (AIME, 4K: $-36.1$ pp; GPQA, 4K: $-51.0$ pp; LCB-v6, 4K: $-5.1$ pp) and produces the same or only modestly higher accuracy at 64k (AIME, 64K: $0.0$ pp; GPQA, 64K: $+2.7$ pp; LCB-v6, 64K: $+5.0$ pp), despite using $2.58$ – $2.74$ k more tokens (Section C.2).
Stating the budget explicitly in the prompt offers no reliable alternative, as models not trained on such cues largely ignore them (Appendix B).

Budget forcing can therefore improve base-model accuracy at a matched total token allowance, while making the trained model spend more tokens does not restore its test-time scaling.
This suggests that fixed-budget training genuinely compresses the reasoning needed to reach its answers rather than only teaching the model to stop early.
Appendix C.1 gives complete examples of both procedures, while Appendix Figures 25 and 26 report the corresponding results showing the same pattern for Gemma and Nemotron.

2.4 Budget Internalization

Fixed-budget training therefore has three distinct effects: it can improve low-budget accuracy, make reasoning more economical, and reduce how much the model benefits from additional inference compute.
We call the last effect budget internalization.
A model internalizes its training budget when, above that budget, its base model continues to use more tokens and improve with additional compute while the trained model’s response length and accuracy largely saturate.

3 What Affects Budget Internalization?

Having established this behavior, we now inspect the potential causes of budget internalization.
We hypothesize that it arises from how the training objective treats truncated rollouts.
To understand where this behavior may come from, let us inspect the GRPO objective and identify where the generation budget enters training (Shao et al., 2024):

 $\mathcal{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\mathcal{D}}\mathbb{E}_{\{o_{i}\}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot\mid q;B,\Pi)}\left[w_{B}(q,\{o_{i}\})\sum_{i}c_{B}(|o_{i}|)\ell_{i}-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})\right].$ 	

(1)	

The budget $B$ first enters the rollout policy by limiting how long a response can be generated, while the termination protocol $\Pi$ determines what happens when this limit is reached.
Here, the per-response update is $\ell_{i}=\sum_{t}m_{\epsilon^{-},\epsilon^{+}}(r_{i,t})\hat{A}_{i}(R_{\Pi}(o_{i};B))$ .
The budget enters the reward $R_{\Pi}(o_{i};B)$ through the score assigned to complete and truncated responses, the normalization $c_{B}(|o_{i}|)$ through the weight given to responses of different lengths, and the prompt-selection term $w_{B}$ through which sampled groups are retained for training.
The token-level likelihood ratio $r_{i,t}$ is transformed by the clipping rule $m_{\epsilon^{-},\epsilon^{+}}$ , while $\beta$ and the reference policy determine how strongly the update remains anchored to the base model.
Finally, the dataset $\mathcal{D}$ determines how often the cap binds in the first place.
In our standard GRPO run, Qwen3.5-4B is trained on $\mathcal{D}=$ DeepScaleR with $B=8$ k, $R_{\Pi}$ assigns $1$ to a correct completed response and $0$ to an incorrect or truncated one, $\hat{A}_{i}$ standardizes this reward within a group of $G=8$ rollouts, $c_{B}(|o_{i}|)=1/(G|o_{i}|)$ , $w_{B}=1$ so that every group is retained, $m_{\epsilon^{-},\epsilon^{+}}$ is PPO clipping with $\epsilon^{-}=\epsilon^{+}=0.2$ , and $\beta=0$ .
Each ablation below changes exactly one of these factors and keeps everything else, including the optimizer, update count, and evaluation protocol, identical to this run.
For these ablations, we report avg@16 and compute every accuracy delta relative to the base model at the same evaluation budget.

Does high truncation exposure on hard datasets cause budget internalization?

Beyond the RL algorithm, budget internalization depends heavily on the dataset’s difficulty relative to the token cap.
When a dataset requires long reasoning, many rollouts hit the cap and receive zero reward, creating a strong negative gradient against extended thinking.
To isolate this data effect, we vary only the training dataset while holding the algorithm and 8K budget fixed.
We compare GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), DeepScaleR (Luo et al., 2025), and Nemotron-RL-Math-v2 (Du et al., 2025), which span a wide range of natural reasoning lengths (Table 6).
We observe a trade-off between in-budget learning and test-time scaling.
DeepScaleR provides the largest in-budget gain but loses it above the training budget (8K: $+41.3$ pp; 64K: $-22.3$ pp).
Nemotron-RL-Math-v2 follows the same pattern (8K: $+34.2$ pp; 64K: $-24.2$ pp), while the simpler GSM8K dataset produces a smaller gain but preserves more of the base model’s high-budget accuracy (8K: $+11.6$ pp; 64K: $-6.8$ pp).
High truncation exposure on hard datasets causes the model to internalize the budget as a hard limit, whereas training on simpler datasets preserves the model’s ability to reason beyond the training budget.

Does the way we treat truncated rollouts cause budget internalization?
A rollout does not always finish before reaching its token budget, at which point the training procedure has to decide what to do with it.
Standard GRPO assigns it zero reward, so we try three augmentations that treat such rollouts differently: loss masking removes it from the policy-gradient objective; DAPO applies a soft overlength penalty that decreases from $0$ to $-1$ over the final 512 tokens (Yu et al., 2025); and forced-answer training closes the truncated rollout’s reasoning
and forces an answer prefix, giving the model up to 120 extra tokens to answer, similarly to the budget-forcing control in Section 3.
These choices produce substantially different outcomes.
Standard GRPO produces a large in-budget gain but falls below the base model at high budget (8K: $+41.3$ pp; 64K: $-22.3$ pp).
DAPO worsens this trade-off (8K: $+35.0$ pp; 64K: $-28.8$ pp), while loss masking also does not help (8K: $+29.2$ pp; 64K: $-19.1$ pp).
Forced-answer training nearly removes the high-budget gap but gives up the in-budget gain (8K: $-2.2$ pp; 64K: $-1.6$ pp).
Thus, how truncated rollouts are scored has a direct effect on budget internalization: penalizing them makes it stronger, masking them is insufficient, and recovering an answer can nearly remove it at the cost of in-budget learning.
This also suggests that the effect depends on the response-length distribution seen during training--in particular, on whether short or long responses provide the successful training signal.

Does response-level length normalization cause budget internalization?
Standard GRPO averages token losses within each response using the $1/|o_{i}|$ factor, where $o_{i}$ is the completion.
This normalization introduces a length bias: it amplifies positive updates on short correct responses and dilutes negative updates on long incorrect responses (Liu et al., 2025b).
Because budget internalization reduces long-horizon reasoning, this bias is a plausible algorithmic mechanism.
We test this by rerunning the 8K-budget Qwen experiment with Dr.GRPO (Liu et al., 2025b), changing only the normalization from response length $|o_{i}|$ to the fixed generation budget $B$ , while keeping the data, reward, optimizer, and update count fixed.
Dr.GRPO (8K: $+39.3$ pp; 64K: $-23.1$ pp) performs similarly to standard GRPO (8K: $+41.3$ pp; 64K: $-22.3$ pp).
Response-level length bias is not the cause of budget internalization in our setting.

Can anchoring the policy to the base model preserve elasticity?
Budget internalization might emerge because the RL update allows the policy to drift too far from its capable initialization.
Because the base model already possesses significant long-budget reasoning capabilities, anchoring the trained policy to the base model via a Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty, which keeps the policy closer to the reference as the penalty grows (Mroueh, 2025), might prevent the model from forgetting how to extrapolate.
We evaluate KL regularization ( $\beta=0.001$ and $\beta=0.01$ ) while holding the dataset and 8K training budget fixed.
The weak penalty performs similarly to standard GRPO ( $\beta=0.001$ : 8K: $+37.9$ pp; 64K: $-22.5$ pp).
Increasing the penalty preserves more high-budget accuracy but reduces the in-budget gain ( $\beta=0.01$ : 8K: $+23.2$ pp; 64K: $-8.6$ pp).
KL regularization serves as a highly effective control for preserving above-budget capability at the cost of in-budget learning.

Is budget internalization an artifact of RL?
The preceding results suggest that budget internalization depends on the response-length distribution and how truncated rollouts are penalized in RL.
However, the dynamics of GRPO might also play a key role and be critical for this effect to emerge.
To isolate the influence of the training data, we ablate the training method.
We test supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the model’s own successful rollouts (rejection fine-tuning, RFT), keeping only those that terminate naturally within 4K tokens.
We then compare this to artificially shortened traces of the same length constructed via budget forcing (Figure 3). Specifically, we take rollouts that naturally exceed 4K tokens, truncate their reasoning at 4096 tokens, prompt the model for its final answer with the budget-forcing mechanism, and keep only the trajectories whose forced answer matches the ground truth.
Both sets of traces are at most 4K tokens long and differ only in whether the reasoning ends naturally.
To match training exposure, both runs use the same hyperparameters and the same number of optimization steps.
Short-trace RFT does not produce the elasticity drop seen in GRPO and exceeds the base model at high budget despite no in-budget gain (8K: $-1.4$ pp; 64K: $+4.3$ pp), whereas budget-forced RFT reproduces the GRPO failure (8K: $+9.3$ pp; 64K: $-38.4$ pp).
Budget internalization is therefore not specific to RL: supervised training induces it as well, but only when the training traces contain truncation.
Naturally terminated traces reinforce concise reasoning where the model already concludes within the budget, without imposing the budget as a stopping rule, which parallels the GSM8K results, where few rollouts reach the cap.
Avoiding truncation thus mitigates budget internalization, but yields no improvement at 4K or 8K, unlike standard GRPO (8K: $+41.3$ pp).
Overall, budget internalization emerges whenever the training signal explicitly enforces a budget constraint (whether through truncation penalties on hard datasets in RL, or artificially shortened trajectories in SFT).

Taken together, the ablations point away from a single GRPO-specific objective term and toward the distribution of responses reinforced during training.
Changing response-length normalization or applying an overlength penalty does not restore scaling, while reducing truncation exposure preserves substantially more high-budget accuracy.
The way truncated rollouts are scored has the largest effect: penalizing them strengthens internalization, masking them is insufficient, and recovering an answer nearly removes the high-budget gap only by giving up the in-budget gain.
KL regularization exposes the same trade-off more gradually, preserving more of the base model’s high-budget capability as anchoring increases but reducing accuracy at the training budget.
The SFT controls show that this behavior is not specific to RL, since naturally completed trajectories preserve scaling whereas artificially shortened trajectories reproduce the contraction.
Across these interventions, stronger in-budget gains generally coincide with greater budget internalization, and none preserves both the full low-budget improvement and the base model’s test-time scaling.

4 Related Work

Reasoning in LLMs.

Reasoning has become a central capability of recent large language models.
It was first elicited through chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022; Kojima et al., 2022), and theoretical work shows that some problems cannot be solved without such intermediate computation (Feng et al., 2023; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024; Li et al., 2024).
Subsequent work moved from eliciting reasoning at inference time to training it directly.
Guo et al. (2025) showed that reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) alone can induce extended reasoning without human demonstrations, and current models typically combine supervised bootstrapping on externally generated traces with a subsequent RL stage (Yang et al., 2025; NVIDIA et al., 2026).
This RL stage runs under a fixed generation budget, and it is the setting in which we study budget internalization.
Some recent models additionally expose a reasoning-effort setting that selects the amount of reasoning at inference time (Qwen Team, 2026b; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2026).
Such explicit budget conditioning may mitigate budget internalization by turning the training cap from an implicit prior into a clearly defined condition.

Limits of test-time reasoning.

The benefits of reasoning are uneven.
Gains concentrate on mathematical and symbolic tasks and are weaker or negative elsewhere (Sprague et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a), and even where reasoning helps, performance remains brittle under counterfactual or perturbed problems (Wu et al., 2024; Mirzadeh et al., 2025; Dziri et al., 2023).
Longer reasoning is also not uniformly better: models overthink, and accuracy can plateau or decline as the budget grows (Ma et al., 2025; Gema et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026; Shojaee et al., 2025).
Our budget-forcing experiments are consistent with this picture, as base models often reach a correct answer well before they stop reasoning.
Budget internalization adds a complementary failure mode, in which training removes the model’s ability to benefit from additional sequential compute.

Efficient and budget-aware reasoning.

Overthinking has motivated a large body of methods that reduce unnecessary inference.
Some allocate compute adaptively by deciding when to think, estimating a token budget, or conditioning on a user-specified budget (Damani et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Wen et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026), while others directly optimize reasoning length through length-controlled training, pruning, anytime objectives, or adaptive length penalties (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025; Hou et al., 2026; Qi et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026; Xiang et al., 2025; Yi et al., 2025).
These approaches shorten reasoning by design.
We instead show that standard fixed-budget RLVR shortens it as a side effect, and that this compression transfers beyond the training domain.
The effect can be desirable when additional tokens bring little gain, as on LiveCodeBench, but harmful when they do, as on AIME.
Related studies examine how the training horizon should relate to the base policy’s solution length (Wachi et al., 2026; Xue et al., 2026) and how exploration can let test-time compute extrapolate beyond the training budget (Setlur et al., 2026).
We complement them by isolating which components of the training signal cause budget internalization.

5 Limitations and Future Work

Our experiments show that fixed-budget post-training can change test-time scaling, but they do not establish how universal this effect is.
The tested models have four to five billion active parameters, and most experiments use mathematical reasoning; larger scales and different reasoning tasks remain to be tested.
Only the main DeepScaleR/AIME comparison uses three training seeds and both selected and final checkpoints; other runs have one training seed.
Algorithmic generality is also unresolved because our RL experiments focus on GRPO and its variants.
However, the signals implicated by our results are not specific to GRPO: any RL algorithm must decide how to score truncated trajectories, whether to anchor the policy with KL regularization, and which response lengths enter through the training data.
We therefore expect budget internalization to depend as much on these shared choices as on the optimizer itself, but direct comparisons with other RL algorithms are needed to test this hypothesis.
Our behavioral measurements also cannot determine whether the model represents the cap explicitly or whether shorter successful trajectories simply shift its response distribution.
The ablations suggest that changing a single objective term is insufficient, while changing which response lengths receive a successful training signal has a larger effect.
Training across multiple rollout budgets, conditioning explicitly on available inference compute, and retaining long successful responses are promising ways to provide that signal.
The central open problem is whether such training can preserve the low-budget gains of fixed-cap RLVR without removing the model’s ability to benefit from more compute, which we leave to future work.

6 Conclusion

In this work, we introduce the term budget internalization: a learned constraint on test-time scaling induced by fixed-budget training. Across three model families and multiple benchmarks, we demonstrate that accuracy improves near the cap while response length and performance saturate beyond it, effectively damaging the model’s capacity for test-time scaling.
No tested method removes this trade-off, and the largest changes come from the training-data distribution and the treatment of truncated rollouts.
Evaluation across reasoning budgets is therefore essential, since evaluation only near the training cap can miss a substantial loss of test-time scaling.
At the same time, budget internalization is not always harmful.
On AIME, it limits the model’s ability to solve problems that require longer reasoning.
On GPQA and LiveCodeBench, however, fixed-budget checkpoints often perform better with shorter responses, showing that internalization can improve inference efficiency.
Budget forcing further shows that even base models can often produce an answer earlier without completing their natural reasoning trace, suggesting that overthinking is itself an important problem.
This efficiency effect could be deliberately exploited on tasks that benefit little from extended reasoning, provided that accuracy and output-length curves are used to separate useful compression from lost capability.

Appendix B Weak Budget Conditioning

We test whether a stated numeric budget can control generation without changing the available compute.
In the budget-cue condition, we insert You have a $N$ -token reasoning budget. immediately after the opening <think> tag.
The no-cue condition leaves the prompt unchanged.
Both conditions use the same 64k generation cap, while the cue states either 4k or 16k tokens.
The checkpoints trained with these cues receive the same modified prompt during evaluation and are mechanically truncated at the stated budget.
These 4k and 16k cue-conditioned checkpoints are separate training runs from the headline fixed-budget checkpoints, and the base-model control was rerun as part of this experiment.
The resulting 66.7% no-cue base accuracy at 32k in Figure 7 therefore differs from the 64.4% headline value because it comes from an independent evaluation sample.

Figure 6: Budget-cue ablation under a shared 64k generation cap.
Left: no cue versus a 4k cue for the base model and 4k cue-conditioned checkpoint.
Right: no cue versus a 16k cue for the base model and 16k cue-conditioned checkpoint.
The stated budget changes, but the available generation budget does not.

The results are presented in Figure 6. The base model does not follow either stated budget as a hard limit.
The 4k cue leaves its AIME accuracy unchanged at $76.1\%$ and shortens its mean response only from 27.3k to 23.4k tokens.
For the 4k cue-conditioned checkpoint, the cue shortens the mean response from 5.6k to 2.5k tokens but also reduces accuracy from $52.2\%$ to $38.3\%$ .
The 16k cue has a milder effect: the base model changes from $76.1\%$ to $77.2\%$ while again averaging 23.4k tokens, and the 16k cue-conditioned checkpoint shortens from 10.6k to 9.3k while accuracy falls from $65.0\%$ to $59.4\%$ .
Thus, a cue can shift length, particularly for a checkpoint trained with that cue, but it does not provide calibrated budget control and can reduce accuracy.

B.1 Cue Placement Ablations

The base-model ablation varies whether the cue is placed in the user prompt, system prompt, or immediately after the opening <think>
tag. We evaluate no-cue and stated-budget-cue conditions at a shared 32k-token
generation cap. The prompt states that the reasoning budget is either 4k or 16k tokens. We report these results in Figure 7.

Additionally, we train the base model with three listed budget cue placement variants at 4k reasoning cap (the prompt during training is faithful, meaning that it always states that the reasoning budget is 4k tokens). We evaluate with
the cue in the same location used during training, also stating that the available budget is 4k, while the real truncation happens only at 32k. The results are displayed in Figure 8.

We observe that these alternative budget cue placements have a marginal effect on both base and trained models’ output length and accuracy.

C.1 Budget-Forcing Prompt Presets

We match each budget-forcing preset to the model’s chat template and the domain’s answer format.
Figures 9–17 report the complete rendered prompt for every preset.
For each of them, we apply the same procedure: take a truncated model answer, insert the end-of-thinking token and benchmark-specific prefill, and allow the model a short response window for completing the answer.

For AIME, we allow 120 tokens for continuation and apply the standard last-5-lines scorer (meaning that we look for a parseable answer in the last five lines of the response).
For GPQA, we allow 16 tokens and stop once the model has generated a newline.
For LiveCodeBench v6, we allow 1024 additional tokens and attempt to extract a fenced Python block from the answer.

Did it learn to stop early, or lose the ability?

[[S2.SS3|§2.3]]

Two decoding interventions separate the two readings, and the answer is "lost". If the trained model had only learned to stop, making it keep thinking should bring back the base model's high-budget accuracy. The paper uses budget forcing in both directions, plus a third test that states the budget in the prompt; Fig. 4 shows each as a change in accuracy, on Qwen3.5-4B ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}, {{Appendix B|ax:A2}}, {{Appendix C|ax:A3}}).

Fig. 4 · Each intervention's change in accuracy, points

  1. Force the base model to answer. At a matched total budget, cutting the base model's thinking at the 4k setting and demanding an answer lifts AIME from 0.56% to 9.44% (+8.9), GPQA from 4.04% to 60.27% (+56.2) and LiveCodeBench from 5.33% to 19.81% (+14.5) ({{Table 7|ax:A3.T7}}). The base model often has an answer long before it stops thinking.
  2. Force the 4k-trained model to keep thinking, read at 4k. Delete its answer, append "Wait", up to four times: at 4k it now runs out of tokens, −36.1 on AIME, −51.0 on GPQA and −5.1 on LiveCodeBench against its own ordinary decoding.
  3. The same, read at 64k. With room to spare it spends 2.58k to 2.74k more tokens and changes accuracy by 0.0 on AIME, +2.7 on GPQA and +5.0 on LiveCodeBench, nowhere near the 30.2 AIME points it trails its base model by at 64k. Its extra tokens do not reason the way the base model's do.
  4. Tell the model its budget instead. Inserting "You have a 4k-token reasoning budget." leaves the base model at 76.1% on AIME and shortens its answers only from 27.3k to 23.4k tokens; a checkpoint trained with that cue shortens from 5.6k to 2.5k but drops from 52.2% to 38.3% (−13.9) ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}). Moving the cue between system prompt, user prompt and the start of the thinking changes little.

The paper concludes that fixed-budget training "genuinely compresses the reasoning needed to reach its answers rather than only teaching the model to stop early". Two cautions: the forcing-up numbers exist only as deltas in the text and as a figure (no table of the levels), and only on Qwen; the base-model answer forcing is repeated on Gemma and Nemotron with mixed results (Gemma GPQA −3.0 at 4k, Nemotron +21.9; {{Appendix H|ax:A8}}).

Where the budget enters training

[[S3|§3]]

The paper writes GRPO's objective with every place the budget touches made explicit, then changes one place at a time ({{Eq. 1|ax:S3.E1}}). B limits how long a rollout can run and the termination protocol Π says what happens at the limit; the reward RΠ scores finished and truncated rollouts; cB weights responses by length; wB decides which groups are kept; m is the clipping rule; β anchors the policy to the base model; and the data 𝒟 decides how often the cap binds at all. The mechanics of each term belong to {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}. Every ablation is one run of Qwen3.5-4B at an 8k cap, scored with avg@16 on AIME; Fig. 5 places each at its gain over the base model at 8k (across) and its difference from the base model at 64k (up), from {{Table 3|ax:S3.T3}}. The shaded corner, a gain at the cap with no loss at 64k, is what nobody reaches.

Fig. 5 · One term at a time

  1. The standard run. DeepScaleR, B = 8k, reward 0 for a truncated rollout, 1/(G|oi|) normalisation, every group kept, no KL: +41.3 at 8k, −22.3 at 64k, where it sits flat at 56.5% against the base model's 78.8%.
  2. The data 𝒟. Only the training set changes. GSM8K, whose answers Qwen finishes inside 8k three times in four (24% truncated, median 3,744 tokens), gains 11.6 and loses only 6.8; Nemotron-RL-Math-v2 (99% truncated, median 60,879 tokens) gains 34.2 and loses 24.2, like DeepScaleR (79% truncated) ({{Table 6|ax:A1.T6}}). The more often the cap binds, the more gain and the more internalization.
  3. How truncated rollouts are scored, RΠ. Dropping them from the loss (masking): +29.2, −19.1. DAPO's soft penalty, 0 falling to −1 over the last 512 tokens: +35.0, −28.8. Closing the truncated reasoning and letting the model answer in 120 more tokens (forced answer): −2.2, −1.6, close to the base model at both ends: internalization gone, and the gain with it.
  4. Length normalisation cB. Dr. GRPO divides by the fixed budget instead of each response's length, removing the bias toward short correct answers: +39.3, −23.1, the same as GRPO. The length bias is not the cause here.
  5. KL anchoring β. β = 0.001: +37.9, −22.5, no change. β = 0.01: +23.2, −8.6 (70.1% at 64k). A stronger anchor keeps more scaling and gives up in-budget gain, gradually.
  6. Not RL at all. Supervised fine-tuning on the model's own correct answers that finished naturally under 4k tokens: −1.4 at 8k, +4.3 at 64k (83.0%, above the base model). The same on answers cut at 4,096 tokens and forced to a correct answer: +9.3, −38.4. Both sets are at most 4k tokens long; only the truncation differs ({{§3|ax:S3}}).

Two terms were never varied: group retention wB (every group is kept, so DAPO-style dynamic sampling is untested) and the clipping rule. The paper's reading of the ablations: no single objective term is the cause; what matters is which response lengths receive a successful training signal, through the data and through the scoring of truncated rollouts. "Stronger in-budget gains generally coincide with greater budget internalization, and none preserves both."

@@CARD@@

The idea in one sentence: when one policy is trained on several verifiable rewards, standardise each reward within its group (as GDPO does), then multiply each objective's weight by (1 − s)γ, where s is the share of that reward's range the batch already achieves, so the update stops polishing objectives that are nearly solved and goes to the ones with headroom left.

You need: GRPO's group-standardised advantage ({{RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, where one group is worked through by hand; the algorithm's paper is {{DeepSeekMath|n:3c65c17b0d0d817f9fc5cb9a9fbcbee5}}). New terms: scalarisation (adding the rewards with fixed weights before standardising); GDPO (standardise each reward within the group, then sum; SA-MRPO's γ = 0 case); saturation sk (objective k's batch-mean reward as a fraction of its range); γ (the exponent that sets how hard saturated objectives are discounted); Exceed (the share of answers over the 4,000-token budget).

Problem

[[S1|§1, §3]]

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains a reasoning model on answers a rule can check. GRPO scores each answer against its group of answers to the same question ({{§3|ax:S3}}; how, on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}) and assumes one scalar reward per answer. In practice a reasoning model is rarely judged on one axis: an answer must be correct, and also respect a length budget, a format, a safety rule or, for code, simply run ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

The standard recipe scalarises: add the rewards with fixed weights, rsum = Σk wk rk, then standardise the sum within each group as GRPO does. The paper names two failure modes of that recipe ({{§1|ax:S1}}, {{§4|ax:S4}}):

  1. Scalarisation loses reward resolution. Different reward profiles can collapse to the same scalar and get the same advantage. With equal weights, (1, 0) and (0, 1) are indistinguishable, so an answer with perfect format and zero correctness is reinforced as strongly as the reverse.
  2. Fixed weights ignore saturation. Each objective keeps its share of the update all through training, however close it is to its ceiling. Easy auxiliary objectives (format, length) saturate early yet keep drawing the same share, so training keeps polishing what is already solved while correctness, the hard objective, still has most of its headroom.

GDPO (group reward-decoupled normalisation, {{Liu et al., 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05242}}) fixes the first: it standardises each reward separately within the group, then sums. The paper's point is that the second survives decoupling, because GDPO's weights are still fixed.

Concurrent methods that adapt the weights by other signals

Concurrent methods adapt the weights by other signals: DVAO by reward variance, GD2PO by conflicts between reward-specific advantages, SAW by reward variability, Focal Reward by saturation of rubric criteria ({{§2|ax:S2}}). None, the authors argue, ties the allocation directly to the fraction of the attainable reward range still unrealised.

The proposal, SA-MRPO (Saturation Aware Advantage Reweighting for Multi-Reward Policy Optimization): keep GDPO's per-objective standardisation, and multiply each objective's weight by (1 − sk)γ, where sk is how much of that objective's reward range the current batch already achieves. Nothing else about the GRPO update changes.

The idea on one group of four answers

[[S4.F1|§4, Figure 1]]

The paper's {{Figure 1|ax:S4.F1}} shows four answers to one question, scored on a format objective that is nearly solved (saturation 0.98) and a correctness objective that is nearly unsolved (0.02). The rewards are printed as fractions of 100. Stage 1 sends the same group through each recipe: rewards on the floor (format orange, correctness teal), per-objective advantages on the plinths behind, the final advantage in blue. The toggle switches recipe:

  1. The rewards. Four answers to one question. Format is nearly solved (97 to 100 out of 100); correctness is nearly unsolved (0 to 5).
  2. Sum the rewards. GRPO with several objectives first adds them with fixed weights (here equal). Answers 2 and 3 both sum to 100: their different profiles are now invisible.
  3. Standardise the sum within the group. Subtract the group mean (100) and divide by the group standard deviation (2.12). Answers 2 and 3 both get exactly 0: the resolution loss of §4.
  1. The rewards. The same four answers: format 97, 99, 100, 98; correctness 0, 1, 0, 5.
  2. Standardise format within the group. Format spreads only from 97 to 100, but standardising stretches it to unit spread: answer 3 (100) gets +1.34, answer 1 (97) gets −1.34. A nearly solved objective speaks as loudly as an unsolved one.
  3. Standardise correctness within the group. Correctness 0, 1, 0, 5 becomes −0.73, −0.24, −0.73, +1.70.
  4. Add them with the fixed weights. GDPO sums the standardised terms; the dots carry both rows into the blue one. Answers 2 and 3 now differ, but answer 3, with zero correctness, ranks above answer 2: the format term decides it.
  1. The rewards. The same four answers: format 97, 99, 100, 98; correctness 0, 1, 0, 5.
  2. Standardise each objective (as GDPO). The same per-objective terms as GDPO: format ±1.34 and ±0.45, correctness −0.73 to +1.70.
  3. Measure saturation. Each objective's mean reward, as a share of its range, fills its glass gauge: format at s = 0.98, correctness at s = 0.02, the two values Figure 1 states.
  4. Discount by saturation. Each weight becomes w(1 − s)γ. Format at s = 0.98 keeps a factor of 0.02γ; correctness at s = 0.02 keeps 0.98γ. The format bars shrink (move γ to see by how much).
  5. Add them. Now correctness decides: answer 3 turns negative (a sign reversal, not just a smaller step) and answer 4, the most correct, gets the largest advantage.

Bars are drawn to one scale across all three recipes. Defaults reproduce the printed Figure 1 independently: GRPO −1.41, 0, 0, +1.41; GDPO −2.07, +0.20, +0.61, +1.25; SA-MRPO −0.74, −0.23, −0.69, +1.65, which come out (to within 0.005) only at γ = 1 and without the batch standardisation of Eq. 1 (with it: −0.76, −0.23, −0.70, +1.70; {{recomputed|tab:t-tables:tbFig1}}). The paper's main recommendation is γ = 0.5, and the figure does not say which γ it used.

GRPO's tie of answers 2 and 3 (both sum to 100) is the resolution loss. GDPO's wrong order comes from per-group standardisation, which stretches the format objective's tiny spread (97 to 100) to the same unit scale as correctness. SA-MRPO shrinks the format term to almost nothing, and the ranking follows correctness.

Under GDPO, answer 3 (perfect format, zero correctness) gets +0.61. With the format objective at saturation 0.98 and correctness at 0.02, what does SA-MRPO give it at the paper's recommended γ = 0.5?

Negative: −0.53 at γ = 0.5 (before Eq. 1). The weight ratio of format to correctness is (0.02 / 0.98)γ, 0.14 at γ = 0.5, and answer 3's correctness term (−0.73) then outweighs its format term (+1.34 × 0.14). The paper's claim that reweighting can reverse the sign of an update, not just rescale it ({{§4|ax:S4}}), holds here for every γ above (derived: the γ where 0.02γ × 1.34 equals 0.98γ × 0.73). Method

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, Algorithm 1, Eq. 1]]

Setup ({{§3|ax:S3}}): a batch of B questions, G ≥ 2 answers to each from the frozen sampling policy, n rule-based objectives with known bounds [rmin(k), rmax(k)] and prescribed weights wk ≥ 0. Four steps ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}, {{Algorithm 1|ax:S4.SS1}}), run in Stage 2 on one real training batch of this page's toy ({{Train the toy|tab:t-run}}): the graded-length setting (Table 2 style), trained by SA-MRPO at γ = 0.5, seed 1, at step 80, 32 questions × 8 answers. Four of its groups are drawn, the first of each kind.

  1. One batch of the toy. Each answer has a correctness bar (teal, 0 or 1) and a length bar (orange: 1 up to 1,024 tokens, falling to 0 at 2,048). The four groups are the first in which only correctness varies, both vary, only length varies, and neither varies.
  2. Per-objective group advantage (the GDPO part): each objective standardised within its group of G answers, with the group's mean and population standard deviation. This keeps the per-objective resolution that summing destroys. In the toy batch a reward with no spread in a group is silent there (no bar: correctness in group 3, both rewards in group 4), while in group 2 one answer that ran long among seven short ones gets a large negative length advantage, as loud as anything correctness says (the question below asks how large).
    Ak(i,j) = ( rk(i,j) − μk(i) ) / σk(i)
  3. Saturation ratio (the new part): r̄(k) is objective k's mean reward over all B × G answers in the batch. Nothing is estimated: for bounded rule-based rewards the bounds come with the reward function. In the stage every answer of the batch, all 256, flows into the two gauges: correctness s = 0.40, length s = 0.95. Length is nearly solved; correctness is not.
    s(k) = ( r̄(k) − rmin(k) ) / ( rmax(k) − rmin(k) ) ∈ [0, 1]
  4. Saturation-aware aggregation, with exponent γ ≥ 0, the method's one new hyperparameter. At γ = 0.5 the batch's weights become 0.77 for correctness and 0.22 for length, so the length bars shrink to under a third of correctness's scale. At step 1 of the same run the gauges read 0.30 and 0.36 and the weights 0.84 and 0.80: nearly equal, as GDPO's would be.
    w̃k = wk (1 − s(k))γ,   Ã(i,j) = Σk w̃k Ak(i,j)
  5. Re-standardise over the whole batch, so the advantage's scale stays stable while the saturations drift. ÂSA then goes into the standard clipped GRPO surrogate unchanged; the KL term is omitted as independent of the reward construction ({{§3|ax:S3}}). In group 2 the correct answer that ran long ends at +0.27, where GDPO on the same batch (the outlines) gives it −1.49; length's share of this batch's update (the advantage's sum of squares before Eq. 1) falls from 40.0% under GDPO to 5.2%.
    ÂSA(i,j) = ( Ã(i,j) − mean(𝒜) ) / std(𝒜)   (Eq. 1)

γ = 0 is GDPO exactly, and only the ratios of the weights matter, because Eq. 1 rescales the whole batch ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}).

Special cases, the ratio form and the cost
  • Special cases. γ = 0 gives w̃k = wk, which is GDPO's advantage exactly (GDPO also standardises over the batch at the end, {{GDPO §3.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.05242}}). With one objective it reduces further to GRPO; since every group's advantages already average zero, Eq. 1 then only rescales the batch by one positive number (derived). So SA-MRPO strictly generalises both.
  • γ is the single knob. For two objectives, w̃a / w̃b = (wa / wb) · ((1 − sa) / (1 − sb))γ, strictly decreasing in γ when a is the more saturated. Because Eq. 1 rescales the whole batch, only these ratios matter: the overall size of the weights cancels.
  • Cost. One batch mean per objective and one multiply; the paper reports no timing, but the extra work is negligible beside sampling (derived).
Why does saturation still matter after GDPO's decoupling? Take a group of 8 answers where 7 meet a binary length budget and 1 does not. What advantage does GDPO's length term give the one long answer?

−2.65, and +0.38 to each of the other seven: per-group standardisation gives an objective unit spread in every group where it varies at all, however rare the failures. A nearly solved objective is therefore silent in most groups (zero spread; the paper does not say how a zero standard deviation is handled, and implementations add a small ε, which makes the advantage 0) and loud in the few where it fails. How much of the update that is depends on how often groups fail. With equal weights, the length objective's share of the advantage's sum of squares is the share of groups where it varies, among all groups where either objective varies (derived: every varying group contributes exactly G). A 0.2% failure rate puts it in of groups; if 60% of groups vary in correctness, length gets about of the update. That is the budget SA-MRPO can reallocate in the paper's binary-budget runs, and why the toy finds little to gain there ({{the toy|tab:t-run:runShare}}).

What it does not promise

[[S4.SS2|§4.2, Eq. 2]]

The authors state two limits themselves ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}), which counts in their favour.

  • No retention guarantee. Shrinking a saturated objective's weight also weakens the term that protects it, so a conflicting gradient from a less saturated objective can erode what was already learned.
    The first-order argument (Eq. 2)

    With gk the gradient of objective k's surrogate and update direction d = Σk w̃k gk, objective a changes to first order by w̃a‖ga‖² + Σk≠a w̃k gaᵀgk, and it decreases whenever Σk≠a w̃k gaᵀgk < −w̃a‖ga‖² ({{Eq. 2|ax:S4.E2}}). Shrinking w̃a as a saturates deliberately weakens the self-improvement term that protects it, so a conflicting gradient from a less saturated objective can erode an objective already learned.

    SA-MRPO is "an adaptive objective allocation rule rather than a constrained multi objective method"; whether the gain outweighs the erosion is an empirical question.
  • Nominal, not achievable, headroom. 1 − s(k) is the unrealised fraction of the prescribed range, exactly observable, but not a certificate that the model can realise it. The paper does not count this as a failure of the measure, since the unreachable part is imposed by the policy class.

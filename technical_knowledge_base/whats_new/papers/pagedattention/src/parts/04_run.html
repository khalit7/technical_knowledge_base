Simulate the KV cache

The same eight requests served from the same KV cache memory by the four systems of the paper's evaluation ({{§6.1|ax:S6.SS1}}): Orca (Max) reserves the maximum length for every request, Orca (Pow2) reserves prompt plus the output rounded up to a power of two, Orca (Oracle) knows each true output length, and all three take contiguous chunks from a buddy allocator. vLLM takes 4-slot blocks on demand. Every step every running request adds one token, as in iteration-level scheduling ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}), and a new request is admitted, first come first served, as soon as its memory can be found. Memory is drawn to scale: one square per token slot.

Try: Orca (Max) first: two requests run at a time and most of each chunk is red, reserved for a maximum length the request never reaches. Then Orca (Oracle): no red, but the pale reserved slots and the grey rounding of the buddy allocator still keep others out. Then vLLM: almost every slot holds a token, up to seven requests run at once, and the eight finish in about half the iterations; watch for a preemption, when blocks run out and vLLM evicts the latest request to recompute it later ({{§4.5|ax:S4.SS5}}).

Each system, stretch by stretch

  1. Eight requests wait. Nothing is allocated yet. Orca (Max) will reserve the 32-slot maximum for every request, in a chunk from the buddy allocator.
  2. Iterations 1 to 6: A and B take all 64 slots. Each gets a 32-slot chunk though A needs 13 slots (7 + 6) and B 12, so 19 and 20 slots are reserved for a length never reached (red). C waits while most of memory holds nothing.
  3. Iterations 7 to 13: one out, one in. A finishes at iteration 7 and C takes its chunk; B finishes at 10 and D enters; C finishes at 11 and E enters. Never more than two run.
  4. Iterations 14 to 23: D's long output holds a chunk. E finishes at 14 and F enters; F finishes at 22 and G enters. D (5 + 14 tokens) is still running.
  5. Iterations 24 to 29: the last two. D finishes at 24 and H enters; G and H finish at 29. All eight took 29 iterations, two at a time.
  1. Eight requests wait. Orca (Pow2) will reserve prompt + the output rounded up to a power of two (at most 2×), and the buddy allocator rounds the whole chunk up to a power of two again.
  2. Iterations 1 to 4: three fit. A reserves 7 + 8 = 15 slots, rounded to a 16-slot chunk; B 3 + 16 = 19, rounded to 32; C 12 + 4 = 16. The 64 slots are gone.
  3. Iterations 5 to 9: free, but not usable. C finishes at iteration 5 and frees 16 slots, but D needs a 32-slot chunk (5 + 16 = 21, rounded up), so it waits: external fragmentation. When A finishes at 7 its 16 slots merge with C's and D gets in.
  4. Iterations 10 to 17: two small chunks. B finishes at 10 and E and F enter with 16 slots each; E finishes at 13, and G, needing 32 slots, waits with H behind it.
  5. Iterations 18 to 26: the tail. F finishes at 18 and G enters; D finishes at 21 and H enters; G finishes at 25 and H at 26.
  1. Eight requests wait. Orca (Oracle) is told each output length and reserves exactly prompt + output; the buddy allocator still rounds each chunk up to a power of two.
  2. Iterations 1 to 4: exact reservations, still rounded. A (13 slots), B (12) and C (16) get 16-slot chunks and run; D needs 19 slots, rounded to 32, and only 16 are free.
  3. Iterations 5 to 9: a merge lets D in. C finishes at iteration 5; its chunk merges with the free 16 into 32 and D enters. A finishes at 7 and E enters.
  4. Iterations 10 to 17: D and F. B and E finish at 10 and F enters. G needs 10 + 7 = 17 slots, rounded to 32, and only 16 are free, so it waits; H would fit, but waits behind G (first come, first served).
  5. Iterations 18 to 25: the tail. F finishes at 18 and G enters; D finishes at 19 and H enters; H finishes at 24 and G at 25: one iteration faster than Pow2, far behind vLLM.
  1. Eight requests wait. vLLM will hand out 4-slot blocks as tokens arrive; every block has the same size, so the buddy allocator is not used.
  2. Iteration 1: seven admitted, prompts only. A to G get just the blocks their prompts need, 15 of the 16 blocks; H waits.
  3. Iterations 2 and 3: blocks on demand, a preemption. At iteration 2 C and F each need a new block and one is free: G, the latest arrival, is preempted, gives back its blocks and returns to the front of the queue, to be recomputed in one prefill later. At 3 A and B get new blocks.
  4. Iterations 4 to 6: G again. E finishes at iteration 4 and G is readmitted; at 5 D needs a block and G is preempted again, while C finishes; at 6 G and H are admitted.
  5. Iterations 7 to 9: H preempted once. At 7 A and B need blocks and H, now the latest, is preempted; A finishes. H is back at 8; F finishes at 9.
  6. Iterations 10 to 15: done in half the iterations. B finishes at 10, G and H at 13, D at 15: all eight in 15 iterations against Orca (Max)'s 29, with up to seven running at once and three preemptions on the way.

Toy sizes, chosen so the whole memory fits on screen: 64 token slots, a maximum sequence length of 32, vLLM blocks of 4 (the paper's figures use 4 too; vLLM's default is 16). The eight requests (prompt + output tokens) are illustrative: A 7 + 6, B 3 + 9, C 12 + 4, D 5 + 14, E 9 + 3, F 4 + 8, G 10 + 7, H 6 + 5. The simulator is the same code that runs at the paper's scale below; see src/sim.py for every rule. It plays by itself once in view; any control stops it, and each step of the list plays its stretch of iterations.

At the paper's scale

The same simulator at the size of the paper's OPT-13B server: 12 GB of KV cache at 800 KB per token, 15,728 slots ({{Table 1|ax:S5.T1}}), with requests whose prompt and output lengths are drawn from the paper's own ShareGPT or Alpaca histograms ({{Figure 11|ax:S5.F11}}, read from its vector graphics) and filtered as the authors' released benchmark does. The queue never empties, as in {{Figure 13|ax:S5.F13}}, where every system is past its capacity. Each system runs 6,000 steps; the averages skip the first 1,500.

What the simulator leaves out, so read its batch sizes as memory limits: time (it counts steps, not seconds; prefill, recomputation and the kernel cost nothing), arrivals (the queue is always full, so a system whose real batch is set by the arrival rate, as vLLM's probably is on Alpaca at 30 requests/s, comes out larger here), the pairing of prompt and output lengths in the real traces (drawn independently here), swapping (preemption is by recomputation, the released default for single sequences), and anything Orca's real implementation did that the paper's reimplementation did not. Limits it keeps from the released vLLM v0.1.0 code, not stated in the paper: 2,560 tokens and 256 sequences per step, and 1% of blocks kept free.

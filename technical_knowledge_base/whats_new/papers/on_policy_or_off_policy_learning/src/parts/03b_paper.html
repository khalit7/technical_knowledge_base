Why: the two gradients react differently to the rollouts

[[S5.SS1|§5.1, Eq. 4 and 5, Appendices B and C]]

Section 5 asks when the rollout policy matters at all, and starts from the gradient each divergence sends to the student's logits (the scores z before the softmax) at one prefix ({{Eq. 4 and 5|ax:S5.SS1}}, derived in {{Appendix B|ax:A2}}):

∂ forward KL / ∂zv = πS(v) − πT(v)

The same shape as cross-entropy's gradient on the logits ({{Loss functions|n:3c65c17b0d0d8161a72bc8c572f37d55}}). A difference of two probabilities: each entry lies in [−1, 1], and the whole vector's length is at most √2. It is non-zero wherever the two distributions differ, whoever is more confident.

∂ reverse KL / ∂zv = πS(v) [ log ( πS(v) / πT(v) ) − DR ]

Weighted by the student's own probability, times a log-ratio that has no upper limit. DR is the reverse KL at that prefix.

Two consequences, both visible on one prefix. Pick a case and move the slider; the student and teacher distributions are the paper's own two-token counterexample ({{Theorem C.2|ax:A3.SS3}}) or its mirror image.

Exact arithmetic, two-token vocabulary, direct logits (the setting of Theorem C.2). Gradient bars on one scale; the dashed lines at ±1 are the most any forward-KL entry can be. Defaults reproduce Equation 51's gradient (1/4) log((1 − δ)/δ) (1, −1) independently; {{the check|tab:t-tables:tbClaims}}.

  • Spikes. When the student puts real probability on a token the teacher considers nearly impossible, the reverse-KL gradient grows like log(1/δ), without limit. Forward KL's stays below √2.
  • Blind spots. When the teacher likes a token the student gives almost no probability, reverse KL's gradient on it is multiplied by that tiny probability and vanishes: reverse KL "may therefore struggle to recover teacher modes omitted by the student". Forward KL pushes that token up at full strength.

Averaged over a rollout policy's prefixes, the bounded gradient becomes a guarantee: forward-KL updates under two rollout policies differ by at most a constant times the distance between them ({{Theorem C.1|ax:A3.SS2}}), while reverse KL has no such bound ({{Theorem C.2|ax:A3.SS3}}) unless the spread of log-ratios at visited prefixes is capped ({{Corollary C.3|ax:A3.SS4}}).

The three results, with their constants (Appendix C)

Now average over the prefixes a rollout policy visits. {{Theorem C.1|ax:A3.SS2}} turns the bounded gradient into a statement about rollout policies: if the student's logit Jacobian is bounded by B, the forward-KL updates under two rollout policies differ by at most 2√2 B times the total-variation distance between the sequence distributions they produce. Close rollout policies give close updates. {{Theorem C.2|ax:A3.SS3}} shows reverse KL has no such bound: shift ε of the rollout probability onto a prefix like the first case above and the expected update moves by ε (√2/8) log((1 − δ)/δ), which grows without limit as δ shrinks. A bound returns ({{Corollary C.3|ax:A3.SS4}}) only if the spread of log-ratios at visited prefixes is capped at some R: then 2BR times the distance. With a vocabulary of over a hundred thousand tokens and a teacher sharpened to temperature 0.5, nothing keeps R small (this page's toy has 5 tokens and a spread of about 8 nats; {{Move the rollout policy|tab:t-run}}).

The paper's reading ({{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1}}): forward KL gives useful signal at any prefix where the models disagree, so it should not care much who wrote the rollouts; reverse KL concentrates on tokens the student already likes and the teacher dislikes, so it "depends more strongly on visiting the student's own prefix distribution" and should favour on-policy rollouts. The authors are careful: neither result proves that reverse KL must be unstable in a given run ({{C.4|ax:A3.SS4}}). The {{Move the rollout policy|tab:t-run}} tab computes both bounds on a small student and slides the rollout policy continuously.

The rollout-policy spectrum

[[S5.SS2|§5.2, Figures 3 and 4, Appendix D.5]]

To test the prediction, the paper replaces the on/off switch with a dial. At each generation step it samples the next token from a blend of the two models' log-probabilities ({{§5.2|ax:S5.SS2}}, {{Eq. 75 and 76|ax:A4.E75}}):

πλ(v) ∝ exp( ((1 + λ)/2) log πS(v) + ((1 − λ)/2) log πT(v) )

λ = −1 is the teacher (OffPD), λ = +1 the student (OnPD), λ = 0 their normalised geometric mean. Beyond ±1 the blend extrapolates: λ = 2 boosts tokens the student likes more than the teacher does. To stop it boosting tokens both models find unlikely, the extra push is given only to tokens with at least 20% of the favoured model's top probability and is capped at 2 log 3 in log-odds, so at λ = ±2 no pair of tokens has its odds changed by more than a factor of three ({{Appendix D.5|ax:A4.SS5}}; {{recomputed|tab:t-tables:tbClaims}}).

Nine values of λ from −2 to 2, both KL directions, both learning rates, three seeds: 108 runs on Countdown-3. The paper's {{Figure 3|ax:S5.F3}}, rebuilt:

Means over three seeds, whiskers one SEM, read from figure (decoded from the vector SVG; tick fits exact to 10−5). Dashed: learning rate 10−5; solid: 5 × 10−5, as in the paper. Accuracy is on 200 Countdown-3 questions at temperature 0.5. {{All values|tab:t-tables:tbF3}}.

At the lower learning rate, reverse KL trained on teacher-favoured rollouts (λ = −2 to −0.5) against student-favoured ones (λ = 0.5 to 2): what happens?

About 31 points worse: 49% on average for λ < 0 against 80% for λ > 0, and the SEM for λ < 0 is 11 to 17 points (one seed in three can fail outright). Forward KL at the same rate stays between 82.8% and 88.0% at every λ, a 5.2-point range, as the paper states ({{§5.2|ax:S5.SS2}}). At 5 × 10−5 reverse KL is erratic everywhere and collapses to 0% at λ = 1.5 (all three seeds).

Three things the figure shows beyond the text. Forward KL at the higher learning rate is the best setting on Countdown-3 (86.5 to 94.5%), so the low rate is not free: it trades some task accuracy for no forgetting. Forgetting and sparsity barely move with λ (out-of-distribution change −0.8 to +2.4 points at 10−5, −12.0 to −16.0 at 5 × 10−5): the learning rate sets them, as in Section 4. And on Qwen2.5 the same spectrum gives the same asymmetry, though at the high rate student-favoured rollouts forgot more ({{Appendix A.5|ax:A1.SS5}}), the opposite of the "on-policy forgets less" claim.

A low forward KL can hide nonsense rollouts (Appendix A.10)

Forward KL equals the cross-entropy from teacher to student minus the teacher's entropy, so a small KL can be the difference of two large numbers ({{Appendix A.10|ax:A1.SS10}}). In on-policy forward-KL runs on Science and MedReason, between steps 10 and 20 the KL got small while both entropies approached 12 nats, the scale of the whole vocabulary (e12 ≈ 163,000 equally likely tokens): the student was writing near-random text and the teacher, reading it, was just as lost. Useful signal survived only at early positions, and the coherent region then grew along the answer step by step. The step-50 students still scored 37% (Science) and 56% (MedReason) at evaluation temperature 0.5 and lost only 1.3 and 0.8 points out of distribution; resampled at temperature 1.0, the training temperature, accuracy fell sharply. Same final score, very different path: on-policy training can pass through a phase where the teacher's grades carry almost no information.

Beyond pass@1: coverage, harder tasks, RL afterwards

[[S5.SS3|§5.3, Figures 5, 6, 12, 24, 26]]

Coverage. Pass@k is the chance that at least one of k samples is correct (pass@1 against pass@k under RL in general: {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}); pass@10 above pass@1 measures how many different correct answers the model can still find. The paper's {{Figure 5|ax:S5.F5}} evaluates the λ-spectrum checkpoints (learning rate 10−5) on Countdown-3 and, without further training, on Countdown-4E, a harder variant with four operands from 1 to 10:

Means over three distillation seeds, read from figure (the SEM caps decoded from the vector SVG). 200 questions, 10 samples each. Dashed: pass@1; solid: pass@10. {{All values|tab:t-tables:tbF5}}.

  • Forward KL covers more. For λ > 0, reverse KL's pass@1 is as good as forward KL's or better (77.2 to 82.7% against 75.7 to 78.2%), but its pass@10 is lower (89.2 to 94.2% against 98.2 to 99.0%). This is the mode-seeking trade in numbers: reverse KL concentrates on fewer ways to be right. Qwen2.5 shows the same ({{Figure 16|ax:A1.F16}}).
  • On-policy rollouts generalise better, by pass@10. On Countdown-4E, moving to λ > 0 raises pass@10 by 11.6 points (forward KL) and 13.3 points (reverse KL) on average, roughly the "10 to 15%" the text states. Pass@1 rises only 3.4 and 3.8 points, from 3.6% to 7.0% for forward KL. The text's "pass@k" covers both; the 10 to 15 applies to pass@10 only ({{recomputed|tab:t-tables:tbClaims}}). It is the one place in the paper where on-policy data clearly helps under both KL directions.
  • The edge does not survive RLVR. The Countdown-3 checkpoints then get 300 steps of GRPO on Countdown-4 (reward 2 if correct, else 0; one RL seed; three distillation seeds per setting). Decoded from {{Figure 6|ax:S5.F6}}: off-policy forward-KL checkpoints, which started near zero, climb steadily and finish highest (mean reward 0.20 to 0.23 over the last 50 steps, about 11% correct). Reverse-KL checkpoints rise fast, five of six peak between steps 55 and 85, then fall back (off-policy ones to about 0.03, on-policy ones to 0.08 to 0.19). And the on-policy forward-KL checkpoints, the best Countdown-4E generalisers, never learn at all: peak reward at most 0.08, ending under 0.01. The paper's text does not mention that last result; it is the strongest evidence in the figure that a better distillation score is not a better starting point for RL.
  • Style. With a teacher told to reason in Spanish, every setting taught the student Spanish except on-policy reverse KL, which kept English ({{Appendix A.11|ax:A1.SS11}}): on English student prefixes the teacher still gives English continuations probability, and reverse KL can match that mode without covering the Spanish one. One run per condition, judged by GPT-5.

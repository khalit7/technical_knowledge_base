Expert dispatch: PipelinedLLEP

[[S3.SS1|§3.1, Figures 1 and 2, Appendix B]]

Under expert parallelism a token routed to an expert travels to the GPU (rank) that owns it, its destination d (expert parallelism in general: {{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}}); the routes Rd arriving there size its dispatch buffers and grow with batch size, top-k and routing imbalance ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}). The authors' earlier LLEP (Least-Loaded Expert Parallelism, {{arXiv 2601.17111|https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17111}}) removes the third: it moves a popular expert's overflow, and that expert's weights, onto ranks that would otherwise sit idle, without changing any token's expert choice. But LLEP still materialises the rank's whole routed batch in one all-to-all tensor.

PipelinedLLEP cuts the batch into chunks with a per-source token budget c: each sending rank puts at most c of its own tokens into any one chunk ({{Eq. 1|ax:S3.E1}}):

K = min(⌈N/c⌉, Kmax),   ceff = ⌈N/K⌉,   Rd(i) ≤ Ep k ceff

Each of the Ep senders puts at most ceff tokens in chunk i, each token makes k routes, so no destination can receive more than Epkceff routes in a chunk, whatever the router does, and no token is dropped to make it hold. The buffers then take at most 2EpkcHb bytes; past N = cKmax the chunk count stops at Kmax and each buffer is capped at 1/Kmax of the batch. Either way the limit is known before launch, and deriving K from c absorbs micro-batches of varying length without running out of memory.

The glass plane is Eq. 1's guarantee for one chunk, Epkceff = 419,456 routes: at this shape it is 80% of the whole batch, so the saving comes from chunks realising close to the even share, not from the bound.

Why overlap alone is not enough. The chunks run through a dispatch, expert-matmul, combine pipeline, as in FasterMoE or Tutel, which pick the chunk count for speed; this paper picks it to cap memory ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}). To make the cap hold in training each chunk is checkpointed, so the price is that the expert matmul runs three times per update instead of twice; route payload is unchanged.

Why each chunk needs its own checkpoint (Appendix B.1)

A loop that calls the expert layer per chunk keeps every chunk's autograd graph alive until backward, so the peak would not fall in training. So each chunk's expert matmul is wrapped in a reentrant checkpoint nested inside the decoder layer's non-reentrant one, which lets the outer checkpoint drop the inner tensors ({{B.1|ax:A2.SS1}}).

Exactness. Only the expert-weight gradient changes summation order (it is the one sum split across chunks); at K = 1 it is bitwise identical to LLEP's ({{Eq. 7|ax:A2.SS1}}).

LLEP decides which rank serves a route from the token's position in the expert's global token list. Cut a rank's 65,536 tokens into 10 chunks of consecutive positions, or into 10 strided chunks (positions i, i+10, i+20, ...). Same tokens, same destinations. What changes?

The spread within a chunk. Consecutive positions share a destination under LLEP's plan, so a chunk that covers the relocated tail of a popular expert sends almost everything to one helper rank, and the imbalance LLEP removed comes back chunk by chunk ({{Figure 1c|ax:S3.F1}}). Strided chunks spread each chunk over the destinations. Results (one node of eight H200s, identical input for every system, {{Figure 2|ax:S3.F2}}, {{Table 6|ax:A2.T6}}). At 65,536 tokens per rank, H = 7,168, 128 experts, top-8 and K = 10, PipelinedLLEP's peak is 56.9 to 59.3% below LLEP's (about 53 GiB down to 21.4 to 22.9) at 1.01 to 1.10 times its forward speed, unchanged with backward included ({{Table 9|ax:A2.T9}}); standard expert parallelism runs out of memory on every skewed profile (95/16 sends 95% of tokens to the 16 hottest experts). Choosing the chunk budget c (Figure 8, Table 7)

Choosing c. Peak falls with the chunk count at a decaying rate and latency does not follow it, so c is picked from a measured curve ({{Figure 8|ax:A2.F8}}); deriving K from the budget matters for short micro-batches, where a fixed K = 10 runs at under half LLEP's speed ({{Table 7|ax:A2.T7}}). Vocabulary projection: Ring-DTP

[[S3.SS2|§3.2, Figure 3, Appendix C]]
To compute one token's exact cross-entropy over a 200,000-word vocabulary, visiting the vocabulary one block at a time, how many numbers per token must you carry between blocks?

Three: the running maximum m, the running sum of exponentials z, and the target's logit yt once its block passes. Each new block rescales the old sum to the new maximum ({{Eq. 3|ax:S3.E3}}), and at the end the loss is m + log z − yt. This is the online softmax that {{FlashAttention|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e4a3e8e7a2c4771381}} also uses (the general mechanism: {{Writing kernels|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b9ab89c1bb7238cb6c}}). One token, 24 random logits, folded block by block:

Fused cross-entropy kernels (Cut Cross-Entropy, Liger) already do this on one device; here both the batch and the projection weight are sharded across ranks ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}). Rank r holds its own batch Xr (N × H) and owns the vocabulary slice Wr of V/P columns. {{Megatron's|n:3c65c17b0d0d8133ae92fbe90a1f308e}} vocabulary-parallel loss uses the same weight split but needs every rank to hold the same batch, which shrinks the effective batch and hurts long context. Ring-DTP (ring data-tensor-parallel) keeps a data-parallel layout, distinct batches per rank, and runs P ring rounds in which every batch meets every shard exactly once. Each meeting forms only the strip Yj,r = XjWr (N × V/P), folds it into the per-token state Sj = (mj, zj, ytj), and frees it, so logit memory is O(NVb/P) ({{Eq. 2|ax:S3.E2}}). Exactly one shard contains each target, so exactly one meeting supplies yt. Backward replays the same rounds from the saved normaliser and forms the softmax residual one strip at a time, so neither pass stores a full logit or logit-gradient tensor ({{Appendix C|ax:A3}}).

Which tensor travels. Either the activations or the weight shard can move. A hop costs O(NH) bytes if activations move and O(HV/P) if weights move, so Ring-DTP moves weights exactly when N > V/P. Moving weights keeps each batch and its state at home, so forward needs no return hop ({{Figure 9|ax:A3.F9}}). The cost is P − 1 sequential hops per pass, so the paper takes the smallest P whose strip fits.

Step through it on real numbers (seeded random weights and activations): four ranks (P = 4) on a ring, each with its own batch of 2 tokens (Xj, hidden width 3, the blue bars) and one quarter of a 12-word vocabulary (Wr, 3 columns, the glass plate). The orange bars are the logit strip a rank forms this round, negative values hanging below; the green blocks are each token's running state (m, z, yt), whose values are in the table under the steps.

  1. Round 1: every rank meets its own shard. Rank r forms XrWr, a 2 × 3 strip of logits, folds it into the running state of its two tokens ({{Eq. 3|ax:S3.E3}}) and frees it. If a token's target word is in this shard, its logit yt is recorded.
  2. Round 2: the weight shards hop. Each rank sends its shard (H × V/P, 3 × 3 numbers) to the next rank and receives the previous one, so rank r now holds W(r − 1) mod 4. It forms the new strip, folds it in and frees it. The batches never move.
  3. Round 3. Another hop, another strip per rank: rank r meets W(r − 2) mod 4.
  4. Round 4. The last hop: every batch has now met every shard exactly once, and exactly one of those meetings supplied each token's yt.
  5. Done: exact losses, at home. Each token's loss is m + log z − yt, equal to the dense log-softmax to floating-point precision, and no rank ever held more than one 2 × 3 strip. Forward needs no return hop, since the states never left.
  1. Round 1: every rank meets its own batch. Rank r forms XrWr and folds it into the state of its tokens, exactly as in the other schedule.
  2. Round 2: batches and their states hop. Each rank passes the batch it holds, its targets and its partial state to the next rank, receives the previous rank's, and folds XjWr with its own fixed shard. This schedule is chosen when N ≤ V/P, because a hop then moves N × H numbers instead of H × V/P.
  3. Round 3. Another hop: each batch and its state move on, and meet the next shard.
  4. Round 4. Every batch has met every shard, but each state now sits one rank away from home.
  5. Return hop, then done. One more hop sends each state back to its own rank (line 21 of Algorithm 2). The losses equal the dense ones.
  1. Each rank holds the whole weight. The standard reference keeps the full H × V projection weight on every rank: four plates here, 4 times the shard (5.34 GiB against 0.67 GiB at the paper's shape with P = 8).
  2. Form all the logits. Each rank forms its tokens' full 2 × 12 logit matrix at once; then log-softmax and, in backward, its gradient: three tensors of that size ({{Table 10|ax:A3.T10}}).
  3. Done: the same losses. The same numbers as either ring schedule, with four times the live logits at P = 4, or eight times at the paper's P = 8.

Results ({{Table 2|ax:S3.T2}}, P = 8, H = 7,168, V = 200,000, FP32, forward plus backward, one measured pass each): at N = 16,384 per GPU (activations move) the peak falls from 42.462 to 7.322 GiB, 82.8%, for 5.1% more time; at N = 32,768 (weights move) from 79.521 to 10.622 GiB, 86.6%, for 4.4%. Doubling N nearly doubles the standard peak but moves Ring-DTP's only from 7.3 to 10.6 GiB, so the saving widens with context, which is what keeps the vocabulary head on the device at 1M tokens. The standard reference's extra memory is exactly three N × V FP32 tensors (logits, log-softmax and their gradient: 3 × 12.207 = 36.621 GiB against the measured 36.6212, {{Table 10|ax:A3.T10}}), and the shards are exactly 1/4 and 1/8 of the 5.3406 GiB weight; see {{the checks|tab:t-tables:tbChk}}.

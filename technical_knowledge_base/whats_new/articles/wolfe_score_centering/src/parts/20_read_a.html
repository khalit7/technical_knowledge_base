@@CARD@@

The idea in one sentence: when rollouts come from a sampler whose probabilities differ slightly from the trainer's, every policy-gradient update carries a hidden term, the expected reward times the trainer's expected score under the sampler, that pulls the trainer toward the sampler whatever the rewards say, and because the sampler is then refreshed from the trainer the pull compounds; subtracting that expected score from each token's score (score centering) cancels it exactly, with no importance ratios.

You need: the score function and why a baseline keeps a policy gradient's mean ({{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}, sections 2 to 4); GRPO's group-centred advantage and the engine-trainer mismatch with its importance-sampling fixes ({{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, sections 3 and 8).

New terms: sampler q and trainer p (the inference engine that generates rollouts and the training stack that computes gradients on them: the same weights, slightly different probabilities); TIM (training-inference mismatch, q ≠ p); score (∇θ log p(yt | prefix), the gradient of the trainer's log-probability of the sampled token); s̄ (the expected score under the sampler at a prefix, Σv qv ∇θ log pv); drift and signal (the two terms the expected update splits into); score centering (using s − s̄ in place of s); head and tail (the sampler's top k tokens at a position, logged at generation, and the rest of the vocabulary, modelled from the trainer).

1. Fast RL systems sample with a slightly different policy than they train

[[ah:§what-is-training-inference-mismatch-tim|What is Training-Inference Mismatch (TIM)?]]

The article opens on the efficiency trades of large-scale RL: separate inference and training engines, asynchronous updates on stale rollouts, and lower precision for generation ({{in the article|ar:Disaggregated architectures: separating rollout generation}}). Each makes the sampler's token probabilities q differ from the trainer's p for the same weights. It lists four causes: different kernels or bugs, different precision, floating-point non-associativity (a different batch shape changes the order of a reduction), and staleness ({{in the article|ar:Floating-point non-associativity, where different batch sizes}}). The paper's own example of non-associativity: in fp4, (0.5 + 0.5) + 2 = 3 but 0.5 + (0.5 + 2) = 2 ({{paper §2.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20807v1#S2.SS1}}). Removing the gap at the source costs throughput (batch-invariant kernels) or is incomplete (fp16 for both engines), and staleness remains even with identical kernels. How large the gap is on real rollouts, and the routing version of it in mixture-of-experts models: {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, section 8.

The running notation follows the paper: q is the sampler, p the trainer, R a completion's reward, and the score of a sampled token is the gradient of the trainer's log-probability of it. With a group of rollouts per prompt, the article's baseline objective is REINFORCE with group-centred rewards, R minus the mean reward of the prompt's group, which is also the paper's shared objective for every experiment ({{in the article|ar:This formulation uses a similar approach to GRPO}}).

2. Importance sampling removes the bias exactly, then gets clipped and brings it back

[[ah:§limitations-of-importance-sampling|Limitations of Importance Sampling]]

The standard fix multiplies each token's contribution by the ratio p/q, computed as exp(trainer log-prob − sampler log-prob) from probabilities the engine logs at generation. Exact importance sampling turns an expectation under q into one under p, so the bias disappears, but the ratio is a random multiplier: a token with trainer probability 0.1 and sampler probability 0.001 gets weight 100 ({{in the article|ar:would receive an importance ratio of 100}}). Practice therefore caps the ratio (truncated importance sampling, TIS; CISPO), masks tokens outside a band (MIS, as in IcePop), or clips PPO-style, and every bound re-introduces bias. The mechanics and the real ratio distributions are on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, section 8; the article's contribution here is the paper's grid of methods, below.

Two ratios under one name, and the paper's grid of corrections (Table 1) as each was run

Two different ratios hide under one name. PPO's ratio compares the current trainer with the trainer before this batch's updates (several updates per batch); the mismatch ratio compares trainer with sampler at the time of the rollout, and is not refreshed ({{in the article|ar:Given that the PPO-style importance ratio captures drift}}). The paper's experiments take one SGD step per batch and compute every ratio against the sampler's logged probabilities, so PPO's clip there acts on mismatch ratios it was not designed for, which is the paper's explanation of why PPO and DAPO survive staleness but collapse under quantisation ({{paper §5.3|https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20807v1#S5.SS3}}).

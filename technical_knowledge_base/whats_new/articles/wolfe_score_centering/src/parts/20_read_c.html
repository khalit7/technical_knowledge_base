4. Drift: a term in the expected update that ignores which rollout won

[[ah:§motivation-for-score-centering|Motivation for Score Centering]]

Start with a constant reward: on-policy, the expected update is exactly zero; under mismatch it is not. On-policy, the expected score at any prefix is Σv pv ∇ log pv = ∇ Σv pv = ∇ 1 = 0, so a reward that is the same for every completion teaches nothing. When the token is drawn from q but scored through p, the probabilities no longer cancel and s̄ = Σv qv ∇ log pv is not zero ({{in the article|ar:TIM has the potential to create a spurious learning signal}}). In the last layer's coordinates the vector is plain to see: the score of token v with respect to the logits is ev − p (a one-hot minus the trainer's probabilities), so s̄ = q − p, a push on every logit toward the sampler's distribution (derived here; the paper states the same thing as the gradient of the cross-entropy with the sampler as teacher).

With rewards that vary, the covariance identity splits the expected update at a prefix into drift plus signal (equation in the figure, paper Equation 3; {{in the article|ar:referred to as drift and signal in}}). Drift is the expected reward times s̄: it depends on the rewards only through their mean, so it cannot tell a good rollout from a bad one. Signal is Covq(R, s): how the reward co-varies with each token's score, the part RL is for. With a constant reward the signal is zero, which is why the paper uses that case to see drift alone.

Drift is distillation toward the sampler, and distillation toward a moving copy of yourself compounds. With q held fixed, R · s̄ is the negative gradient of the cross-entropy, equivalently the forward KL(q ‖ p), so the update teaches the trainer to imitate the sampler ({{in the article|ar:equal to minimizing the forward KL divergence}}). Against a fixed teacher that converges to the teacher, harmlessly. But the sampler is the trainer's own weights, quantised or stale; after the sync it carries the trainer's drifted weights plus the same bias again, and the next step pulls further. That loop, not the size of the mismatch, is the paper's explanation of the online result in section 3 ({{paper §4|https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20807v1#S4}}): with +1/0 rewards the expected reward at every prefix is positive, so the pull always points the same way.

5. Group centring shrinks drift but leaves it wherever a prefix is better or worse than average

[[ar:Relation to group-centered rewards.|Relation to group-centered rewards]]

Drift lives at each prefix, and group centring subtracts a mean over whole completions. With advantage R − Eq[R | x], the drift at prefix y<t becomes (Eq[R | x, y<t] − Eq[R | x]) · s̄: the prefix's expected advantage times the pull. A prefix heading for a correct answer has positive expected advantage and pulls the trainer toward the sampler; a prefix that already contains a mistake pushes it away; only an exactly average prefix is free of drift. None of this depends on whether the next token affects the reward ({{in the article|ar:Group centering can reduce drift by changing its magnitude and direction}}). Removing drift through the reward would need E[R | prefix] at every position, a token-level value function, which is exactly the critic GRPO-style training avoids.

The toy beside makes this concrete. One prompt, two steps: a path token (A earns reward 1, B earns 0), then one of four filler tokens w, x, y, z that the reward ignores. The true gradient on the filler logits is exactly zero. The sampler is the trainer plus a fixed logit offset of size σ (the paper's synthetic mismatch adds a fixed offset to the weights; the logits, offsets and step size here are illustrative). Everything is computed exactly, summing over the eight outcomes, never sampled.

Predict before moving the slider: at σ = 1, which of the seven updates leave the filler tokens alone in expectation? And which has the noisiest single-sample update?

At σ = 1 the uncorrected +1/0 update has drift 0.154 on the filler logits and group centring 0.090: smaller, not gone. Over 400 expected-update steps with the sampler refreshed every step, the trainer's probability of filler token w after A goes from 0.376 to 0.997 under +1/0 rewards, a collapse onto the token the offset happens to favour, and to 0.593 under group centring, whose drift fades only because p(A) approaches 1 and the prefix stops being better than average. With the sampler frozen at its starting point instead (offline), +1/0 training stops at 0.598, the sampler's own probability: distillation toward a fixed teacher converges. In a real network the filler logits share weights with everything else, so this movement is not harmless there.

6. Score centering: subtract the sampler's expected score from each token's score

[[ah:§score-centering|Score Centering]]

What is centred is the score of each sampled token, not the reward and not the advantage. At every prefix the update uses syt − s̄, where s̄ = Σv qv ∇ log pv is summed over the next-token vocabulary under the sampler ({{in the article|ar:subtracting the expected score under the sampler from the score for each token}}). s̄ is the same for every token that could be sampled at that prefix and changes from prefix to prefix. In last-layer coordinates the token's score ey − p becomes ey − q: the gradient as if the sampler were the policy, pushed back through the trainer's network (derived here from the paper's Equation 4).

Two lines of algebra give the result. Eq[s − s̄] = s̄ − s̄ = 0, so the drift term vanishes at every prefix whatever the mismatch; and subtracting a constant leaves a covariance unchanged, so the signal is untouched. The update becomes Covq(R, s): the on-policy update except that the covariance is measured under the sampler, not the trainer ({{in the article|ar:the key difference is the distribution under which covariance is measured}}). When q = p, s̄ = 0 and nothing changes. As a loss for autograd: −R (log py − Σv sg(qv) log pv), sg a stop-gradient; its gradient is R (sy − s̄) (paper Equation 13).

Against importance sampling: additive and deterministic instead of multiplicative and random. Exact importance sampling also cancels drift, but its weight depends on the sampled token and can be huge, so practice clips it and drift returns. In the toy at σ = 1, truncation at 2 leaves drift 0.014 and moves w to 0.389; exact importance sampling has no drift but a single-sample spread of 0.499 against 0.409 for score centering, and at σ = 3 the gap is 1.151 against 0.404. Score centering's cost is the covariance under q: in the toy its update on the path logit is 0.978 of the on-policy one at σ = 1 and 0.822 at σ = 3. That is the residual importance sampling corrects, which is why the two compose ({{in the article|ar:these two techniques are complementary}}).

Composing them centres the weighted score: R (wy sy − Eq[w s]), with w = f(p/q) any clipped or masked weight. The subtracted term makes the expectation zero for any f, so a clip can no longer bring drift back; with exact weights (f(r) = r) the correction is zero, since exact importance sampling already has zero expected weighted score ({{paper A.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20807v1#A1.SS2}}). In the toy, score centering with TIS keeps w at 0.376 and its path update at exactly the on-policy value. The paper is explicit that this is a bias correction, not a variance reduction: a classical baseline uses Ep[s] = 0 to cut variance without moving the mean; score centering moves the mean.

7. In practice: the sampler's top 128 tokens, and a tail borrowed from the trainer

[[ah:§implementing-score-centering-in-practice|Implementing Score Centering in Practice]]

The exact correction needs the sampler's full next-token distribution at every position, which is far too much to store. The paper's arithmetic: 151,936 Qwen3 tokens × 4 bytes is about 608 KB per position, so a batch of 1,024 rollouts of 32K tokens would need about 20 TB ({{paper A.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20807v1#A1.SS1}}; recomputed: 20.4 TB). So the engine logs only its top k = 128 log-probabilities per position ({{in the article|ar:use the top 128 tokens}}), and the tail is modelled as the trainer's tail rescaled to the sampler's leftover mass: q̂v = qv on the head, ρ pv on the tail, with ρ = (1 − Σhead q) / (1 − Σhead p).

The trick that makes it cheap: the trainer's own expected score is zero, so the tail's contribution can be written through the head. The centring term becomes Σv in head (qv − ρ pv) sv, a sum over 128 tokens even though the modelled tail covers the whole vocabulary; with importance weights, Σhead (qv wv − α pv) sv with α = ρ f(1/ρ) (paper Equations 10 and 12; both identities checked here on random distributions, agreement to 1e-16). The sampled token's own sampler log-probability is logged separately, since it may fall outside the head. The paper reports k = 128, and even k = 32, matching full-vocabulary centring "in every setting we tested", with runs finishing within 1% of the baselines' wall-clock time; the article says k = 32 works "in certain cases", more cautious than the paper.

The article's PyTorch code matches the paper's loss (Equation 14) term by term: detached head coefficients q·w − α·p, the sampled token's detached weight, the centred log-probability multiplied by the group-centred advantage, normalised by the number of completion tokens as in the paper. Two things to know before copying it: completion_mask is used but never defined in the snippet, and with temperature, top-k or top-p sampling the logged probabilities must be those of the distribution actually sampled from (TRL's documentation requires vLLM's processed log-probabilities for exactly this reason).

How the formulas and the code were checked

In src/recompute.py: the head-only identity (paper Equations 10 and 12) on 200 random pairs of 40-token distributions with a 6-token head, for f = 1, TIS and MIS, largest difference 1.1e-16; the loss of Equation 14 differentiated by central differences on a 12-token example with TIS weights, gradient equal to A (wy sy − Σhead(q w − α p) sv) to 1.9e-10; the storage estimate 151,936 × 4 = 607,744 bytes per position and 20.4 TB for 1,024 × 32,768 positions. The article's code was compared line by line with the paper's JAX listing (Appendix A.3) and the reference repository's README; no difference in the estimator. The paper's own base objective uses rewards in {−1, +1} before group centring (Equation 15), which centring makes equivalent to +1/0 up to a factor of 2.

8. The evidence: deliberately severe mismatch as a stand-in for long runs

[[ar:Score centering is compared to several different correction methods|Empirical analysis]]

The paper could not find natural settings where the best methods separate within a few GPU hours, so it made the mismatch severe on purpose and reads the results as a proxy for long training under mild mismatch, consistent with drift accumulating ({{in the article|ar:authors in [1] intentionally amplify TIM}}). Every method runs on the same group-centred REINFORCE objective, one SGD step per batch, learning rate 10−2, 8 rollouts per prompt, sequences of 512 tokens (0.6B) or 1,024 (30B) ({{paper B.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2609.20807v1#A2.SS1}}).

  • Synthetic weight noise, Qwen3-0.6B on Countdown, three noise sizes, 3 seeds. Larger noise collapses methods earlier (DPPO at roughly step 160, 80 and 20; TIS stable at the smallest noise, collapsing at roughly 180 and 40 at the larger two); at the largest noise only score centering, alone or with TIS or MIS, trains stably ({{in the article|ar:only score centering--alone or combined with truncated or masked importance sampling--is stable}}).
  • An int8 sampler (weights, activations, KV cache) against a bf16 trainer, and a sampler synced only every 64 steps. Under quantisation score centering alone matches its compositions; under staleness it does not, and composing it with TIS or MIS works best, which the paper attributes to the covariance being measured under the stale sampler ({{in the article|ar:Under severe staleness, however, score centering alone cannot stabilize training}}).
  • Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base on INTELLECT-2 math, three sampler quantisations, 800 steps (chart beside). The article quotes only the harshest panel.

How much of this to believe

The explanation holds: drift is an identity, not a hypothesis, and the article gets it right.

  • The decomposition is exact algebra (the covariance identity at one prefix), and the article's derivations, its forward-KL reading of drift (it states the assumption that the sampler is held constant) and its account of why group centring leaves drift all agree with the paper and with Nathan Lambert's independent write-up. The toy above reproduces each qualitative claim exactly: drift under +1/0 and group-centred rewards, none under score centering, drift returning under clipped or masked ratios, larger spread under exact importance sampling, and the covariance-under-q residual.
  • Its attributions check out. The cited mismatch papers say what the article says they say (fp16 for both engines, learning-rate decay as mismatch grows, routing replay, the zero-mismatch diagnostic), the method grid matches the paper's Table 1, and the numbers it quotes (Qwen3-1.7B and 0.6B, int8, every 64 steps, k = 128, 30% against 12%) match the paper.
  • The method is cheap and public. One extra sum over 128 logged tokens, under 1% wall-clock, MIT-licensed reference code, and implementations merged in several open RL frameworks within weeks of the paper.

What the evidence does not show.

  • The experiments are a proxy by design. Mismatch made severe on purpose (a fixed weight offset, int8 and FP4 or INT4 caches on the sampler only, syncing every 64 steps), short sequences, plain SGD at a fixed rate, Countdown and one math set. The paper says so in its Limitations; the claim that mild mismatch over long runs behaves the same is an extrapolation it argues for but cannot test.
  • The headline gap comes from one panel, with few seeds. At 30B, score centering beats TIS only with the INT4 cache (30% to 12%, 4 and 3 seeds); with an FP4 cache they tie (52% to 51%), and with FP8 uncorrected training is fine (58%). Most other 30B curves are single runs, and all three panels report training accuracy on the sampler's own rollouts, not held-out accuracy (the weight-noise and online-offline figures use held-out prompts; the quantisation and staleness figure does not).
  • It is not the whole fix for staleness, or for mixture-of-experts routing. Under heavy staleness it needs importance sampling, as the article says; a later paper finds it falling behind on agentic tasks and at long refresh intervals, and finds the top-128 shortcut weaker than Monte Carlo centering, which disagrees with the original paper's ablation (responses below). Expert-choice mismatch needs routing replay, as Wolfe answers a reader.
  • "Many labs use it, quietly" is not evidence. It is one unsourced sentence in Lambert's RLHF Book, quoted in the article's opening. What can be checked is framework support (on by default in TRL's AsyncGRPOTrainer; merged in verl, slime, Miles and NVIDIA's Molt; open pull requests at PyTorch, AllenAI, Google and NVIDIA NeMo RL), which shows interest and availability, not that any frontier model was trained with it.

Verdict. Read the article for the mechanism: it explains drift and score centering correctly and more slowly than the paper, and its code is the paper's loss. Believe that drift exists and that score centering removes it exactly at little cost; treat "score centering beats importance sampling" as shown only under deliberately harsh quantisation on short runs, use it together with TIS or MIS when rollouts can be stale, and do not read the adoption line as a lab's report.

Where the responses are right. Regularized Score Centering is the strongest: its Monte Carlo centring beat top-128 centring in every variant it tried, though its math comparisons are single-seed, and its staleness results agree with the original paper's own recommendation to add importance sampling. Han and Hu sharpen a point the article skips: score centering matters for token-level corrections with clipped or masked ratios, the case every popular recipe uses.

Where it moves the knowledge base

  • {{RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, section 8: its fixes for the engine-trainer mismatch are all importance-sampling ones (TIS, MIS, the FP32 head, routing replay); it should add drift (the expected update's E[R] · Eq[∇ log p] term), score centering as the additive correction that composes with TIS or MIS, and that group centring does not remove drift.
  • {{Policy gradients and actor-critic|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8101a262d3620e75747c}}, Baselines: Ep[∇ log π] = 0 is the identity both a baseline and score centering rely on; off-policy it fails, and subtracting Eq[∇ log π] restores it as a bias correction rather than a variance reduction.
  • {{bf16 Rounding Silently Discards Most RL Policy Updates (Fair Cast)|n:40c5c17b0d0d81c39641d49880e39f73}}: score centering, like the ratio corrections, trusts the logged sampler probabilities; an export lag sits upstream of all of them.
  • The 23 November tech-news items on bitwise-identical sampler and trainer log-probabilities and on Gumbel substitution are the two other answers to the same drift: remove the mismatch at the source, or remove the need to trust the sampler.

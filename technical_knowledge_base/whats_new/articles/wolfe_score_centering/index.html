Score Centering: Understanding and Fixing Instability in RL

Cameron R. Wolfe (Netflix) · published 9 November 2026

In one line: A long explainer of Marek and Ryabinin's score-centering paper: when rollouts come from a sampler q that differs from the trainer p, the expected policy-gradient update at each prefix splits into drift (the expected reward times the trainer's expected score under the sampler, a pull toward the sampler that ignores which rollouts won) and signal (a covariance), and subtracting that expected score, computed from the sampler's top 128 log-probabilities, cancels drift without importance ratios; the evidence is the paper's, on deliberately severe mismatch with Qwen3-0.6B on Countdown and Qwen3-30B-A3B on math, measured as training accuracy.

The idea in one sentence: when rollouts come from a sampler whose probabilities differ slightly from the trainer's, every policy-gradient update carries a hidden term, the expected reward times the trainer's expected score under the sampler, that pulls the trainer toward the sampler whatever the rewards say, and because the sampler is then refreshed from the trainer the pull compounds; subtracting that expected score from each token's score (score centering) cancels it exactly, with no importance ratios.

You need: the score function and why a baseline keeps a policy gradient's mean (Policy gradients and actor-critic, sections 2 to 4); GRPO's group-centred advantage and the engine-trainer mismatch with its importance-sampling fixes (RL for LLMs, sections 3 and 8).

New terms: sampler q and trainer p (the inference engine that generates rollouts and the training stack that computes gradients on them: the same weights, slightly different probabilities); TIM (training-inference mismatch, q ≠ p); score (∇θ log p(yt | prefix), the gradient of the trainer's log-probability of the sampled token); s̄ (the expected score under the sampler at a prefix, Σv qv ∇θ log pv); drift and signal (the two terms the expected update splits into); score centering (using s − s̄ in place of s); head and tail (the sampler's top k tokens at a position, logged at generation, and the rest of the vocabulary, modelled from the trainer).

1. Fast RL systems sample with a slightly different policy than they train

What is Training-Inference Mismatch (TIM)?

The article opens on the efficiency trades of large-scale RL: separate inference and training engines, asynchronous updates on stale rollouts, and lower precision for generation (in the article). Each makes the sampler's token probabilities q differ from the trainer's p for the same weights. It lists four causes: different kernels or bugs, different precision, floating-point non-associativity (a different batch shape changes the order of a reduction), and staleness (in the article). The paper's own example of non-associativity: in fp4, (0.5 + 0.5) + 2 = 3 but 0.5 + (0.5 + 2) = 2 (paper §2.1). Removing the gap at the source costs throughput (batch-invariant kernels) or is incomplete (fp16 for both engines), and staleness remains even with identical kernels. How large the gap is on real rollouts, and the routing version of it in mixture-of-experts models: RL for LLMs, section 8.

The running notation follows the paper: q is the sampler, p the trainer, R a completion's reward, and the score of a sampled token is the gradient of the trainer's log-probability of it. With a group of rollouts per prompt, the article's baseline objective is REINFORCE with group-centred rewards, R minus the mean reward of the prompt's group, which is also the paper's shared objective for every experiment (in the article).

2. Importance sampling removes the bias exactly, then gets clipped and brings it back

Limitations of Importance Sampling

The standard fix multiplies each token's contribution by the ratio p/q, computed as exp(trainer log-prob − sampler log-prob) from probabilities the engine logs at generation. Exact importance sampling turns an expectation under q into one under p, so the bias disappears, but the ratio is a random multiplier: a token with trainer probability 0.1 and sampler probability 0.001 gets weight 100 (in the article). Practice therefore caps the ratio (truncated importance sampling, TIS; CISPO), masks tokens outside a band (MIS, as in IcePop), or clips PPO-style, and every bound re-introduces bias. The mechanics and the real ratio distributions are on RL for LLMs, section 8; the article's contribution here is the paper's grid of methods, below.

Two ratios under one name, and the paper's grid of corrections (Table 1) as each was run

Two different ratios hide under one name. PPO's ratio compares the current trainer with the trainer before this batch's updates (several updates per batch); the mismatch ratio compares trainer with sampler at the time of the rollout, and is not refreshed (in the article). The paper's experiments take one SGD step per batch and compute every ratio against the sampler's logged probabilities, so PPO's clip there acts on mismatch ratios it was not designed for, which is the paper's explanation of why PPO and DAPO survive staleness but collapse under quantisation (paper §5.3).

3. Offline training breaks with negative rewards; online training breaks with positive ones

Exploring Instability (and TIM) in RL

The puzzle the paper starts from: supervised fine-tuning learns happily from data another model wrote, yet RL breaks under tiny numerical differences. Policy gradient with rewards +1/0 trained fully offline is exactly SFT on the successful samples (rejection sampling), so the two differences between SFT and RL are the reward's sign and whether the data is regenerated by the policy being trained (in the article).

The diagnostic: Qwen3-1.7B on Countdown with a sampler whose weights carry a fixed Gaussian offset, so the mismatch is controlled, under three reward modes, offline and online. The results point in opposite directions (table beside): offline is stable only with non-negative rewards, online is least stable with them and most stable with group-centred rewards (in the article).

Offline failure is the known one: a negative reward makes the objective unbounded, replaying failed completions keeps pushing their log-probabilities down, and the softmax hands the freed mass to tokens already likely (the "squeezing effect", in the article). Online sampling stops it by itself; the paper sets it aside.

Online failure needs a new explanation. Online training on +1/0 rewards under mismatch is distillation from a teacher that is a slightly biased copy of the student and is re-copied from it after every step; offline distillation from a fixed teacher is stable, so the moving teacher must be the cause (in the article). Sections 4 to 6 make that precise.

4. Drift: a term in the expected update that ignores which rollout won

Motivation for Score Centering

Start with a constant reward: on-policy, the expected update is exactly zero; under mismatch it is not. On-policy, the expected score at any prefix is Σv pv ∇ log pv = ∇ Σv pv = ∇ 1 = 0, so a reward that is the same for every completion teaches nothing. When the token is drawn from q but scored through p, the probabilities no longer cancel and s̄ = Σv qv ∇ log pv is not zero (in the article). In the last layer's coordinates the vector is plain to see: the score of token v with respect to the logits is ev − p (a one-hot minus the trainer's probabilities), so s̄ = q − p, a push on every logit toward the sampler's distribution (derived here; the paper states the same thing as the gradient of the cross-entropy with the sampler as teacher).

With rewards that vary, the covariance identity splits the expected update at a prefix into drift plus signal (equation in the figure, paper Equation 3; in the article). Drift is the expected reward times s̄: it depends on the rewards only through their mean, so it cannot tell a good rollout from a bad one. Signal is Covq(R, s): how the reward co-varies with each token's score, the part RL is for. With a constant reward the signal is zero, which is why the paper uses that case to see drift alone.

Drift is distillation toward the sampler, and distillation toward a moving copy of yourself compounds. With q held fixed, R · s̄ is the negative gradient of the cross-entropy, equivalently the forward KL(q ‖ p), so the update teaches the trainer to imitate the sampler (in the article). Against a fixed teacher that converges to the teacher, harmlessly. But the sampler is the trainer's own weights, quantised or stale; after the sync it carries the trainer's drifted weights plus the same bias again, and the next step pulls further. That loop, not the size of the mismatch, is the paper's explanation of the online result in section 3 (paper §4): with +1/0 rewards the expected reward at every prefix is positive, so the pull always points the same way.

5. Group centring shrinks drift but leaves it wherever a prefix is better or worse than average

Relation to group-centered rewards

Drift lives at each prefix, and group centring subtracts a mean over whole completions. With advantage R − Eq[R | x], the drift at prefix y<t becomes (Eq[R | x, y<t] − Eq[R | x]) · s̄: the prefix's expected advantage times the pull. A prefix heading for a correct answer has positive expected advantage and pulls the trainer toward the sampler; a prefix that already contains a mistake pushes it away; only an exactly average prefix is free of drift. None of this depends on whether the next token affects the reward (in the article). Removing drift through the reward would need E[R | prefix] at every position, a token-level value function, which is exactly the critic GRPO-style training avoids.

The toy beside makes this concrete. One prompt, two steps: a path token (A earns reward 1, B earns 0), then one of four filler tokens w, x, y, z that the reward ignores. The true gradient on the filler logits is exactly zero. The sampler is the trainer plus a fixed logit offset of size σ (the paper's synthetic mismatch adds a fixed offset to the weights; the logits, offsets and step size here are illustrative). Everything is computed exactly, summing over the eight outcomes, never sampled.

Predict before moving the slider: at σ = 1, which of the seven updates leave the filler tokens alone in expectation? And which has the noisiest single-sample update?

At σ = 1 the uncorrected +1/0 update has drift 0.154 on the filler logits and group centring 0.090: smaller, not gone. Over 400 expected-update steps with the sampler refreshed every step, the trainer's probability of filler token w after A goes from 0.376 to 0.997 under +1/0 rewards, a collapse onto the token the offset happens to favour, and to 0.593 under group centring, whose drift fades only because p(A) approaches 1 and the prefix stops being better than average. With the sampler frozen at its starting point instead (offline), +1/0 training stops at 0.598, the sampler's own probability: distillation toward a fixed teacher converges. In a real network the filler logits share weights with everything else, so this movement is not harmless there.

6. Score centering: subtract the sampler's expected score from each token's score

Score Centering

What is centred is the score of each sampled token, not the reward and not the advantage. At every prefix the update uses syt − s̄, where s̄ = Σv qv ∇ log pv is summed over the next-token vocabulary under the sampler (in the article). s̄ is the same for every token that could be sampled at that prefix and changes from prefix to prefix. In last-layer coordinates the token's score ey − p becomes ey − q: the gradient as if the sampler were the policy, pushed back through the trainer's network (derived here from the paper's Equation 4).

Two lines of algebra give the result. Eq[s − s̄] = s̄ − s̄ = 0, so the drift term vanishes at every prefix whatever the mismatch; and subtracting a constant leaves a covariance unchanged, so the signal is untouched. The update becomes Covq(R, s): the on-policy update except that the covariance is measured under the sampler, not the trainer (in the article). When q = p, s̄ = 0 and nothing changes. As a loss for autograd: −R (log py − Σv sg(qv) log pv), sg a stop-gradient; its gradient is R (sy − s̄) (paper Equation 13).

Against importance sampling: additive and deterministic instead of multiplicative and random. Exact importance sampling also cancels drift, but its weight depends on the sampled token and can be huge, so practice clips it and drift returns. In the toy at σ = 1, truncation at 2 leaves drift 0.014 and moves w to 0.389; exact importance sampling has no drift but a single-sample spread of 0.499 against 0.409 for score centering, and at σ = 3 the gap is 1.151 against 0.404. Score centering's cost is the covariance under q: in the toy its update on the path logit is 0.978 of the on-policy one at σ = 1 and 0.822 at σ = 3. That is the residual importance sampling corrects, which is why the two compose (in the article).

Composing them centres the weighted score: R (wy sy − Eq[w s]), with w = f(p/q) any clipped or masked weight. The subtracted term makes the expectation zero for any f, so a clip can no longer bring drift back; with exact weights (f(r) = r) the correction is zero, since exact importance sampling already has zero expected weighted score (paper A.2). In the toy, score centering with TIS keeps w at 0.376 and its path update at exactly the on-policy value. The paper is explicit that this is a bias correction, not a variance reduction: a classical baseline uses Ep[s] = 0 to cut variance without moving the mean; score centering moves the mean.

7. In practice: the sampler's top 128 tokens, and a tail borrowed from the trainer

Implementing Score Centering in Practice

The exact correction needs the sampler's full next-token distribution at every position, which is far too much to store. The paper's arithmetic: 151,936 Qwen3 tokens × 4 bytes is about 608 KB per position, so a batch of 1,024 rollouts of 32K tokens would need about 20 TB (paper A.1; recomputed: 20.4 TB). So the engine logs only its top k = 128 log-probabilities per position (in the article), and the tail is modelled as the trainer's tail rescaled to the sampler's leftover mass: q̂v = qv on the head, ρ pv on the tail, with ρ = (1 − Σhead q) / (1 − Σhead p).

The trick that makes it cheap: the trainer's own expected score is zero, so the tail's contribution can be written through the head. The centring term becomes Σv in head (qv − ρ pv) sv, a sum over 128 tokens even though the modelled tail covers the whole vocabulary; with importance weights, Σhead (qv wv − α pv) sv with α = ρ f(1/ρ) (paper Equations 10 and 12; both identities checked here on random distributions, agreement to 1e-16). The sampled token's own sampler log-probability is logged separately, since it may fall outside the head. The paper reports k = 128, and even k = 32, matching full-vocabulary centring "in every setting we tested", with runs finishing within 1% of the baselines' wall-clock time; the article says k = 32 works "in certain cases", more cautious than the paper.

The article's PyTorch code matches the paper's loss (Equation 14) term by term: detached head coefficients q·w − α·p, the sampled token's detached weight, the centred log-probability multiplied by the group-centred advantage, normalised by the number of completion tokens as in the paper. Two things to know before copying it: completion_mask is used but never defined in the snippet, and with temperature, top-k or top-p sampling the logged probabilities must be those of the distribution actually sampled from (TRL's documentation requires vLLM's processed log-probabilities for exactly this reason).

How the formulas and the code were checked

In src/recompute.py: the head-only identity (paper Equations 10 and 12) on 200 random pairs of 40-token distributions with a 6-token head, for f = 1, TIS and MIS, largest difference 1.1e-16; the loss of Equation 14 differentiated by central differences on a 12-token example with TIS weights, gradient equal to A (wy sy − Σhead(q w − α p) sv) to 1.9e-10; the storage estimate 151,936 × 4 = 607,744 bytes per position and 20.4 TB for 1,024 × 32,768 positions. The article's code was compared line by line with the paper's JAX listing (Appendix A.3) and the reference repository's README; no difference in the estimator. The paper's own base objective uses rewards in {−1, +1} before group centring (Equation 15), which centring makes equivalent to +1/0 up to a factor of 2.

8. The evidence: deliberately severe mismatch as a stand-in for long runs

Empirical analysis

The paper could not find natural settings where the best methods separate within a few GPU hours, so it made the mismatch severe on purpose and reads the results as a proxy for long training under mild mismatch, consistent with drift accumulating (in the article). Every method runs on the same group-centred REINFORCE objective, one SGD step per batch, learning rate 10−2, 8 rollouts per prompt, sequences of 512 tokens (0.6B) or 1,024 (30B) (paper B.1).

  • Synthetic weight noise, Qwen3-0.6B on Countdown, three noise sizes, 3 seeds. Larger noise collapses methods earlier (DPPO at roughly step 160, 80 and 20; TIS stable at the smallest noise, collapsing at roughly 180 and 40 at the larger two); at the largest noise only score centering, alone or with TIS or MIS, trains stably (in the article).
  • An int8 sampler (weights, activations, KV cache) against a bf16 trainer, and a sampler synced only every 64 steps. Under quantisation score centering alone matches its compositions; under staleness it does not, and composing it with TIS or MIS works best, which the paper attributes to the covariance being measured under the stale sampler (in the article).
  • Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base on INTELLECT-2 math, three sampler quantisations, 800 steps (chart beside). The article quotes only the harshest panel.

How much of this to believe

The explanation holds: drift is an identity, not a hypothesis, and the article gets it right.

  • The decomposition is exact algebra (the covariance identity at one prefix), and the article's derivations, its forward-KL reading of drift (it states the assumption that the sampler is held constant) and its account of why group centring leaves drift all agree with the paper and with Nathan Lambert's independent write-up. The toy above reproduces each qualitative claim exactly: drift under +1/0 and group-centred rewards, none under score centering, drift returning under clipped or masked ratios, larger spread under exact importance sampling, and the covariance-under-q residual.
  • Its attributions check out. The cited mismatch papers say what the article says they say (fp16 for both engines, learning-rate decay as mismatch grows, routing replay, the zero-mismatch diagnostic), the method grid matches the paper's Table 1, and the numbers it quotes (Qwen3-1.7B and 0.6B, int8, every 64 steps, k = 128, 30% against 12%) match the paper.
  • The method is cheap and public. One extra sum over 128 logged tokens, under 1% wall-clock, MIT-licensed reference code, and implementations merged in several open RL frameworks within weeks of the paper.

What the evidence does not show.

  • The experiments are a proxy by design. Mismatch made severe on purpose (a fixed weight offset, int8 and FP4 or INT4 caches on the sampler only, syncing every 64 steps), short sequences, plain SGD at a fixed rate, Countdown and one math set. The paper says so in its Limitations; the claim that mild mismatch over long runs behaves the same is an extrapolation it argues for but cannot test.
  • The headline gap comes from one panel, with few seeds. At 30B, score centering beats TIS only with the INT4 cache (30% to 12%, 4 and 3 seeds); with an FP4 cache they tie (52% to 51%), and with FP8 uncorrected training is fine (58%). Most other 30B curves are single runs, and all three panels report training accuracy on the sampler's own rollouts, not held-out accuracy (the weight-noise and online-offline figures use held-out prompts; the quantisation and staleness figure does not).
  • It is not the whole fix for staleness, or for mixture-of-experts routing. Under heavy staleness it needs importance sampling, as the article says; a later paper finds it falling behind on agentic tasks and at long refresh intervals, and finds the top-128 shortcut weaker than Monte Carlo centering, which disagrees with the original paper's ablation (responses below). Expert-choice mismatch needs routing replay, as Wolfe answers a reader.
  • "Many labs use it, quietly" is not evidence. It is one unsourced sentence in Lambert's RLHF Book, quoted in the article's opening. What can be checked is framework support (on by default in TRL's AsyncGRPOTrainer; merged in verl, slime, Miles and NVIDIA's Molt; open pull requests at PyTorch, AllenAI, Google and NVIDIA NeMo RL), which shows interest and availability, not that any frontier model was trained with it.

Verdict. Read the article for the mechanism: it explains drift and score centering correctly and more slowly than the paper, and its code is the paper's loss. Believe that drift exists and that score centering removes it exactly at little cost; treat "score centering beats importance sampling" as shown only under deliberately harsh quantisation on short runs, use it together with TIS or MIS when rollouts can be stale, and do not read the adoption line as a lab's report.

  • earlier analysisBaselines and Score Centering in Policy Gradient Estimation
    Shuo Han and Ziqing Hu, 15 October 2026

    Treats both the group-mean baseline and score centering as centering in a covariance estimate (a sample mean against a population mean). With sequence-level importance sampling, score centering adds nothing once the group-mean baseline is used; with token-level importance sampling and a clipped or masked ratio, the group baseline alone is biased and score centering is needed. The article, which centres token-level scores throughout, does not cite it.

  • reader comments, answeredThree readers' questions, answered by the author
    readers, Substack comments, 9 November 2026

    On the 30B result: uncorrected training was stable at FP8, and Wolfe notes FP8 is the common setting (Qwen3 releases FP8 checkpoints). On mixture-of-experts models: score centering does not cover different expert choices, so keep routing replay (the paper did not replay routing at 30B and says it should be). On stale rollouts: stack importance sampling for the version lag and centering for the precision gap, "probably", as the paper recommends.

  • experimentRegularized Score Centering
    Hao Bai, Chenlu Ye, Xuanchang Zhang, Aviral Kumar, Tong Zhang, 16 November 2026

    Finds two failure modes: centering from the top 128 tokens plus a modelled tail scored below Monte Carlo centering (auxiliary tokens drawn from the sampler) in every tested variant (math runs single-seed), and score centering fell behind when the sampler was refreshed rarely or on agentic tasks such as Crafter, where a reverse-KL penalty toward the sampler (their RSC) helped most. On math at the same refresh interval, adding the penalty changed final accuracy by under 1 point.

  • alternativeGumbel Substitution: Removing Sampler Drift from RL Without Trusting the Sampler
    Romain Fanucci and Malik El Hassani, 20 November 2026

    Accepts the drift diagnosis and removes the need for sampler-reported probabilities, which every correction including score centering trusts: with shared Gumbel noise the trainer recomputes the token its own policy would have drawn. On a 2B model under strong mismatch it was comparable to masked importance sampling with score centering given the true q.

Where the responses are right. Regularized Score Centering is the strongest: its Monte Carlo centring beat top-128 centring in every variant it tried, though its math comparisons are single-seed, and its staleness results agree with the original paper's own recommendation to add importance sampling. Han and Hu sharpen a point the article skips: score centering matters for token-level corrections with clipped or masked ratios, the case every popular recipe uses.

Where it moves the knowledge base

  • RL for LLMs: RLHF, GRPO, RLVR, section 8: its fixes for the engine-trainer mismatch are all importance-sampling ones (TIS, MIS, the FP32 head, routing replay); it should add drift (the expected update's E[R] · Eq[∇ log p] term), score centering as the additive correction that composes with TIS or MIS, and that group centring does not remove drift.
  • Policy gradients and actor-critic, Baselines: Ep[∇ log π] = 0 is the identity both a baseline and score centering rely on; off-policy it fails, and subtracting Eq[∇ log π] restores it as a bias correction rather than a variance reduction.
  • bf16 Rounding Silently Discards Most RL Policy Updates (Fair Cast): score centering, like the ratio corrections, trusts the logged sampler probabilities; an export lag sits upstream of all of them.
  • The 23 November tech-news items on bitwise-identical sampler and trainer log-probabilities and on Gumbel substitution are the two other answers to the same drift: remove the mismatch at the source, or remove the need to trust the sampler.

Responses, in date order

Baselines and Score Centering in Policy Gradient Estimation

Shuo Han and Ziqing Hu, 15 October 2026, earlier analysis: Treats both the group-mean baseline and score centering as centering in a covariance estimate (a sample mean against a population mean). With sequence-level importance sampling, score centering adds nothing once the group-mean baseline is used; with token-level importance sampling and a clipped or masked ratio, the group baseline alone is biased and score centering is needed. The article, which centres token-level scores throughout, does not cite it.

(15 min)

Three readers' questions, answered by the author

readers, Substack comments, 9 November 2026, reader comments, answered: On the 30B result: uncorrected training was stable at FP8, and Wolfe notes FP8 is the common setting (Qwen3 releases FP8 checkpoints). On mixture-of-experts models: score centering does not cover different expert choices, so keep routing replay (the paper did not replay routing at 30B and says it should be). On stale rollouts: stack importance sampling for the version lag and centering for the precision gap, "probably", as the paper recommends.

(5 min)

Regularized Score Centering

Hao Bai, Chenlu Ye, Xuanchang Zhang, Aviral Kumar, Tong Zhang, 16 November 2026, experiment: Finds two failure modes: centering from the top 128 tokens plus a modelled tail scored below Monte Carlo centering (auxiliary tokens drawn from the sampler) in every tested variant (math runs single-seed), and score centering fell behind when the sampler was refreshed rarely or on agentic tasks such as Crafter, where a reverse-KL penalty toward the sampler (their RSC) helped most. On math at the same refresh interval, adding the penalty changed final accuracy by under 1 point.

(50 min)

Gumbel Substitution: Removing Sampler Drift from RL Without Trusting the Sampler

Romain Fanucci and Malik El Hassani, 20 November 2026, alternative: Accepts the drift diagnosis and removes the need for sampler-reported probabilities, which every correction including score centering trusts: with shared Gumbel noise the trainer recomputes the token its own policy would have drawn. On a 2B model under strong mismatch it was comparable to masked importance sampling with score centering given the true q.

EPRO: Robust Policy Optimization Through an Imperfect Execution-Policy Predictor

24 November 2026: Centres the score against a predicted current sampler rather than the one that generated the rollout (Transported Score Centering), with soft clipping by policy shift; highest average held-out accuracy among its compared methods on seven math benchmarks across four Qwen3 settings (the authors' own comparison).

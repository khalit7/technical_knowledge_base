Language Models for Text Classification: From Bag-of-Words to Jev

Sebastian Raschka · published 29 September 2026

The idea in one sentence: every text classifier from word counts to fine-tuned transformers had to be trained on its task; Jev's novelty is breadth, one hosted model that reads the label descriptions in each request and returns a probability per label with no training, which Raschka shows end to end on IMDb movie reviews.

New terms: bag-of-words (word counts over a fixed vocabulary, order lost); classification head (a small layer mapping one hidden state to class scores, in place of the output layer); decision model (TypeSafe's "System One model": text and typed questions in, a probability per allowed answer out, no text generated); Choice, Noul, Score (Jev's question types: one of N options, a yes probability, an ordered rating); RLCR (RL with calibration rewards); independence of irrelevant alternatives (adding an option cannot change the odds between two others).

1. Before transformers: count the words, or read them in order

Section 1

Bag-of-words with logistic regression scored 89.9%, and the author still starts every text problem with it. Each vocabulary word gets one position holding its count, so any text becomes a fixed vector and a linear model learns which words push towards which label; word order is lost (the dog bites the man and its reverse are one vector). He calls it logistic regression remains my go-to baseline, the instinct a later test of Jev took up (responses). A Hacker News commenter named the step the article skips: averaged word vectors, as in fastText.

Reading in order did not help until the model was pretrained. An LSTM trained from scratch overfit: an LSTM RNN achieved only 85.66% accuracy. ULMFiT (2018) pretrained the same kind of network as a language model, fine-tuned it, and achieved an impressive 95.4% test accuracy on IMDb: on the same test set, pretraining bought 9.74 points, the lesson the rest of the article builds on and one our Sequence models page does not tell. A text CNN slides filters over windows of word vectors and max-pools over positions so any length gives a fixed vector (global max-pooling would be a better option here); such a CNN gets about 90.07% accuracy. One aside is loose: state-space models are not still has to be processed sequentially in training, where a parallel scan covers the sequence (Mamba).

2. Transformers: three ways to get one label

Section 2

An encoder reads a classification token; a decoder must read its last token. BERT-style models have a classification token at the first position that sees the whole text, so a small head on it is fine-tuned (ModernBERT gets approximately 95% accuracy). A GPT-style model gets a small head too, but under the causal mask only the last position has seen everything, so the head reads the final non-padding token's hidden state (GPT-2 124M, about 92%). An encoder-decoder can do either, or train its decoder to write the label, which the article names call this approach text-to-text classification; prompting a chat model is that without training, unnecessarily brittle and inefficient for a fixed domain. Which family wins at equal size is on Families and objectives; its newest encoder-decoder example, being DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, is not there yet.

The hero's accuracies are not one experiment. They come from three test sets (checked in the linked notebooks). Read the ladder for its two big steps, pretraining (about +5 points) and no training at all at the top, not for one-point gaps.

3. Jev: three question types, and 96.47% on IMDb without training

Section 3

Jev takes text and typed questions and returns probabilities, not text. TypeSafe launched it on 15 September 2026 with three main API types: Choice (one of N described options, a probability each and a confidence), Noul (one yes probability; several make a multi-label classifier) and Score (a level on an ordered rubric). The article's framing is the useful one, the ChatGPT moment for classification: one general model instead of a classifier per task. Prices, vendor speed claims and the decision models that followed are on Other notable providers. The confidence field is different from the probability assigned to the winning class: TypeSafe's docs define it as (pmax − 1/n) / (1 − 1/n), so a binary answer at 0.74 has confidence 0.48; calibration lives in the probabilities.

On the 25,000 test reviews Choice scored 96.47% in 22 minutes 24 seconds for $0.6492 (Noul 96.20%, $0.6345), above every trained model in the article, with no IMDb examples. The author names the catch: we don't know if the IMDb test set was in Jev's training data, and IMDb has been public since 2011. A repeat run differed slightly, and the Choice to Noul gap (67 reviews, 0.27 points) is within that noise. A fine-tuned ModernBERT matched it after 23 min to fine-tune on the training set, and can do nothing else. So the old rule (a cheap LLM for one-off decisions, fine-tune a custom classifier if we want to repeat them) gains a middle option, still beaten by a tuned specialist on a narrow high-volume task.

One comparison is attributed loosely. The article says Jev claims to be on par with GPT-5.6 Luna; TypeSafe's launch text names GPT-5.6 Terra as closest "at intelligence to Jev on average", and its workflow evaluations measure agreement with GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1, not correctness.

4. A Jev-like API on any backbone: one score per option

Section 4

The recipe gives the head a single output and runs it once per option. For each option the model reads the text, the instructions and that option's description; the head turns the final hidden state hi ([CLS] in an encoder, the last non-padding token in a decoder) into a score si = w·hi + b, and a softmax across the options gives the probabilities. The same w and b score every option, so options can be added or reworded without changing the model; backbone and head train together with cross-entropy. It is a cross-encoder reranker's shape (Rerankers). The author built one on ModernBERT and kept it: trivial to put a Jev-like API on a model, not to make it work on unfamiliar tasks.

Two consequences go unstated. It costs one forward pass per option (up to 255 at Jev's limit), which two readers raised. And it obeys the independence of irrelevant alternatives: each score ignores the others, so the odds between two options stay es1−s2 whatever is added (the figure).

Jev does not behave like that. Twelve days before the article, Archer Hume added an irrelevant fifth option to a four-way question and the log-odds between two existing options fell from +0.38 to +0.11, lower in all ten blocks; TypeSafe's own docs warn that Choice answers lean toward the first option. Jev reads a question's options together. The recipe still works for a clone; it is not a description of Jev.

5. Guessing Jev: a small encoder, synthetic data, a calibration reward

Section 5

The architecture guess is contradicted by what was known. The author guesses using something small similar to ModernBERT, to explain the low latency. TypeSafe's FAQ says "Jev is neither small nor an LLM", and Hume measured 84.6% on MMLU-Pro, which he reads as frontier-scale pretraining. Latency fits a large model too: Jev only reads, with no token-by-token decoding. On data the article is on firmer ground: 100% of our data is synthetic matches the launch post, and his anecdote (hand-labelling 300 more examples resulted in a >10-20% accuracy boost, where days of tuning gave 2 to 5) is the right lesson for a clone builder.

RLCD is unpublished, so the article explains a published cousin, RLCR, with no officially established connection between the two. RLVR rewards a right answer 1 and a wrong one 0, so guessing pays. RLCR (Damani et al., MIT, 2025) has the model state a confidence q after its answer and rewards R = c − (q − c)²: correctness minus the Brier penalty. Wrong at 0.9 earns −0.81, wrong at 0.2 earns −0.04, right at 0.9 earns 0.99. Because the Brier score is proper, honesty maximises the expected reward: a model right 70% of the time earns 0.49 by saying 0.7, 0.45 by saying 0.9 and 0.40 by saying 1.0 (the figure). With Qwen2.5-7B on HotpotQA it cut ECE from 0.37 to 0.03; on six other datasets from 0.46 to 0.21, with accuracy 53.9% to 56.2% (Table 1(a), checked). The paper's baselines are RLVR and post-hoc classifiers, not over regular RLVR with temperature scaling.

For a model that writes no text, the author drops the RL and adds a Brier term to cross-entropy: on his ModernBERT, a modest improvement over regular temperature scaling across nine datasets, worse on one (AG News, a note a reader prompted). His analogy names since Direct Policy Optimization (DPO) is a cross-entropy alternative to RLHF: it is Direct Preference Optimization (Alignment).

6. Is calibration needed if you train with cross-entropy?

Section 5.3

In expectation no, in practice yes, in a section added on publication day after a reader asked. Cross-entropy is proper, so a model that reached the true probabilities would be calibrated. A real network keeps lowering its training loss by growing more confident, so networks overfit to NLL without overfitting to the 0/1 loss (Guo et al.). Hence the article's conclusion: a Brier term adds little in supervised training and matters in RL, where nothing else scores the stated probability. A reader added what it leaves out: one fitted temperature does not survive a shift in inputs, which is where an agent's decisions go.

7. Who it is for, the clones, and OpenAI's answer

Section 6

The use the article cares about is inside agent harnesses: a pre-screener for regular LLM-agent harnesses looking for prompt injection, choosing a reasoning effort or a skill, judging. One measurement cuts against the screener: one ordinary sentence inside a complaint moved 55% of Jev's routing decisions (responses), so the screen can be steered by what it screens (Guardrails; decision models as judges on LLM-as-judge).

The clones fall short on breadth, and the comparison the article cites hides a calibration failure. Contrastive Language Models scored its IMDb test accuracy is just 82.90%, Laya 92.33%, and both failed his Tetris test (a reader noted Jev's launch demos played games, so the test favours Jev's training data). For GLiNER the article says based on a benchmark, Jev is definitely stronger; that benchmark, 100 examples per dataset, also found Jev tied on emotion with far worse probabilities, ECE 0.351 against 0.117. For an article about calibrated decisions, that is the result to report. Its one reported weak spot, legal reviews in non-English languages, is a reader's anecdote.

The conclusion holds: nothing in Jev is new as an idea, its breadth is, and the bar to justify fine-tuning a specialized model is higher. The same day OpenAI announced a Jev-like Decisions API on GPT-6 Luna (Update 1).

How much of this to believe

The history and the mechanics are right wherever we checked them. The accuracies match the linked notebooks, ULMFiT's 95.4% is its paper's 4.6% error, the Jev run is internally consistent (24,117 of 25,000; 15,456,663 tokens at $0.042 per million is $0.6492; the 14-token gap per review between Choice and Noul matches the example responses), and the RLCR rewards and Table 1(a) values are exact (closed block below). The author marks his guesses and the contamination risk, and changed the post within three days in answer to four reader comments.

  • The ladder mixes three test sets and single runs: one or two points between rows say little.
  • The Jev-like design and the "small ModernBERT" guess are not Jev. Measurements published twelve days earlier show Jev reads options jointly and has frontier-scale knowledge; TypeSafe says "neither small nor an LLM". The article does not cite them.
  • Calibration is the theme and is not tested on Jev. The benchmark it links shows Jev's ECE at 0.351 on emotion, worse than the open model; a later test found Jev at 1.00 on 58% of complaints while right on 90.2% of those. Hume found ECE 0.031 on 1,200 MMLU items: calibrated on some tasks, not all.
  • Small slips: Luna where TypeSafe names Terra; "Direct Policy Optimization"; temperature scaling put in the RLCR comparison; state-space models "processed sequentially" in training.

Verdict. The best single walk from bag-of-words to decision models, with a cheap, honest test of Jev on one task. Take its history and its RLCR explanation as written, its Jev-like recipe as a way to build a clone, and "calibrated" as the vendor's word until a reliability diagram on your own task says so.

4. A Jev-like API on any backbone: one score per option

[[ah:§4-bert-and-gpt-style-models-with-jev-api|Section 4]]

The recipe gives the head a single output and runs it once per option. For each option the model reads the text, the instructions and that option's description; the head turns the final hidden state hi ([CLS] in an encoder, the last non-padding token in a decoder) into a score si = w·hi + b, and a softmax across the options gives the probabilities. The same w and b score every option, so options can be added or reworded without changing the model; backbone and head train together with cross-entropy. It is a cross-encoder reranker's shape ({{Rerankers|n:3f85c17b0d0d813fba53fc883f8a2c8f}}). The author built one on ModernBERT and kept it: {{trivial to put a Jev-like API|ar:trivial to put a Jev-like API}} on a model, not to make it work on unfamiliar tasks.

Two consequences go unstated. It costs one forward pass per option (up to 255 at Jev's limit), which two readers raised. And it obeys the independence of irrelevant alternatives: each score ignores the others, so the odds between two options stay es1−s2 whatever is added (the figure).

Jev does not behave like that. Twelve days before the article, Archer Hume added an irrelevant fifth option to a four-way question and the log-odds between two existing options fell from +0.38 to +0.11, lower in all ten blocks; TypeSafe's own docs warn that Choice answers lean toward the first option. Jev reads a question's options together. The recipe still works for a clone; it is not a description of Jev.

5. Guessing Jev: a small encoder, synthetic data, a calibration reward

[[ah:§5-jev-architecture-and-training-algorithm|Section 5]]

The architecture guess is contradicted by what was known. The author guesses {{using something small similar to ModernBERT|ar:using something small similar to ModernBERT}}, to explain the low latency. TypeSafe's FAQ says "Jev is neither small nor an LLM", and Hume measured 84.6% on MMLU-Pro, which he reads as frontier-scale pretraining. Latency fits a large model too: Jev only reads, with no token-by-token decoding. On data the article is on firmer ground: {{100% of our data is synthetic|ar:100% of our data is synthetic}} matches the launch post, and his anecdote (hand-labelling 300 more examples {{resulted in a >10-20% accuracy boost|ar:resulted in a >10-20% accuracy boost}}, where days of tuning gave 2 to 5) is the right lesson for a clone builder.

RLCD is unpublished, so the article explains a published cousin, RLCR, with {{no officially established connection between the two|ar:no officially established connection between the two}}. RLVR rewards a right answer 1 and a wrong one 0, so guessing pays. RLCR (Damani et al., MIT, 2025) has the model state a confidence q after its answer and rewards R = c − (q − c)²: correctness minus the Brier penalty. Wrong at 0.9 earns −0.81, wrong at 0.2 earns −0.04, right at 0.9 earns 0.99. Because the Brier score is proper, honesty maximises the expected reward: a model right 70% of the time earns 0.49 by saying 0.7, 0.45 by saying 0.9 and 0.40 by saying 1.0 (the figure). With Qwen2.5-7B on HotpotQA it cut ECE from 0.37 to 0.03; on six other datasets from 0.46 to 0.21, with accuracy 53.9% to 56.2% (Table 1(a), checked). The paper's baselines are RLVR and post-hoc classifiers, not {{over regular RLVR with temperature scaling|ar:over regular RLVR with temperature scaling}}.

For a model that writes no text, the author drops the RL and adds a Brier term to cross-entropy: on his ModernBERT, {{a modest improvement over regular temperature scaling|ar:a modest improvement over regular temperature scaling}} across nine datasets, worse on one (AG News, a note a reader prompted). His analogy names {{since Direct Policy Optimization (DPO) is|ar:since Direct Policy Optimization (DPO) is}} a cross-entropy alternative to RLHF: it is Direct Preference Optimization ({{Alignment|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d5bed7e608d4061c7a}}).

6. Is calibration needed if you train with cross-entropy?

[[ah:§53-side-note-is-calibration-necessary|Section 5.3]]

In expectation no, in practice yes, in a section added on publication day after a reader asked. Cross-entropy is proper, so a model that reached the true probabilities would be calibrated. A real network keeps lowering its training loss by growing more confident, so networks {{overfit to NLL without overfitting to the|ar:overfit to NLL without overfitting to the}} 0/1 loss (Guo et al.). Hence the article's conclusion: a Brier term adds little in supervised training and matters in RL, where nothing else scores the stated probability. A reader added what it leaves out: one fitted temperature does not survive a shift in inputs, which is where an agent's decisions go.

7. Who it is for, the clones, and OpenAI's answer

[[ah:§6-who-is-jev-for|Section 6]]

The use the article cares about is inside agent harnesses: {{a pre-screener for regular LLM-agent harnesses|ar:a pre-screener for regular LLM-agent harnesses}} looking for prompt injection, choosing a reasoning effort or a skill, judging. One measurement cuts against the screener: one ordinary sentence inside a complaint moved 55% of Jev's routing decisions (responses), so the screen can be steered by what it screens ({{Guardrails|n:3c65c17b0d0d819bbfa5dffa9246c297}}; decision models as judges on {{LLM-as-judge|n:3c65c17b0d0d810ba2a2ecc08c4c233b}}).

The clones fall short on breadth, and the comparison the article cites hides a calibration failure. Contrastive Language Models scored {{its IMDb test accuracy is just 82.90%|ar:its IMDb test accuracy is just 82.90%}}, Laya 92.33%, and both failed his Tetris test (a reader noted Jev's launch demos played games, so the test favours Jev's training data). For GLiNER the article says {{based on a benchmark, Jev is definitely stronger|ar:based on a benchmark, Jev is definitely stronger}}; that benchmark, 100 examples per dataset, also found Jev tied on emotion with far worse probabilities, ECE 0.351 against 0.117. For an article about calibrated decisions, that is the result to report. Its one reported weak spot, {{legal reviews in non-English languages|ar:legal reviews in non-English languages}}, is a reader's anecdote.

The conclusion holds: nothing in Jev is new as an idea, its breadth is, and {{the bar to justify fine-tuning a specialized|ar:the bar to justify fine-tuning a specialized}} model is higher. The same day OpenAI announced a Jev-like Decisions API on GPT-6 Luna (Update 1).

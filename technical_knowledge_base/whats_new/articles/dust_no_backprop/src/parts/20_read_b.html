Why per token: fewer noise dimensions behind each reward

[[ah:activation-space-perturbation|2.1 Activation-Space Perturbation]]

A zeroth-order estimate degrades with the number of noise dimensions behind each scalar reward. Weight-space ES perturbs all dout×din weights for one number per pass; EGGROLL makes that cheap, but each member is still one sequence ({{in the article|ar:the population is bounded by the forward passes one can afford}}). Activation noise alone does not fix it: on a sequence it is a T×dout tensor, as large as the weights once T ≥ din ({{in the article|ar:Naively, the dimensionality argument doesn't hold}}). What fixes it is rewarding each token by its own loss change: then only dout dimensions sit behind each reward, and a 2,048-token sequence is 2,048 trials, the post's "virtual population" ({{in the article|ar:We call this a virtual population.}}).

The toy beside this runs all three on one small layer, the two activation variants on identical noise so only the credit differs.

Predict before you move the slider: at 64 forward passes, which estimate points closest to the exact gradient, and which is worst?

Per-token credit, by far (cosine 0.82 at width 32, mean of 8 seeds); weight noise second (0.33); activation noise with one batch reward worst (0.19), since it hides 64×32 = 2,048 dimensions behind each reward, more than the layer's 512 weights: the post's "naive" case.

Each curve follows the law the post fits to its transformer, cos(K) = cmax/√(1 + c/K), with c close to the noise dimensions behind each reward: dout, din×dout or T×dout. So per-token credit needs about 18 times fewer forward passes than weight noise here, at every width (din is 16): the factors Werfel, Xie and Seung derived for linear networks, node perturbation slower than gradient descent by the number of outputs and weight perturbation slower again by the number of inputs ({{Werfel et al. 2005|https://doi.org/10.1162/089976605774320539}}). The toy lands on them independently, from random weights and data.

What an update costs: thousands of forward passes

[[ah:interference-and-tuning|2.3 Interference and Tuning]]

At an affordable population the problem is interference: every jitter in a pass moves every token's loss, so each reward also carries everyone else's noise ({{in the article|ar:the main cost is interference}}). So each layer type and block gets its own passes, reusing the cached clean forward (a draw at block l reruns blocks l onward); attention internals are scored by how they move the block's attention output against its estimated error; the head is jittered on cached logits. Noise scales, credit decay (γ 0.98 to 0.99 for keys, values, gates and value embeddings, 0 elsewhere) and the draw split were tuned once and largely carried across budgets ({{in the article|ar:the tuning is mostly a one-time cost}}).

Counting compute from Appendix E's allocation gives about 14,700 forward passes' worth per update at 16,384 draws, against about three for backprop: a draw averages 0.89 of a forward pass, because the draws concentrate on the first blocks. That is about 19 times backprop's compute at 64 draws and 4,900 times at 16,384. The post reports no FLOPs or times; the lab's later benchmark timed the 10M-token run at 1 h 19 min on eight H100s, where backprop needs about 2×1015 FLOP, seconds on one GPU.

How the compute was counted

Multiply-adds per token of the base model (Appendix A: 8 blocks of width 512, MLP 2,048, vocabulary 4,096, 2,048 tokens, a 1,024-token window in three blocks of four): 3.93M per windowed block, 4.19M per full-attention block, 2.10M for the head, 34.1M for a forward pass. A draw at block l reruns blocks l to 8 and the head; an attention-internal draw reruns only its block's attention; a token-embedding draw reruns everything. With Table 4's draws per block at 1M and 10M tokens (writers and MLP hidden layer 2,432, 2,048, 1,024, 640, 384, 256, 128, 128 each; attention outputs 384, 384, 256, then 128) and Table 3's counts, one update at K = 16,384 costs 14,659 forward-equivalents. The head's 65,536 slab draws on cached logits are left out (under 0.1% even if each rescored the full vocabulary). Backprop: 3 forward-equivalents (a backward pass costs about two forwards). Ratio: 4,886. Smaller populations scale every count by K/16,384. A calculation in src/recompute.py, not a measurement; it ignores that reruns are batched (more efficient than a lone forward) and the clean forward per GPU. The Noise timing is consistent with it: 14,659 × 610 updates × 16,384 tokens × 68M FLOP is about 1.0×1019 FLOP, which in 1 h 19 min on eight H100s is about 30% of their dense bf16 peak.

Results: below backprop over the first 61 updates, behind it after

[[ah:main-results|3.2 Main Results]]

The setup: a 37.7M-parameter GPT (8 layers, width 512) on FineWeb, batch 16,384 tokens, one epoch, SGD with momentum at a constant learning rate, three seeds per cell, every method tuned at every budget ({{in the article|ar:SGD with momentum at a constant learning rate}}). The budgets, 100k to 20M tokens, are 6 to 1,221 updates.

At 100k and 1M tokens Dust ends below backprop (7.170 against 7.216, 5.916 against 5.959, at 16,384 draws); at 10M and 20M above (5.049 against 4.989, 4.802 against 4.633). At 20M the post reads a power-law limit, 4.431 (95% interval 3.89 to 4.58), as evidence the gap keeps closing, while calling the fit loosely constrained ({{in the article|ar:we read it as evidence that the gap keeps closing}}).

Under Adam, at 1M tokens only, Dust stays just above backprop (5.368 at 65,536 draws against 5.361); only the fitted limit, 5.248, is below ({{in the article|ar:Dust closes on backprop with a large population}}). Adam improves backprop by 0.60 there, so the SGD baseline is far from backprop's best. The lab's benchmark a week later: Dust 0.05 behind with SGD at 10M tokens, 0.40 behind with Adam.

"103 to 104 times more efficient" is an extrapolated population ratio

[[ah:main-results|3.2 Main Results]]

It compares populations at equal loss, and it is extrapolated. The authors' EGGROLL-Transformer at 16,384 passes never reaches Dust at 64 draws (within 0.02 at 100k tokens, 0.4 to 0.6 behind beyond; {{in the article|ar:With 256 times the population}}); extending its still-falling curve with three kinds of fit gives 3,000 to 23,000 times 64, and one leave-one-out refit never matches at 1M (Appendix D). Per member the comparison favours EGGROLL, since from 256 draws up a Dust draw costs less than its forward pass ({{in the article|ar:so the comparison is lenient to EGGROLL}}).

The size of the ratio is what theory predicts. Node perturbation beats full-rank weight noise by about din per layer, here 512 to 2,048, so a ratio in the thousands confirms that argument at transformer scale more than it reveals a new effect (low-rank noise changes the constant, not the order). It is also against the authors' own port of EGGROLL, not the EGGROLL paper's code.

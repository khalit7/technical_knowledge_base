Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation

Samip Dahal, Bishwas Mandal, Serdar Gülbahar, Akshay Vegesna (Q Labs) · published 5 October 2026

In one line: A research post with code: Dust adds independent noise to each layer's activations at every token, credits each token's noise with that token's own loss change, and forms the weight update as backprop does (estimated error times input); on a 37.7M-parameter GPT trained with constant-rate SGD (three seeds per cell), 16,384 draws per update end slightly below backprop at 100k and 1M tokens (6 and 61 updates) and 0.06 and 0.17 above it at 10M and 20M, at about 5,000 times backprop's compute per update by our count, while the authors' weight-space ES baseline would need an extrapolated 3,000 to 23,000 times the population to match Dust's smallest setting.

The idea in one sentence: add independent noise to a layer's outputs at every token, score each token's noise by the change in that token's loss, and average over many noisy forward passes: this estimates the error backprop would deliver to the layer, the update is formed as backprop forms it (error times input), and since every token is a separate trial, one pass runs thousands of trials where weight-space evolution strategies run one.

You need: that a layer's weight gradient is the error arriving at its output times its stored input, an outer product, and that a backward pass costs about two forward passes (Matrix calculus and backprop, sections 5 and 8); SGD with momentum and Adam (Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers); a cosine between two vectors. Helpful, not required: the score-function estimator, reward times noise (Policy gradients and actor-critic).

New terms: zeroth-order method (uses loss values only, never derivatives); evolution strategies, ES (perturb the weights at random; the reward-weighted average perturbation estimates the gradient); node perturbation (perturb a layer's outputs instead of its weights); draw (one forward pass in which every token gets its own noise); population K (draws per update); EGGROLL (a recent weight-space ES method with cheap low-rank perturbations, the baseline); cosine to backprop (1: the exact gradient's direction; 0: no better than chance).

The bet: search may beat gradients once compute is plentiful

1 Introduction

The post argues that backprop and differentiability help while compute is scarce and may limit learning once it is plentiful, citing the bitter lesson and AlphaGo Zero (in the article). It does not try to be compute-efficient, nor to train the models it would enable, such as long-looped transformers (in the article). So every "competitive with backprop" below is at equal tokens, not equal compute.

The estimator: reward-weighted noise is the layer's output error

2.2 Credit Assignment

For a linear layer yt = W xt, Dust adds Gaussian noise σat to the output at every token and runs the forward pass (in the article). Token t's reward is its centred loss reduction ct (the mean perturbed loss there across draws, minus this draw's), plus, with a decay γ, the reductions at later tokens its jitter reaches through attention:

rt = Σs≥t γs−t cs,   ĝt = −(1/Kσ) Σi rt(i) at(i),   ĜW = Σt ĝt xt⊤

The middle term is the idea: the reward-weighted noise, averaged over K draws, points along the error at the layer's output (Equations 1 to 3); it is REINFORCE for a Gaussian policy with a baseline (Policy gradients and actor-critic). The last term is backprop's outer product with the same cached input: "The only difference is that it gets the output error from the chain rule and we get it from the population" (in the article). Each draw's error has no preferred direction, so the signal grows as K and the error as √K (in the article); the table beside this shows one token converging.

Why per token: fewer noise dimensions behind each reward

2.1 Activation-Space Perturbation

A zeroth-order estimate degrades with the number of noise dimensions behind each scalar reward. Weight-space ES perturbs all dout×din weights for one number per pass; EGGROLL makes that cheap, but each member is still one sequence (in the article). Activation noise alone does not fix it: on a sequence it is a T×dout tensor, as large as the weights once T ≥ din (in the article). What fixes it is rewarding each token by its own loss change: then only dout dimensions sit behind each reward, and a 2,048-token sequence is 2,048 trials, the post's "virtual population" (in the article).

The toy beside this runs all three on one small layer, the two activation variants on identical noise so only the credit differs.

Predict before you move the slider: at 64 forward passes, which estimate points closest to the exact gradient, and which is worst?

Per-token credit, by far (cosine 0.82 at width 32, mean of 8 seeds); weight noise second (0.33); activation noise with one batch reward worst (0.19), since it hides 64×32 = 2,048 dimensions behind each reward, more than the layer's 512 weights: the post's "naive" case.

Each curve follows the law the post fits to its transformer, cos(K) = cmax/√(1 + c/K), with c close to the noise dimensions behind each reward: dout, din×dout or T×dout. So per-token credit needs about 18 times fewer forward passes than weight noise here, at every width (din is 16): the factors Werfel, Xie and Seung derived for linear networks, node perturbation slower than gradient descent by the number of outputs and weight perturbation slower again by the number of inputs (Werfel et al. 2005). The toy lands on them independently, from random weights and data.

What an update costs: thousands of forward passes

2.3 Interference and Tuning

At an affordable population the problem is interference: every jitter in a pass moves every token's loss, so each reward also carries everyone else's noise (in the article). So each layer type and block gets its own passes, reusing the cached clean forward (a draw at block l reruns blocks l onward); attention internals are scored by how they move the block's attention output against its estimated error; the head is jittered on cached logits. Noise scales, credit decay (γ 0.98 to 0.99 for keys, values, gates and value embeddings, 0 elsewhere) and the draw split were tuned once and largely carried across budgets (in the article).

Counting compute from Appendix E's allocation gives about 14,700 forward passes' worth per update at 16,384 draws, against about three for backprop: a draw averages 0.89 of a forward pass, because the draws concentrate on the first blocks. That is about 19 times backprop's compute at 64 draws and 4,900 times at 16,384. The post reports no FLOPs or times; the lab's later benchmark timed the 10M-token run at 1 h 19 min on eight H100s, where backprop needs about 2×1015 FLOP, seconds on one GPU.

How the compute was counted

Multiply-adds per token of the base model (Appendix A: 8 blocks of width 512, MLP 2,048, vocabulary 4,096, 2,048 tokens, a 1,024-token window in three blocks of four): 3.93M per windowed block, 4.19M per full-attention block, 2.10M for the head, 34.1M for a forward pass. A draw at block l reruns blocks l to 8 and the head; an attention-internal draw reruns only its block's attention; a token-embedding draw reruns everything. With Table 4's draws per block at 1M and 10M tokens (writers and MLP hidden layer 2,432, 2,048, 1,024, 640, 384, 256, 128, 128 each; attention outputs 384, 384, 256, then 128) and Table 3's counts, one update at K = 16,384 costs 14,659 forward-equivalents. The head's 65,536 slab draws on cached logits are left out (under 0.1% even if each rescored the full vocabulary). Backprop: 3 forward-equivalents (a backward pass costs about two forwards). Ratio: 4,886. Smaller populations scale every count by K/16,384. A calculation in src/recompute.py, not a measurement; it ignores that reruns are batched (more efficient than a lone forward) and the clean forward per GPU. The Noise timing is consistent with it: 14,659 × 610 updates × 16,384 tokens × 68M FLOP is about 1.0×1019 FLOP, which in 1 h 19 min on eight H100s is about 30% of their dense bf16 peak.

Results: below backprop over the first 61 updates, behind it after

3.2 Main Results

The setup: a 37.7M-parameter GPT (8 layers, width 512) on FineWeb, batch 16,384 tokens, one epoch, SGD with momentum at a constant learning rate, three seeds per cell, every method tuned at every budget (in the article). The budgets, 100k to 20M tokens, are 6 to 1,221 updates.

At 100k and 1M tokens Dust ends below backprop (7.170 against 7.216, 5.916 against 5.959, at 16,384 draws); at 10M and 20M above (5.049 against 4.989, 4.802 against 4.633). At 20M the post reads a power-law limit, 4.431 (95% interval 3.89 to 4.58), as evidence the gap keeps closing, while calling the fit loosely constrained (in the article).

Under Adam, at 1M tokens only, Dust stays just above backprop (5.368 at 65,536 draws against 5.361); only the fitted limit, 5.248, is below (in the article). Adam improves backprop by 0.60 there, so the SGD baseline is far from backprop's best. The lab's benchmark a week later: Dust 0.05 behind with SGD at 10M tokens, 0.40 behind with Adam.

"103 to 104 times more efficient" is an extrapolated population ratio

3.2 Main Results

It compares populations at equal loss, and it is extrapolated. The authors' EGGROLL-Transformer at 16,384 passes never reaches Dust at 64 draws (within 0.02 at 100k tokens, 0.4 to 0.6 behind beyond; in the article); extending its still-falling curve with three kinds of fit gives 3,000 to 23,000 times 64, and one leave-one-out refit never matches at 1M (Appendix D). Per member the comparison favours EGGROLL, since from 256 draws up a Dust draw costs less than its forward pass (in the article).

The size of the ratio is what theory predicts. Node perturbation beats full-rank weight noise by about din per layer, here 512 to 2,048, so a ratio in the thousands confirms that argument at transformer scale more than it reveals a new effect (low-rank noise changes the constant, not the order). It is also against the authors' own port of EGGROLL, not the EGGROLL paper's code.

Model size: bigger Dust models reach lower loss, but fall further behind backprop

4.1 Overparameterization

Zeroth-order methods are expected to fail on large networks, since one reward per pass must cover more perturbed dimensions (in the article). The post trains four sizes, 2.0M to 243M parameters, at 10M tokens. Among Dust runs bigger is usually better: from 256 draws up the loss falls from 2M to 38M parameters, 243M is slightly worse, and past 1k draws the larger models gain about 30% more from population (in the article).

Against backprop at the same size the picture reverses, which the post mentions in one sentence (in the article): at every population the largest model trails its backprop most (0.67 at 64 draws, 0.04 at 16k; the smallest 0.53 and −0.01). And 10M tokens starves 243M parameters even under backprop (5.048, worse than 38M's 5.015), so this cannot show how Dust does where a large model pays off. Per-token noise grows with width, not parameter count, which may keep the decline gentle. These runs used an earlier protocol (Appendix F).

Backprop-like gradients: cosines that rise with population, measured on backprop's checkpoints

4.2 Emergence of Backprop-Like Gradients

The post measures the cosine between Dust's estimate and the exact gradient, per layer type, at populations 64 to 128k, on checkpoints backprop trained to 10M, 100M and 1B tokens, and fits cos(K) = cmax/√(1 + c/K) to each (RMSE 0.003 to 0.037 at 100M; in the article). The curves barely move from 10M to 1B tokens, which it calls encouraging for scaling. EGGROLL's estimate stays below 0.05 at 128k for every type but the head.

At the training population the cosines are moderate (beside this): about 0.5 for MLP and attention outputs and the token embedding, 0.16 for keys, 0.98 for the head. Several ceilings sit below 1 (0.67 for value gates): those estimates are biased, consistent with interference and decayed credit. The post calls this a good property, a different trajectory that "can even be better" (in the article); the evidence for better is the 100k and 1M results. And "up to 1B tokens" is a measurement on backprop's weights; Dust's longest run is 20M tokens.

How much of this to believe

What holds up:

  • The mechanism is simple, standard and public (MIT code). The toy here reproduces its core argument independently: per-token credit needs about din times fewer passes than weight noise.
  • The lead over weight-space ES is measured: EGGROLL-Transformer at 16k passes never reaches Dust at 64 draws from 1M tokens up; only the size of the multiple is extrapolated.
  • The protocol is careful for a blog post: three seeds per cell, every cell tuned on a stated grid with stated rejection rules, backprop on the same grid (Appendix F). The authors call the 20M limit loosely constrained and say Dust needs "orders of magnitude more compute efficiency". Both count in their favour.

Where it is weaker:

  • "Competitive with backprop" rests on short runs against constant-rate SGD. The measured wins are at 6 and 61 updates; under Adam no measured point is below backprop, and the lab's own benchmark puts Dust 0.40 behind at 10M tokens. A challenger's gain shrinking against a stronger baseline is the familiar pattern (Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers).
  • The trend runs the wrong way. At 16k draws the gap goes from −0.04 to +0.17 between 61 and 1,221 updates. The one sub-backprop number at 20M is a limit through a still-falling ladder whose top cell alone had an extra tuning campaign (Appendix F).
  • Compute is never reported. By our count an update costs 19 to 4,900 backprop updates, and the wins are at 4,900. At equal compute backprop is not close.
  • The scale is small: 37.7M parameters and 20M tokens at most; 243M only at 10M tokens, data-starved, with the largest gap.
  • Little is checkable from the release: the repository is a minimal version for two budgets, with no logs, results, EGGROLL port, Adam runs or cosine code.

VerdictBelieve that per-token node perturbation gives far better gradient estimates per forward pass than weight-space ES, and that with tens of thousands of draws it trains a small transformer about as well as constant-rate SGD backprop over a short run. Do not read it as matching backprop in general: the gap opens with training and with Adam, an update costs thousands of backprop updates, and nothing here exceeds 243M parameters or 20M training tokens.

Hacker News: 280 points and 82 comments (read 27 November 2026), mostly on whether derivative-free optimisation can matter and on the brain. No independent replication or measured rebuttal was found by 27 November; the repository's two open issues are questions.

  • discussionHacker News: the gradient tells you where to go
    blt and okintheory, Hacker News, 6 October 2026

    The top comment bets no derivative-free optimiser will matter for neural networks: their objectives are smooth, and the gradient's edge over random directions grows with the parameter count.   • discussionHacker News: is this a bitter-lesson method at all?
    syntacticsalt, Hacker News, 6 October 2026

    Argues that zeroth-order estimates smooth the loss without addressing non-convexity, that the post never discusses convexity, and that better first-order methods will keep the lead wherever gradients exist.

Where it moves the knowledge base

  • Matrix calculus and backprop says reverse mode wins because its cost does not grow with the number of weights. It gains the third option this post scales up: estimate each layer's output error from forward passes (node perturbation, per-token credit) and keep the outer product; the cost grows with the population instead, about 0.3K backprop updates here, and Werfel et al.'s factors give the scaling.
  • Optimisers and learning-rate schedulers gains a clean case for its evidence section: a method level with SGD backprop at 10M tokens (+0.05) and 0.40 behind once both use Adam (Noise benchmark).
  • Policy gradients and actor-critic can note that the score-function estimator with a baseline, applied per token to a layer's activations, is how Dust trains a transformer without a backward pass.

What it rests on

Evolution strategies at the hyperscale (EGGROLL)

Sarkar et al., arXiv 2511.16652: The weight-space baseline: low-rank weight perturbations that make large populations cheap on GPUs. The post re-implements it on its own transformer (EGGROLL-Transformer) rather than running the paper's code.

(40 min)

Learning curves for stochastic gradient descent in linear feedforward networks

Werfel, Xie and Seung, NeurIPS 2003: Node perturbation against weight perturbation and backprop on linear networks: the journal version (Neural Computation, 2005) states that node perturbation learns slower than gradient descent by a factor of the number of output units, and weight perturbation slower again by the number of input units; the toy on this page lands on both factors.

(30 min)

Evolution strategies as a scalable alternative to reinforcement learning

Salimans, Ho, Chen, Sidor and Sutskever, arXiv 1703.03864: The modern weight-space ES recipe (antithetic Gaussian perturbations, fitness shaping, a shared seed so workers exchange only scalars) that EGGROLL scales up.

(25 min)

Fine-tuning language models with just forward passes (MeZO)

Malladi et al., NeurIPS 2023: The best-known zeroth-order result for language models: fine-tuning, not pretraining, with one random weight direction per step and memory close to inference.

(35 min)

Scaling forward gradient with local losses

Ren, Kornblith, Liao and Hinton, ICLR 2023: Activation perturbation with forward-mode derivatives and local losses to cut the estimator's variance; the closest earlier attempt to make perturbation scale with width.

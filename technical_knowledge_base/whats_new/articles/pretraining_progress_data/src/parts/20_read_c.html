5. The authors' reading: recipes made scale possible; data may matter less at scale

[[ar:making larger amounts of compute usable in the first place|Discussion]]

The authors argue against the naive reading of their headline: model research mostly made large compute usable at all (stable norms, mixture of experts, FlashAttention), which cannot pay at 1019 FLOPs ({{in the article|ar:making larger amounts of compute usable in the first place}}; their sailboat against container ship). And filtering may matter less for large, overtrained models ({{in the article|ar:might matter less for larger models}}), citing Mohri, Duchi and Hashimoto, whose crossover Colin Raffel puts near 1030 FLOPs. Both are arguments; nothing large was trained.

The open questions the authors list

If progress is loading more cargo, the stock of web text is the limit, and whether synthetic data extends it was not tested ({{in the article|ar:are we running out of cargo}}; {{Synthetic data|n:3c65c17b0d0d8171b5d9e36e05b7a5d0}} and {{Data mixing|n:3c65c17b0d0d819ea5d6c59471e66875}} own repetition and synthetic tokens). Data ablations are the experiments automated researchers could run, so data progress may speed up once AI research is automated, a point from Ryan Greenblatt ({{in the article|ar:automated researchers would be able to just test empirically}}). And pretraining alone matters less now that much recent progress comes from RL ({{in the article|ar:so many of the gains over the last two years have come from RL}}).

6. Against Epoch's 3x a year: a different quantity

[[ar:Inference efficiency optimizations|the appendix]]

The study's 1.57x a year is far below the roughly 3x usually quoted, and measures something else. The cited "3x per year (95% CI: 1.5x to 64x)" ({{in the article|ar:much lower than Anson Ho et al.}}) is Epoch's recalculation; Ho et al. 2024 give an 8.4-month halving (95% CI 4.5 to 14.3), 2.7x a year (derived; 1.8x to 6.3x): about 380x over six years against 15x here, measured in perplexity on published models at their own scales.

Both estimates include data, so this shows not that Ho's figure was mostly data, but that at small scale data is most of what remains.

How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • The numbers are published and reproduce to the printed digit from the charts' data: 3.7x, 12.0x, 15x, the recipe table, R2 and residuals.
  • The design isolates one ingredient at a time, curves average at least three seeds, and the authors state most limits themselves: small scale, one benchmark, one entry per year, limited tuning ({{in the article|ar:we expect the actual uncertainty in the compute multipliers}}), curations of one Common Crawl rather than new data.
  • The headline gap is not noise. A bootstrap here (each corner-curve point redrawn from its mean with standard error = seed spread / √3, 4,000 draws) puts the recipe at 3.7x (68% range 3.0 to 4.6) and the corpus at 12.0x (9.6 to 14.9).

What it does not show.

  • Independence in compute. The same bootstrap puts the product of the single-axis gains at 3.0x the joint gain (95% range 1.8 to 4.4, above 1 in 99.9% of draws), and the recipe on the 2025 corpus at 1.25x (1.01 to 1.51). Quote 12.0x with "on the GPT-2 recipe", and 15x as the total.
  • One seed per cell cannot exclude a small interaction in points, and the corpus table does not reproduce from its own chart (section 3).
  • Benchmark effects on the data side. Newer corpora are filtered towards educational and question-answer text, may contain benchmark items, and were built while watching tasks OLMES aggregates (Soldaini, Bakouch, below); held-out loss was used across recipes but not across corpora. Contamination's effect on scores: {{Topic: benchmarks|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fb43fece56e40041a}}.
  • Range of recipes and release. Only dense transformers, nothing above 1019 FLOPs, and no code, checkpoints or per-run tables.

Verdict. Believe the direction at this scale and on this benchmark: the newer corpus bought more than the newer recipe, by a wide, reproducible margin. Never quote a single-axis multiplier without its baseline: together the two gave 15x, and the recipe adds little on a modern corpus. Extrapolation to frontier models, either way, is argued, not measured.

Pretraining progress is mostly coming from data

Dwarkesh Patel, Jerry Han (Dwarkesh Podcast) · published 8 September 2026

In one line: Training seven yearly open recipes (GPT-2 in 2019 to OLMo-2 in 2025) on seven yearly open corpora (OpenWebText to Ultra-FineWeb) at up to 1e19 FLOPs and scoring on OLMES (ten mostly multiple-choice tasks, at least three seeds per curve), the 2025 corpus matched the 2019 pair's score with 12.0x less compute and the 2025 recipe with 3.7x less; together they saved 15x, a third of the product, though score gains in a one-seed grid are 88% additive.

How far to trust it: Believe the direction at this scale and on this benchmark: the curves reproduce the headline exactly and the corpus explains most of the grid. Read "mostly independent" as true in score points only: in compute the gains overlap (15x together; the 2025 recipe is worth 1.25x on the 2025 corpus), and benchmark leakage or OLMES-tuned corpora could inflate the data side. The evidence, judged.

The idea in one sentence: train every year's open model recipe on every year's open corpus at small budgets, ask how much less compute each newer ingredient needs to match the 2019 pair's benchmark score, and the corpora from 2019 to 2025 turn out to be worth 12.0 times the compute and the recipes 3.7 times; together they are worth 15 times, not 45, because the two gains overlap.

You need: compute-optimal allocation and the 6ND cost of a run (Pretraining, section 3); what curation stages and quality classifiers do to a web corpus (Topic: data-curation-and-datasets, sections 1 and 5).

New terms: recipe (a year's published model and training setup: architecture, optimiser, initialisation, schedule, hyperparameters); compute multiplier (reference compute divided by the compute a candidate needs to reach the reference's score); OLMES (Ai2's evaluation suite: ten fairly easy tasks, mostly multiple choice; every score here is its average).

1. The experiment: seven recipes crossed with seven corpora

the opening

One open recipe and one public corpus stand for each year from 2019 to 2025, and every pairing is trained from scratch at 1017 to 1019 FLOPs (in the article): at most a 290M-parameter model on 6B tokens (derived, 6ND at 20 tokens a parameter).

Loss cannot compare corpora, so every score is OLMES (in the article): each scaling-curve point averages at least three seeds, the 7 × 7 grid of all pairings one seed per cell (in the article).

The training setup in detail

Shared by every run: GPT-2's BPE tokenizer (50,257 entries), 2,048-token context, 262,144-token batch (in the article). Budgets 1017, 3.16 × 1017, 1018, 3.16 × 1018 and 1019 FLOPs, counted as 6ND with N the non-embedding parameters (in the article). At each budget several sizes are trained and the one with the lowest held-out loss on its own corpus is kept (in the article; allocation is Pretraining, section 3). Peak learning rate is the one hyperparameter tuned: swept at five anchor points (three sizes, two tokens-per-parameter ratios) and fitted as a power law in both, except OLMo-2, which uses Ai2's published small-model settings (in the article).

2. A compute multiplier is a horizontal gap between two curves

the appendix

Take the 2019 pair's score at a reference budget, find where the candidate's curve first reaches it, and divide the two computes (in the article). Reading between budgets along a straight line in log compute (the article does not say how) reproduces its 3.7x, 12.0x and recipe table to the printed digit.

The answer depends on the reference. The newer recipe saves 3.7x at 1019, 1.6x at 1018, and 0.66x (it is worse) at 3.16 × 1017; the corpus gain reads 9.1x, 8.3x, 12.0x from 1018 up. The headline is the highest level measured.

Predict first: the recipe gain grows from 1.6x to 3.7x between the 1018 and 1019 references. At a frontier budget, 1025, larger or smaller than 3.7x?
Reveal

The data cannot say: nothing was trained above 1019. The authors expect larger, since the recipes' main value is making big runs work at all (section 5).

3. The headline: 12.0x from data, 3.7x from recipes

the result

From the 2019 pair, the 2025 corpus saves 12.0x compute and the 2025 recipe 3.7x, so data gave 3.24 times as much (in the article). Per year: 1.24x for recipes, 1.51x for data, and 1.57x for both at once, measured, not multiplied (in the article).

The year-by-year tables show when each gain arrived: recipes (on FineWeb-Edu) were done by 2021, corpora (with OLMo-2) jumped in 2024 with FineWeb-Edu, the first here filtered by a classifier trained on LLM grades. NeoX and the Pile dip below 1x (in the article).

The recipe table reproduces from its chart; the corpus table does not (FineWeb-Edu 4.80x and Ultra-FineWeb 5.40x from the curves against 8.63x and 8.66x printed). The article does not explain it; the headline comes from the four-corner chart, which reproduces exactly.

The two tables, recomputed

With the article's rule and linear interpolation in log compute, every recipe multiplier comes out to the printed digit (GPT-3 1.36, GPT-J 1.71, LLaMA 1.68, Llama 3 1.69, OLMo-2 1.82). The corpus curves give C4 1.40 (printed 1.37), OLM CC 2.39 (2.59), RefinedWeb 1.81x against 2.62, FineWeb-Edu 4.80 (8.63), Ultra-FineWeb 5.40 (8.66). NeoX and the Pile never reach the reference score, and their printed multipliers are extrapolated (in the article). Datawrapper's metadata dates the tables to 7 September and the curve charts' last edits to 8 September, so the tables may come from an earlier run or another estimator; the cause is unconfirmed. The per-year rates are the six-year roots of the four-corner multipliers (3.721/6 = 1.245, 12.021/6 = 1.51, 15.071/6 = 1.57), not of the tables.

4. "Mostly independent": true in score points, not in compute

the claim

The article's additive fit to the 7 × 7 grid reproduces exactly: R2 = 0.88 (in the article), the corpus explaining 81% of the variance and the recipe 7% (F = 3.5 on 6 and 36 degrees of freedom, p = 0.008), with residuals (0.70 points) near seed noise (0.60). In score points, a better corpus adds about the same to every recipe.

In compute, the gains overlap: together they are worth 15x, a third of their 45x product. The same points of score buy less compute on a steeper curve, and the better corpus steepens it (GPT-2 gains 1.4 points over the last half-decade on OpenWebText, 2.5 on Ultra-FineWeb). So OLMo-2's recipe is worth 3.7x on OpenWebText but 1.25x on Ultra-FineWeb (the hero); the article's "don't interact" misses this.

Split fairly, data takes 72% of the 15x and recipes 28%: averaging each upgrade's log gain over both orders (Shapley) gives the corpus 7.0x and the recipe 2.2x of the 15.1x (derived). The 3.24 ratio survives (12.0 / 3.7 = 4.05 / 1.25); single-axis multipliers need the other ingredient named.

5. The authors' reading: recipes made scale possible; data may matter less at scale

Discussion

The authors argue against the naive reading of their headline: model research mostly made large compute usable at all (stable norms, mixture of experts, FlashAttention), which cannot pay at 1019 FLOPs (in the article; their sailboat against container ship). And filtering may matter less for large, overtrained models (in the article), citing Mohri, Duchi and Hashimoto, whose crossover Colin Raffel puts near 1030 FLOPs. Both are arguments; nothing large was trained.

The open questions the authors list

If progress is loading more cargo, the stock of web text is the limit, and whether synthetic data extends it was not tested (in the article; Synthetic data and Data mixing own repetition and synthetic tokens). Data ablations are the experiments automated researchers could run, so data progress may speed up once AI research is automated, a point from Ryan Greenblatt (in the article). And pretraining alone matters less now that much recent progress comes from RL (in the article).

6. Against Epoch's 3x a year: a different quantity

the appendix

The study's 1.57x a year is far below the roughly 3x usually quoted, and measures something else. The cited "3x per year (95% CI: 1.5x to 64x)" (in the article) is Epoch's recalculation; Ho et al. 2024 give an 8.4-month halving (95% CI 4.5 to 14.3), 2.7x a year (derived; 1.8x to 6.3x): about 380x over six years against 15x here, measured in perplexity on published models at their own scales.

How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • The numbers are published and reproduce to the printed digit from the charts' data: 3.7x, 12.0x, 15x, the recipe table, R2 and residuals.
  • The design isolates one ingredient at a time, curves average at least three seeds, and the authors state most limits themselves: small scale, one benchmark, one entry per year, limited tuning (in the article), curations of one Common Crawl rather than new data.
  • The headline gap is not noise. A bootstrap here (each corner-curve point redrawn from its mean with standard error = seed spread / √3, 4,000 draws) puts the recipe at 3.7x (68% range 3.0 to 4.6) and the corpus at 12.0x (9.6 to 14.9).

What it does not show.

  • Independence in compute. The same bootstrap puts the product of the single-axis gains at 3.0x the joint gain (95% range 1.8 to 4.4, above 1 in 99.9% of draws), and the recipe on the 2025 corpus at 1.25x (1.01 to 1.51). Quote 12.0x with "on the GPT-2 recipe", and 15x as the total.
  • One seed per cell cannot exclude a small interaction in points, and the corpus table does not reproduce from its own chart (section 3).
  • Benchmark effects on the data side. Newer corpora are filtered towards educational and question-answer text, may contain benchmark items, and were built while watching tasks OLMES aggregates (Soldaini, Bakouch, below); held-out loss was used across recipes but not across corpora. Contamination's effect on scores: Topic: benchmarks.
  • Range of recipes and release. Only dense transformers, nothing above 1019 FLOPs, and no code, checkpoints or per-run tables.

Verdict. Believe the direction at this scale and on this benchmark: the newer corpus bought more than the newer recipe, by a wide, reproducible margin. Never quote a single-axis multiplier without its baseline: together the two gave 15x, and the recipe adds little on a modern corpus. Extrapolation to frontier models, either way, is argued, not measured.

The classifier point is conceded: much of the 12.0x is trained filters' work (Quality classifiers and data selection). Nobody raised the overlap in compute.

The least understood driver of AI progress

Anson Ho, Epoch AI Gradient Updates, 2026-02-25 (third-party): The essay the post starts from: estimates of software progress range from about 1.1x to 300x a year, and much of it may be data quality and a few scale-dependent algorithms. On the Origin of Algorithmic Progress in AI

Gundlach, Fogelson, Lynch, Trisovic et al., 2025 (third-party): The model-side twin of this experiment: ablations of 2012 to 2023 innovations account for under 10x at small scale; most of the rest is scale-dependent (LSTM to Transformer).

(1 hour)

A Bitter Lesson for Data Filtering

Mohri, Duchi, Hashimoto, 2026 (third-party): The post's evidence that filtering may matter less for large models: with enough compute, the best filter is no filter.

Updates, June 2026 (on A Bitter Lesson for Data Filtering)

7. SFT: imitate a frontier agent to learn the harness's language

[[ah:off-policy-distillation-via-supervised-fine-tuning|Off-policy distillation via supervised fine-tuning]]

First the model had to speak the harness; that came from copying GPT-6 Astra's trajectories. This is off-policy distillation: the student is trained with next-token loss on the teacher's tool calls and replies, never on its own samples ({{in the article|ar:Off-policy distillation is a training method}}; the method: {{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}}). Three practical steps: rendering the OpenAI Responses-format traces into Qwen's token format (Prime Intellect's renderers), masking the loss to the model's own turns, and unrolling each trajectory into one example per assistant reply, each with everything before it as context. The weights were adapted with a LoRA of 21.2 million parameters, 42.5 MB against 9.32 GB of bf16 weights ({{in the article|ar:contained only 21.2 million trainable parameters}}; {{PEFT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81eba08eef56b3e3b4eb}}).

Astra was the teacher although its API returns reasoning summaries, not reasoning. Its trajectories were shorter (22,009 tokens of final context on average against 45,144 for Qwen 3.8 2.4T, all under the ~50,000 a 24 GB card could train on) and faster; trace inversion (expanding summaries into plausible reasoning) was the fallback if summaries hurt. They did not, by the results.

Two epochs helped, a third hurt, and validation loss saw neither. On 100 Astra trajectories from CEB (382 training rows after unrolling), one epoch lifted queries with a valid candidate from 14 to 48 of 113 but scored 0.72x; two epochs 1.08x; three 0.82x, while held-out loss sat at 0.305 to 0.306 through epoch 2 and rose to 0.322 by the end of epoch 3 ({{in the article|ar:Two epochs improved our results, but three}}). A further 300 trajectories (6 that kept the default without trying dropped) and two more epochs gave the RL starting point: 1.16x geometric mean, 1.06x workload, 20 wins and 5 regressions. The harness also changed between epochs one and two (keeping the default became allowed), so the first two rows are not like for like.

8. RL: a reward that ignores noise, and an advantage anchored to Postgres

[[ah:designing-per-rollout-rewards-and-relative-advantages|Designing per-rollout rewards and relative advantages]]

The first reward taught the model to play safe. It was ln(speedup) minus 0.1 per invalid candidate and 0.05 for repeating the default plan, and a flat −3 for a search with no valid candidate. Against −3, returning Postgres's own plan for −0.05 was the best bet, and the model learned it ({{in the article|ar:terrified of producing invalid plans}}).

Plain GRPO made it worse, because it rewards beating the group, not beating Postgres. prime-rl's GRPO subtracts the group's mean reward (it does not divide by the spread). In the article's four-rollout example two rollouts are rejected (−3 each), one finds a slower plan (0.80x, ln 0.80 = −0.23) and one repeats the default (−0.05): the mean is −1.57, so the slower plan gets +1.34 and the copy of Postgres's plan +1.52, the most reinforced of all ({{in the article|ar:reinforcing bad behavior by telling the model}}). Group-relative advantages in general: {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}, section 3.

The reworked rule anchors every rollout to the default. A rollout's quality is ln of its clipped speedup, soft-thresholded by 0.05 (anything within about 5% counts as zero and real gains or losses shrink by 0.05: the noise floor of section 5, built into the reward); keeping the default scores exactly zero. Its baseline is the mean quality of its scored siblings, leaving itself out (RLOO's leave-one-out baseline), floored at zero, so a rollout is never credited for being the least bad. Advantage is quality minus baseline, minus 0.02 if it picked a duplicate of the default; a search with no valid candidate gets −0.1 instead of −3 ({{in the article|ar:prime-rl-flavored GRPO was swapped out}}). In the same example all four rollouts are pushed down: −0.10, −0.10, −0.18 and −0.02. The figure recomputes both tables.

9. Training: a cautious start, then ten times the learning rate

[[ah:training-commences|Training commences]]

RL ran across two machines: generation and training on a rented 2 × H100 node, timing on the four Postgres containers at home, joined over Tailscale. The SFT adapter was merged into the base and a fresh LoRA trained. A first run (learning rate 1e-6, batch 8, four rollouts a query, 120 updates) changed nothing: 1.14x against the SFT model's 1.16x ({{in the article|ar:evaluated against JOB, and completely flopped}}). The second setting (1e-5, batch 16, eight rollouts, 600 updates, twice in a row) worked. Since 92% of a rollout's time was inference, rollouts were changed to lease a Postgres worker only while timing, which let 20 run at once on four workers ({{in the article|ar:92% of the rollout’s time was spent}}).

After 600 updates: 1.35x geometric mean, 1.16x workload, 34 wins, 0 regressions, a valid candidate on 99 of 113 queries; after 1,200: 1.41x, 1.29x, 38 wins, 2 regressions. The share of training rollouts that beat Postgres rose steadily through both runs. All these rows are one search per query, and the hero's band shows how far one search moves on its own.

10. The headline: the best of three searches

[[ar:Best feedback across all three|The final results table]]

The final checkpoint was run three times on every JOB query and scored three ways ({{in the article|ar:Model’s own choice, per trajectory}}):

  • The model's own choice, all 339 searches: 1.40x geometric mean, 1.24x workload, 119 wins, 7 regressions.
  • A fixed rule within each search (take the candidate whose single feedback timing was best, if at least 1.05x, else the default): 1.44x, 1.29x, 129 wins, 1 regression.
  • The same rule across a query's three searches, one answer from up to 15 candidates: 1.81x geometric mean and 1.81x workload, 68 wins, 0 regressions ({{in the article|ar:we saw a 1.81x geometric mean speedup}}).

"81% faster" and "44.7% latency reduction" are the same measurement. The chosen plans' medians sum to 31.756 s against 57.398 s for the defaults (repository, experiment 033): 57.398 / 31.756 = 1.81 times the speed, 1 − 31.756 / 57.398 = 44.7% less time. The selection used only the single feedback timings; the score comes from the separate three-pair measurement, so the 1.81 is not inflated by picking the luckiest timing. It is, by the author's design, a different system from the trained model: three searches and up to 15 executed candidates per query, whose time is not counted.

What the model does, from the 339 final searches: it inspected tables, statistics or the default plan first in 295 of 337 searches that submitted candidates and used all five candidates in 235. Of 1,347 actions it gave scan hints 1,141 times, join trees 917 times, parallelism hints 572 times and row-count corrections only 146 times; it often forced nested loops and index scans, and often set enable_sort=off and random_page_cost=1.1 ({{in the article|ar:The model regularly used enable_sort=off}}). Its gains came mostly from rewriting the join order, from a single scan fix, and from parallelism; job-01d went 90 times faster with a forced bitmap scan.

Cost: $1,200, about $800 for some 95 hours of the 2 × H100 node and $400 of API fees for the Astra trajectories ({{in the article|ar:I paid ~$800 to rent}}). The author's conclusion: frontier models plus a small budget let a company train a small open model on its own niche task, and he lists a Bao-style hint sweep, on-policy distillation, trace inversion and noise measurements on dedicated cloud machines as next steps.

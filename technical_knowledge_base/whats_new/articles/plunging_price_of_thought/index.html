The plunging price of thought

Luke Emberson, David Roodman (Epoch AI) · published 22 September 2026

In one line: On five benchmarks (AIME, FrontierMath tiers 1-3, GPQA Diamond, chess and a secret game's puzzles), from 2023 to mid-2026, the cheapest per-question cost of reaching a fixed score (tokens used times archived list prices, lower budgets simulated by truncating runs) fell about 47% a quarter, 13x a year, averaged over a lattice of dates and scores; 43% to 53% by benchmark, with no uncertainty reported by design.

The idea in one sentence: fix a benchmark score, track the cheapest dollars per question that any released model needs to reach it, and that record cost falls by about half every quarter: 47% a quarter, 13 times a year, on five benchmarks from 2023 to mid-2026.

You need: why a reasoning model's bill depends on tokens spent, not only on its price per token (Topic: llm-landscape, section 6; effort levels and thinking budgets on Test-time compute and reasoning); what GPQA Diamond and FrontierMath test (Knowledge and reasoning benchmarks, Math benchmarks).

New terms: price of thought (dollars per benchmark question at a fixed score); cost frontier C(t, a) (the lowest cost per question among runs of models released by date t that score at least a); record (a run that sets that lowest cost); SOTA debut (the date a score level is first reached by any model); model-free rate (the frontier's own quarter-on-quarter fall, averaged over a lattice, with no fitted curve).

1. A price is dollars per question, at every budget

Data

The cost of a run is the tokens it actually used, reasoning included, times the list price on the model's API, archived from OpenRouter; a few open-weight models with no API were run on rented GPUs and priced at that hardware cost, checked within 30% on five models that had both (in the article). Per-token prices, which earlier estimates used, miss that a reasoning model can be cheap per token and dear per question (in the article).

One run gives a whole cost curve. Following CAISI, a run's transcript is cut at a budget of X tokens per question: questions answered within X count as solved, the rest score the chance of guessing (in the article). Sweeping X traces the model's cost against score without paying for new runs; telling models their budget gave about the same curves as lower effort settings (in the article). Scores are rescaled from the guessing floor, so GPQA's "75%" is 81.25% raw (in the article).

2. The frontier is a staircase for each score level

The challenge

For each benchmark the record cost C(t, a) can only fall: a new model either undercuts it or leaves it alone (in the article). The staircase explorer (beside this section; below it on a phone) draws it from the authors' released runs: pick a benchmark and a level and the steps are the models that set each record. It opens on the article's example: o3 (high) first reached GPQA's 75% level at $0.30 a question; GPT-5.6 Luna (low) does it for $0.0004, 725 times less (in the article).

The steps are rare and big. A model that moves the frontier is sparse, one benchmark result can shift it for months, and the authors only tested models that looked near the frontier, so they treat the staircase as a noisy view of a true frontier (in the article). Move the level: at GPQA's 25% the record fell 42% a quarter, at 50% 61%, at 75% 64% (each over the quarters since that level was first reached).

Predict first: the article dates o3 to 31 January 2025. Tick "corrected release dates" in the explorer: does the 725x get bigger, smaller, or happen faster?
Reveal

Same 725x, faster: o3 was released on 16 April 2025, so the drop took 14.8 months, not 17.2 (about 212x a year instead of 99x). The dataset's lookup gave o3 the date of o3-mini (section 7).

3. 47% a quarter is an average over a lattice, not a fitted slope

Average rates of decline

The headline is the staircase's own fall, averaged. For each benchmark, lay a lattice over the data: dates every 13 days, 100 score levels spread evenly over the benchmark's observed range and kept only below that date's best score; at each node divide the record cost a quarter later by the record cost now (in the article). The geometric mean of those ratios, subtracted from 1, gives 43.0% (Chess Puzzles) to 53.1% (FrontierMath tiers 1-3); pooled over the five, 47.0% a quarter, which is 1 / 0.534 = 12.7x a year (Table 1). Our port of the authors' R code reproduces all eleven benchmarks' model-free rates to the printed digit.

Most nodes never move. The mean is carried by a minority of quarters with big drops: on GPQA 46% of the 5,039 nodes see the record fall within the quarter, on Mystery Game Puzzles only 25% of 3,518 (computed here). That is what a staircase averages to; it also means a few records decide the rate.

Fitted models agree only when they fit the frontier. A smooth cost surface fitted to the staircase (log cost in Box-Cox transforms of date and accuracy odds, the authors' preferred "Box-Tidwell" model) averages 47.0% too; forcing the surface under every data point, or fitting all runs rather than the frontier, gives implausible rates down to rising costs (in the article). The authors report no standard errors at all: the frontier is an extreme, the sampling ad hoc, and a resampling bootstrap can never push the frontier outward, so they treat every number as a rough measurement (in the article).

4. Faster than any earlier technology, compared in log points

Discussion and conclusion

On the authors' series, 13x a year beats every transformative technology they could price: 1.84x a year for DNA sequencing (from 2001), 1.51x for compute (1940 to 2001), 1.16x for lithium batteries (1991 to 2024) and 1.05x for US residential electricity (1892 to 1973) (in the article). Measured in log points the AI decline is 4, 6, 18 and 54 times faster (in the article).

The longer claim rests on one pair of models. That prices fell "at least this fast" since November 2021 comes from MMLU: GPT-3 at $60 per million tokens scored 43.9; Llama 2-7B, July 2023, scored 45.3 at $0.20, a 31x yearly fall per token (in the article). Neither model reasons, so tokens per question are plausibly similar, but it is one comparison, per token, from Epoch's own earlier data.

The windows do not match: three years for AI against 24 to 81 years for the others, which include their slow decades. Over its fastest three years, DNA sequencing fell 37.9% a quarter (the authors' Figure 1 data), close to AI's 47%.

5. Fastest right after a level is first reached, on three of five benchmarks

Variation in the rate of cost decline

The fitted model says a fresh score level gets cheap fastest: averaged over the five, 66% a quarter at SOTA debut, 42% a year later and 32% after two years (in the article). It holds on AIME (83% to 18%), FrontierMath and GPQA (81% to 25%); on Chess and Mystery Game Puzzles the rate creeps up instead, by a few points. The authors' story: the first maker charges a premium until rivals, closed and open, catch up (in the article).

A model-free check agrees on direction, with a different shape. Binning the lattice nodes of section 3 by how long ago their level was first reached, the pooled rate is 59% and 63% in the first two quarters, 42% to 50% in quarters two to four, and 5% to 18% from the fifth quarter on (computed here). The late quarters hold only low levels first reached in 2023, so age, level and calendar time are entangled, as they are in the model. Chess and Mystery do not follow the pattern model-free either.

The "second dot" claim depends on a date. The article notes that in only one of its traces does an open-weight model take the second record (QwQ-32B at AIME's 25%) (in the article). QwQ-32B is dated to its November 2024 preview; at its own release, 5 March 2025, it is no longer a record, and the second dot becomes DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, also open weights. Figure 2 has seven traces, not eight.

6. The caveats the authors state, and the one Toby Ord forced

Limitations

The authors list five limits themselves (in the article): labs may train for known benchmarks (the secret-game benchmark falls a little slower, 44.0% against 47.0%, "consistent with the benchmaxxing critique being valid but not fatal"); a benchmark score is not useful work; no real user switches to the cheapest adequate model for every task; three years is short; and there are many defensible ways to average, which on GPQA alone give 42.9% to 58.0% instead of 47.0% (our port gives 42.9% and 57.9% for the two ends) (Figure 7).

The sixth caveat was added a day after publication. Averaging falls across dates, benchmarks and score levels chains price drops for a changing basket of services, like an inflation index in chained dollars; it is "not tracking a conceptually unitary 'price of thought'", so comparisons with electricity or batteries are partly apples and oranges (in the article). It footnotes Toby Ord's objection on X (What the responses said).

How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • It is reproducible. Data and code are public; our independent port of the model-free method reproduces every rate in Table 1's first column to the printed digit, and the GPQA example to the cent.
  • It measures the right thing. Cost per question with reasoning tokens counted, across every effort setting and budget, is closer to what a buyer pays than price per token, and the method was checked against announced budgets and against hardware costs.
  • The order of magnitude is robust. Every variant tried here (the chart beside this section) keeps the pooled rate between 42.6% and 55.5% a quarter, 9x to 25x a year. Fixing the data errors below moves it by at most a point; using only untruncated runs raises it to 51%; dropping any one of the 110 models that ever hold a record leaves it between 43.0% (without o1) and 52.1% (without o3).

What it does not show.

  • Release dates are wrong for some key models. A model's date is the earliest registry entry whose name starts with its cleaned name, so o3 gets o3-mini's 31 January 2025 (released 16 April), o1 gets o1-mini's September 2024 (released 17 December), QwQ-32B its preview's, and Gemini 2.5 Pro (June) the March experiment's. The article's own text and Figure 2 inherit this: "On January 31, 2025, OpenAI released ... o3" (in the article). Corrected, the pooled rate barely moves (46.5%), but single benchmarks shift by up to 4 points, and Epoch's launch-thread example (o3 first above 25% on FrontierMath, then a 377-fold drop) fails: o4-mini, released the same day as o3, already did it at $0.13.
  • Prices can postdate the release. The archived list price is the snapshot nearest the run, a median 28 days after release, and more than 180 days after for 27 of 179 models, so a later price cut is dated to the launch. Dropping those prices moves the pooled rate to 46.0%.
  • Versions are merged. Cleaning names folds gpt-4o's May, August and November 2024 versions, with different prices, into one model dated May.
  • No uncertainty, by choice. The authors argue the bootstrap would mislead here; the consequence is that the spread across averaging choices (42.6% to 55.5%) is the only error bar on offer, and it is wider than the gaps between benchmarks the article interprets (39% to 43% for games against 50% to 52% for math).
  • Fastest at SOTA is fragile. It comes from a model's derivative near the edge of the data plus an auxiliary fit of when each level debuted; model-free it shows as a step after about a year, on three of five benchmarks.
  • Price is not cost. List prices may be subsidised; the article measures what a buyer is charged, as its title says, not what serving costs a lab.

Verdict. Believe the order of magnitude, roughly 10x to 25x a year for a fixed benchmark score since 2023: it survives every correction tried here. Treat 47% as one reasonable averaging choice, the technology multiples as rough (different windows, a chained index), the release dates behind the article's examples as partly wrong, and the SOTA-premium pattern as suggestive.

Where the responses are right. Ord's point stands and the authors conceded it in print: the 47% is a chained index over a changing basket, and the "54 times faster than electricity" comparison is the part to read most loosely. The subsidy point is real but outside the article's claim, which is about prices paid. Nobody noticed the release dates.

Where it moves the knowledge base

  • Topic: llm-landscape, section 6 ("Price per token is not cost per task"): it explains cost per task at one date; it should add how fast that cost falls for a fixed score (about 47% a quarter, 13x a year, on five benchmarks, 2023 to 2026, a chained index), linking here.
  • Test-time compute and reasoning, the cost of reasoning tokens: the CAISI truncation method (one run's per-question token counts give its score at every lower budget) is a cheap way to trace a model's cost curve, and announced budgets gave about the same curves as lower effort.
  • Topic: benchmarks: a benchmark kept secret (Mystery Game Puzzles) as a check on benchmark-specific training; its cost falls a little slower.

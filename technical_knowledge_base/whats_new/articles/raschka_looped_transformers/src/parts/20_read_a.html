The idea in one sentence: a looped transformer runs the same stack of blocks several times per token, which saves weights but costs the compute and KV cache of the deeper model it imitates, so Raschka argues it is just a bigger model with shared weights, and that GPT-6 Astra's shorter reasoning traces mean it is more capable, not that looping hides its chain of thought.

What this page is for. The mechanism belongs to {{Test-time compute and reasoning|n:4035c17b0d0d813f9fb2d62cff1f09ef}} (section 9), which already cites this article as the sceptical voice in the Astra monitorability debate. This page follows the argument in the article's order, recomputes its cost arithmetic from Nanbeige4.2-3B's config, and weighs its central claim against what came after: the system card it summarises, measurements of Astra's reasoning without a chain of thought, and the first experiment on an open looped model. Section 8's calculator shows where the "bigger model" equivalence breaks.

You need: what a transformer block is and why decoding keeps a KV cache ({{The transformer from zero|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8174980ddde4a20c7b3c}}, section 9); chain-of-thought reasoning as test-time compute ({{Test-time compute and reasoning|n:4035c17b0d0d813f9fb2d62cff1f09ef}}, sections 1 and 9).

New terms: block application (one run through one block; a looped model applies each block several times); pass or loop (one trip through the shared stack); no-CoT capability (what a model solves with reasoning switched off); CoT controllability (how well a model obeys instructions about what its chain of thought says, which makes the chain weaker evidence); monitorability (how well a monitor reading the chain catches bad behaviour); recurrent KV sharing (early passes of a token read keys written by the last pass of earlier tokens).

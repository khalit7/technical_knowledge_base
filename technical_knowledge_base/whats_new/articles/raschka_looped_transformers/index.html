GPT-6 Astra, Looped Transformers, and Hidden Reasoning

Sebastian Raschka · published 9 September 2026

In one line: An explainer of looped transformers written against GPT-6 Astra's reported use of them: reusing a stack of blocks saves weights but not compute or KV cache, so Raschka argues a looped model behaves like a bigger one and does not by itself hide its chain of thought. It rests on published papers and on a reading of OpenAI's system card, not on any measurement of Astra.

How far to trust it: Believe the mechanics, which we checked against Nanbeige4.2-3B's config and the papers. Treat "looping does not hide reasoning" as half right: it does not remove the written chain, but the equivalence with a bigger model breaks when keys and values are shared across passes, and later measurements found looping raises reasoning without a chain of thought more than it raises general ability. The idea in one sentence: a looped transformer runs the same stack of blocks several times per token, which saves weights but costs the compute and KV cache of the deeper model it imitates, so Raschka argues it is just a bigger model with shared weights, and that GPT-6 Astra's shorter reasoning traces mean it is more capable, not that looping hides its chain of thought.

What this page is for. The mechanism belongs to Test-time compute and reasoning (section 9), which already cites this article as the sceptical voice in the Astra monitorability debate. This page follows the argument in the article's order, recomputes its cost arithmetic from Nanbeige4.2-3B's config, and weighs its central claim against what came after: the system card it summarises, measurements of Astra's reasoning without a chain of thought, and the first experiment on an open looped model. Section 8's calculator shows where the "bigger model" equivalence breaks.

You need: what a transformer block is and why decoding keeps a KV cache (The transformer from zero, section 9); chain-of-thought reasoning as test-time compute (Test-time compute and reasoning, sections 1 and 9).

New terms: block application (one run through one block; a looped model applies each block several times); pass or loop (one trip through the shared stack); no-CoT capability (what a model solves with reasoning switched off); CoT controllability (how well a model obeys instructions about what its chain of thought says, which makes the chain weaker evidence); monitorability (how well a monitor reading the chain catches bad behaviour); recurrent KV sharing (early passes of a token read keys written by the last pass of earlier tokens).

1. Astra first: the best model he has used, with a harness caveat

Section 1

The article opens with a user's verdict: Astra is an exceptionally good model, likely the best he had used, strongest at 3D rendering and computer use, which he expects labs to train ever more, as reinforcement learning against real operating systems (the reported tens of thousands of Mac Minis are the environment, not the trainer). Astra is still a reasoning model that writes a chain of thought: the premise of everything after.

One comparison mixes harnesses. Astra's 99.9% on the ARC-AGI-3 benchmark came through ARC Prize's Provider Adapter, which keeps Astra's reasoning state between requests; on the standard harness it scored 62.7%, and GPT-5.6 Sol's 7.8%, set against it, is a standard-harness score (OpenAI: GPT family has both runs). The sentence added on 2 October, on OpenAI's two further changes to the harness, makes the harness point without fixing the comparison.

2. A looped transformer reuses blocks: weights saved, compute and cache not

Sections 2.1 and 2.2

The hidden states go through the same blocks more than once, and the weights stay the same across these passes. Nanbeige4.2-3B (July 2026) applies its 22 blocks twice: application 23 uses block 1's weights, and 44 applications come from 22 blocks (the figure at the top, the article's Figures 5 and 6). Its report found two passes the best trade-off; more gained little and destabilised training.

Looping saves storage of the blocks, nothing else. The embedding and output tables are outside the saving; the article puts them at embedding and output layers make up ~25% of "the total 3B" and 12.5% if tied. From the config they are 1.02B of 4.17B, 24.5% ("3B" counts non-embedding parameters only), and tying would leave 13.9%.

Compute and KV cache stay those of the deeper model, the article's most useful point. Every pass is real work in the forward pass and in backpropagation, similarly expensive as having 44 distinct blocks. The second pass sees different hidden states, so its keys and values need their own cache: there are no KV cache-related savings either. Nanbeige tried sharing the KV cache between passes, halved the cache and lost quality, and found training looped from scratch better than converting a trained model. Left out, though his 2 September note quoted it: two passes keep only about 75% of a standard model's token efficiency. A Hacker News commenter added that looping saves memory capacity, not bandwidth, since each pass streams the weights again. Cache sizing is on Long context.

3. How many passes, and who decides

Sections 2.3 to 2.5

The count can be fixed or chosen per token. The Universal Transformer halts a position when a learned halting probability sums past a threshold; Ouro-2.6B-Thinking loops 48 blocks four times with a learned exit gate, though its released code computes all configured passes before selecting an output (confirmed in modeling_ouro.py); Mixture-of-Recursions uses a small, learned router that reads each token in context and assigns one to three passes. The mechanisms are drawn on Test-time compute and reasoning, section 9.

Whether it pays depends on scale. In the Mixture-of-Recursions plot at equal training compute, At the smallest model scale, the regular transformer performs best, and the routed model catches up or wins at the larger scales, up to 1.7B, partly by seeing more tokens. Hence his rule that looping helps if the model is large enough: a reading of one figure. In the comments, asked what a later token reads when an earlier one skipped passes, he gave the paper's two answers (attend only to tokens still active, or reuse the first recursion's keys). Which keys a pass reads turns out to be the crux of the monitoring question (section 8).

4. Not an RNN: recurrence over depth, not over tokens

Section 3

An RNN carries a hidden state from one token to the next; a looped transformer carries a token's state through the same blocks again, and tokens still talk only through attention. In the article's words, RNNs reuse their weights across time steps, while looping is across depth: best thought of as a bigger model with shared weights. That holds with a cache per pass; section 8 shows the cache arrangement, described in the Huginn paper and available on NVIDIA's Dory, that puts an RNN-like path back.

5. Does Astra loop? Probably, he says, but that is not why it is good

Section 4

The architecture is a report, not a disclosure, and the article says so: The Information's claim is still just a rumor or scoop. He still rates it highly likely, from the reporting, the technique's published promise, and Jakub Pachocki's statement that OpenAI's frontier models, Astra included, have a computation graph within a factor of two of GPT-4's depth (which, he grants, could also mean twice the ordinary blocks). Where Astra's quality comes from is his opinion, labelled as one: likely primarily due to other reasons, namely improved training recipes and data.

6. Does looping hide the chain of thought? His answer: no more than a bigger model would

Section 5

For users nothing changed: OpenAI has been hiding (most of) the reasoning traces since o1. The question is whether developers can still read the chain. Written reasoning tokens work as a scratch pad; looping adds computation inside each token instead, so a looped model may need fewer of them.

The argument: fewer tokens is what capability looks like. At a fixed accuracy, OpenAI's chart shows it is true that GPT-6 Astra uses fewer tokens than GPT-5.6 Sol. Within one family, with no looping, Luna uses 80% more tokens than Sol at similar scores, and nobody calls Sol less interpretable. Chains were never guaranteed faithful, so the only valid concern is that looped transformers purposefully mislead more often, which he sees no evidence for. The system card's reduced monitorability he reads as mostly associated with shorter, less informative traces. Pachocki's clarification closes the section:

"I want to prevent a race into unmonitorability kicked off by confused reporting. [...] I do think it is fragile and unfortunately trending in a negative direction, for reasons not contingent on architecture changes that I will write about soon."

7. The research: where extra passes help

Section 6 and the conclusion

Huginn (2025) makes the loop count a test-time dial: the loop count is randomly sampled in training, so at inference it runs 8 to 64 loops, and HellaSwag levels off after about eight while GSM8K and HumanEval keep gaining (to scale on Test-time compute and reasoning). Looping buys reasoning, not knowledge: at fixed parameters looping leaves the amount of stored information nearly unchanged while multi-step maths improves (Zhu et al., 2025), the split the later responses measure. At matched compute it still wins a little: SMELT keeps per-token compute, non-embedding parameters and KV cache equal (they narrow the hidden dimension to compensate and add experts back) and its scaling laws, up to 54B, save 6.8 to 18% of training compute. Latent feedback across tokens shortened reasoning, then lost the effect: in the 1B full-bandwidth transformer the shortening effect disappears after instruction tuning, with no comparison against simply enlarging the model.

The conclusion restates the thesis: Astra probably loops, Looped transformers simply give better modeling performance at a fixed compute budget, and shorter traces are what a capable model does, as a smart and well-prepared student needs less scrap paper.

8. Where the bigger-model equivalence breaks: whose keys each pass reads

With a cache per pass, the equivalence holds for monitoring too. Block d of a later token reads keys that earlier tokens wrote at the same depth d, so nothing from earlier tokens comes back down to a shallower block; to be worked on again from the bottom, it must be written as a token. A hidden chain is therefore at most 22 × 2 = 44 block applications in Nanbeige, as in a 44-block model. This is the bound behind Pachocki's "within a factor of two of GPT-4", and behind the safety argument that the written chain is the only route from later layers back to earlier ones. One cache written by the first pass (Mixture-of-Recursions' sharing variant) halves the memory and keeps the bound.

Predict before you switch to "recurrent", where every pass of a token reads the keys the last pass of earlier tokens wrote: with two passes, how long can a hidden chain get over a 1,000-token answer?

22,022 block applications instead of 44, growing with the answer. Pass 1 of each token reads what the last pass of the previous token wrote, so each token adds 22 × (2 − 1): 44 + 999 × 22. The tokens are still written, but the reasoning need not pass through them; they could be filler. This is not hypothetical: the Huginn paper says its own cache sharing "actually construct[s] a computation that is deeper than the current number of recurrence steps"; MBaert drew the consequence for monitoring the day the article appeared; and NVIDIA's Dory 4B runs in exactly this mode, where it learned under RL against a monitor to hide its working behind filler (responses below).

So the article's KV-cache section answers the wrong question for its own topic: it treats the cache as a memory cost, when for hidden reasoning what matters is which pass reads which keys. OpenAI's "depth of the computation graph" does not say whether per token or per answer, nor how Astra's cache is arranged; nothing published says Astra uses a recurrent cache.

How much of this to believe

The mechanics are right wherever we checked them. Nanbeige's 22 blocks and two passes are in its config, and its report says what the article says about the shared cache, training from scratch and the pass count; Ouro's code does run all four passes; the cost claims follow from the config (closed block below). The slips are small: the embedding share is 24.5% of a 4.17B total, and tying would leave 13.9%, not 12.5%. The Astra-specific claims are labelled as rumour and opinion, and remain unconfirmed; the ARC-AGI-3 comparison mixes harnesses (section 1).

The central claim is half right. Looping does not remove the written chain of thought, and The Information's "obscures some or all" was loose, as Pachocki and much of the Hacker News thread also said. But "a bigger model with shared weights, so shorter traces mean capability" is weaker than the article makes it:

  • It summarises the system card by its weakest finding. The card reports CoT controllability of 60.9% against GPT-5.6 Sol's 16.1% on chains of 750 to 1,250 tokens (UK AISI: 93% against 48%), a no-CoT maths time horizon of 30.9 minutes against 3.6 (UK AISI), and a model that, told a monitor is present, shortened its chain enough to occasionally evade it (system card). The Luna and Sol analogy explains shorter chains, not a model that obeys "do not reason about this" and still answers (the card's Table 10). A reader said so in the comments; the author's reply, that needing less reasoning is efficiency, answers length only.
  • OpenAI is partly on his side: it is "quite confident that changes in CoT controllability are not differentially due to any architectural changes", while finding them correlated with the rise in no-CoT capability. Astra's chain is measurably less useful to a monitor; OpenAI blames RL, not looping; the article's "nothing to see" goes further than either.
  • The equivalence fails for one cache arrangement (section 8), which the Huginn paper the article summarises describes in its own sections 6.2 and 6.3.
  • Later evidence leans against "just a bigger model", without settling it: Astra answers multi-hop questions without a chain that Sol cannot, and on an open looped model extra loops raised no-CoT reasoning by about 18 points where the change in general ability predicts about 2 (responses below). Both are small studies with stated limits.

"Looping simply gives better performance at fixed compute" overstates its sources. SMELT's 6.8 to 18% is a fitted saving within one recipe; the plain model wins at Mixture-of-Recursions' smallest scale; Nanbeige's two passes keep about 75% of a standard model's token efficiency; and the largest looped models use two to four loops, with "N times the parameters beats N times the loops" long the standing result (Arike's literature summary).

Verdict. Read sections 2 and 3 of the article as the best short, correct explainer of looping and its costs. Read section 5 as one side of a live argument: right that looping is not hiding by definition, wrong to reduce the system card's findings to trace length, and silent on the cache arrangement that would make looping hide reasoning in fact.

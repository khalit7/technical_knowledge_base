How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • The central control is right and its effects are large: 22% to 80% from subgoals is about 23 times the 2.5-point spread between two single-seed runs, and 22% to 42% from physics is about 8 times.
  • The author attacks his own result first (easy test, lucky official 50, seed noise, collapse without nearby memories), before the critics did.
  • The pusher experiment is paired, model-free and correctly tested (30 to 0, sign test 1.9 in a billion).

What it does not show.

  • The memory graph is retrieval from the training set the test is drawn from. LeWM's evaluation samples its tasks from the training demonstrations the graph embeds, and 84% of standard goals have a near-copy there. The harder test still takes its goals from demonstration frames.
  • The "60 points" belongs to the simulator planner and the author's success test. Push-T's standard success test (LeWM's code) requires the pusher near its goal position too; the harder test counts the block alone, so dropping the pusher aligns the cost with the new test. The comparable change for LeWM gave 11% to 23%.
  • One run per number, on task sets reused across 600 experiments, with no held-out set and no count of failed variants. The same setup is quoted as 78%, 80% and 85%, the full graph as 85% and 93% (below).
  • No code, logs or simpler baselines. Which success test the standard numbers use, and what the opening's simulator planner scored, are not stated.
Why each objection matters (under 2 min)
  • If the test episode's own demonstration stays in the graph, the exact goal frame is a node and the planner only has to find the path into it; the post does not say it was removed. Its 84% figure counts near-copies from other demonstrations, so even with removal most goals have a stand-in.
  • The section 4 simulator planner scores true block and pusher positions, which LeWM's embedding cannot separate. If the opening's simulator planner also scored positions, its 77% changes two things at once (dynamics and cost), and "everything else the same" does not hold.
  • The post's one check that embedding distance means something physical: given one subgoal reachable within 25 steps and one that is not, LeWM's distance ranks the reachable one closer 87% of the time ({{in the article|ar:ranks the reachable one as closer}}). Useful, but 13% wrong rankings is why every jump needs a cost.
  • With 38 of 250 tasks flipping between two seeds, single-run differences under about 5 points mean nothing; the effects above are 10 to 60 points, so the direction survives, while the 78% against 77% is a tie, not a ranking.
  • Prior work the post reinvents without citing: Search on the Replay Buffer (2019) and Semi-parametric Topological Memory (2018) plan over graphs of stored observations with learned distances; equivariance augmentation (rotating and mirroring data) is standard in robot learning.

Verdict. Believe the direction: on Push-T, over 25 steps, LeWM's predictor is not what limits it, and splitting long tasks into short searches is what pays. Read 22% to 85% as retrieval of subgoals from demonstrations that also supply the test goals, the 60-point gain as the simulator planner under a block-only success test (LeWM gained 12 points from the analogous change), and every number as one run on reused tasks until the code is out.

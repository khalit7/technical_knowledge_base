After 600 experiments, here's what I discovered about JEPA world models

Haddy Alchaer (independent) · published 11 November 2026

In one line: On Push-T, in one author's single runs without retraining, LeWM (a 15M-parameter JEPA world model) planned as well as the simulator when both were given expert subgoals every 25 steps (78% against 77%, 250 tasks with the goal 75 steps away); subgoals found in a graph of its own embedded training demonstrations, with a cost on each jump between demonstrations, took such tasks from 22% to 85%. On the author's harder test the best planner reaches 56%. No code released.

The idea in one sentence: on Push-T, LeWM's learned predictor plans as well as the simulator over 25 steps, so far goals fail because one search cannot reach 75 steps; chaining short searches through subgoals found among its own embedded training demonstrations lifts 75-step success from 22% to 85%, on a test whose goals come from those demonstrations.

You need: JEPA (Contrastive and self-supervised learning, the JEPA section); model-predictive control with the cross-entropy method, and compounding model error (Model-based RL and planning, sections 11 to 13).

New terms: Push-T (a 2D simulation: a round pusher shoves a T-shaped block to the pose in a goal image); LeWM (LeWorldModel, 2026: a 15M-parameter JEPA world model from pixels whose CEM planner looks 25 steps ahead); subgoal (an intermediate goal image); memory graph (embedded training frames linked into a graph); jump (an edge between two demonstrations); harder test (the author's: start and goal from different demonstrations, block held on target for the last 10 steps).

1. The predictor is not the bottleneck; the search horizon is

The opening and section 1

Swapping LeWM's predictor for the simulator's exact physics changes almost nothing. With an expert frame as subgoal every 25 steps, on 250 tasks with the goal 75 steps away, LeWM succeeds 78% and the simulator 77% (in the article): a tie, since two seeds of one planner disagree on 15% of tasks.

Far goals fail because of the search, not compounding error. LeWM as published solves 22% of 75-step tasks but 89.7% of 25-step ones, within a point of the simulator (in the article). Exact physics, which cannot drift, lifts one 75-step search only to 42%; a subgoal every 25 steps lifts LeWM to 80% (the bars). CEM cannot find a 75-step plan with or without a correct model, and four planner runs added under a point (in the article).

2. Subgoals from a graph of the model's own memories

2. A latent memory graph

The memory graph replaces the expert's subgoals. LeWM's encoder embeds every frame of its training demonstrations, about 460,000 states, each linked to its successor and to nearby states of other demonstrations; a shortest path from the current state to the goal gives the subgoals (in the article).

A cost on every jump took it from 62% to 85%, the expert-subgoal score (in the article). With free jumps the search chained them into routes the block could not follow (318 of 1,015 plans used six or more). Nearness in embedding space is not a move the pusher can make (the toy). The idea is Search on the Replay Buffer (Eysenbach et al., 2019), which the post does not cite.

3. The standard test is easier than it looks

The standard Push-T test is easier than it looks

85% flatters the method, as the author shows. Test goals lie further along the start's own demonstration, and all demonstrations end in one place, so for 84% of goals the graph held a nearly identical state (in the article). Further: LeWM's evaluation samples its tasks from pusht_expert_train, the training set (config/eval/pusht.yaml), so the test demonstrations are in the graph unless removed, which the post does not say.

The sample is also small and noisy: 68% on the official 50 episodes, 44% on 250 fresh ones; a new seed flipped 38 of 250 (in the article). On the harder test the memory graph falls from 75% to 45% (in the article).

4. Fill gaps with rotated and mirrored memories

3. Fill gaps in memory

The graph is only as good as its coverage. Deleting the stored states near the goal dropped standard success from 93% to 32% (in the article). Push-T's physics is unchanged by shifting, rotating or mirroring the table, so transformed segments from elsewhere can fill the gap: back to 85%, and 1% of the demonstrations plus copies did about as well as 10%. On the harder test copies help most where the toy needs jumps: 45% to 56% overall, 27% to 46% against the flow.

5. The goal image also scores the pusher

4. The cost was "wrong"

A goal image shows the pusher somewhere, so a planner matching the whole image tries to put the pusher there too. In one run the robot reached the block's target, then pushed it away chasing the pusher's goal position.

With the model removed, the effect is large. The simulator planner ran on the same 50 harder-test tasks with a cost on block and pusher, then on the block alone: 9 against 39 successes, 30 tasks flipping one way and none the other (the table), a split with chance probability 2 × 0.530, about 1.9 in a billion. LeWM's goal embedding mixes block and pusher, so its workaround scores each prediction against 16 copies of the goal with the pusher moved and keeps the closest: 11% to 23% on the harder test (in the article).

6. What comes next: subgoals beyond the data

What comes next

The open problem is goals no demonstration leads to: the best planner reaches 56% on the harder test, failing mostly against the flow. The author wants a high level that proposes its own subgoals, better than H-JEPA, which "match or slightly beat flat LeWM" on Push-T (in the article).

How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • The central control is right and its effects are large: 22% to 80% from subgoals is about 23 times the 2.5-point spread between two single-seed runs, and 22% to 42% from physics is about 8 times.
  • The author attacks his own result first (easy test, lucky official 50, seed noise, collapse without nearby memories), before the critics did.
  • The pusher experiment is paired, model-free and correctly tested (30 to 0, sign test 1.9 in a billion).

What it does not show.

  • The memory graph is retrieval from the training set the test is drawn from. LeWM's evaluation samples its tasks from the training demonstrations the graph embeds, and 84% of standard goals have a near-copy there. The harder test still takes its goals from demonstration frames.
  • The "60 points" belongs to the simulator planner and the author's success test. Push-T's standard success test (LeWM's code) requires the pusher near its goal position too; the harder test counts the block alone, so dropping the pusher aligns the cost with the new test. The comparable change for LeWM gave 11% to 23%.
  • One run per number, on task sets reused across 600 experiments, with no held-out set and no count of failed variants. The same setup is quoted as 78%, 80% and 85%, the full graph as 85% and 93% (below).
  • No code, logs or simpler baselines. Which success test the standard numbers use, and what the opening's simulator planner scored, are not stated.
Why each objection matters (under 2 min)
  • If the test episode's own demonstration stays in the graph, the exact goal frame is a node and the planner only has to find the path into it; the post does not say it was removed. Its 84% figure counts near-copies from other demonstrations, so even with removal most goals have a stand-in.
  • The section 4 simulator planner scores true block and pusher positions, which LeWM's embedding cannot separate. If the opening's simulator planner also scored positions, its 77% changes two things at once (dynamics and cost), and "everything else the same" does not hold.
  • The post's one check that embedding distance means something physical: given one subgoal reachable within 25 steps and one that is not, LeWM's distance ranks the reachable one closer 87% of the time (in the article). Useful, but 13% wrong rankings is why every jump needs a cost.
  • With 38 of 250 tasks flipping between two seeds, single-run differences under about 5 points mean nothing; the effects above are 10 to 60 points, so the direction survives, while the 78% against 77% is a tie, not a ranking.
  • Prior work the post reinvents without citing: Search on the Replay Buffer (2019) and Semi-parametric Topological Memory (2018) plan over graphs of stored observations with learned distances; equivariance augmentation (rotating and mirroring data) is standard in robot learning.

Verdict. Believe the direction: on Push-T, over 25 steps, LeWM's predictor is not what limits it, and splitting long tasks into short searches is what pays. Read 22% to 85% as retrieval of subgoals from demonstrations that also supply the test goals, the 60-point gain as the simulator planner under a block-only success test (LeWM gained 12 points from the analogous change), and every number as one run on reused tasks until the code is out.

Where it moves the knowledge base

  • Model-based RL and planning, section 13: a measured counterweight to compounding error. On Push-T a 75-step search fails even with exact physics (42%), and a learned JEPA predictor matches the simulator once each search is 25 steps.
  • Model-based RL and planning, section 11: the world-model table has V-JEPA 2-AC but no small JEPA world model; LeWM (15M parameters, 96.0% on Push-T at 25 steps) is the one this post and H-JEPA build on.
  • Contrastive and self-supervised learning, the JEPA depth block: it ends at V-JEPA 2; LeWM with SIGReg (from LeJEPA) is the 2026 step to a JEPA trained end to end from pixels without a moving-average teacher or pretrained encoder.

Numbers in the post that disagree with each other (1 min)
  • LeWM with an expert subgoal every 25 steps on the same 250 tasks: 78% in the opening, 80% in section 1's bars, and section 2 calls 85% "the same score received by the planner using expert subgoals".
  • The full memory graph on the 250 standard tasks: 85% in section 2, 93% as the "full memory" bar of section 3. Different success tests or later improvements would explain both pairs; the post does not say which.
  • 89.7% and 90.5% at 25 steps are not whole numbers of 250 tasks (224 and 225 of 250 are 89.6% and 90.0%), so they come from another task count or several seeds, not stated.
  • The hero of the post shows 24 of "the hardest tasks it solved"; its data file holds those 24 episodes, ordered by how far the block travels. It is a showcase of successes, not a sample.
  • The section 3 heading reads "transformed memories"; its anchor still says "rotated and mirrored", and section 4's anchor says "score goals on the block alone", earlier titles. All three archived copies already carry the current text.

What it rests on

LeWorldModel: Stable End-to-End Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture from Pixels

Maes, Le Lidec, Scieur, LeCun, Balestriero, arXiv 2603.19312 (third-party): LeWM: a 15M-parameter JEPA trained from pixels with two loss terms (prediction and SIGReg); 96.0% on Push-T over 50 tasks with the goal 25 steps away, CEM planner, receding horizon of 25 steps. Its limitations section already names short horizons and dataset coverage.

(45 min)

lucas-maes/le-wm and the stable-worldmodel library

LeWM authors (maker), MIT licence: config/eval/pusht.yaml: 50 episodes from pusht_expert_train, seed 42, goal 25 steps ahead, 50-step budget. The Push-T success test (stable-worldmodel, envs/pusht/env.py) needs the pusher and the block together within 20 pixels and the block within 20 degrees.

(20 min)

LeJEPA: Provable and Scalable Self-Supervised Learning Without the Heuristics

Balestriero and LeCun, arXiv 2511.08544 (third-party): Where SIGReg, the regulariser that keeps LeWM's embeddings from collapsing, comes from.

(1 hour)

H-JEPA: End-to-End Learning of Hierarchical World Models for Visual Planning

Zhang, Terver, Rabbat, LeCun, Balestriero, arXiv 2610.06805 (third-party): The learned hierarchy the post names as today's alternative: on Push-T two levels match or exceed LeWM, three levels fall back because Push-T's short episodes leave too few training clips. Its Push-T tasks use held-out expert frames 75 steps apart.

(50 min)

Search on the Replay Buffer: Bridging Planning and Reinforcement Learning

Eysenbach, Salakhutdinov, Levine, NeurIPS 2019 (third-party): The memory-graph idea in 2019: a graph over stored states, edges weighted by a learned distance, shortest path as waypoints for a short-horizon controller.

(40 min)

Semi-parametric Topological Memory for Navigation

Savinov, Dosovitskiy, Koltun, ICLR 2018 (third-party): An earlier graph of observations from a walkthrough, with learned shortcut edges and subgoals for a local policy.

(35 min)

DINO-WM: World Models on Pre-trained Visual Features enable Zero-shot Planning

Zhou, Pan, LeCun, Pinto, arXiv 2411.04983 (third-party): The Push-T dataset, success test and planning setup LeWM inherits.

What Drives Success in Physical Planning with Joint-Embedding Predictive World Models?

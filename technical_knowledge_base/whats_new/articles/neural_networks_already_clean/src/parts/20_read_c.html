Squeezed vision models made neurons coarser, not mixed

[[ah:image-models|Exploring Image Models]]

The essay studies small image models first because they can be seen most clearly: feature visualization exists for them, and they are where the worry began. The author trained three ConvNeXts of 92 thousand, 7.06 million and 28.6 million parameters (about 300× apart), the middle one the size of InceptionV1 ({{in the article|ar:I trained three ConvNeXt models spanning roughly 300× in size}}).

Neurons are read as gates in a flow, not as nouns. A neuron's positive side lets some things through and its negative side keeps others out: cars and dogs share an early pose neuron, a gate in front of the car specialists responds positively to cars and negatively to dogs, and later an ambulance neuron may have a taxi on its negative pole ({{in the article|ar:Now I think of them more as control flow}}). In ReLU networks the negative side gets no gradient of its own, but each input weight is a vote shaped whenever the neuron is on, so it carries meaning too. In the large model's last layer each neuron separates a pattern from its nearest neighbour: taxis from ambulances, broccoli from cauliflower, spoonbills from flamingos; the smallest model's last layer stops at "ground vehicles" against boats and planes.

[[ah:crushing-neurons|What Neurons Do Under Pressure]]

If too few neurons caused superposition, squeezing a layer should produce it; it produced coarser neurons. These are the experiments that test Figure 1's two predictions.

  • InceptionV1 layer 3b, cut from 480 neurons to 10 and retrained from scratch (no residual path to route around it; 58.35% top-1, 81.14% top-5 on ImageNet). Superposition predicts overlapping mixtures such as curve plus spiral plus web; the result was one "curves and loops" neuron instead of ten curve detectors, two circle neurons instead of several ({{in the article|ar:reducing the amount from 480 to 10 and retraining}}). A sparse autoencoder trained on the squeezed layer did not recover ten curve detectors either: the nearest units are broad and bunched ({{in the article|ar:We don’t see anything like clean curve neurons}}).
  • The medium ConvNeXt's last layer, cut from 1,024 to 13 neurons with the residual bypass removed: the same broadening, and the smallest ConvNeXt behaves alike.
  • The residual stream itself narrowed four and eight times (down to 46 dimensions in the last block, all neurons kept): accuracy fell from 72% to 60% and 39%, and the stream's map got messier but showed nothing interleaved; a dark-bird-on-water neuron leaked toward alligators and a swimming retriever, not clocks ({{in the article|ar:Accuracy fell from 72% to 60% and 39%}}).
[[ah:dictionaries|What Dictionaries Learn]]

SAEs trained on these layers found no building block the neurons lack. The SAE units were the neurons' blocks grouped by context (spiders in settings, against the neurons' leg patterns and webs). On the last layer 88% of the SAE's activity is near-copies of single neurons ({{in the article|ar:On the last layer, 88% of its activity is near-copies}}); removing every dictionary direction that matches no neuron leaves accuracy unchanged; and searching rotations of a layer for the one where a few units carry most activity, from a random start, lands back on the neuron basis in every block ({{in the article|ar:you land back on the neurons, in every block}}). The SAE did help with "what is in this image" in the smallest model, whose neurons are coarse.

Rare things and memorisation used ordinary routes. Rare classes are more consolidated (one Triceratops neuron); images far from all others are misread by ordinary neurons (a hummingbird as ice cream). Teaching the medium model that a photo of an umbrella is a bee took nine steps, umbrella to maypole to bee, and used existing bee neurons, nearly all in the last three blocks: swapping only those blocks into the original model makes it say "bee" ({{in the article|ar:In nine training steps, the top guess went from umbrella}}). The appendix adds that adversarial examples recruit the target class's real neurons (a triceratops skull read as a jaguar).

Weak activations: cheap to remove, and related when traced

[[ah:do-weak-activations-matter|Do Weak Activations Matter?]]

The essay's answer to "is it hiding in the weak activations?" is that they can be removed at little cost, and what they lose is fine distinctions. Of 100 million tokens through one Qwen3-32B neuron almost all sit near zero and about 160 reach the strong half ({{in the article|ar:about 160 reach the strong half}}). Ablating almost all weak activations of a network costs little ({{in the article|ar:you can ablate almost all the weak activations}}); in Inception's final layer, keeping roughly the strongest quarter of positive activations gives 69.47% accuracy against 70.08% ({{appendix|ar:69.47% accuracy, versus 70.08% originally}}). Where removal costs most is easy to explain: a language model's last layer suppressing single tokens, and early vision layers working like dials. The answers that flip are near-ties between classes the model already confused.

Strong-but-not-maximal activations radiate outward semantically: CLIP's Trump neuron goes from photos of Trump to related people and art to Fox News studios. Of the hundreds of weak, unrelated-looking activations the author traced by hand, almost all came through a reasonable circuit (a Zen koan neuron firing weakly on recursion in maths). Two practical notes: a neuron's raw activation shape tells a dial from a detector, which percentiles flatten; and percentiles mislead intuition, since the 99th percentile is once every quarter page and the 99.9th every two or three pages ({{in the article|ar:the 99.9th percentile is every two or three pages}}).

This is the section the strongest replies contest (Nanda and leogao, Responses); the companion paper's measurement is in How much to believe.

Language models: neurons carry the circuits, with two pitfalls

[[ah:language-models|Exploring Language Models]]

This section, done with Curt Tigges and drawing on the companion paper, tests whether the vision story holds in language models. A medical diagnosis graph from Anthropic's On the Biology of a Large Language Model (built on Claude 3.5 Haiku with cross-layer transcoder features) is rebuilt from Qwen3-32B's own neurons, with no reconstruction error; the essay notes Anthropic's largest transcoder predicted the same next word as the model only half the time and needs error nodes. Its diagnosis hub is four neurons; removing them makes the model ask about itching, the sign of a different liver condition of pregnancy ({{in the article|ar:The diagnosis hub is just four neurons}}).

Negative poles matter as much as positive ones. In a Santa graph, removing the eight neurons whose negative side is Santa lore drops Santa from near-certain to 6% (the model blames the pet); removing the twenty neurons actually labelled Christmas or Santa leaves 76% ({{in the article|ar:Remove the eight neurons with a Santa negative side}}). The essay also shows a neuron-only attribution graph of Kimi K3, which it calls the first published for a multi-trillion-parameter model: a found wallet linked to lost property and honesty, answer "return"; full audits at that scale have not begun.

In samples of "neuron papers" (agent-written studies of single neurons, 1,000 random ones shown), about 90% of neurons have basically one job, offshoots are usually related, a fair number have two or three jobs, and none has been seen with about ten unrelated ones. Offshoots look rarer and less important in their own context than the main job in its, so not "sleeper agents".

A big source of noise is SwiGLU's double negative (Figure 4): when gate and value are both negative their product is positive, so the positive pole collects firings that look like the negative pole. For about 4% of neurons this echo is the whole top of the activation list ({{in the article|ar:for about 4% of neurons, it’s the whole top of the list}}); ablating these activations across the network raises most benchmarks but lowers some reasoning ones, which the essay leaves unexplained and asks architecture designers to fix. In vision SwiGLU models the value learns broad families (small grey birds) and the gate opens only for one (juncos).

Experts, it says, are far more specialised than reported ({{in the article|ar:Experts are far more specialized than previously reported}}): judged by agents reading summaries of their neurons, Qwen3.6's 10,240 experts narrow with depth (one is devoted to bosses in RPG board games), and measures like token similarity miss this. It argues mixture of experts is an anti-superposition architecture: a packed expert would need the router, one small linear layer, to recognise ten thousand unrelated things.

Explanations that can lose, and old models revisited

[[ah:testing-explanations|Explanations That Can Lose]]

The essay's guard against explaining anything is to make each explanation risk a prediction. In the "blind maximum" test one agent studies a neuron and writes a short explanation; a second agent sees only that explanation and writes a sentence meant to fire the neuron as hard as possible; it often beats every example in 10 billion tokens of corpus ({{in the article|ar:One we found particularly useful is blind maximum}}). The appendix's near-miss test (Figure 5) is the clearest number in the essay: for 200 random gallery neurons, an agent read only the explanation and wrote five examples that should fire it and five near misses that should not, before anything was measured. Of the 183 whose explanation describes the positive side, examples fired strongly 76% of the time and near misses 11%; the explanation won for 164, tied for 15, lost for 4 ({{in the article|ar:The explanation won for 164}}). Texts on the most distant concept the agent could invent (what superposition could pack in) fired about 1% of the time, and 179 of 183 neurons never fired on them.

The author's own trust in metrics is limited: "cats and cars" would pass many tests of the V neuron; the decisive check (it connects to flames) took judgment, as Mercury's orbit did for Newtonian gravity. The appendix argues simulation scores mostly measure where attention puts information and the fine slope of weak activations, not what a neuron is.

[[ah:revisiting-gpt2-xl|Slouching Towards Monosemanticity]]

Older language models look cleaner with today's tools too. For GPT-2 XL, where fewer than 2% of neurons came out clean in 2023, 500 new neuron papers with almost no iteration find about 70% doing roughly one thing and about 5% pristine; the rest do a few broad things, mostly two ({{in the article|ar:Now about 70% look like roughly one thing}}). The neuron Towards Monosemanticity opened with ("academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text") may be a "don't say a dot" neuron: nearly all its firings sit on or near a dot, eight of its ten strongest negative output connections begin with one, and the Korean firings fall inside unfinished syllables ({{in the article|ar:I think it may be a “don’t say a dot” neuron}}); without that model, whether Korean is a second job cannot be settled. The author now thinks small simplified models were the wrong place to start, since the "bells and whistles" help neurons learn well.

Residual stream and attention, briefly. The ConvNeXt residual stream would not visualise until the author found it puts almost all its mass into one unread dimension that scales the rest through LayerNorm; set aside, the maps make sense ({{in the article|ar:into a single unread dimension}}). Attention is "mostly outside the scope": where a head looks and which neurons it reads and writes give most of the value, and both the author and an agent reading 100 attribution graphs put the residual value of naming every head at about 5% ({{in the article|ar:might be about 5%}}).

Zooming out: become another reader of the network

[[ah:zooming-out|Zooming Out]]

"Clean" in the title means more than one-job neurons: a network's parts are built to be read, and built on, by its own downstream readers, so interpretability's goal is to become another reader ({{in the article|ar:a network’s parts are built to be read}}). The essay calls the result a fractal of explanations: coherent groupings at the scale of districts and circuits, and, through recursive generalization, inside each neuron.

Two styles of interpretability follow ({{in the article|ar:There are two broad styles of interpretability}}). Enumerative interpretability wants a complete list of features to audit and fears the combinatorics of units that mean several things; exploratory interpretability starts from questions and moves between attribution graphs, neurons and heads like a map, and is robust to some polysemanticity. The essay backs the second: "are there more features than dimensions?" becomes mostly irrelevant, and features, famously hard to define, need not be defined. It would take understanding the circuit motifs behind a few questions (an agent's psychology, how it thinks about its goals) over every neuron made monosemantic.

A city, not a knot. A bigger network is easier to navigate the way New York's financial district is easier to find than a small town's: agents found dog-ear neurons faster in the larger ConvNeXts, where three kinds feed one hanging-ears neuron. The risk is fracturing the city to fit our map: an SAE trained on 4e:55's layer splits the V into a car feature and a cat feature ({{in the article|ar:An SAE trained on the layer fractures it the same way}}). Probes and dictionaries belong at the end, as views, not at the start, as replacements.

On safety, the essay's fear is not a hidden activation but an unread one: every error in the essay was something in plain sight that we, or our tools, misread; deception must be readable by the model's own circuits, so "my fear is that deception will be right there, in plain sight, and we won't know how to read it" ({{in the article|ar:My fear is that deception will be right there}}). The errors are also asymmetric: assume clean and be wrong, and a messy neuron is a lead; assume packed and be wrong, and it is the end of inquiry, which the author says cost him most of a decade ({{in the article|ar:the mistakes aren’t symmetric}}).

Its forecast: frontier networks are where those vision models were eight years ago, legible but waiting on many small fixes ({{in the article|ar:My guess is that frontier networks are where those vision models were}}), and automated scientists (agents labelling neurons, finding and testing motifs, following data through circuits) may do in months what took humans most of a decade.

The appendix in brief: definitions, toy models, why networks generalize, the title, open cases
  • Which "superposition". Before Zoom In it meant vector addition; Zoom In attached it to one circuit (now retracted); Toy Models (2022) defined it as more features than dimensions; Olah's 2023 note framed composition against superposition as strategies, the framing closest to the essay's. The essay keeps the word for "neurons learning unrelated things together because they rarely interfere" and says agents in particular read it as the count definition (perhaps because that note opens with a base64 image that exhausts agents' web readers).
  • Toy Models of Superposition: "careful work" with no problems found in its experiments, but it prices the cost of sharing a neuron (interference) and not the benefit (reuse); in its reconstruction task nothing downstream builds on shared structure ({{in the article|ar:That prices the cost of sharing a neuron, interference}}). With correlated inputs and hidden neurons in one toy, related inputs grew onto neurons. Packing should appear where memories are related to nothing, which real data rarely has.
  • Why networks generalize ("circuit racing"). A broad circuit fires on more of an image, takes more of the gradient and starves a pixel-exact shortcut; a network trained on one Labrador photo mostly learned "dark and green". Trained on ten single photos it labels about one in five new photos correctly, twice chance, mostly by colour. Grokking is the exception where memorisation wins first. The same head start is offered as the source of recursive generalization and of the prediction that rare facts ride related circuits rather than hiding in unrelated ones.
  • Missing curves. SAE "missing curve detectors" in InceptionV1 decompose into existing curve neurons; an SAE trained on curve activations alone uses 84,000 of its 100,000 dictionary items, showing how compositions inflate the count of apparent building blocks.
  • Memorisation. In Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, removing a Poe neuron drops "dreary" after "Once upon a midnight" from 87% to 50% though its direct output effect alone leaves 85%; zeroing neurons above their 99.9th percentile made the model deny Psalm 23 exists while matched random interventions did not.
  • On the title. The authors considered "cleaner than we thought" and rejected it as a change of degree when their update is a change of kind (like moving from design to selection); about 85% confident, in the hypothesis, not in any cleanliness score.
  • Unresolved. An InceptionV1 neuron (mixed5a:719) for a pinched-centre shape (bow ties, butterflies, swimsuit tops) also picks up dress shoes and flip-flops, "generalization with semantic residue" the author does not fully understand.
  • Also argued: attention superposition may be a terminology difference; meta-models (models that read activations) could sit on top as the top map level; and, for neuroscience, a cat-and-car neuron seen through a few electrodes should be discounted almost to zero.

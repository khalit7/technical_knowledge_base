The idea in one sentence: the essay argues that the famous neurons which seemed to pack unrelated things together (InceptionV1's cats and cars) were misread through weak tools and a weak architecture, that what mixing remains is a neuron's job branching into neighbouring territory (recursive generalization) rather than unrelated things packed where they rarely collide (superposition), and that networks can therefore be studied through their own neurons instead of being decomposed first.

The knowledge base has no interpretability page; the terms below are all this page needs.

New terms: polysemantic neuron (one that seems to have several unrelated jobs, or none anyone can name); superposition (in this essay only: a network packing unrelated things into one neuron because they rarely occur together, so they do not interfere; the field also uses the word for "more features than dimensions", which the essay sets aside); recursive generalization (a neuron's job grows along one similarity, then sprouts offshoots from those, each step local: "iy", then "diy", then "do it yourself"); pole (the positive or the negative side of a neuron, read as two detectors); feature visualization (an image optimised by gradient ascent to excite a neuron); sparse autoencoder, SAE (a wide autoencoder with a mostly-zero code, trained on a layer's activations, whose units are studied instead of the neurons: a dictionary); attribution graph (the graph of which units, on one input, pushed which other units toward the output); InceptionV1 (GoogLeNet, the 2014 image classifier that was the circuits work's model organism).

The claim: polysemanticity had ordinary causes, and superposition was the wrong explanation

[[ah:a-note|The Short Version]]

The essay names two ways to do mechanistic interpretability and says the field took the wrong one for the wrong reason. The first is ordinary science on the network itself: read its neurons and circuits, watch data flow through them, test explanations. The second starts from a list of hypothesised features and trains a second model (usually a sparse autoencoder) to extract them, then studies that instead. The field moved to the second because canonical neurons looked like tangled knots, and superposition explained why: a network short of neurons folds unrelated things together, so a neuron's grouping tells you nothing until the knot is undone ({{in the article|ar:What looked like arbitrary packing was mostly a motif}}).

Its four steps ({{in the article|ar:Put together, the argument has four steps}}): networks use one learning principle, generalization, not two, so nothing blocks studying them directly; with modern models and tools that study goes much better than expected; understanding compounds, like a crossword whose filled squares constrain the rest; and, flagged by the author as highly speculative, this might be most of what interpretability needs. The essay places its own confidence at "maybe 85%" that nearly all mechanisms come from generalization ({{On the Title|ar:We’re maybe 85% sure}}).

The numbers it sets against the old view. Anthropic's Towards Monosemanticity (2023) gave the median neuron of a one-layer model 0 out of 14 on its rubric ({{in the article|ar:gave the median neuron a 0 on its rubric}}); the author's own OpenAI automated-interpretability project (2023) found about 1.7% of GPT-2 XL's neurons interpretable "by our most generous measure" ({{in the article|ar:found about 1.7% of neurons interpretable}}). Against those, preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B: about 90% of neuron poles with one main job, about 7% with two related jobs, 2 to 4% not yet understood, with similar results said for models up to Kimi K3 (2.8 trillion parameters) and left to a future companion paper ({{in the article|ar:we estimate that roughly 90% of neuron poles}}). A footnote warns that the figure moves with where "one thing" and "weak activation" are cut, and that on strong activations alone the author's smaller samples are closer to 97%. These are different measures on different models; How much to believe puts them side by side.

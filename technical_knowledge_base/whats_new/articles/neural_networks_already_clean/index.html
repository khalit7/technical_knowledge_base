Neural Networks Are Already Clean

Nick Cammarata, with Curt Tigges on the language-model section (Goodfire) · published 15 October 2026

In one line: An essay, with interactive figures but no released code, arguing from case studies that the canonical evidence for superposition in InceptionV1 was misread (the cat-and-car neuron groups a shared V shape; the car-into-dogs circuit is a shared pose, seen through reward-hacked feature visualizations), that most remaining mixing in a neuron is related offshoots it calls recursive generalization, and that squeezing layers of small vision models made neurons coarser rather than mixed. Its language-model numbers are preliminary, agent-assisted audits: about 90% of Qwen3-32B neuron poles with one main job, under thresholds the essay says move the number.

How far to trust it: Believe the diagnosis of the old examples and the method lessons (read both poles, trace the circuit, distrust free feature visualization deep in a network): the author co-wrote the original readings and overturns them with retrained models. Treat the percentages as one team's judgement rather than measurements: the essay publishes no rubric or sample for its 90%, its companion paper measures something narrower on another model (69% of 5,468 poles dominated by one mode), and the absence of superposition is argued from not finding it, under a definition (packing unrelated things) narrower than the field's usual one. The evidence, judged.

Updated: on 30 October a footnote softened the central example ("it's not a cat and car neuron, it's a V-shape neuron" became a neuron that brings cats and cars together because of a shared V shape); by 27 November the companion paper was linked where it had been "forthcoming" and a Prior work list was added; no number changed (compared with the first archived copy of 15 October in Provenance).

The idea in one sentence: the essay argues that the famous neurons which seemed to pack unrelated things together (InceptionV1's cats and cars) were misread through weak tools and a weak architecture, that what mixing remains is a neuron's job branching into neighbouring territory (recursive generalization) rather than unrelated things packed where they rarely collide (superposition), and that networks can therefore be studied through their own neurons instead of being decomposed first.

You need: what a language-model neuron is (one hidden unit of a block's feed-forward layer, The transformer from zero); SwiGLU's gate and value (Activation functions); experts and routers (Mixture of experts); what an autoencoder is (Autoencoders and VAEs). The knowledge base has no interpretability page; the terms below are all this page needs.

New terms: polysemantic neuron (one that seems to have several unrelated jobs, or none anyone can name); superposition (in this essay only: a network packing unrelated things into one neuron because they rarely occur together, so they do not interfere; the field also uses the word for "more features than dimensions", which the essay sets aside); recursive generalization (a neuron's job grows along one similarity, then sprouts offshoots from those, each step local: "iy", then "diy", then "do it yourself"); pole (the positive or the negative side of a neuron, read as two detectors); feature visualization (an image optimised by gradient ascent to excite a neuron); sparse autoencoder, SAE (a wide autoencoder with a mostly-zero code, trained on a layer's activations, whose units are studied instead of the neurons: a dictionary); attribution graph (the graph of which units, on one input, pushed which other units toward the output); InceptionV1 (GoogLeNet, the 2014 image classifier that was the circuits work's model organism).

The claim: polysemanticity had ordinary causes, and superposition was the wrong explanation

The Short Version

The essay names two ways to do mechanistic interpretability and says the field took the wrong one for the wrong reason. The first is ordinary science on the network itself: read its neurons and circuits, watch data flow through them, test explanations. The second starts from a list of hypothesised features and trains a second model (usually a sparse autoencoder) to extract them, then studies that instead. The field moved to the second because canonical neurons looked like tangled knots, and superposition explained why: a network short of neurons folds unrelated things together, so a neuron's grouping tells you nothing until the knot is undone (in the article).

Its four steps (in the article): networks use one learning principle, generalization, not two, so nothing blocks studying them directly; with modern models and tools that study goes much better than expected; understanding compounds, like a crossword whose filled squares constrain the rest; and, flagged by the author as highly speculative, this might be most of what interpretability needs. The essay places its own confidence at "maybe 85%" that nearly all mechanisms come from generalization (On the Title).

The numbers it sets against the old view. Anthropic's Towards Monosemanticity (2023) gave the median neuron of a one-layer model 0 out of 14 on its rubric (in the article); the author's own OpenAI automated-interpretability project (2023) found about 1.7% of GPT-2 XL's neurons interpretable "by our most generous measure" (in the article). Against those, preliminary audits of Qwen3-32B: about 90% of neuron poles with one main job, about 7% with two related jobs, 2 to 4% not yet understood, with similar results said for models up to Kimi K3 (2.8 trillion parameters) and left to a future companion paper (in the article). A footnote warns that the figure moves with where "one thing" and "weak activation" are cut, and that on strong activations alone the author's smaller samples are closer to 97%. These are different measures on different models; How much to believe puts them side by side.

The two canonical examples were a shared shape and a shared pose

Cars and Animals

InceptionV1's neuron 4e:55, the field's best-known polysemantic neuron, groups things by a shared V, a pointed two-sided shape. Feature Visualization (2017) and Zoom In (2020), which the author co-wrote, presented it as an arbitrary mixture of cat faces and car fronts; Zoom In said it "isn't responding to some commonality". Looking again at the image grid, the author saw cat and fox faces and cars at similar angles sharing one V, and so did pool tables and the Shinkansen train (in the article). Three checks followed: its strongest upstream connections include V-shaped flames and a fireboat's V-shaped water jets; its negative pole is an O, round where the V is pointed (in the article); and a feature visualization of what its clustered inputs share shows a pointed shape.

Revised by the authorThe first version said "it's not a cat and car neuron, it's a V-shape neuron". A footnote dated 30 October 2026 calls that "too strong": as a 1×1 convolution it does take in upstream cat, fox and car detectors; the claim is about why those ones, a shared shape rather than things packed because they never meet (the footnote). A fuller study of 4e:55 is promised, not yet published (checked 27 November 2026).

The car-into-dogs circuit, the original example of superposition, is a shared pose. The author found it in 2019: a car neuron (4c:447) feeding a set of dog neurons, read as the car being hidden in a high-dimensional place for later neurons to read back out. The essay finds no later neuron reads that combination; the dog neurons are among more than a hundred pose neurons, each detecting a way of facing the camera shared by cars, dogs and other objects, and later layers keep refining them into car specialists (in the article). The misreading had a tool cause too: deep in InceptionV1, feature visualization falls into the many dog neurons and draws dogs whatever the route (in the article). Nudging the optimisation along the paths that real top images take (often for only the first 64 steps) shows two of the four readers as car neurons and two as pose neurons shared by cars and dogs. The same shape appears in DINOv2, ten times larger, so it is not only a small-model compromise.

The lesson the essay draws: describe a neuron as a pattern, not a list of nouns. Human and automated interpreters favour "detects cats and cars"; asking "how would I describe it without nouns?" often reveals the shared pattern, and the negative pole is a quick check. The author suspects this is the largest single source of misattributed polysemanticity in middle layers.

InceptionV1's last layer: the microscope and the architecture, not the network

A History of Microscope Errors

The last layer of InceptionV1 was the main evidence that networks are almost fundamentally uninterpretable, and the essay shows it was mostly fixable. With the tools of 2014 to 2020 almost none of its neurons could be read: Liv Gorton wrote "essentially no interpretable neurons", the author puts it near 5% (in the article). Two kinds of fix change that, without changing the model's size or data:

  • A better microscope. Feature visualization once lacked transformation robustness and came out blurred; deeper, it reward-hacks into dogs and its regularisation was slightly off. With those fixed and attribution views added, the same 2014 checkpoint reads at about 50% (in the article).
  • A model that learns well. Retraining InceptionV1's architecture with modern regularisation gives about 70% (in the article); a ConvNeXt with the same parameter count (7.06 million) and dataset, which adds residual connections and LayerNorm, about 99% (in the article).

The essay's reading: superposition was proposed to explain InceptionV1's late layers, and they were a learning failure, not a second learning principle. Residual connections were invented to train deeper networks, by people with no interest in interpretability, and appear to make deep layers conceptually clean as a side effect. The author trained ConvNeXts across a 300× range of size; all are legible, the smaller ones with coarser neurons (one car neuron where a bigger model has many). The last layers can be browsed in the essay's microscope.

Two explanations of a mixed neuron, with opposite predictions

Recursive Generalization

Recursive generalization is a neuron generalizing along more than one axis, then sprouting offshoots from the offshoots. Retraining InceptionV1 and saving checkpoints let the author watch a last-layer neuron form: it began on yellow rapeseed fields, in photos that sometimes held a dog, then grew toward other nature, green objects such as balls, and dogs with no flowers, until the fields were nearly gone; the ConvNeXt neuron that starts on the same fields drops the side branches (in the article). The language example is a neuron for "iy" (as in Miyazaki) that grows to "diy" and then to "do it yourself", which has no "iy" at all. The essay credits the idea to the "conjoined features" subsection of Multimodal Neurons (2021), which the author co-wrote and had forgotten.

Superposition

Superposition, in the sense the essay tests, is a different learning principle: unrelated things placed together because they do not interfere. The two predict opposite shapes, which Figure 1 draws: generalization is local (a floppy-ear-dog neuron covers many floppy ears), superposition non-local ("a floppy ear dog + Latin poetry neuron", in the article). The essay is explicit that "superposition" has several meanings (in the article): Toy Models of Superposition (2022) defines it as representing more features than dimensions, which the essay says even a fully monosemantic network satisfies, because neurons compose. It sets that sense aside and tests only arbitrary packing in neurons.

Superposition of the Gaps

"Polysemantic" says more about the observer than the neuron: it means no good explanation yet. The essay lists five causes and argues that superposition, the most radical and least testable, became the default when it should be the last hypothesis (in the article):

The author anticipates the obvious objection, an explanation of the gaps, and gives two answers (in the article): each other cause can be tested and fails visibly when wrong, while superposition cannot; and real explanations are narrow (a Colorado neuron either makes the model talk about hiking when steered or not). Also cited: Anthropic's Softmax Linear Units (2022), "superposition is often treated as the default hypothesis simply because it seems intuitively more compelling than other explanations, but there is little evidence" (the essay notes that paper calls its own results moderate, circumstantial evidence for it).

Squeezed vision models made neurons coarser, not mixed

Exploring Image Models

The essay studies small image models first because they can be seen most clearly: feature visualization exists for them, and they are where the worry began. The author trained three ConvNeXts of 92 thousand, 7.06 million and 28.6 million parameters (about 300× apart), the middle one the size of InceptionV1 (in the article).

Neurons are read as gates in a flow, not as nouns. A neuron's positive side lets some things through and its negative side keeps others out: cars and dogs share an early pose neuron, a gate in front of the car specialists responds positively to cars and negatively to dogs, and later an ambulance neuron may have a taxi on its negative pole (in the article). In ReLU networks the negative side gets no gradient of its own, but each input weight is a vote shaped whenever the neuron is on, so it carries meaning too. In the large model's last layer each neuron separates a pattern from its nearest neighbour: taxis from ambulances, broccoli from cauliflower, spoonbills from flamingos; the smallest model's last layer stops at "ground vehicles" against boats and planes.

What Neurons Do Under Pressure

If too few neurons caused superposition, squeezing a layer should produce it; it produced coarser neurons. These are the experiments that test Figure 1's two predictions.

  • InceptionV1 layer 3b, cut from 480 neurons to 10 and retrained from scratch (no residual path to route around it; 58.35% top-1, 81.14% top-5 on ImageNet). Superposition predicts overlapping mixtures such as curve plus spiral plus web; the result was one "curves and loops" neuron instead of ten curve detectors, two circle neurons instead of several (in the article). A sparse autoencoder trained on the squeezed layer did not recover ten curve detectors either: the nearest units are broad and bunched (in the article).
  • The medium ConvNeXt's last layer, cut from 1,024 to 13 neurons with the residual bypass removed: the same broadening, and the smallest ConvNeXt behaves alike.
  • The residual stream itself narrowed four and eight times (down to 46 dimensions in the last block, all neurons kept): accuracy fell from 72% to 60% and 39%, and the stream's map got messier but showed nothing interleaved; a dark-bird-on-water neuron leaked toward alligators and a swimming retriever, not clocks (in the article).
What Dictionaries Learn

SAEs trained on these layers found no building block the neurons lack. The SAE units were the neurons' blocks grouped by context (spiders in settings, against the neurons' leg patterns and webs). On the last layer 88% of the SAE's activity is near-copies of single neurons (in the article); removing every dictionary direction that matches no neuron leaves accuracy unchanged; and searching rotations of a layer for the one where a few units carry most activity, from a random start, lands back on the neuron basis in every block (in the article). The SAE did help with "what is in this image" in the smallest model, whose neurons are coarse.

Rare things and memorisation used ordinary routes. Rare classes are more consolidated (one Triceratops neuron); images far from all others are misread by ordinary neurons (a hummingbird as ice cream). Teaching the medium model that a photo of an umbrella is a bee took nine steps, umbrella to maypole to bee, and used existing bee neurons, nearly all in the last three blocks: swapping only those blocks into the original model makes it say "bee" (in the article). The appendix adds that adversarial examples recruit the target class's real neurons (a triceratops skull read as a jaguar).

Weak activations: cheap to remove, and related when traced

Do Weak Activations Matter?

The essay's answer to "is it hiding in the weak activations?" is that they can be removed at little cost, and what they lose is fine distinctions. Of 100 million tokens through one Qwen3-32B neuron almost all sit near zero and about 160 reach the strong half (in the article). Ablating almost all weak activations of a network costs little (in the article); in Inception's final layer, keeping roughly the strongest quarter of positive activations gives 69.47% accuracy against 70.08% (appendix). Where removal costs most is easy to explain: a language model's last layer suppressing single tokens, and early vision layers working like dials. The answers that flip are near-ties between classes the model already confused.

Strong-but-not-maximal activations radiate outward semantically: CLIP's Trump neuron goes from photos of Trump to related people and art to Fox News studios. Of the hundreds of weak, unrelated-looking activations the author traced by hand, almost all came through a reasonable circuit (a Zen koan neuron firing weakly on recursion in maths). Two practical notes: a neuron's raw activation shape tells a dial from a detector, which percentiles flatten; and percentiles mislead intuition, since the 99th percentile is once every quarter page and the 99.9th every two or three pages (in the article).

This is the section the strongest replies contest (Nanda and leogao, Responses); the companion paper's measurement is in How much to believe.

Language models: neurons carry the circuits, with two pitfalls

Exploring Language Models

This section, done with Curt Tigges and drawing on the companion paper, tests whether the vision story holds in language models. A medical diagnosis graph from Anthropic's On the Biology of a Large Language Model (built on Claude 3.5 Haiku with cross-layer transcoder features) is rebuilt from Qwen3-32B's own neurons, with no reconstruction error; the essay notes Anthropic's largest transcoder predicted the same next word as the model only half the time and needs error nodes. Its diagnosis hub is four neurons; removing them makes the model ask about itching, the sign of a different liver condition of pregnancy (in the article).

Negative poles matter as much as positive ones. In a Santa graph, removing the eight neurons whose negative side is Santa lore drops Santa from near-certain to 6% (the model blames the pet); removing the twenty neurons actually labelled Christmas or Santa leaves 76% (in the article). The essay also shows a neuron-only attribution graph of Kimi K3, which it calls the first published for a multi-trillion-parameter model: a found wallet linked to lost property and honesty, answer "return"; full audits at that scale have not begun.

In samples of "neuron papers" (agent-written studies of single neurons, 1,000 random ones shown), about 90% of neurons have basically one job, offshoots are usually related, a fair number have two or three jobs, and none has been seen with about ten unrelated ones. Offshoots look rarer and less important in their own context than the main job in its, so not "sleeper agents".

A big source of noise is SwiGLU's double negative (Figure 4): when gate and value are both negative their product is positive, so the positive pole collects firings that look like the negative pole. For about 4% of neurons this echo is the whole top of the activation list (in the article); ablating these activations across the network raises most benchmarks but lowers some reasoning ones, which the essay leaves unexplained and asks architecture designers to fix. In vision SwiGLU models the value learns broad families (small grey birds) and the gate opens only for one (juncos).

Experts, it says, are far more specialised than reported (in the article): judged by agents reading summaries of their neurons, Qwen3.6's 10,240 experts narrow with depth (one is devoted to bosses in RPG board games), and measures like token similarity miss this. It argues mixture of experts is an anti-superposition architecture: a packed expert would need the router, one small linear layer, to recognise ten thousand unrelated things.

Explanations that can lose, and old models revisited

Explanations That Can Lose

The essay's guard against explaining anything is to make each explanation risk a prediction. In the "blind maximum" test one agent studies a neuron and writes a short explanation; a second agent sees only that explanation and writes a sentence meant to fire the neuron as hard as possible; it often beats every example in 10 billion tokens of corpus (in the article). The appendix's near-miss test (Figure 5) is the clearest number in the essay: for 200 random gallery neurons, an agent read only the explanation and wrote five examples that should fire it and five near misses that should not, before anything was measured. Of the 183 whose explanation describes the positive side, examples fired strongly 76% of the time and near misses 11%; the explanation won for 164, tied for 15, lost for 4 (in the article). Texts on the most distant concept the agent could invent (what superposition could pack in) fired about 1% of the time, and 179 of 183 neurons never fired on them.

The author's own trust in metrics is limited: "cats and cars" would pass many tests of the V neuron; the decisive check (it connects to flames) took judgment, as Mercury's orbit did for Newtonian gravity. The appendix argues simulation scores mostly measure where attention puts information and the fine slope of weak activations, not what a neuron is.

Slouching Towards Monosemanticity

Older language models look cleaner with today's tools too. For GPT-2 XL, where fewer than 2% of neurons came out clean in 2023, 500 new neuron papers with almost no iteration find about 70% doing roughly one thing and about 5% pristine; the rest do a few broad things, mostly two (in the article). The neuron Towards Monosemanticity opened with ("academic citations, English dialogue, HTTP requests, and Korean text") may be a "don't say a dot" neuron: nearly all its firings sit on or near a dot, eight of its ten strongest negative output connections begin with one, and the Korean firings fall inside unfinished syllables (in the article); without that model, whether Korean is a second job cannot be settled. The author now thinks small simplified models were the wrong place to start, since the "bells and whistles" help neurons learn well.

Residual stream and attention, briefly. The ConvNeXt residual stream would not visualise until the author found it puts almost all its mass into one unread dimension that scales the rest through LayerNorm; set aside, the maps make sense (in the article). Attention is "mostly outside the scope": where a head looks and which neurons it reads and writes give most of the value, and both the author and an agent reading 100 attribution graphs put the residual value of naming every head at about 5% (in the article).

Zooming out: become another reader of the network

Zooming Out

"Clean" in the title means more than one-job neurons: a network's parts are built to be read, and built on, by its own downstream readers, so interpretability's goal is to become another reader (in the article). The essay calls the result a fractal of explanations: coherent groupings at the scale of districts and circuits, and, through recursive generalization, inside each neuron.

Two styles of interpretability follow (in the article). Enumerative interpretability wants a complete list of features to audit and fears the combinatorics of units that mean several things; exploratory interpretability starts from questions and moves between attribution graphs, neurons and heads like a map, and is robust to some polysemanticity. The essay backs the second: "are there more features than dimensions?" becomes mostly irrelevant, and features, famously hard to define, need not be defined. It would take understanding the circuit motifs behind a few questions (an agent's psychology, how it thinks about its goals) over every neuron made monosemantic.

A city, not a knot. A bigger network is easier to navigate the way New York's financial district is easier to find than a small town's: agents found dog-ear neurons faster in the larger ConvNeXts, where three kinds feed one hanging-ears neuron. The risk is fracturing the city to fit our map: an SAE trained on 4e:55's layer splits the V into a car feature and a cat feature (in the article). Probes and dictionaries belong at the end, as views, not at the start, as replacements.

On safety, the essay's fear is not a hidden activation but an unread one: every error in the essay was something in plain sight that we, or our tools, misread; deception must be readable by the model's own circuits, so "my fear is that deception will be right there, in plain sight, and we won't know how to read it" (in the article). The errors are also asymmetric: assume clean and be wrong, and a messy neuron is a lead; assume packed and be wrong, and it is the end of inquiry, which the author says cost him most of a decade (in the article).

Its forecast: frontier networks are where those vision models were eight years ago, legible but waiting on many small fixes (in the article), and automated scientists (agents labelling neurons, finding and testing motifs, following data through circuits) may do in months what took humans most of a decade.

The appendix in brief: definitions, toy models, why networks generalize, the title, open cases
  • Which "superposition". Before Zoom In it meant vector addition; Zoom In attached it to one circuit (now retracted); Toy Models (2022) defined it as more features than dimensions; Olah's 2023 note framed composition against superposition as strategies, the framing closest to the essay's. The essay keeps the word for "neurons learning unrelated things together because they rarely interfere" and says agents in particular read it as the count definition (perhaps because that note opens with a base64 image that exhausts agents' web readers).
  • Toy Models of Superposition: "careful work" with no problems found in its experiments, but it prices the cost of sharing a neuron (interference) and not the benefit (reuse); in its reconstruction task nothing downstream builds on shared structure (in the article). With correlated inputs and hidden neurons in one toy, related inputs grew onto neurons. Packing should appear where memories are related to nothing, which real data rarely has.
  • Why networks generalize ("circuit racing"). A broad circuit fires on more of an image, takes more of the gradient and starves a pixel-exact shortcut; a network trained on one Labrador photo mostly learned "dark and green". Trained on ten single photos it labels about one in five new photos correctly, twice chance, mostly by colour. Grokking is the exception where memorisation wins first. The same head start is offered as the source of recursive generalization and of the prediction that rare facts ride related circuits rather than hiding in unrelated ones.
  • Missing curves. SAE "missing curve detectors" in InceptionV1 decompose into existing curve neurons; an SAE trained on curve activations alone uses 84,000 of its 100,000 dictionary items, showing how compositions inflate the count of apparent building blocks.
  • Memorisation. In Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, removing a Poe neuron drops "dreary" after "Once upon a midnight" from 87% to 50% though its direct output effect alone leaves 85%; zeroing neurons above their 99.9th percentile made the model deny Psalm 23 exists while matched random interventions did not.
  • On the title. The authors considered "cleaner than we thought" and rejected it as a change of degree when their update is a change of kind (like moving from design to selection); about 85% confident, in the hypothesis, not in any cleanliness score.
  • Unresolved. An InceptionV1 neuron (mixed5a:719) for a pinched-centre shape (bow ties, butterflies, swimsuit tops) also picks up dress shoes and flip-flops, "generalization with semantic residue" the author does not fully understand.
  • Also argued: attention superposition may be a terminology difference; meta-models (models that read activations) could sit on top as the top map level; and, for neuroscience, a cat-and-car neuron seen through a few electrodes should be discounted almost to zero.

How much of this to believe

What holds up. The strongest part is the author overturning his own canonical examples with mechanisms anyone can check against the essay's microscope: the V neuron's negative pole and its flame inputs, the car circuit's readers seen through guided feature visualization, InceptionV1's last layer read again and retrained. The squeezing experiments test a clear prediction (Figure 1) and came out the way generalization predicts. Exceptions are reported rather than hidden: the bow-tie neuron, the unsettled Korean firings, the 30 October softening of "it's a V-shape neuron". The near-miss test wrote its examples before measuring and includes a distant-concept control. Outside checks so far point the same way in direction: Julian Bradshaw's spot checks of the 1,000 released Qwen3 neurons found the claims "hold up reasonably well", and Jasper Blank's GPT-2 XL rerun found a modern model explains far more neurons than GPT-4 did (8% against 0.3% on the 2023 metric).

Where the evidence is weaker than the wording.

  • The 90% cannot be checked from the essay. It comes from "preliminary audits" with no rubric, sample size or judge stated, and the essay itself says thresholds move it. The companion paper's measured figure, on a different model, is 69% of 5,468 neuron poles dominated by one mode (47% with exactly one). "1.7% in 2023, 90% now", as secondary coverage put it, compares different models, judges and definitions (Figure 6).
  • "No superposition" is argued from not finding it, with a narrow definition. The essay concedes representing more things than dimensions; Nanda's point that memorised facts outnumber neurons and may sit in weak activations is answered with an argument (rare facts ride related circuits) and the umbrella experiment, not a measurement in a language model.
  • Weak activations: cheap for loss, not for reasoning. The essay's evidence is mostly vision (69.47% against 70.08%) and one Qwen neuron. The companion paper measures that keeping only the strongest 7.8% of routed activations in Qwen3.6-35B-A3B moves web-text loss from 1.886 to 1.940, but reasoning collapses into loops until a light fine-tune, which it ties to the double-negative quadrant. leogao reports that ablating small activations substantially degrades models (his data not yet shared).
  • Agents judging agents, on the authors' own models. Neuron papers, labels, near-miss texts and their scoring are LLM-written; the ConvNeXts and their squeezed variants were trained by the author, and no code is released. leogao's objection applies: a pattern that fits the top examples can still miss which inputs of the pattern never fire the neuron, which a downstream-KL simulation would catch (the near-miss test covers part of this, at five texts each way).
  • Recursive generalization explains after the fact. The Colorado neuron that also fires on the Python framework Tornado is causally real (asked where Tornado was developed, Qwen3-32B gives Colorado 30% and California 0.7%) though rare (about 1.2% of the neuron's effect, companion paper). It fits "rare, unimportant offshoots", but no one predicted the combination; the essay says it has not tested recursive generalization across a whole model.

Verdict. The diagnosis of the old examples is convincing and the method lessons (both poles, circuits, guided visualization, explanations that can lose) are worth adopting now. "Already clean" for frontier language models is a well-argued working hypothesis, as the essay's own byline calls it, not a measured result: the numbers that would decide it (what share of a frontier model's computation its neurons explain, measured by downstream effect) are only beginning to appear, in the companion paper, and are lower and more qualified than the essay's headline.

    "Interesting work, but I don't think it's a significant update to the big picture." Agrees neuron-based exploratory work (neuron attribution graphs) may be practical, and that it is far easier on a frontier model than training dictionaries; disagrees with "solving interpretability" readings. Still holds superposition is real: more concepts than residual dimensions, and far more memorised facts than neurons, which he expects to sit in weak activations whose ablation would hurt; doubts the vision results transfer; finds the essay's definition of monosemantic too loose.

  • rebuttalNeural networks are not already clean
    leogao (LessWrong; 371 karma, 24 comments on 27 November), 17 October 2026

    Accepts the observation that people wrote neurons off as polysemantic too fast. Argues a pattern that fits the top examples is not an explanation: it can miss which inputs of the pattern never fire it (precision against recall) and what the activation magnitudes carry. Proposes simulation scored by downstream KL on a large dataset, which would have flagged "cats and cars" too; reports that in his results ablating small activations substantially degrades models (data not yet shared); raised the Colorado and Tornado neuron as a counterexample.

  • critiqueWhy Clean Neurons Are Not Enough
    Goutham Nalagatla and Carlos Guerrero Alvarez (LessWrong), 20 October 2026

    Grants the empirical claim and asks what follows: a coherent abstraction (the bow-tie neuron with its dress shoes and flip-flops) need not have a compact description, and knowing what each neuron responds to is not knowing how they compose into a computation at frontier scale. Asks for a fixed-budget comparison of neuron-based against SAE-based explanations.

  • replicationIs GPT 2XL already clean?
    Jasper Blank (LessWrong), 25 October 2026

    Reran OpenAI's 2023 explain-and-simulate method with Claude Opus 5.5 on 50 random GPT-2 XL neurons (layers 8 to 40): about 8% scored 0.8 or more (3 to 19% across his thresholds), against about 0.3% for GPT-4 in 2023; "if clean means every neuron can be explained in a single sentence, GPT-2 XL is not clean." leogao replied that the 2023 method itself should not be replicated.

  • commentaryTakes on "Already Clean"
    neverix (LessWrong), 6 November 2026

    From someone who worked on neuron-basis interpretability: the agenda is promising, and neurons give an "overlay" of probes and steering handles native to the model; but high activations are probably not expressive enough for gears-level explanation (the wallet graph does not say why honesty connects to the wallet), and steering single neurons worked less well in MoE models.

  • critiqueDatasets Determine the Difficulty of Interpretability
    testingthewaters (LessWrong), 14 November 2026

    On the companion paper: the distribution of text a neuron is explained on decides how clean it looks, and interpretability is wanted for behaviour on inputs nobody anticipated, where a corpus-based explanation has not been tested.

Where it moves the knowledge base

The knowledge base has no interpretability page and is not getting one; the essay touches the pages below.

  • Activation functions (gated units): SwiGLU's double-negative quadrant (gate and value both negative give a positive output, at most about 0.278 × |value|) dominates the top activations of about 4% of Qwen3-32B neurons and about a third of all activations in Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, and is tied to long reasoning; a property of the gate the page does not yet name.
  • Activation functions (Inside real models, "sparse activations"): in a large SwiGLU MoE most weak activations are functionally removable for loss (strongest 7.8% kept: 1.886 to 1.940 on web text) but not for reasoning.
  • Mixture of experts (what the router learned): beside "routing tracks the token, not the topic", the essay's agent-judged finding that Qwen3.6's 10,240 experts are narrowly themed and narrow with depth, and its argument that experts resist superposition. Different measures (router predictability from the token against what an expert's neurons respond to), so an extension, not a contradiction.
  • The transformer from zero: a feed-forward hidden unit has a negative pole that carries meaning (Santa lives on one) and can act as a gate between routes.
  • Sparks of Weight-Space Programming: the two articles meet at steering; that page's Qwen edit was a direction written into one matrix, this essay checks single neurons by steering them.
Since the article (checked 27 November 2026): the companion paper and its discussion

What a language-model neuron is here: one hidden unit of a block's feed-forward layer, which reads from the residual stream through its input row and writes back through one column of the down projection.

Routers and experts; that page's measured "routing tracks the token, not the topic" sits beside the essay's claim that experts are far more specialised than reported.

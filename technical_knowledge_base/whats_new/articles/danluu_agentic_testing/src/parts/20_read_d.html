How much of this to believe

What holds up: the reading of the runs, the negative result, and the cost of the big skills.

  • The transcript reading is specific and checkable in principle. Counts such as 159 of 160 TLA+ models with no change to the code, 10 structured fuzzers out of 160, and 63 single-property QuickCheck runs come from reading what the agents did, and they describe one consistent failure; the post's comment thread, and practitioners in it, report the same thing from their own work (next section).
  • "Naming a technique does not help" survives the statistics. No condition differs from Default at the 5% level by Fisher's exact test, at either effort (0 of 29 each; about 1.5 would be expected by chance alone), and the largest gain over Default, Audit's +5.0 points at xhigh (p = 0.52), is under a third of what the design can detect.
  • The cost differences are real. Cost per run varies far more between conditions than its sampling spread allows (chi-square 190 and 103 on 29 degrees of freedom, using the chart's own bootstrap spreads): the Hegel skill's +26% and +41% are 3.5 and 4.1 standard errors, Kani's +28% at medium 3.9.
  • He guards against himself. Guesses were written down first, the ordering comes with its own warning, the chart prints n and bootstrap uncertainty, and the eval's setup bugs (leaked hidden tests, uneven tooling) were found and fixed before the language results were trusted.
Predict before you draw: suppose no instruction mattered at all, so every condition's 80 xhigh runs pass with the same 77.5% chance. How far apart would the best and the worst of the 30 land: about 3 points, about 8, or about 18? Then press "Draw a no-effect world" beside this text.

What it does not show.

  • The order of the 30 conditions is mostly noise, and the post's sections follow it. With 80 runs a side, a difference from Default has to be about 21 points at medium and 17 at xhigh to be detected four times in five. At xhigh the 30 conditions are as alike as 30 draws of one 77.5% coin (chi-square 22.6 on 29 degrees of freedom, p = 0.80), and their 17.5-point spread is what such draws give on average (18.8); every condition with 80 runs sits inside the range where 95% of single no-effect conditions land, 67.5% to 86.3% at xhigh (all 29 conditions). At medium the spread is a little wider than chance (p = 0.04; 23.8 points against 21.8 expected) and the band, 52.5% to 73.8% at medium (27 of 29 inside, Verus below and Fuzz risky above), misses two; but no single condition stands out once 29 comparisons are corrected for.
  • A condition's medium score does not predict its xhigh score (correlation −0.03 across the 30; Fig. 5). If instructions had stable effects, the same ones would lead at both efforts; Fuzz risky going from 1st of 30 at medium to 25th at xhigh, and ECC from 5th to 28th, is what pure noise looks like.
  • So the per-technique verdicts are not established: TDD "underperformed" (−7.5 points at xhigh, p = 0.34), Default did "well above average" (15th of 30 at medium, 6th at xhigh), Proptest and PBT scored "well above average", his skill was best. His prediction scorecard counts "TDD underperforms" as true on this evidence.
  • One model, one harness, one task, single turns. GPT-5.6 Sol in Codex on a bit-manipulation decoder with a precise spec. His follow-up shows how fast this moves: with GPT-6 Astra the same eval is saturated and agents use several techniques properly. Commenters note Claude is more eager with property-based tests.
  • Nothing is released. No prompts (the exact wording of each condition, or of "audit"), transcripts, hidden tests or scoring code, so the counts from reading runs cannot be checked and nobody can rerun a condition. The 37 hidden tests were written by an agent, and the score counts only runs passing all of them. ACL2 dropped 28 runs that ran out of memory. The post could not be compared with an archived copy: the blog is not in the Wayback Machine.

Verdict. Believe the qualitative finding: a bare instruction to use a technique produced a caricature of it, and long tutorial skills cost more for nothing. Believe the negative result in its narrow form: on this task, no instruction measurably beat saying nothing. Treat every per-technique ranking, including TDD's and the author's skill's, as unmeasured; the design could not have seen differences under about 17 points.

The rank test in one picture. Each condition is a dot at its medium score (across) and its xhigh score (up). Effects that belong to the instruction would line the dots up from lower left to upper right; the cloud has no slope at all (correlation −0.03, p = 0.87). The author saw something related: results at max and xhigh were highly correlated with each other and quite different from medium ({{in the article|ar:results were highly correlated between max and xhigh}}), which fits effort changing behaviour more than the instruction does; the max runs are not published.

What the responses said

The Hacker News thread (191 points, 71 comments on 27 November 2026) agreed with the finding and objected to the method's opacity; the one tool author who replied agreed his skill was weak and rewrote it. No rebuttal with its own measurements was found. Practitioners' reports point the same way as the post: formal tools help when a person writes the specification, mutation testing helps when it gates the build, and fresh-context review is where they spend their tokens.

Where the responses are right. The reproducibility complaint is correct and is part of the verdict. The specification and gate points are fair limits of scope rather than errors: the post tests what a bare instruction does, as its first paragraph says, and the follow-up shows that a stronger model starts to use the techniques properly on its own.

Where it moves the knowledge base

  • {{Testing and quality|n:3c65c17b0d0d81548848e2ebdd2f63ee}}, section 13 (AI-written changes): extends "check the tests would fail if the code were wrong" with measured evidence that an agent's own tests are the weak point: told to use a technique, agents wrote palindromic or identical inputs and expected values copied from their own output; property-based tests and fuzzers fed rejected random bytes.
  • {{Building a harness|n:3c65c17b0d0d817ca14fc32338707c2f}}, the verifier as stop rule: a verifier the agent writes itself is only as strong as its tests; at xhigh agents nearly always got their own tests to pass while 14% to 31% of runs, depending on the condition, still failed hidden tests.
  • {{Eval statistics|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8187a7b4e5b4ec4a546d}}: a worked case of a 30-way comparison at 80 runs a side, where the spread of the arms equals what one shared rate produces, and of the medium-against-xhigh rank check.

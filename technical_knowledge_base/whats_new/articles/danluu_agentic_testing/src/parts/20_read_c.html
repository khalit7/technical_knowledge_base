6. Skills: tutorials cost tokens; a five-line nudge did best

[[ah:hegel|Hegel, and the skill sections]]

The three public skills read like tutorials, and they cost more without helping. Hegel's official skill (34k characters plus a 45k-character Rust reference, over 20k tokens) was loaded in 157 of 160 runs and re-read on many steps; even at a 99.85% cache hit rate this added 16% (medium) and 18% (xhigh) to the bill, and its prescribed workflow added more work on top, for a total of 26% and 41% more than Hegel without the skill. Correctness was slightly worse, within noise ({{in the article|ar:This was loaded at the start of the run}}). The extra tests were no-panic checks and round trips of properties already likely to hold. Trail of Bits' skill was opened in only 108 of 160 runs, and its advice to add Proptest needed an approval that single-turn runs could not give.

ECC's score looks fine only because many agents ignored it. Agents read it in 153 of 160 runs, and the earlier they read it the more tests they added and the worse they did; the 7 that never read it and the 9 that read it late (nearly all at medium) were all fully correct. Scored only on runs it influenced, it falls between the built-in framework and Creusot, below average ({{in the article|ar:ECC seemed to do ok in terms of raw score because the 7 agents}}). He adds that 160 runs of a skill is more than anyone reasonable would do, and the top-line number still misleads.

His own skill scored highest, 73.8% and 83.8%, though not for the reasons it was written. Five lines: before implementing, list likely subtle mistakes and a check where the alternatives differ, using asymmetric inputs on both sides of each boundary; re-derive high-risk results in a fresh context; prefer property-based or randomised inputs but spend little on no-crash fuzzing; randomise towards interesting states; check unsure details independently ({{in the article|ar:Think about areas likely to have subtle bugs before implementing}}). Agents almost never used a fresh context, and in medium run 35 an agent flagged the bitstream reversal as risky, audited it, and still passed a palindromic test (Fig. 3, panel A). The skill is written to push the model away from its default behaviour rather than teach it a technique, which he argues is what a skill for a model that already knows the subject should do.

His conclusion about skills is narrower than it sounds. Two further unpublished experiments with companies' official skills also made results worse; prompting style that works changes between model versions (GPT-5.5 to 5.6) and between Claude and Codex, so he doubts generic testing skills and expects to write small personal ones for one harness, model and effort. Skills that teach a workflow or an interface, such as driving a browser, look different and he expects them to work ({{in the article|ah:naive-thoughts-on-skill-writing}}).

7. What does work: a structure, then a few sentences of steering

[[ah:how-do-you-get-agents-to-write-good-tests|How do you get agents to write good tests?]]

His experience, not measured here, is that agents test well once a person sets up the test and triage structure and looks at what they did. He leans on randomised testing; Jamie Brandon reports the same for snapshot tests, after moving tests into a separate crate and stating in AGENTS.md that they stay there and the public interface does not change. Without that, Brandon's agents claimed to write snapshot tests and wrote unit tests. "If you look at what it does and type a few more sentences", agents get to a good place quickly; his guess is that the knowledge is in the model but far from its default, too far for naming a technique to reach ({{in the article|ar:somewhere inside the model is some kind of understanding how to do this stuff}}).

His pre-registered guesses and how he scored them; the appendices

Predictions, as scored in the post ({{in the article|ah:prediction-accuracy}}): TDD underperforms (55%), scored true; formal methods do not outperform (52%), true, "but not for the reason I expected"; "Make no mistakes" does not beat no instruction (95%), true; the ECC, Hegel and ToB skills will not outperform (65%, 65%, 55%), all true. Two implicit guesses were wrong: Lean would do well among formal tools, and his own skill would be mediocre (Max Bittker had predicted it would work, and why). Read against the statistics in the next section, the four "will not outperform" guesses are consistent with the data; "TDD underperforms" is not established, since TDD is within chance of Default at both efforts.

IMAP details ({{in the article|ah:appendix-experimental-details}}): only one run of all conditions reached a perfect IMAP score ("Make no mistakes", medium: 2.5%). TLA+, a natural fit for IMAP state, was used after coding in 75 of 80 runs, modelled a small mailbox mutation and the CONDSTORE and QRESYNC extensions (where Default already passed over 99.6% of tests) and never the observers, event queues or UIDVALIDITY epochs where the bugs were. All his evals are RFCs, whose specs are clearer than what users give agents; he expects real tasks to fail the same way or worse. Two early "compelling" results turned out to be setup errors and vanished when fixed.

Agent silliness: a subagent ran a catastrophically backtracking Perl regex over a 44 kB file for 2 hours 20 minutes after it had itself stopped; his own FRE engine and Rust's regex crate each finished in under a second. Footnotes: languages' agent-friendliness, like poker's "range advantage", is a set of nice-sounding explanations that the measured failures do not support.

The idea in one sentence: PyTorch fills a new weight matrix on an NVIDIA GPU with 2,048 threads per streaming multiprocessor, each drawing its own fixed stream of random numbers from the seed, so the seed decides which numbers a model starts with and the chip's SM count decides where they land; weights that training never touched still hold that arrangement, and DePue reads it back to find that six recent Chinese models were started on Blackwell-class chips (148 or 152 SMs) where the same labs' earlier models were started on Hopper (132).

You need: what a streaming multiprocessor is and how many each chip has ({{GPU architecture|n:3c65c17b0d0d810682aae39bd614ec64}}); threads, blocks and grids ({{The CUDA programming model|n:3c65c17b0d0d8162865eec15d9dae87e}}); that a model's starting weights are random normal values ({{Normalisation and initialisation|n:3c65c17b0d0d81369e1cc38ed4e11d48}}); a correlation coefficient and a z-score. Nothing about export law: the page says only what the report and its sources say.

New terms: seed (the number a pseudorandom generator starts from; same seed, same numbers); Philox (the counter-based generator cuRAND uses: any thread computes its own stream from the seed and its thread number, with no shared state); G (this page's name for the total thread count PyTorch launches, 2,048 times the SM count for a large tensor: the quantity the weights actually record); untouched row (an embedding row for a token that never occurred in training, so no gradient ever changed it); sigma (how many standard deviations a match sits above what chance gives; for a correlation r over n values, about r√n).

The claim: four labs' newest models read as Blackwell, their earlier ones as Hopper

[[ah:executive-summary|Executive Summary, Table 1]]

Six models from DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax and Ant Group, released from April 2026, carry a starting-weight arrangement that only a Blackwell-class layout reproduces. DeepSeek-V4-Flash and MiniMax-M3 match the 148-SM layout the report assigns to the B200 and B300; DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi K3, Kimi K3.1-Pro and Ling-3.1-flash match the 152-SM layout of the GB200 and GB300 ({{in the article|ar:Six models from four Chinese labs read as Blackwell-trained}}). K3.1-Pro uses K3's seed and may be a further-trained checkpoint of it, so the six are five independent starts.

The switch is recent and lab by lab. Each lab's earlier models read as Hopper: DeepSeek through V3 (December 2024), Moonshot through K2.5 (January 2026), MiniMax through M2.7 (April 2026), Ant Group through Ling-3.0-flash (August 2026) ({{in the article|ar:Chinese labs started training on Blackwell relatively recently}}). Kimi K2.7-Code (June 2026) is a blend: about 80% of its untouched rows are K2.5's Hopper values and about 20% a new 148-SM draw, the same mix in every row, which the report reads as a merged checkpoint and does not count ({{Appendix 1|ar:The blend is the same in every row}}). The newest models it could read from Alibaba, Zhipu, Tencent, Xiaomi and StepFun all read as Hopper ({{in the article|ar:Not every Chinese AI lab has switched}}).

A Hopper reading names a class, not a chip. The H800 (the China-market Hopper), H100 SXM and H200 all have 132 SMs, so they leave the same arrangement; the "lab disclosed H800" labels in Table 1 come from the labs' own reports, not from the method ({{Table 1 note|ar:Hopper fingerprint cannot distinguish an H800}}).

Its scope, in its own words: the findings "provide technical evidence of access to advanced computing hardware, but do not establish where the chips were located, how access was obtained, or whether export controls were violated" ({{in the article|ar:do not establish where the chips were located}}).

How big the runs were: estimates the author revised twice on launch day

[[ah:table-2|Main Findings, Table 2]]

If each model was trained on the hardware that started it, the two largest runs needed thousands of Blackwell GPUs. The current version estimates about 4,500 for Kimi K3 and 3,000 for DeepSeek-V4-Pro, roughly 11,500 and 7,500 H100 equivalents, and about 12,000 Blackwell GPUs across the five runs; at about $70,000 a GPU (the price implied by a reported $2.5 billion for 36,000 Blackwell chips in Malaysia, $69,444 each), the largest cluster would cost $200 to $300 million and the five more than half a billion dollars ({{in the article|ar:At roughly $70,000 per GPU}}). The arithmetic: 6 times active parameters times tokens, divided by one GPU's output at an assumed 20% of peak (25% for DeepSeek) over a run length guessed from release dates.

The author says he is not confident in them ({{in the article|ar:We are not confident in these estimates}}): utilisation and run length are unpublished, most token counts come from an unpublished method, and halving the run length doubles the count. At the pace of Microsoft's MAI-Base-2 (16,000 GPUs, 38 days) the two largest runs would have needed 8,000 to 9,000.

The archived copies show how soft the numbers are. The first version (12 November, 21:56 UTC) assumed 35% utilisation, gave 500 to 3,000 GPUs per run, and checked the arithmetic on DeepSeek-V3: 2,000 to 2,500 against the 2,048 H800s DeepSeek disclosed. Four hours later the utilisation was lowered, the best estimates rose (Kimi K3 from 2,000 to 3,000 to 4,500; MiniMax-M3 from 500 to 600 to 1,500), and that one calibration against a disclosed cluster was gone; the revision note explains the first change but not the second ({{the revision note|ar:The first version of this report gave}}).

Why the weights record the chip: the seed fixes the numbers, the thread count fixes where they go

[[ah:investigation|Our investigation, Figures 1 to 3]]

Philox gives every GPU thread its own stream, computed from the seed and the thread's number alone. It is a counter-based generator: value k of thread t is a fixed function of (seed, t, k), with no state shared between threads, so the same seed yields the same streams on any chip ({{Philox paper|https://www.thesalmons.org/john/random123/papers/random123sc11.pdf}}). That is what makes parallel initialisation reproducible, and it is half of the fingerprint.

The other half is how many threads PyTorch launches, which the chip decides. For normal_ and uniform_ on CUDA, PyTorch launches blocks of 256 threads, as many as the GPU can hold at once (its SM count times 2,048 threads per SM, divided by 256), capped only by the tensor's size; thread t then writes positions t, t + G, t + 2G and so on, where G is the total thread count, four values per Philox call ({{PyTorch, DistributionTemplates.h lines 49 to 81|https://github.com/pytorch/pytorch/blob/58c7687e2fc0e919f1fcc5ec5989ed4b32ed845a/aten/src/ATen/native/cuda/DistributionTemplates.h#L49-L81}}, read 27 November 2026; {{in the article|ar:a sufficiently large initialization uses the maximum}}). So position i holds value (seed, i mod G, i div G). On a Hopper G is 132 × 2,048 = 270,336; on the report's B200 and B300 303,104; on its GB200 and GB300 311,296.

Change G and the same numbers land in different places. The figure is the report's toy, rebuilt with the real generator: 48 threads (6 SMs of 8) wrote the "published" grid; pick another SM count to regenerate it with the same seed. A grid is reproduced only when both the seed and G are right ({{in the article|ar:Reproducing the weights therefore requires two things}}).

Predict, then try 5 SMs: which cells will still match?

The first 40, one full pass of the threads, and nothing after. For i below both thread counts, thread i writes its first value at position i on either chip, so the start of every tensor is identical everywhere; past that, the placements disagree. That is why the evidence must come from positions far beyond G, such as embedding rows deep in a table of about a billion entries. The report's own toy shows 6% matching for the same reason ({{in the article|ar:only 6 percent of the values match}}).

What survives training: untouched rows exactly, trained weights faintly

[[ah:figure-4|Our investigation, Figures 4 to 6]]

Rows of the embedding table whose token never appears in training keep their starting values exactly. No gradient reaches them, so they can be regenerated bit for bit ({{in the article|ar:Some weights, such as those reserved for words}}). Typical cases are reserved special tokens and the 13 byte values that cannot occur in valid UTF-8, as one reimplementation notes ({{peterjliu's README|https://github.com/peterjliu/checkpoint-forensics-repro}}). Regenerate such a row under the right seed and G and the correlation is above 0.999; under any other G it is near zero.

Weights that training did change keep a weak trace. A final weight is its start plus every update, so across many weights the starts still predict the ends slightly: averaged over 745,472 weights per layer in 42 layers of DeepSeek-V4-Flash, r = 0.066, 57 sigma, under the Blackwell layout and nothing under Hopper's ({{Figure 5|ar:r = 0.066, 57 sigma}}). The report calls this a finding in these weights, not a guarantee for every run ({{in the article|ar:Even weights that training has changed can retain}}). It is the evidence behind the readings marked "partial survival".

The seed comes from a search, the verdict from rows the search did not use. Seeds were brute-forced over all 232 values and layouts, about six trillion combinations; the chosen pair is then tested on a different set of weights without refitting ({{in the article|ar:We then test that combination on weights}}). Move the slider to see what a wrong layout looks like on real data: a round cloud, r within a few hundredths of zero.

How strong is one row? Over n = 1,024 values a correlation from chance has a standard deviation of about 1/√n, 0.031, so the peak, r about 1, is about 32 sigma, and the best of the 60 wrong layouts sits near 3. The report's bar for a whole search is about 8 sigma: five sigma happens by chance once in 3.5 million comparisons, which six trillion comparisons would see many times over ({{in the article|ar:We therefore use approximately eight sigma}}). Its six Blackwell readings score 49 to 1,385 sigma, the weakest single test 20.

What the fingerprint cannot say

[[ah:limitations|Limits of our investigation]]

It needs published weights and a generator that leaves a layout. Closed models, PaddlePaddle's generator (ERNIE-5.5) and CPU-drawn weights (several Qwen3 models) give nothing; Meituan's LongCat-2.0 and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash gave no result after an exhaustive search ({{in the article|ar:only works for certain models}}).

It names the hardware that drew the starting weights, not the hardware that trained them. A lab could draw them on one system and train on another, or continue an old model on new chips; a mixed cluster could leave an NVIDIA layout and no trace of, say, Huawei chips that trained later ({{in the article|ar:not every chip that later helped train}}). Against the one-workstation reading, the report notes that Kimi K3's weights came from at least 64 separately seeded processes, all with the 152-SM layout ({{in the article|ar:In Kimi K3, at least 64 separately seeded}}); MiniMax-M3's embedding likewise came in four shards seeded 2760 plus the shard number, in the author's code. Both look like initialising inside a sharded parallel job, which makes a single borrowed machine less likely but does not prove the run stayed there.

It says nothing about where the chips were or who owned them. Blackwell systems can be rented in data centres outside China; the evidence concerns access, not smuggling or a violation ({{in the article|ar:The weights also cannot reveal who owned}}). Renting abroad was then legal; a US rule of 16 November now requires licences for such data centres ({{our 2026-11-23 issue|n:4215c17b0d0d816998d5f63c4ab8fd94}}).

What it measures is G; the chip is read from a table. Different machines can give the same G, and the table on the right lists the ones that matter. Older NVIDIA chips cannot reach 148 or 152 (software can use fewer SMs, never more), and the China-market Blackwell graphics cards use a different number of threads per SM. An AMD MI300X can: limited to 148 of its compute units, or split into two partitions of 152, it reproduces both Blackwell layouts on a physical chip ({{in the article|ar:AMD presents a different possibility}}). The report separates the vendors by one step of the generator that AMD and NVIDIA compute slightly differently: in the 377 values of DeepSeek-V4-Flash where they disagree, the published weight matches NVIDIA's in 363 and AMD's in none ({{in the article|ar:in the 377 values where the two vendors}}). For the other five models that test could not decide, and the AMD route rests on its unlikelihood (a whole cluster configured to imitate a Blackwell's SM count) ({{in the article|ar:The same test could not settle the other five}}).

Verification: real chips, a one-off seed change, and 24 known answers

[[ah:verification|Verifying our findings]]

Stock PyTorch on physical GPUs reproduces the rows only where the layout says it should. DeepSeek uses one seed, 953049583, for both V3 and V4-Flash. On an H100 it regenerates all 57,344 untouched values of V3 bit for bit and none of V4-Flash; on a B300 it regenerates 461 of V4-Flash's 469 selected rows above r 0.99 (445 bit for bit; the other eight were partly trained) and none of V3. On a rented GB200, Moonshot's seed 20256586 regenerates all 13 of Kimi K3's rows. The seed plus one regenerates nothing on any machine ({{in the article|ar:Changing the seed by one reproduced no matching rows}}). These are the author's runs; the code to repeat them is public.

Where a developer named its hardware, the reading agrees, 24 times out of 24. Llama 3.1 (H100) reads 132, eight Pythia models (A100) read 108, Nemotron-3 Ultra (GB200 for one phase) reads 152, Zyphra's ZAYA1 reads as a stock MI300X ({{in the article|ar:There are 24 cases where a developer}}). The report itself warns that 11 of these are step-zero checkpoints and related models share initialisations, so this is a consistency check, not an accuracy rate ({{Appendix 2|ar:Eleven of these are step-zero checkpoints}}).

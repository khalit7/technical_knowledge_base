Top Chinese Frontier AI Models Carry the Fingerprint of Export-Controlled NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs

Will DePue (Unemployment Labs) · published 12 November 2026

In one line: A forensic report with public code: it regenerates embedding rows that training never touched from a recovered seed and finds they line up only under one GPU thread count (2,048 threads per SM times the SM count), so six open-weight models from DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax and Ant Group released April to November 2026 read as initialised on Blackwell-class layouts (148 or 152 SMs) where the same labs' earlier models read as Hopper (132); by the report's own scoping this identifies the hardware class that created the starting weights, not where the chips were, who owned them, whether the whole run used them, or whether any law was broken.

Updated: within about nine hours of publication (archived copies from 12 November 21:56 UTC to 13 November 06:59 UTC) the GPU-count estimates were revised twice, a DeepSeek-V3 sanity check of that arithmetic was removed, and "available to reviewers on request" became a public code repository; the report labels the revision 12 November (US time), and nothing has changed since.

The idea in one sentence: PyTorch fills a new weight matrix on an NVIDIA GPU with 2,048 threads per streaming multiprocessor, each drawing its own fixed stream of random numbers from the seed, so the seed decides which numbers a model starts with and the chip's SM count decides where they land; weights that training never touched still hold that arrangement, and DePue reads it back to find that six recent Chinese models were started on Blackwell-class chips (148 or 152 SMs) where the same labs' earlier models were started on Hopper (132).

You need: what a streaming multiprocessor is and how many each chip has (GPU architecture); threads, blocks and grids (The CUDA programming model); that a model's starting weights are random normal values (Normalisation and initialisation); a correlation coefficient and a z-score. Nothing about export law: the page says only what the report and its sources say.

New terms: seed (the number a pseudorandom generator starts from; same seed, same numbers); Philox (the counter-based generator cuRAND uses: any thread computes its own stream from the seed and its thread number, with no shared state); G (this page's name for the total thread count PyTorch launches, 2,048 times the SM count for a large tensor: the quantity the weights actually record); untouched row (an embedding row for a token that never occurred in training, so no gradient ever changed it); sigma (how many standard deviations a match sits above what chance gives; for a correlation r over n values, about r√n).

The claim: four labs' newest models read as Blackwell, their earlier ones as Hopper

Executive Summary, Table 1

Six models from DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax and Ant Group, released from April 2026, carry a starting-weight arrangement that only a Blackwell-class layout reproduces. DeepSeek-V4-Flash and MiniMax-M3 match the 148-SM layout the report assigns to the B200 and B300; DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi K3, Kimi K3.1-Pro and Ling-3.1-flash match the 152-SM layout of the GB200 and GB300 (in the article). K3.1-Pro uses K3's seed and may be a further-trained checkpoint of it, so the six are five independent starts.

The switch is recent and lab by lab. Each lab's earlier models read as Hopper: DeepSeek through V3 (December 2024), Moonshot through K2.5 (January 2026), MiniMax through M2.7 (April 2026), Ant Group through Ling-3.0-flash (August 2026) (in the article). Kimi K2.7-Code (June 2026) is a blend: about 80% of its untouched rows are K2.5's Hopper values and about 20% a new 148-SM draw, the same mix in every row, which the report reads as a merged checkpoint and does not count (Appendix 1). The newest models it could read from Alibaba, Zhipu, Tencent, Xiaomi and StepFun all read as Hopper (in the article).

A Hopper reading names a class, not a chip. The H800 (the China-market Hopper), H100 SXM and H200 all have 132 SMs, so they leave the same arrangement; the "lab disclosed H800" labels in Table 1 come from the labs' own reports, not from the method (Table 1 note).

Its scope, in its own words: the findings "provide technical evidence of access to advanced computing hardware, but do not establish where the chips were located, how access was obtained, or whether export controls were violated" (in the article).

How big the runs were: estimates the author revised twice on launch day

Main Findings, Table 2

If each model was trained on the hardware that started it, the two largest runs needed thousands of Blackwell GPUs. The current version estimates about 4,500 for Kimi K3 and 3,000 for DeepSeek-V4-Pro, roughly 11,500 and 7,500 H100 equivalents, and about 12,000 Blackwell GPUs across the five runs; at about $70,000 a GPU (the price implied by a reported $2.5 billion for 36,000 Blackwell chips in Malaysia, $69,444 each), the largest cluster would cost $200 to $300 million and the five more than half a billion dollars (in the article). The arithmetic: 6 times active parameters times tokens, divided by one GPU's output at an assumed 20% of peak (25% for DeepSeek) over a run length guessed from release dates.

The author says he is not confident in them (in the article): utilisation and run length are unpublished, most token counts come from an unpublished method, and halving the run length doubles the count. At the pace of Microsoft's MAI-Base-2 (16,000 GPUs, 38 days) the two largest runs would have needed 8,000 to 9,000.

The archived copies show how soft the numbers are. The first version (12 November, 21:56 UTC) assumed 35% utilisation, gave 500 to 3,000 GPUs per run, and checked the arithmetic on DeepSeek-V3: 2,000 to 2,500 against the 2,048 H800s DeepSeek disclosed. Four hours later the utilisation was lowered, the best estimates rose (Kimi K3 from 2,000 to 3,000 to 4,500; MiniMax-M3 from 500 to 600 to 1,500), and that one calibration against a disclosed cluster was gone; the revision note explains the first change but not the second (the revision note). The figure shows all three versions.

Why the weights record the chip: the seed fixes the numbers, the thread count fixes where they go

Our investigation, Figures 1 to 3

Philox gives every GPU thread its own stream, computed from the seed and the thread's number alone. It is a counter-based generator: value k of thread t is a fixed function of (seed, t, k), with no state shared between threads, so the same seed yields the same streams on any chip (Philox paper). That is what makes parallel initialisation reproducible, and it is half of the fingerprint.

The other half is how many threads PyTorch launches, which the chip decides. For normal_ and uniform_ on CUDA, PyTorch launches blocks of 256 threads, as many as the GPU can hold at once (its SM count times 2,048 threads per SM, divided by 256), capped only by the tensor's size; thread t then writes positions t, t + G, t + 2G and so on, where G is the total thread count, four values per Philox call (PyTorch, DistributionTemplates.h lines 49 to 81, read 27 November 2026; in the article). So position i holds value (seed, i mod G, i div G). On a Hopper G is 132 × 2,048 = 270,336; on the report's B200 and B300 303,104; on its GB200 and GB300 311,296.

Change G and the same numbers land in different places. The figure is the report's toy, rebuilt with the real generator: 48 threads (6 SMs of 8) wrote the "published" grid; pick another SM count to regenerate it with the same seed. A grid is reproduced only when both the seed and G are right (in the article).

Predict, then try 5 SMs: which cells will still match?

The first 40, one full pass of the threads, and nothing after. For i below both thread counts, thread i writes its first value at position i on either chip, so the start of every tensor is identical everywhere; past that, the placements disagree. That is why the evidence must come from positions far beyond G, such as embedding rows deep in a table of about a billion entries. The report's own toy shows 6% matching for the same reason (in the article).

What survives training: untouched rows exactly, trained weights faintly

Our investigation, Figures 4 to 6

Rows of the embedding table whose token never appears in training keep their starting values exactly. No gradient reaches them, so they can be regenerated bit for bit (in the article). Typical cases are reserved special tokens and the 13 byte values that cannot occur in valid UTF-8, as one reimplementation notes (peterjliu's README). Regenerate such a row under the right seed and G and the correlation is above 0.999; under any other G it is near zero.

Weights that training did change keep a weak trace. A final weight is its start plus every update, so across many weights the starts still predict the ends slightly: averaged over 745,472 weights per layer in 42 layers of DeepSeek-V4-Flash, r = 0.066, 57 sigma, under the Blackwell layout and nothing under Hopper's (Figure 5). The report calls this a finding in these weights, not a guarantee for every run (in the article). It is the evidence behind the readings marked "partial survival".

The seed comes from a search, the verdict from rows the search did not use. Seeds were brute-forced over all 232 values and layouts, about six trillion combinations; the chosen pair is then tested on a different set of weights without refitting (in the article). Move the slider to see what a wrong layout looks like on real data: a round cloud, r within a few hundredths of zero.

How strong is one row? Over n = 1,024 values a correlation from chance has a standard deviation of about 1/√n, 0.031, so the peak, r about 1, is about 32 sigma, and the best of the 60 wrong layouts sits near 3. The report's bar for a whole search is about 8 sigma: five sigma happens by chance once in 3.5 million comparisons, which six trillion comparisons would see many times over (in the article). Its six Blackwell readings score 49 to 1,385 sigma, the weakest single test 20.

What the fingerprint cannot say

Limits of our investigation

It needs published weights and a generator that leaves a layout. Closed models, PaddlePaddle's generator (ERNIE-5.5) and CPU-drawn weights (several Qwen3 models) give nothing; Meituan's LongCat-2.0 and DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash gave no result after an exhaustive search (in the article).

It names the hardware that drew the starting weights, not the hardware that trained them. A lab could draw them on one system and train on another, or continue an old model on new chips; a mixed cluster could leave an NVIDIA layout and no trace of, say, Huawei chips that trained later (in the article). Against the one-workstation reading, the report notes that Kimi K3's weights came from at least 64 separately seeded processes, all with the 152-SM layout (in the article); MiniMax-M3's embedding likewise came in four shards seeded 2760 plus the shard number, in the author's code. Both look like initialising inside a sharded parallel job, which makes a single borrowed machine less likely but does not prove the run stayed there.

It says nothing about where the chips were or who owned them. Blackwell systems can be rented in data centres outside China; the evidence concerns access, not smuggling or a violation (in the article). Renting abroad was then legal; a US rule of 16 November now requires licences for such data centres (our 2026-11-23 issue).

What it measures is G; the chip is read from a table. Different machines can give the same G, and the table on the right lists the ones that matter. Older NVIDIA chips cannot reach 148 or 152 (software can use fewer SMs, never more), and the China-market Blackwell graphics cards use a different number of threads per SM. An AMD MI300X can: limited to 148 of its compute units, or split into two partitions of 152, it reproduces both Blackwell layouts on a physical chip (in the article). The report separates the vendors by one step of the generator that AMD and NVIDIA compute slightly differently: in the 377 values of DeepSeek-V4-Flash where they disagree, the published weight matches NVIDIA's in 363 and AMD's in none (in the article). For the other five models that test could not decide, and the AMD route rests on its unlikelihood (a whole cluster configured to imitate a Blackwell's SM count) (in the article).

Verification: real chips, a one-off seed change, and 24 known answers

Verifying our findings

Stock PyTorch on physical GPUs reproduces the rows only where the layout says it should. DeepSeek uses one seed, 953049583, for both V3 and V4-Flash. On an H100 it regenerates all 57,344 untouched values of V3 bit for bit and none of V4-Flash; on a B300 it regenerates 461 of V4-Flash's 469 selected rows above r 0.99 (445 bit for bit; the other eight were partly trained) and none of V3. On a rented GB200, Moonshot's seed 20256586 regenerates all 13 of Kimi K3's rows. The seed plus one regenerates nothing on any machine (in the article). These are the author's runs; the code to repeat them is public.

Where a developer named its hardware, the reading agrees, 24 times out of 24. Llama 3.1 (H100) reads 132, eight Pythia models (A100) read 108, Nemotron-3 Ultra (GB200 for one phase) reads 152, Zyphra's ZAYA1 reads as a stock MI300X (in the article). The report itself warns that 11 of these are step-zero checkpoints and related models share initialisations, so this is a consistency check, not an accuracy rate (Appendix 2).

How much of this to believe

The readings hold up as well as a blog post's evidence can.

  • The mechanism is a documented code path, not a statistical pattern. Thirty lines of PyTorch, a deterministic generator, and a match at r above 0.9999 under one G only. Nothing to tune.
  • It was reproduced from outside within hours. Peter J. Liu's reimplementation recovered the seeds blind at the report's layouts and read V4-Pro and K3.1-Pro too; our own CPU replay and the figure above give the same peaks.
  • The author argues against himself. He raises the AMD alternative and excludes it only where his test can, calls his 24 for 24 a consistency check, and grades the conclusion ("conclusive" for one model's initialisation, "strong" for six, "strongly suggests" for training; Conclusion). That counts in his favour.

Where it is weaker, step by step along the chain in the hero figure:

  • From G to a chip name. The weights fix a thread count; that 148 means a B200 or B300 and 152 a GB200 or GB300 comes from the author's own devices, since NVIDIA does not publish those enabled SM counts. His runs on physical chips have not been repeated by others; the outside reimplementations check the arithmetic, not the hardware table. The MI300X route is excluded for one model of six.
  • From "initialised on" to "trained on". An inference the weights cannot prove, as the report says. What supports it: initialisation inside a sharded job (Kimi K3's 64 seeded processes, MiniMax-M3's four seeded shards), the 24 known cases, where the disclosed training hardware always matched the starting layout, Ant Group's public GB300 benchmark from March 2026, DeepSeek's own inference engine shipping SM100 kernels (published 16 October), and the US allegations the report cites. None of these is a measurement of the training run.
  • Where, whose, and whether legal: not addressed at all, by design. The coverage was looser than the report: The Register's headline says "training"; the author's own launch thread on X called the evidence "conclusive" for all four labs, where the report keeps that word for V4-Flash's initialisation.
  • The run sizes and costs are the softest part. Utilisation, run lengths and most token counts are assumed (the non-DeepSeek token counts from a method not yet published); the table changed twice in four hours; the only check against a disclosed cluster (DeepSeek-V3, 2,048 H800s) was dropped when the utilisation assumption fell, with no word on why.
  • The sigma figures assume a statistical model (independent values, a normal null). For exact copies that hardly matters: r near 1 over thousands of values is decisive under any model. For the "partial survival" readings in trained weights (the report's V4-Pro entry at 49 sigma, Kimi K2 at 292) the null model carries more weight; for V4-Pro the outside reimplementation found untouched rows anyway.
  • The author has a stake in the policy debate (a former OpenAI engineer, the thread noted), and "verified by senior researchers at OpenAI and Anthropic" names no one. The public code and rows answer both.
Two small loose ends

Seed 2760 appears twice. The report gives 2760 as the seed it recovered for Baidu's ERNIE-5.5 (in the article), and the author's code gives 2760 as MiniMax-M3's seed, which the outside reimplementation recovered blind. Either two labs chose the same small number or the ERNIE figure is a slip; it does not touch the MiniMax reading. Unconfirmed.

DeepSeek-V4-Pro's evidence is stronger than the report says. Appendix 1 lists it as partial survival at 49 sigma, the weakest of the six; the outside reimplementation found 468 untouched rows at r 0.9999 (1,831 sigma) at the same layout.

VerdictBelieve that these six models' starting weights were drawn by PyTorch's CUDA generator under 148- or 152-SM layouts, and that DeepSeek-V4-Flash's were drawn on NVIDIA hardware. Treat Blackwell training as likely, not shown. Take the GPU counts and dollar figures as order-of-magnitude guesses. The report does not say where the chips were, who supplied them or whether a law was broken, and neither does this page.

Where it moves the knowledge base

  • GPU architecture owns SM counts and already carries a short note on this report; the note gains that the weights record G (SMs times 2,048) and only past the first G positions, that 132 cannot separate the H800 from the H100 or H200, and that the author's B300 exposed 148 SMs and his GB200 152, where the page says a shipping B300's count is not stated.
  • DeepSeek, Moonshot AI: Kimi and MiniMax gain each lab's readings as dated, scoped facts: initialised on a Blackwell-class layout from V4, K3 and M3, on Hopper before; DeepSeek's single seed since 2024 and V4.1-Flash's missing fingerprint; Kimi K2.7-Code's blend.
  • Making training fast, which owns determinism and seeding, gains a sharp example: torch.cuda.manual_seed plus normal_ gives different tensors on GPUs with different SM counts, so a seed alone does not reproduce an initialisation across hardware.
  • The CUDA programming model can cite this as a grid-stride loop whose grid size leaks the hardware.

How this page checked the replay

The page's generator (Philox4x32-10 with Box-Muller, PyTorch's placement) was written for this page from PyTorch's DistributionTemplates.h at commit 58c7687 and the Random123 known-answer vectors; it does not run the author's code. src/replay/replay_rows.py applies it to the rows in the author's repository (MIT, commit b633870, rows hashed against pinned Hugging Face revisions) on a CPU: DeepSeek-V3 peaks at 132 SMs (8 of 8 rows above r 0.99, median 0.999999), DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 148 (461 of 469, median 0.999999), Kimi K3 at 152 with the generator offset of 3,776 values from the author's file (13 of 13, median 0.999996); the seed plus one gives 0 rows in each, and the best wrong layout scores r 0.015 or less over four rows. The in-browser replay ships 1,024 values of one row per model (6 KB) and recomputes its curve on load; src/check_page.mjs checks its generator against the Python values. MiniMax-M3 and Ling-3.1-flash were not replayed here (tensor-parallel seeds and a truncated-normal path); the outside reimplementation covers them.

What it rests on

PyTorch: DistributionTemplates.h (calc_execution_policy and the grid-stride kernel)

PyTorch (maker): Lines 49 to 61 set the grid to min(SMs times maxThreadsPerMultiProcessor / 256, the tensor's size / 256) blocks of 256 threads; lines 73 to 81 give thread idx Philox subsequence idx and its values at idx, idx + G, idx + 2G and so on. The whole fingerprint is in these 30 lines.

(10 min)

Parallel Random Numbers: As Easy as 1, 2, 3 (Philox)

Salmon, Moraes, Dror and Shaw, SC 2011 (maker): The counter-based generator behind cuRAND's Philox4x32-10: any thread can compute its own stream from (key, counter) with no shared state, which is why the numbers are identical on every chip and only their placement differs.

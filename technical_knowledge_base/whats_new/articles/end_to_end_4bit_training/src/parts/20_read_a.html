@@CARD@@

The idea in one sentence: 4-bit arithmetic is safe where rounding error is random and gets averaged away, and unsafe where it is systematic and compounds, so Thinking Machines put NVFP4 only in the forward matmuls of the expert layers (and the RL sampler, which runs the same forward), kept every backward matmul in MXFP8, and made the hand-over between the two unbiased with stochastic rounding.

You need: the formats and block scales (E2M1, E4M3, MXFP8, NVFP4: {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}, sections 1, 2 and 6); round-to-nearest against stochastic rounding ({{Numerical computing for ML|n:3f05c17b0d0d81d6af0be4a9a2edba8d}}, section 2); what dispatch and combine are in an expert-parallel MoE layer ({{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}}).

New terms: compute efficiency (CE) (the article's cost measure: a technique with a CE cost of 2% reaches the loss that the MXFP8 baseline would reach with 2% fewer FLOPs); re-quantisation (de-quantise the forward pass's 4-bit tensor, transpose it, round it again into the backward pass's blocks); S2F6 (an 8-bit fixed-point format, two signed integer bits and six fraction bits, values from −2 to 127/64); RHT (random Hadamard transform, a rotation that spreads an outlier over many values); Inkling 2 Small (Thinking Machines' open MoE, 594B total and 17B active parameters, 48 layers, 8 of 384 experts per token, by its {{model card|https://huggingface.co/thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Small}}).

1. Fewer bits buy speed; the question is where their error is harmless

[[ar:We manage this tradeoff through our use|The introduction]]

The article's frame is one trade, judged separately at four stages of training. Fewer bits mean less memory and faster arithmetic but coarser rounding, and rounding errors pile up across layers and steps ({{in the article|ar:these errors accumulate over training as rounding happens}}). On a GB300, NVFP4 matmuls peak at 15 PFLOPS against 5 for MXFP8 and 2.5 for BF16; NVFP4 stores 4.5 bits a value (16 four-bit values plus one 8-bit scale), MXFP8 8.25 (32 eight-bit values plus one scale) ({{the format table|ar:runs up to 6x faster on GB300s}}). The article's "6x" is against BF16; against the MXFP8 baseline every result is measured from, the peak is 3 times.

Random error and systematic error are judged differently, and that decides the recipe. Speed matters most in pretraining (trillions of tokens) and RL sampling, where every generated token reloads the weights, so fewer bits per weight is fewer bytes per token ({{in the article|ar:Each sampled token also requires loading the model’s weights}}). Backward passes average over many tokens, which dilutes random rounding error, but they are "very sensitive to quantization bias": rounding that leans one way compounds across layers, samples and steps ({{in the article|ar:backward passes are very sensitive to quantization bias}}). The hero figure is the article's table of these priorities, with the answer each stage got.

The result: one numerics configuration from the first pretraining step to the last RL rollout. The RL sampler runs the same NVFP4 forward the model was trained with, so no 4-bit checkpoint has to be calibrated at the end ({{in the article|ar:eliminates the need for calibration when a quantized checkpoint}}). The lab calls Inkling 2 Small the largest model it knows of trained end to end with a native 4-bit forward pass, and reports, in its largest jobs, an 8% wall-clock speedup in pretraining and 20% in RL over full MXFP8 ({{in the article|ar:this recipe provides us with an 8% wall-clock speedup}}).

2. The target: the expert layers, and only their forward pass

[[ar:Choosing a target for quantization|Choosing a target for quantization]]

Mixture-of-experts layers were chosen because they hold most of the matmul work and, empirically, lose the least accuracy when quantised ({{in the article|ar:Mixture of Experts (MoE) layers, which are computationally expensive}}). Attention, the router and the rest of the model are not part of the 4-bit recipe.

Full NVFP4 training failed the lab's bar; forward-only did not. Full MXFP8 in the MoE layers had "barely affected" compute efficiency ({{in the article|ar:barely affected our model’s compute efficiency}}); full NVFP4 gave "very large hits" to it ({{in the article|ar:very large hits to our model’s CE}}); 4 bits in the forward pass alone gave the better trade ({{in the article|ar:focusing on the forward pass generally offered better tradeoffs}}). No numbers are given for any of the three. Keeping the backward in 8 bits creates the rest of the article's problems: the forward pass's 4-bit tensors must be handed to a backward pass that groups them differently (section 4), and the faster forward exposes the network (section 5).

3. NVFP4 in brief: 16 values share one fine-grained scale

[[ar:Low-precision formats on Blackwell|Low-precision formats on Blackwell]]

An FP4 (E2M1) value is one of ±{0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6}; NVFP4 multiplies every 16 consecutive values by one shared E4M3 scale, chosen from the block's largest magnitude ({{in the article|ar:NVFP4 scales each block of 16 consecutive values}}). A block whose largest value is −54 stores it as −6 with a scale of 9. The scale is itself rounded: in the article's Figure 1, 593.7 wants a scale of 98.95, the nearest E4M3 value is 96, so it saturates at 96 × 6 = 576, an error of −17.7. Bit layouts and a bit explorer: {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}, sections 1 and 2.

Why NVFP4 and not MXFP4: MX formats share a power-of-two (E8M0) scale per 32 values; NVFP4's E4M3 scale has 3 mantissa bits, so it sits closer to each block's real maximum, which the article says gives less error on LLM data ({{in the article|ar:The increased precision offered by NVFP4 scale factors}}). A third, FP32 scale over a large group (here one expert's weights or one token's activations) slides the 2−10 to 2,688 window up or down ({{in the article|ar:second outer-level scale factor which is saved in FP32}}).

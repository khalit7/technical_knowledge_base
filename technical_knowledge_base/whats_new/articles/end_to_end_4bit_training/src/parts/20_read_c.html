5. Faster matmuls exposed the network, so the traffic went to 4.5 and 8.25 bits

[[ar:Quantized communications|Quantized communications]]

With NVFP4 matmuls the MoE forward should have run about three times faster than in MXFP8; instead it started waiting on expert-parallel communication ({{in the article|ar:should theoretically have gotten about three times faster}}). Tokens travel to their experts' GPUs (dispatch) and the outputs travel back to be summed (combine), over NVLink at 900 GB/s each way on a GB300 NVL72 rack, while the matmuls run at up to 15 PFLOPS: 16,667 floating-point operations per byte that arrives (derived; the article says "as many as 16,000") ({{in the article|ar:each GPU could perform as many as 16,000}}).

Dispatch was cheap to fix: the expert matmul quantises its input to NVFP4 anyway, so tokens are sent in NVFP4, and the backward pass uses those quantised activations, so nothing in high precision travels with them ({{in the article|ar:During dispatch, we avoid this issue by dispatching}}).

Combine was harder: the outputs feed the residual stream, and plain MXFP8 "impaired performance too much", which the article puts down to outliers ({{in the article|ar:found that this impaired performance too much}}). What worked: a 32-dimensional random Hadamard transform, which spreads each outlier over 32 values so a smaller scale fits, then S2F6 with one E8M0 scale per 32 values, with "no measurable accuracy degradation" ({{in the article|ar:resulted in no measurable accuracy degradation during training}}); how the rotation flattens outliers is {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}, Fig. 8. The transform and quantisation are fused into the second expert matmul's epilogue and undone on arrival. Combine falls from 16 to 8.25 bits a value ({{in the article|ar:from 16 bits per value to 8.25 bits per value}}), shortening the combine tail of the article's Figure 5 (no timings printed).

6. Kernels: the bottleneck moves to the ALUs

[[ar:Kernels for low-precision training|Kernels for low-precision training]]

At 4 bits the tensor cores stop being the only limit: scales, re-quantisation, stochastic rounding and data movement can stall a kernel whose tensor cores are saturated ({{in the article|ar:kernels can stall on ALU-heavy steps}}). The lab's MoE kernel stack, BBBK, is fully deterministic ({{in the article|ar:while maintaining fully deterministic computation}}), feeds the GB300's largest two-SM matrix instruction from deep pipelines for matrices and scales, fuses the Hadamard and S2F6 steps, and keeps NVLink, ALUs and tensor cores busy together. It is not released, so none of this can be checked or reused.

7. The rest of the recipe, and what the 8% is worth after its cost

[[ar:We keep the last 16.6% of layers|Recipe details]]

Four details complete the recipe. The last 16.6% of layers stay in MXFP8 (NVIDIA used 15%; the lab's ladder scales layer count by 6; in the release, layers 40 to 47 of 48) ({{in the article|ar:We keep the last 16.6% of layers in higher precision}}). Activations use Four Over Six (each block may map its maximum to 4 instead of 6; the post's author wrote that paper), weights do not ({{in the article|ar:We forgo 4/6 when quantizing weights}}). Outer FP32 scales are per expert for weights and per token for activations, from Cursor's Composer 2, so no token's scale depends on its batch or on later tokens ({{in the article|ar:per-expert outer scales when quantizing weights}}). It ran unchanged through SFT and RL.

The cost: about 2% in compute efficiency in pretraining, and no visible cost in RL. The article defines it precisely: the NVFP4 run reaches the loss the MXFP8 run would have reached with about 2% fewer FLOPs ({{in the article|ar:it costs about 2% in compute efficiency}}); in RL, "a substantial end-to-end speedup with no discernible difference in reward" ({{in the article|ar:no discernible difference in reward}}).

Two pieces of arithmetic the article leaves to the reader, in the calculator. First, why a 3 times faster matmul gives 8% overall: if the 4-bit matmuls take a share s of an MXFP8 step and run 3 times faster, the step speeds up by 1 / (1 − s + s / 3), and 1.08 needs s = 11.1% (derived, assuming the full 3 times peak; anything less than peak implies a larger share). Second, the net: the run needs 1 / 0.98 as many FLOPs for the same loss, so at 1.08 times the speed it reaches MXFP8's loss in 1 / 0.98 / 1.08 = 0.945 of the time, about 5.5% less (derived). If "8% speedup" instead means 8% less time, the net is 0.92 / 0.98 = 0.939, about 6.1% less. The RL figure implies a larger 4-bit share: 25.0% of the step at 3 times, or 36.7% if sampling is limited by weight bandwidth (8.25 / 4.5 = 1.83 times fewer bytes per weight). The article gives no breakdown of the step, so these shares are what its numbers imply, not measurements.

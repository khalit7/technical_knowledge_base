1. Where position enters, and why it is needed

[[ah:§1-where-position-information-enters-an-llm|Sections 1 and 1.1]]

The article opens on two architectures side by side: GPT-2 adds position before the first block, MiniMax-M3 adds it inside attention, in every block. Its running example is "The red fox jumps over the gray fox" against "The gray fox jumps over the red fox": both "fox" tokens get the same embedding, and without positions attention cannot tell which fox jumps. It then makes the point our owner page makes with a permutation test, in one sentence: the causal mask already gives a decoder some order, but in one layer it does not {{distinguish a separation of one position|ar:distinguish a separation of one position}} from a separation of ten. Our page shows the same thing measured (NoPE's first block does not notice a shuffle, its second does) in {{Positional Encodings, section 1|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a5b7c9e578fcdfac00}}.

2. Adding a vector: GPT-2's table and the 2017 sinusoids

[[ah:§2-adding-a-position-vector-to-each-token-embedding|Section 2]]

The learned table's cost is framed as a share of the model, which is a framing our page lacks. GPT-2 Medium's 1,024 position rows are 0.30% of its weights; stretched to a 100,000-token context they {{would make up >22% of the total|ar:would make up >22% of the total}}. Recomputed here: 1,024 × 1,024 = 1.05M of about 355M is 0.30%, and 100,000 × 1,024 = 102.4M of 456M is 22.4% (derived, the parameter count from GPT-2 Medium's published size). Our page gives the cost in absolute terms (786,432 parameters in GPT-2) and the hard cap.

On the 2017 sinusoids, the article adds one reason we only measured. The Transformer multiplies token embeddings by √dmodel before adding the sinusoids, which its paper never explains; Raschka's reading is that it is meant to {{put the token embeddings and sinusoidal positional encodings|ar:put the token embeddings and sinusoidal positional encodings}} on comparable scales at initialisation, and that GPT-2 needed no such factor because its code initialises token embeddings at standard deviation 0.02 and position embeddings at 0.01. That is his hypothesis, from a discussion, not a source. Our owner page has the measured consequence (the tiny GPT without the scale ends at 4.29 against 2.29), and {{Attention Is All You Need|n:3c65c17b0d0d81999af7f16f8ed8ee9e}} draws the sinusoids.

3. RoPE, one pair at a time

[[ah:§33-rotating-one-pair-in-rope|Sections 3.3 to 3.5]]

The core of the article is one feature pair worked by hand, small enough to check on paper. Query "the" at position 5 and key "fox" at position 2 both start as the pair (1, 5); at 20° per position the key turns 40° and the query 100°. Both keep length √26, they end 60° apart, and {{their exact dot product is 26|ar:Their exact dot product is 26}} × cos 60° = 13. Move the key one place closer and the score becomes 26 × cos 40° ≈ 19.92; shift both tokens by three and it is 13 again. Our page's worked example (θ = 0.5, q = k = (1, 0), 0.5403 = cos 1) is the same derivation with numbers that are less memorable: Raschka's 13 and √26 are integers a reader can carry, and the sentence is the one the whole article is about.

The motivation is the system prompt, which is the best one-line answer to "why relative". Put 100 tokens in front of the sentence and "fox" and "the" move to 102 and 105, but {{a prefix of any length shifts both|ar:a prefix of any length shifts both}} positions equally, so the pair's contribution stays 13. He adds the caveat our page leaves implicit: this holds for fixed unrotated vectors, and in a real model {{a new prefix of 100 tokens can change|ar:a new prefix of 100 tokens can change}} them, by changing what earlier layers attend to. RoPE makes the position part of a score invariant to the shift, not the whole score.

One loose end: after the single pair, the article prints whole eight-number vectors {{If we rotate all 4 pairs|ar:If we rotate all 4 pairs}} without giving their frequencies. Recomputed here, they use 20°, 2°, 0.2° and 0.02° per position for the four pairs, a base-10 version of the schedule that Section 4 then introduces with base 10,000 (which would give 1, 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 radians). The numbers are right for that schedule; a reader recomputing them from Section 4 would not get them. Our page draws the whole head as a bank of clocks (its Fig. 4) and the RoFormer page the paper's eight-pair dials.

4. The frequencies and the PyTorch code

[[ah:§5-bonus-implementing-rope-in-pytorch|Sections 4 and 5]]

The code section is short, correct and better than ours in one respect: it ends with a test any implementation can run. rope_params returns two tables, cosine and sine of position × θi with θi = 10,000−2i/d, one row per position and one column per pair (1,024 × 64 for a 128-wide head), computed once and broadcast over the batch and every head; rope_split_half rotates pair (i, i + d/2) with them. Then, {{adding 3 to each position should change|ar:adding 3 to each position should change}} the positions but not the scores: the code recomputes the scores at positions + 3 and checks them with torch.testing.assert_close. Our page tells the reader to check a RoPE setup by comparing loss with a reference; the shift test is cheaper and catches a wrong base or layout before any loss is measured.

Split-half against interleaved, shown as a permutation. The original paper and Meta's first LLaMA code pair neighbours (0, 1), (2, 3); most code now pairs i with i + d/2. Reordering the features as [0, 4, 1, 5, 2, 6, 3, 7] before the interleaved rotation gives identical outputs and scores, while {{applying the two layouts to the original vector|ar:Applying the two layouts to the original vector}} gives different ones. Our page has the consequence measured (loading the wrong layout takes the tiny GPT's loss from 1.96 to 3.61 with no error) and Hugging Face's conversion permutation; the article's three asserts are the cleanest proof that the two layouts differ only by a reordering of WQ and WK.

5. What recent models do instead of full RoPE

[[ah:§6-positional-choices-in-modern-architectures|Section 6]]

Partial RoPE: MiniMax-M3 rotates 64 of each head's 128 numbers. Its config, read on 27 November 2026, says head_dim 128, rotary_dim 64 (partial_rotary_factor 0.5), rope_theta 5,000,000 and 1,048,576 positions. The article measures the gain by the tables: halving them at a 1M context saves 1,048,576 × 32 pairs × 2 tables × 2 bytes = 128 MiB in BF16, or 36 MiB in NVFP4 with its block scales (both rechecked here), while {{the KV cache still stores the full key|ar:the KV cache still stores the full key}}. It cites the Fractional Rotation paper, where {{rotating around 10% of each head's features|ar:rotating around 10% of each head’s features}} matched full RoPE in 1B to 8B models, and concludes that {{partial RoPE is hardly worth it|ar:partial RoPE is hardly worth it}}. The arithmetic is right and the conclusion does not follow; see the next section.

NoPE layers: a toy for how the causal mask becomes a position. Suppose one value feature is 1 for "The" and 0 elsewhere, and a head attends uniformly to everything the mask allows. At "fox" (position 2) it averages three values and outputs 1/3, at "jumps" 1/4: the {{output feature equals one over the prefix|ar:output feature equals one over the prefix}} length, which a later layer can read as a position. This is Kazemnejad et al.'s construction made concrete, and it is the mechanism behind what our owner page only measures (its probe finds absolute position in NoPE's middle blocks, R² 0.94 by block 3). The paper's 107M models generalised to longer inputs best with NoPE ({{NoPE ranked first overall across the ten tasks|ar:NoPE ranked first overall across the ten tasks}}), but its 1.3B code model's {{predictions got worse at the longest lengths|ar:predictions got worse at the longest lengths}}. Hence mixtures: SmolLM3 {{applies RoPE in three layers and skips|ar:applies RoPE in three layers and skips}} it in the fourth, and its authors report better long context.

A learned relative bias returns, in Inkling. Thinking Machines' Inkling (975B parameters, 41B active, released 15 July 2026) adds a learned number to each score before the softmax that, in Raschka's description, {{depends on the query token's hidden state|ar:depends on the query token’s hidden state}} and on how far back the key is. T5's 2019 bias depended only on the bucketed distance. Thinking Machines' announcement says only that a relative positional embedding beat RoPE and extrapolated better; the dependence on the hidden state is the article's reading and was not checked here (unconfirmed).

The census, from his own gallery. Sorting the entries of his LLM Architecture Gallery (snapshot of 9 November 2026) by release date: RoPE in every softmax attention layer is still the most common choice, but its share fell from 2025 to the first half of 2026 and again in the second half; RoPE and NoPE mixes grew; every NoPE-only entry is a recurrent hybrid; and among RoPE entries, {{partial RoPE became more common from 2025 to 2026|ar:partial RoPE became more common from 2025 to 2026}}, from a minority to a majority. He adds that {{the groups are still small|ar:The groups are still small}}. The exact shares are only in his bar charts, so they are not quoted here. His forecast: RoPE stays dominant and {{I think this will carry over into 2027|ar:I think this will carry over into 2027}}, perhaps with more Inkling-like designs.

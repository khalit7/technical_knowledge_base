4. Groups and advantages: which rollouts teach anything

A reward of 1 says "this went well" but not "better than what you usually do". Policy-gradient methods turn rewards into an advantage: the reward minus a baseline (what the policy was expected to get), so rollouts that beat the policy's usual result are pushed up and those below it pushed down. GRPO's baseline is simply other rollouts of the same task: sample a group of G rollouts, and give rollout i the advantage

Ai = (ri − mean(r)) / std(r)

and every token that rollout sampled gets that same Ai (DeepSeekMath's "outcome supervision", {{arXiv 2402.03300|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300}}, which sampled 64 outputs per question). The derivation, PPO's clipping around it, and Dr. GRPO's correction are on {{RL for LLMs|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}} (Reading sections 5 to 7, with a live group widget); this section runs the formula on agent rollouts and looks at what it does to them. Group sizes in agent training are small because every rollout is an episode in a container: DeepSWE ran 64 problems with 8 rollouts each per step, 512 containers in parallel ({{Together AI and Agentica, 2 Jul 2025|https://www.together.ai/blog/deepswe}}). Ours are 4 rollouts (local model) and 3 (Haiku) per task and harness.

Real groups, real advantages

No disagreement, no signal. If every rollout in a group gets the same reward, every advantage is zero and the group contributes no gradient, only cost. With the pass/fail reward that happened in of the local model's groups (mostly all-fail) and of Haiku's (mostly all-pass). The partial reward rescues some of the local model's groups ( still flat), because its failures fail different numbers of checks; it rescues none of Haiku's ( still flat), because every Haiku rollout in a failing group failed the very same held-out check and scored the same 0.95. DAPO's dynamic sampling handles the rest by dropping groups whose accuracy is exactly 0 or 1 and sampling more until the batch is full; in its ablation that step took Qwen2.5-32B from 42 to 50 on AIME 2024 ({{arXiv 2503.14476|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476}}). For agents the waste is larger than for maths, because a dropped group cost minutes of container time, so curricula that keep tasks near a 50% pass rate matter (EnvHarness accepts a reshaped environment only when its success rate lands between 0.4 and 0.6, {{EnvHarness|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e88ab9c396a160f813}}).

Dividing by the spread changes who gets pushed hardest. In a group of four with one success, GRPO gives the winner +1.73 and each loser −0.58; Dr. GRPO, which drops the division, gives +0.75 and −0.25. A group with two successes gets ±1 under GRPO and ±0.5 under Dr. GRPO. Dividing by the standard deviation weights rare successes and rare failures more, which Dr. GRPO calls a difficulty bias: "questions with lower standard deviations (e.g., those that are too easy or too hard) ... are given higher weights" ({{arXiv 2503.20783|https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783}}). DeepSWE's recipe dropped the division, used a leave-one-out baseline (RLOO: each rollout's baseline is the mean of the other G−1, {{arXiv 2402.14740|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14740}}), and added compact filtering: trajectories that hit the maximum context, step count or time limit are masked from the loss rather than scored 0, so the model is not punished for running out of room. of our rollouts hit the 16-call cap, the case compact filtering is for.

Predict: a group's rewards are 1, 1, 1, 0. Under GRPO, which rollout gets the largest push, and in which direction?
The single failure: mean 0.75, standard deviation 0.433, so the failure gets (0 − 0.75)/0.433 = −1.73 and each success +0.58. In a mostly-solved group the rare failure is what the update is about; in a mostly-failed group, the rare success.

10. Routing by difficulty: routers and cascades

Sections 2 to 9 route between copies of the same model. Here the gateway picks which model: the cheapest that will answer well. Tier prices differ by a factor of 2 to 40 (dated table, section 11), so savings are large if most traffic is easy, but every misrouted hard question is a worse answer no error counter sees. So a router is measured as a classifier on labelled traffic: answer quality against the share of calls sent to the strong model, beside random routing at the same share (any router can trade cost for quality; a good one does it better than a coin).

Rules come first. Most production routers start as tier rules ("summaries go small") or a small classifier: Huyen describes "an intent classifier that predicts what the user is trying to do" so "simpler queries" go "to cheaper models" (Building A Generative AI Platform, 25 Jul 2024). Context rules send long prompts to pools configured for long context; getting one wrong is what happened in August 2025, when a routing bug sent some Claude Sonnet 4 requests to servers configured for a different context length, up to 16% of them in the worst hour, with normal error rates (Anthropic, 17 Sep 2025). Policy rules pin a tenant to a contracted vendor, keep data in a region, or send sensitive data only to self-hosted models.

A router guesses difficulty from the prompt and adds no latency; a cascade sees the real answer but pays the cheap call, and its latency, on every hard request. The details, with weights, quotes and reported savings, are in the closed block below the experiment.

The experiment. 48 questions written for this page with exactly gradable answers, four families of 12: one-step facts, arithmetic word problems of 2 to 7 steps, "what does this Python print", and letter puzzles (count the r's in a made-up word, or write it backwards). Each was answered once by the local model on Apple M1 Pro (Qwen3 4B, temperature 0, up to 512 tokens) and once by Claude Haiku 4.5 (Claude Code CLI, no tools, the same instruction), each ending with an "ANSWER:" line; LiteLLM's complexity router then scored every question offline. The keyword router recovers two thirds of the gap, because it spots code, not difficulty

Vertical: questions right out of 48; horizontal: share sent to Haiku. Dashed: random routing (expected value); green: a perfect router; purple: every threshold of the complexity score, the dot at the one chosen. The default threshold sends the router's MEDIUM tier up.

  • The two models: local 38 of 48, Haiku 44 of 48. Both got every fact and every arithmetic problem; the local model missed 4 code questions and 6 letter puzzles (8 of its 10 misses ran out of its 512-token allowance without an ANSWER line); Haiku missed 4 backwards-spelling puzzles. Haiku's 48 answers cost $0.19 at API list prices (the CLI's total_cost_usd; the subscription paid).
  • The complexity router scored all 12 code questions MEDIUM (they contain code) and all 36 others SIMPLE. Sending MEDIUM up: 42 right with 25% of calls to Haiku. Random routing at 25% would expect 39.5; a perfect router needs only 6 strong calls (12.5%) for 44. RouteLLM's metric, the share of the quality gap recovered, is (42 − 38) / (44 − 38) = 0.67.
  • Why it worked, and where it did not: the families the small model failed happened to differ in surface features (code), so a keyword rule found half of them. It sent none of the letter puzzles, which look simple and are hard for a small model because it sees tokens, not letters. A router's features must predict your cheap model's failures on your traffic; that is what training on preference data, or on your own graded logs, buys over keywords.
What this experiment is not
A small invented question set, one run per model: it shows how to evaluate a router, not how good routers are. Grading is lenient on units ("2500 grams" counts for 2500); Haiku's raw grade before that was 39 of 48.

8. Debate and voting

When the job is one hard answer rather than a lot of reading, the multi-agent move is to ask several times and combine. Voting (self-consistency) samples the same question independently several times and takes the most common answer. Debate lets solvers read each other's answers and reasoning and revise; Du et al. used "three agents with two rounds of debates" and reported arithmetic accuracy rising from 67.0% for one agent to 81.8% ({{Du et al., May 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14325}}). A judge reads several answers and picks one. The catch the later papers found: compare against voting at equal cost, and most of debate's gain belongs to voting ("Majority Voting alone accounts for most of the performance gains typically attributed to MAD", {{Debate or Vote, Aug 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.17536}}; earlier {{Smit et al., Nov 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.17371}} and {{Wang et al., Feb 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18272}}).

How to read it. Voting explains most of the improvement over one sample (from to ), as the Debate or Vote paper found. Reading other solvers' work added the last couple of puzzles here, and there is a reason it can on this task: a proposed assignment of knights and knaves can be checked statement by statement, so a solver shown a correct solution can verify it, which is easier than finding it. Where checking is no easier than solving (open questions, judgement calls), expect less. And 30 puzzles is a small sample: a two-puzzle gap is within noise. The judge result says something practical: when you already have several samples, one call that checks them can be cheaper than more samples.

The other side of agreement
Debate converges: after one round the solvers were unanimous on every puzzle. That is efficient when the majority is right and dangerous when it is not, because unanimity looks like confidence. Keep a verifier outside the debate when you can (here, the brute-force solver).

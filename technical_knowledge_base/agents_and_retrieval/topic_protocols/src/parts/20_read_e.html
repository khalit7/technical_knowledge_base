5. Security: TLS for every API, SSH for the GPU box

TLS (version 1.3, now {{RFC 9846|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9846}}, July 2026) gives three guarantees on top of a connection: confidentiality (nobody in between can read), integrity (any changed byte is detected) and authentication (the client knows it reached the real server). It is the "S" in HTTPS. In one round trip the two sides agree on a fresh secret in public (Diffie-Hellman key exchange, so stealing the server's key later does not unlock recorded traffic), the server sends its certificate and signs the handshake with the matching private key, and both send a keyed checksum of everything exchanged. The certificate chains up to a root in the client's trust store, through certificates signed by certificate authorities.

SSH, the protocol you use to reach a GPU box, solves the same problem with the same ideas and one different choice: whom to trust.

Never "fix" a certificate error by turning verification off

verify=False, curl -k and InsecureSkipVerify keep the encryption and drop the authentication, which means encrypting to whoever answers, attacker included. The fixes are serving the full chain on the server, or adding the right CA to the client's trust store.

What TLS still leaks, and why that matters for token streams. An eavesdropper still sees the server name and protocol in the ClientHello (unless Encrypted Client Hello is used), the sizes of all records and their timing. In the streamed answer that meant one record per token, 146 to 148 bytes each, about 51 ms apart (measured): the rhythm of generation is visible though the text is not, and researchers reconstructed 29% of assistant responses from token lengths alone ({{Weiss et al., 2024|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09751}}). The defence lives in the streaming layer: pad records or group tokens.

Certificates now expire on a schedule built for automation (200 days since 15 March 2026, 47 days from 15 March 2029), so an expired certificate is the most common TLS outage and automated issuance (ACME) is mandatory in practice. Mutual TLS runs the proof in the other direction and is the strongest way for one service to prove itself to another (section 9).

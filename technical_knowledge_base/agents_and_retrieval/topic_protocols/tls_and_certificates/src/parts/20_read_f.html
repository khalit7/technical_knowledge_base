5. Getting certificates: CAs, ACME, and the 47-day schedule

Who may issue. Any CA can sign anything; what makes a public certificate trusted is that browsers and operating systems ship that CA's root. Their root programs (Chrome, Mozilla, Apple, Microsoft) and the industry body they sit in, the CA/Browser Forum, set the rules in the Baseline Requirements (BR). Before issuing, a CA must prove the requester controls the domain: domain validation (DV). Every leaf in the survey above is DV (policy OID 2.23.140.1.2.1); OV and EV add checks on the organisation that browsers no longer display, so they buy nothing technical.

ACME: issuance as an API

ACME ({{RFC 8555|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8555}}) is the protocol Let's Encrypt created and many public CAs now offer (AWS Certificate Manager since 6 July 2026: {{AWS announcement|https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/07/aws-certificate-manager-acme/}}, 45-day certificates). Every request is a JSON object signed with the client's account key (a JWS), carrying a single-use nonce against replay. Below is a full issuance recorded against Pebble, Let's Encrypt's test CA, running in Docker, with a hand-written client of about 150 lines so every message is visible (src/lab/acme_flow.py). The CA really validated: it resolved the name through its own DNS server and fetched the challenge file over HTTP.

The recording also shows two things that bite real clients: Pebble rejects 5% of nonces on purpose, and one request came back 400 badNonce and had to be retried with the fresh nonce from that response (RFC 8555 section 6.5); and a wrong challenge file gives an unauthorized error naming the expected and received content, after which that authorization is dead and you must start a new order.

ARI (ACME Renewal Information, {{RFC 9773|https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc9773/}}, June 2025) lets the CA tell each client when to renew: Pebble suggested a window . If a CA must revoke many certificates (a mis-issuance incident), it moves the windows earlier, and clients that honour ARI renew in time instead of breaking. At 47-day lifetimes this is the difference between a planned event and an outage; certbot, lego and cert-manager support it.

Lifetimes are collapsing on a fixed schedule

CA/Browser Forum ballot {{SC-081v3|https://cabforum.org/2025/04/11/ballot-sc081v3-introduce-schedule-of-reducing-validity-and-data-reuse-periods/}} (adopted 11 April 2025, now in {{BR 6.3.2 and 4.2.1|https://github.com/cabforum/servercert/blob/main/docs/BR.md#632-certificate-operational-periods-and-key-pair-usage-periods}}) sets the maximum validity of a public TLS certificate and how long a CA may reuse a past domain validation. Why it matters: 200 days is already in force (since 15 March 2026). A certificate renewed by hand in March or April 2026 by someone who assumed "a year" expires in autumn 2026. The 47-day limit from 15 March 2029 is commonly explained as 31 + 15 + 1 days (a long month, half a month, a day of slack); that breakdown is not in the ballot or the BR text. The bigger change is domain-validation reuse falling to 10 days: practically every issuance must re-prove control, so your DNS provider's API or your port-80 path becomes a hard runtime dependency of certificate renewal. CAs already issue below the caps: the survey shows Google Trust Services leaves at 90 days (84 for one), Amazon at 198 days (ACM's default since 18 February 2026, {{AWS|https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/02/aws-certificate-manager-updates-default/}}), and one GlobalSign leaf at 397 days, issued on 28 December 2025, before the first cap.

Let's Encrypt issues 90 days by default, offers 45-day (tlsserver) and 160-hour (shortlived, also for IP addresses, generally available 15 January 2026) profiles, and will move its default to 64 days on 10 February 2027 and 45 days on 16 February 2028 ({{Let's Encrypt, 2 December 2025|https://letsencrypt.org/2025/12/02/from-90-to-45/}}; {{profiles|https://letsencrypt.org/docs/profiles/}}). The Pebble run above issued a 6-day certificate with the shortlived profile.

Guard rails around issuance: CAA, MPIC, DNSSEC, Certificate Transparency

The DNS side of this section (the CAA and _acme-challenge TXT records DNS-01 depends on, caching that slows propagation, DNSSEC, and the HTTPS record that carries the ECH key of section 2) is taught on the {{DNS|n:3c65c17b0d0d812387d8f92b546f07af}} page.

  • CAA ({{RFC 8659|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8659}}): a DNS record listing which CAs may issue for your domain; CAs must check it. Looked up over DNS-over-HTTPS on 5 October 2026: The parameters accounturi and validationmethods ({{RFC 8657|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8657}}) pin issuance to your own ACME account and method; CAs must process them from 15 March 2027 (BR). This is a strong, cheap control: an attacker who briefly hijacks your web server still cannot get a certificate from your CA under a different account.
  • MPIC (multi-perspective issuance corroboration, ballot SC-067): the CA repeats domain validation and the CAA lookup from several network vantage points and they must agree. Enforced since 15 September 2025 with 2 remote perspectives, 3 from 15 March 2026, 4 from 15 June 2026 and 5 from 15 December 2026 ({{BR 3.2.2.9|https://github.com/cabforum/servercert/blob/main/docs/BR.md#3229-multi-perspective-issuance-corroboration}}). It defeats local BGP or DNS hijacks; it also means split-horizon or geo-steered DNS that answers differently by region can now fail issuance.
  • DNSSEC: since 15 March 2026 the BR require CAs to validate DNSSEC on the lookups for domain validation and CAA (see {{DNS|n:3c65c17b0d0d812387d8f92b546f07af}}). A broken DNSSEC signature on your zone now blocks renewal.
  • Certificate Transparency (CT): every public certificate is logged in append-only public logs before browsers trust it. The CA submits a precertificate, each log returns a signed promise (an SCT, signed certificate timestamp), and the CA embeds the SCTs in the certificate. Chrome requires at least 2 SCTs from distinct log operators for certificates valid up to 180 days, 3 above ({{Chrome CT policy|https://googlechrome.github.io/CertificateTransparency/ct_policy.html}}). The surveyed leaves carry exactly that: Let's Encrypt's old RFC 6962 logs shut down on 28 February 2026, replaced by "static CT API" tiled logs ({{Let's Encrypt|https://letsencrypt.org/2025/08/14/rfc-6962-logs-eol}}). Two uses for you: monitor the logs (crt.sh, Cert Spotter) for certificates issued for your domains that you did not request; and remember that CT is public forever, so a public certificate for gpu-cluster-7.corp.example.com publishes that name.
Predict, then reveal: renewal suddenly fails for a domain whose DNS is served by two providers that answer slightly differently by region. Nothing changed on your side. Why now?
MPIC: since September 2025 the CA checks validation and CAA from several regions, and the number of required perspectives rose through 2026 (3 in March, 4 in June). If one region's answer lacks the TXT record or shows a different CAA set, the quorum fails. Make both providers serve identical records, or validate with HTTP-01.

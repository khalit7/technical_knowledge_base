Handshake, decrypted

Every TLS record of the running request, as it crossed the socket, and what is inside it. The client wrote its session secrets to a key log; this page's script derived the traffic keys from them (HKDF-Expand-Label, {{RFC 9846 section 7.3|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9846#section-7.3}}) and decrypted every TLS 1.3 record with AES-256-GCM. Switch between what an observer on the path sees and what the two endpoints see. Three recordings, 5 October 2026, Python 3.13.16 with OpenSSL 3.5.9 on both ends, loopback (times in milliseconds since the TCP connection opened; on a real network each change of direction adds a network trip).

Try: in TLS 1.3, step to record 5 in the observer view (934 bytes of ciphertext), then switch to the endpoint view: it is the server's certificate. Do the same in TLS 1.2: the certificate is readable on the wire. In mTLS, find the client's own certificate and its SPIFFE ID, encrypted.

How the keys are derived: the key log gives CLIENT_HANDSHAKE_TRAFFIC_SECRET, SERVER_HANDSHAKE_TRAFFIC_SECRET and the two application traffic secrets. For each, key = HKDF-Expand-Label(secret, "key", "", 32) and iv = HKDF-Expand-Label(secret, "iv", "", 12); record n of a direction uses nonce = iv XOR n, and the 5-byte record header is the associated data. The inner plaintext ends with the real content type and optional zero padding (none was used here). The TLS 1.2 recording is shown as observed only: decrypting it needs the TLS 1.2 PRF, which this page does not implement, and nothing hidden in it is not also shown by the TLS 1.3 recordings. Scripts: src/lab/hs_record.py, src/lab/tlsparse.py; re-checked by src/recompute.py.

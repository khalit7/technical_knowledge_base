11. UDP: a thin wrapper, and everything else is your problem

What it is. UDP adds an 8-byte header to IP: source port, destination port, length, checksum ({{RFC 768|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc768}}). No connection, no handshake, no acknowledgement, no retransmission, no ordering, no flow or congestion control. What it does keep is message boundaries: each sendto is one datagram, delivered whole or not at all (the recording in section 2 shows three sends arriving as three reads, where TCP merged them).

Why use it. When a late packet is worthless (voice, video, game state), when a question and its answer each fit in one packet (DNS), or when you want to build your own transport in user space without waiting for every operating system and middlebox to change (QUIC). The price is that you must do the rest yourself, including congestion control: an application that blasts UDP at a fixed rate harms everyone sharing the link, which is why the IETF's {{UDP usage guidelines|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8085}} ask UDP applications to control their rate.

No autotuning: the receive buffer drops what it cannot hold

TCP's receive buffer grows and its window tells the sender to slow down. A UDP socket has a fixed buffer and no way to push back: datagrams that arrive while it is full are discarded by the kernel, and only a counter records it. measured on loopback: 5,000 datagrams of 1,200 bytes sent in one burst at a socket that reads only after the burst:

A QUIC implementation treats those drops as network loss and slows down, so an undersized UDP buffer silently caps HTTP/3 throughput. quic-go's documentation recommends raising the Linux limits to about 7.5 MB (net.core.rmem_max and wmem_max = 7,500,000, {{quic-go wiki|https://github.com/quic-go/quic-go/wiki/UDP-Buffer-Sizes}}); Linux's default limit was 212,992 bytes until 6.18 raised it to 4 MB (section 5). HTTP/3 is no longer niche: W3Techs counted it on 40.8 percent of websites on 5 October 2026 ({{W3Techs|https://w3techs.com/technologies/details/ce-http3}}).

Commands. Linux: nstat -az | grep -i udp, where a growing UdpRcvbufErrors means the buffer overflowed; ss -uam shows each socket's buffer use. macOS: netstat -s -p udp, "dropped due to full socket buffers" (the counter this page's measurement read before and after). Where you meet UDP in ML: DNS on port 53 (its packets, truncation and TCP fallback are recorded on the {{DNS|n:3c65c17b0d0d812387d8f92b546f07af}} page), HTTP/3 on 443, NTP for clocks (TLS and token validity depend on them), and RoCEv2 between GPUs (section 14).

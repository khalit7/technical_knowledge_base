4. Reliability: acknowledgements, timers and retransmission

The problem. IP loses packets. TCP must notice which bytes did not arrive and send them again, without waiting too long (the application stalls) or resending too eagerly (wasting the network and confusing itself).

Cumulative acknowledgements. The receiver keeps telling the sender "I have every byte up to n" (the ACK number). A segment the sender has sent but not seen acknowledged is in flight. If segment 5 of 10 is lost, segments 6 to 10 still arrive, but the receiver can only keep acknowledging "up to the end of 4": these repeats are duplicate ACKs, and the bytes of 6 to 10 wait in the receive buffer, invisible to the application, until 5 is repaired. That wait is head-of-line blocking, and it is the reason HTTP/2's many streams on one TCP connection all stall on one loss while HTTP/3's do not (measured in the root's {{On the wire|n:3c65c17b0d0d81ec9355f4eecd6eed02}} tab: one lost packet delayed all three HTTP/2 streams and only one HTTP/3 stream).

Two ways to decide a segment is lost, both still in use:

  • Retransmission timeout (RTO). The sender measures round trips, keeps a smoothed average and a variance, and sets RTO = smoothed RTT + 4 × variance ({{RFC 6298|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6298}}). Nothing acknowledged before the timer fires means resend, and double the timer for the next try (exponential backoff). RFC 6298 says the RTO should never go below 1 second; Linux uses a 200 ms floor (TCP_RTO_MIN (HZ / 5)), a 1 s initial value and a 120 s ceiling ({{tcp.h|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/include/net/tcp.h}}). A timeout is expensive: the pipe sits idle for at least 200 ms, and the congestion window collapses to one segment (section 6).
  • Fast retransmit. Three duplicate ACKs mean later segments are arriving, so the missing one is probably lost rather than delayed: resend it at once, one round trip after the loss instead of a timer later ({{RFC 5681 s3.2|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681#section-3.2}}). SACK (selective acknowledgement, {{RFC 2018|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2018}}) lets the receiver also say which later blocks it holds, so several holes can be repaired in one round trip.

Why this matters for streaming LLM responses. Fast retransmit needs later segments to generate duplicate ACKs. A token stream sends one small segment every 50 ms or so; if the last segments of a burst are lost there is nothing behind them, and only a timer can notice. Linux's answer is RACK-TLP ({{RFC 8985|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8985}}): detect loss by time (a segment is lost if one sent after it has been acknowledged and a reordering window has passed) and, when the tail goes quiet, send a tail loss probe after about two round trips to provoke an ACK that reveals the hole. The {{TCP timeline|#t-tcp}} tab replays the running request with one lost segment recovered each way, to scale.

Loss and reordering on a real path. In two of this page's twelve downloads the receiver reported out-of-order bytes held for repair (36,608 and 254,880 bytes, tcpi_rxoutoforderbytes, measured); the other ten saw none. The application never noticed: TCP repaired the holes and delivered the bytes in order, which is the point, and the cost (a pause of about a round trip, a smaller window) is invisible unless you look at the per-connection counters.

How it shows up and the command that shows it. Retransmissions inside a data centre should be near zero; growth there is a real fabric or NIC problem. Linux: nstat -az TcpRetransSegs before and after a test, and ss -ti per connection (retrans: current/total, lost:, rto:). macOS: netstat -s -p tcp | grep -i retrans. A connection whose RTO has backed off to tens of seconds looks hung; it is waiting for its timer.

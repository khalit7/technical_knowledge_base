6. Congestion control: sharing a link nobody owns

The problem. The receive window protects the receiver; nothing in it protects the routers in between. If every sender transmitted as fast as its window allowed, router queues would overflow, packets would be dropped and resent, and throughput would collapse (this happened to the internet in October 1986, the first of a series of "congestion collapses" described by {{Jacobson and Karels, 1988|https://ee.lbl.gov/papers/congavoid.pdf}}). So each sender also keeps a congestion window (cwnd), its own estimate of what the path can carry, and probes for more while watching for signs of overload.

Slow start. A new connection starts with 10 segments ({{RFC 6928|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6928}}; TCP_INIT_CWND 10 in Linux) and adds one segment per ACK, which doubles the window every round trip, until a loss or until it reaches the slow-start threshold. Reaching a 125 MB window from 14.6 KB takes about log2(125 MB / 14.6 KB) ≈ 13 round trips, 1.3 s on a 100 ms path (derived); a 100 KB BDP like this page's home line is filled in about 3 round trips. Short transfers (a 2 KB API response) finish inside slow start, so for them latency, not bandwidth, is everything. Linux also drops back to slow start after a connection has been idle for one RTO (tcp_slow_start_after_idle, default on), so a long-lived connection that sends a burst after a pause starts small again.

Congestion avoidance, Reno style. After slow start, grow by one segment per round trip and halve on loss: additive increase, multiplicative decrease ({{RFC 5681|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681}}). It is fair and stable, and hopeless on long fat paths: after one loss at a 10,000-segment window, adding one segment per 100 ms round trip needs 5,000 round trips, over 8 minutes, to get back (derived).

CUBIC, the default in the Linux, Windows and Apple stacks ({{RFC 9438 s1|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9438#section-1}}, the 2023 standard; this Mac reports net.inet.tcp.use_newreno: 0 and 209 sockets on CUBIC when recorded), replaces the straight line with a cubic curve in time since the last loss: W(t) = C (t − K)3 + Wmax, with C = 0.4, a decrease to 0.7 of the window on loss instead of 0.5, and K the time to climb back to the window where the loss happened. It returns quickly to the old level, creeps carefully past it, then accelerates. Because it depends on time, not on round trips, long-RTT flows recover as fast as short ones.

illustrative, computed from the RFC formulas One flow, one step per 100 ms round trip, on a path that holds 1,000 segments in flight (about 117 Mbit/s with 1,460-byte segments): a window above that overflows the bottleneck buffer and loses a packet, and both flows also lose one packet the queue did not cause, at 25 s. Reno follows {{RFC 5681|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681#section-3.1}} (halve, then one segment per round trip); CUBIC follows {{RFC 9438 s4|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9438#section-4}} (C = 0.4, β = 0.7, with its Reno-friendly floor; fast convergence not modelled). Both start in slow start; queueing delay and timeouts are left out.

The cost of filling the pipe: queues. Loss-based algorithms such as Reno and CUBIC only learn they went too fast when a queue overflows, so they keep the bottleneck's buffer full. measured on this page's home line: ping to Cloudflare's resolver every 100 ms, with one download of 25 MB in the middle. The download ran at 44.1 Mbit/s and the round trip went from a 14.4 ms median at idle to 37.0 ms while it ran: about 23 ms of queue, roughly 125 KB of packets waiting at the bottleneck (44.1 Mbit/s × 22.6 ms, derived). The same effect is in section 5's runs: the window-limited downloads saw a current RTT of 17 to 22 ms at the end, the ones that filled the link 35 to 49 ms. Any interactive traffic sharing that link (a video call, the next token of a streamed answer) waits in the same queue; this is bufferbloat.

BBR (Bottleneck Bandwidth and Round-trip propagation time, from Google) models the path instead of reacting to loss: it estimates the bottleneck bandwidth and the minimum RTT and paces packets at that rate, aiming to keep the queue near empty. It shines on long paths with random, non-congestive loss (where CUBIC would back off for nothing) and is in Linux since 4.9 ({{commit 0f8782ea1497|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/0f8782ea14974ce992618b55f0c041ef43ed0b78}}). Its current version is still an IETF draft, {{draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr-06|https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/draft-ietf-ccwg-bbr/}} (6 July 2026), not an RFC; which version a given kernel ships is a question for that kernel, so check with sysctl net.ipv4.tcp_available_congestion_control rather than a blog post. The sender alone chooses: on a download, the server's algorithm decides, not yours.

Signals other than loss. ECN ({{RFC 3168|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3168}}) lets a router mark a packet "congestion experienced" instead of dropping it, and the receiver echoes the mark; this Mac negotiated it on every connection recorded here. L4S ({{RFC 9330|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9330}}, 2023) builds on it for very low queueing delay; it is something ISPs deploy, not something you tune on a cluster.

When not to touch it. Inside one data centre on a clean network, the default is almost always right; the wins from switching to BBR are on long, lossy paths (cross-region replication, uploads from a home line). Fairness: one BBR flow can take more than its share from CUBIC flows on a shallow-buffer link, so test before switching a fleet.

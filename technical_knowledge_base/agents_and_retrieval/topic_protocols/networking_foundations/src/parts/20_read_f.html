5. Windows: one connection carries at most one window per round trip

Every ACK carries the receiver's receive window, the free space left in its buffer, and the sender never has more unacknowledged bytes in flight than that (flow control). A second cap, the congestion window (section 6), protects the network; the sender may have min(receive window, congestion window) in flight. It sends one window, then waits a round trip for the acknowledgements, so at most one window arrives per round trip, however fast the link:

throughput ≤ window / RTT    and to fill a link: window ≥ bandwidth × RTT (the bandwidth-delay product, BDP)
window
bytes allowed in flight without acknowledgement
RTT
round-trip time; under load it includes queueing (section 6)
BDP
10 Gbit/s × 100 ms = 125 MB; 25 Gbit/s × 1 ms = 3.1 MB; 100 Gbit/s × 50 µs = 625 KB (derived)

The header's window field is 16 bits, so without help the largest window is 65,535 bytes: over a 100 ms path that caps one connection at 65,535 × 8 / 0.1 = 5.2 Mbit/s whatever the link (derived). The window-scale option ({{RFC 7323 s2|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7323#section-2}}) multiplies the field by 2shift, shift at most 14 (windows up to 1 GiB), agreed once in the SYN. A middlebox that strips it, or a socket whose buffer was small when it connected, is stuck with a small window for life.

The buffer you set is the speed you get: measured goodput follows window / RTT until the link binds

measured Twelve downloads from Cloudflare's speed-test endpoint, one TCP connection each, the receive buffer fixed with SO_RCVBUF before connect() or left to the kernel, goodput over the second half of each transfer. The prediction is the window the kernel reported over the measured smoothed RTT; the link tops out near 46 Mbit/s.

Defaults reproduce the window-over-RTT rule independently: for the two window-limited buffers goodput is within about 10 percent of window / RTT (the RTT is reported in whole milliseconds, so the prediction is only that precise); at 256 KB and with autotuning the prediction exceeds the link, and the link wins. The kernel advertised 28,728 to 29,776 bytes for a 16 KB request: macOS sizes the buffer above what you ask, so read the window the kernel reports (tcpi_rcv_wnd), not the number you passed. In the autotuned runs the advertised window grew from 128 KB to about 0.9 to 1.4 MB within a few hundred milliseconds (measured, first run below), with a window-scale shift of 6 against 3 when the buffer was fixed small: the kernel picks the shift at connect() from the buffer size at that moment.

The trap: setting a buffer can make you slower
Linux documents that setsockopt(SO_RCVBUF) disables receive autotuning for that socket ({{tcp_rmem in ip-sysctl|https://docs.kernel.org/networking/ip-sysctl.html}}). A client "tuned" with a 256 KB buffer is capped at 256 KB / RTT: 21 Mbit/s on a 100 ms cross-region path (derived), far below an untuned client whose window grows to tcp_rmem's maximum (default between 128 KB and 32 MB depending on memory). Two separate knobs: net.ipv4.tcp_rmem's third value caps autotuning; net.core.rmem_max caps only explicit setsockopt calls, and its default rose from 212,992 bytes to 4 MB in Linux 6.18 ({{commit a6d4f25888b8|https://github.com/torvalds/linux/commit/a6d4f25888b8}}), so guides that quote "208 KB" describe older kernels. For a 10 Gbit/s, 100 ms path the third value of tcp_rmem and tcp_wmem must be at least the 125 MB BDP.
How a window-limited transfer shows up (commands)

A transfer far below link speed with no retransmissions, and a throughput that changes with distance rather than load. ss -ti on Linux shows rcv_space, snd_wnd and cwnd; iperf3 -c host -P 1 against -P 8 demonstrates the per-connection cap to anyone who doubts it.

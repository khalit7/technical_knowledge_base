1. Names, zones and delegation

The problem. Packets are delivered to IP addresses, but people and configuration files use names, and the machines behind a name change all the time: load balancers are replaced, a region fails over, a CDN answers from the nearest city. One central table of every name could never keep up (the Internet tried: until the 1980s every host downloaded one file, HOSTS.TXT; {{RFC 1034 s2.1|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1034#section-2.1}}). DNS splits the job by ownership: whoever owns a name runs, or rents, the servers that answer for it, and everyone else finds those servers by asking down a tree ({{RFC 1034|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1034}} and {{RFC 1035|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1035}}, November 1987, still the core of the protocol).

Names are read right to left. api.anthropic.com. is three labels under the root, which is the empty label written as the final dot. Each label is at most 63 bytes and a whole name at most 255 ({{RFC 1035 s2.3.4|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1035#section-2.3.4}}). A name ending in a dot is a fully qualified domain name (FQDN): absolute, never extended. Without the dot, your resolver library may try to complete it with local suffixes first, which is the whole story of section 7.

Zones and delegation. A zone is the part of the tree one set of servers answers for. The root zone answers for . but hands com. to other servers; com. hands anthropic.com. to the servers the company chose. That hand-over is a delegation: the parent zone holds NS records (name server) naming the child's servers, plus glue, the addresses of those servers when they live inside the child zone itself, without which nobody could reach them. The servers that hold a zone's records are its authoritative servers.

The lab tree (measured: the zone files served)
.                 NS  a.root-servers.test.   (10.53.0.10)
test.             NS  ns1.nic.test.          (10.53.0.11)
llm.test.         NS  ns1.llm.test.          (10.53.0.12)
api.llm.test.     A   10.53.0.80             TTL 60
The real tree (root zone file, 5 Oct 2026)
.                 NS  a ... m.root-servers.net.
com.      172800  NS  a ... m.gtld-servers.net.
anthropic.com.    NS  isla.ns.cloudflare.com.
                      randy.ns.cloudflare.com.
api.anthropic.com. A  160.79.104.10

The right-hand column is real: the com. line is copied from the root zone file InterNIC publishes ({{root.zone|https://www.internic.net/domain/root.zone}}, serial 2026100500, downloaded for this page), which delegates 1,437 top-level domains (measured: distinct names with NS records in that file). The anthropic.com lines are the root page's recorded lookups. The lab is the same shape with private addresses; .test is reserved for exactly this ({{RFC 6761 s6.2|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6761#section-6.2}}), so none of these names can leak into the real DNS.

Why exactly 13 root servers. There are 13 root server identities, a to m.root-servers.net, run by 12 organisations; on 5 October 2026 they were served by 2,045 machines in many cities, each identity answering from many places on one address through anycast (the same IP address announced from many sites, routing sends you to the nearest) ({{root-servers.org|https://root-servers.org/}}). The number 13 is a fossil: in the early 1990s the list of root servers had to fit in one 512-byte UDP answer, and renaming them all under root-servers.net let compression squeeze 13 into it ({{RSSAC023 s2.6|https://www.icann.org/en/system/files/files/rssac-023-04nov16-en.pdf}}).

Costs and limits of the design. Delegation makes DNS scale, and it is also why changes are slow and failures are wide. A delegation in the parent is cached for as long as the parent says: 172,800 seconds (48 hours) for com. in the root zone file, and the same for the delegations inside .com, so moving a domain to new name servers can take two days to reach every cache. And every name under a zone depends on that zone's servers and the chain above them: when one record or one signature at a high level breaks, everything below it breaks at once (section 11 has the outages).

5. Caching: TTLs, negative answers and stale answers

The rule. Every record carries a TTL (time to live), chosen by the zone owner: the number of seconds a cache may keep it. A resolver that fetches a record with TTL 300 serves it for up to 300 seconds, counting down, then asks again. There is no way to push a change into caches or to invalidate them from outside. So "DNS propagation" is a myth: the authoritative server has the new data the moment you save it, and you are waiting for every cache that holds the old answer to expire.

The resolver kept serving the old address, its TTL falling 30, 27, 25 ... 1, until its copy expired, about 30 seconds after it was fetched, whatever the authoritative server said. With a TTL of a day the same picture lasts a day. Two public readings make the same point: api.anthropic.com came back with 292 seconds left and, five seconds later from the same resolver address, 212 seconds left, 80 seconds lower instead of 5 (measured over DoT): one address hides many caches filled at different times, so a TTL is a per-cache countdown, not a global clock.

Planning a change (the Cache timeline tab simulates this)

Lower the TTL (to 60 to 300 s) at least one old TTL before the change, because caches that fetched just before you lowered it keep the old TTL. Make the change. Keep the old address serving until the old TTL has passed since the change, then raise the TTL again. For a move between DNS providers (changing NS records) count the parent's TTL, 48 hours for .com. The {{Cache timeline|#t-cache}} tab lets you set these numbers and watch what share of clients is still on the old address.

Negative caching: "no" is cached too

A resolver also caches NXDOMAIN and NODATA, so that a typo hammered in a loop does not reach the authoritative servers every time. How long is set by the zone's SOA record, which the "no" carries in its authority section: the minimum of the SOA record's own TTL and its last field, MINIMUM ({{RFC 2308 s5|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2308#section-5}}; MINIMUM once meant something else and was repurposed for exactly this). In the lab, llm.test's SOA has TTL 300 and MINIMUM 30, so a "no" lives min(300, 30) = 30 seconds:

The name was looked up before it existed, created at the authoritative server a few seconds later, and stayed "does not exist" at the resolver until 30 seconds after the first lookup (measured). For anthropic.com both numbers are 1,800 (SOA ... 604800 1800, measured), so a name looked up a moment before it is created stays missing for up to half an hour for everyone behind that cache; a zone with MINIMUM 86400 would make it a day. Keep MINIMUM between about 300 and 900 seconds on zones that change, and do not let deploy scripts or health checks look up names before they exist. The Failure lab's "NXDOMAIN" case shows what each client library prints.

Failures are cached as well: a resolver that gets no usable answer must remember that for at least 1 second and at most 5 minutes, and back off rather than retry hard ({{RFC 9520 s3|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9520#section-3}}, December 2023), which is why a broken zone can stay broken for a few minutes after it is fixed.

Serving stale answers when the source is down

When an authoritative server is unreachable and a cached record has expired, a resolver normally has nothing to give: SERVFAIL, or, as recorded below, no answer at all before the client gives up. Serve-stale ({{RFC 8767|https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8767}}) lets it return the expired record instead, marked with a short TTL (30 s recommended), after trying the servers for a moment (1.8 s recommended). It trades correctness for availability: a client may get an address that has changed. That is usually the right trade for internal names during an outage of the DNS provider, and it softened the .de outage (section 9). corrected The old page said serve-stale is on by default in Unbound and BIND; it is off by default in both (Unbound serve-expired: no, {{manual|https://unbound.docs.nlnetlabs.nl/en/latest/manpages/unbound.conf.html#unbound-conf-serve-expired}}; BIND 9.20 stale-answer-enable no, {{reference|https://bind9.readthedocs.io/en/v9.20.0/reference.html#namedconf-statement-stale-cache-enable}}). Public resolvers and Kubernetes CoreDNS set their own policies.

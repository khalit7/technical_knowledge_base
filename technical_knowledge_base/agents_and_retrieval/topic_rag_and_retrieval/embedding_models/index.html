The idea in one screen

An embedding model is a transformer plus a pooling step that maps any text to one vector of fixed length, trained with a contrastive loss so that a query and the passage that answers it point the same way; which model works for you is decided on your own labelled queries with paired statistics, not by a leaderboard.

The query and the document go through the same model separately and meet only at the cosine

The section that owns each part is marked on the figure. Query: the shortest SciFact test claim; document: its labelled abstract (both real, from section 1's stepper). The document side runs once, at indexing; the query side runs per search.

You need: InfoNCE as a softmax over a line-up of candidates (Contrastive and self-supervised learning, section "InfoNCE, the workhorse loss"), nDCG@10 and bootstrap intervals (Evaluation metrics), and the parent Topic: rag-and-retrieval for where retrieval sits in a pipeline. The running example: SciFact (5,183 scientific abstracts and 300 test claims) and NFCorpus (3,633 medical documents and 323 test queries).

Bi-encoder
query and document encoded separately by one model, compared by one dot product; this page's subject.
Pooling
collapsing the per-token outputs into one vector: the first token (CLS), the mean, or the last token.
In-batch negative
another query's positive in the same training batch, used as a free wrong answer.
Hard, false negative
a mined wrong answer that looks right; one that is in fact relevant but unlabelled.
Temperature τ
divides the cosines before the softmax; 0.05 by default in sentence-transformers.
Asymmetric prompt
a prefix or instruction added to the query side only (or a different one per side).
Matryoshka
training so that the first m numbers of the vector are a usable embedding on their own.
MTEB
the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark: one protocol, many tasks, a leaderboard by the mean.
Paired bootstrap
resampling the same queries for two models to get an interval for their difference.
How to read the numbers
measured here: run on an Apple M1 Pro (16 GB, shared with other jobs) on the running example with a script in this page's src/scripts; relevance numbers (nDCG@10, recall) are deterministic and do not depend on the machine. published: a paper, model card or results repository, with its date. derived: computed from numbers shown, with the formula. nDCG@10 scores how high the relevant documents sit in the top 10 (1 is perfect); recall@100 is the share of relevant documents anywhere in the top 100.

1. One vector per text: a transformer, a pooling step, a normalisation

Why one vector. Relevance belongs to a pair, but a corpus has millions of documents and a query arrives in real time. A bi-encoder computes every document vector once, at indexing; a query then costs one model pass plus dot products. A cross-encoder reads query and document together (more accurate, nothing precomputable), so it can only reorder a short list; it is owned by Rerankers and late interaction, as is late interaction (ColBERT: one small vector per token). The two steps after the transformer. The transformer returns one vector per token. Pooling keeps one: CLS (the first token; bge-small, gte-modernbert), mean (average of the real tokens, padding ignored; all-MiniLM-L6, e5-small-v2) or last token (Qwen3-Embedding: in a decoder only the last token has seen the whole text). Normalisation divides by the length, so a dot product is a cosine (section 2; gte-modernbert ships without this step). The pooling is fixed by training, written in the model's 1_Pooling/config.json and modules.json: read them before writing your own inference. Step through one real claim:

2. Similarity and normalisation: use what the model was trained with


A small case first. Query q = (0.6, 0.8), length 1. Documents d1 = (1, 0), d2 = (0, 1) and d3 = (2, 0), which points the same way as d1 but is twice as long.


The dot product puts the longest vector first; cosine and L2 agree on d2




Dot products 0.6, 0.8, 1.2: d3 first. Cosines 0.6, 0.8, 0.6: d2 first, d1 and d3 tied. L2 distances 0.894, 0.632, 1.612: d2 first. Check: 2 − 2 × 0.8 = 0.4 = 0.6322. derived


The dot product Σ qidi grows with both lengths; the cosine divides it by both lengths, so only the angle counts; the L2 distance |q − d| is smaller when closer. For vectors of length 1 the three rank documents identically, because |q − d|2 = 2 − 2 cos(q, d). On real vectors: gte-modernbert has no Normalize step, and ranking its raw vectors by dot product drops SciFact nDCG@10 from 0.767 to 0.740, while L2 and cosine agree (the parent's shared runs, src/vec) measured here (parent page).


Rule. For a model trained with cosine (all five here), normalise once at indexing and use the inner product, which every vector index (Vector indexes from the inside) does fastest; never mix normalised documents with an unnormalised query. Hosted APIs usually return normalised vectors: OpenAI's embeddings are "normalized to length 1" (its embeddings guide, read 2026-10-14) published, vendor.







3. Training: pick your positive out of a line-up


The problem. A pretrained transformer's hidden states are not a retrieval space: masked-token or next-token training never asked a claim and its evidence to point the same way. The geometry is taught with (query, positive) pairs: a question and its answer passage, a title and its body, a claim and its evidence, a search and the page clicked. SciFact's train split gives 919 pairs from 809 claims; production models use hundreds of millions (Qwen3-Embedding: about 150 million synthetic pairs for a weakly supervised first stage, then about 12 million selected synthetic pairs plus labelled data, then model merging; arXiv 2506.05176, sections 3.2 and 3.3) published, vendor.


One batch is one big classification: each row must pick its diagonal




Rows are the batch's queries, columns every document the batch embedded: the B positives, then one mined hard negative per query. Each row is a softmax over its cosines divided by τ; the loss is minus the log-probability of the green cell. Every other query's positive is a free in-batch negative; the struck-out cell is a duplicate: q3's labelled abstract is also q2's positive, so it must be masked, not pushed away. Layout illustrative (B = 4).


Li = −log  exp(si,i/τ) / Σj exp(si,j/τ)


si,j
cosine between query i and column j of the figure (B positives plus the mined negatives)
τ
the temperature; sentence-transformers writes it as a scale 1/τ = 20, so τ = 0.05 by default


This is InfoNCE (van den Oord et al. 2018, arXiv 1807.03748) with in-batch negatives as Dense Passage Retrieval used them (Karpukhin et al. 2020, arXiv 2004.04906): free, because the batch's documents are embedded anyway. How its gradient splits among the negatives and what τ does is taught on Contrastive and self-supervised learning (48 real sentence pairs with a τ slider); its reading as a bound on mutual information on Information theory for ML; the same loss on image-text pairs (CLIP, SigLIP) on Vision encoders. The short version: nearly all the push lands on the one or two negatives closest to the query, which is why section 4 is about choosing them.


Batch size. A bigger batch is a bigger line-up. The parent's measurement on real vectors: with 8 candidates the labelled abstract already beats all in-batch negatives for 97.9% of train claims under bge-small; with 512, for 82.8% measured here (parent page). Small batches give a nearly solved task and little gradient, which is why BGE's general-purpose stage trained with batches up to 19,200 and in-batch negatives only (C-Pack, arXiv 2309.07597, section 3.4) published. Section 5 shows that at a fixed number of epochs on a small set this advice can reverse.


Duplicates. In SciFact train, 248 abstracts are the positive of more than one claim (602 of the 919 pairs), so a random batch of 32 holds such a clash about 46% of the time (derived, assuming independent draws). This page's loss masks any column labelled relevant to the row; sentence-transformers offers a no-duplicates batch sampler instead.

4. Hard negatives, and the false negatives among them


Hard negatives teach fine distinctions: a random cardiology abstract is trivially wrong for a claim about a chemokine; an abstract about the same chemokine that does not support the claim is what the model must learn to rank second. They are mined by running a retriever over the corpus and taking high-ranked documents not labelled relevant. Two miners on the 919 SciFact train pairs (scripts/e_mine.py), BM25 and bge-small, have top-10 pools that overlap by only 33% on average: they find different negatives. measured here


False negatives are the catch: "not labelled relevant" is not "irrelevant". SciFact's annotators labelled the abstracts cited for each claim, not every abstract that bears on it.


For a third of the training pairs, two different models put an unlabelled abstract above the labelled one




Per (claim, positive) pair: does the top unlabelled abstract of the whole corpus score above the labelled one? Both bge-small and Qwen3-Embedding say yes for 33.8% of pairs. Counted per claim with its first labelled abstract (the parent's way), the rates are 38.8% for bge-small and 38.4% for Qwen3-Embedding, equal to the parent's 38.8% and 38.4%. measured here


Two models agreeing is a strong hint that a label is missing. For 154 pairs both put the same unlabelled abstract first; they read like this:




Filtering. NV-Retriever (Moreira et al. 2024, arXiv 2407.15831) proposed positive-aware mining: drop candidates scoring above 95% of the positive's own score ("TopK-PercPos"), their best setting published; sentence-transformers' mine_hard_negatives(relative_margin=0.05) is the same rule. Qwen3-Embedding's loss instead masks any negative scoring more than 0.1 above the positive (report, section 3.1). On SciFact train with bge-small scores, the 95% filter removes 42.1% of the top-10 dense candidates; the first surviving candidate sits at rank 3 (median), and 65 pairs have none left in the top 200, so they fall back to a random abstract. measured here Other remedies: skip the very top ranks when sampling, let a guide model mask candidates it scores above the positive (GISTEmbed, section 12), or relabel the candidates with a cross-encoder. Whether filtering helps is an empirical question: the sibling Rerankers and late interaction found random negatives beat BM25-mined ones for a cross-encoder on the same 809 train claims (rerank nDCG@10 0.734 against 0.726, 2 seeds each) and named false negatives as the likely cause; section 5 tests it on a bi-encoder.






5. A tiny fine-tune, measured


The question. Does in-domain contrastive training help a small general model on SciFact, and does the choice of negatives matter once false negatives are counted? bge-small-en-v1.5 (33 M parameters) trained on the 919 SciFact train pairs for 2 epochs, InfoNCE at τ = 0.05, four arms at batch 32: in-batch negatives only; +BM25 one BM25-mined negative per pair; +dense one bge-small-mined negative; +dense, filtered the same after the 95% filter. Plus in-batch only at batch 8 and 128. Three seeds each; the spread across seeds is the noise floor. Deliberately tiny: it shows the mechanics and their size on one corpus, not a production recipe.


Every arm beats the starting model; the arms differ by about a point, the seeds by less
nDCG@10 per arm, every seed a dot, the starting model as a dashed line; SciFact above, NFCorpus below (a domain it never trained on). 




  1. In-domain training helps even at this size: in-batch negatives alone take SciFact from 0.713 to 0.746; every arm's paired interval against the starting model is clear of zero.
  2. Mined hard negatives add about one point: BM25-mined 0.756 (paired difference against in-batch +0.004 to +0.016), bge-mined 0.757 (+0.005 to +0.018). Unlike the sibling cross-encoder run, the bi-encoder keeps its in-batch negatives, so the mined one is an addition, not a replacement.
  3. The false-negative filter took the gain away: 0.748, against in-batch 0.746. It removed 42.1% of the top-10 candidates and pushed the first kept candidate to rank 3, so the negatives left were easier. Whether the removed abstracts were truly relevant needs new labels; on this set, keeping them helped.
  4. At a fixed number of epochs, smaller batches won: batch 8 0.760 (228 steps), 32 0.746 (56 steps), 128 0.732 (14 steps). With 919 pairs a bigger batch means far fewer updates, and that dominates; recipes like BGE's 19,200 train on millions of pairs, and this run cannot test that regime.
  5. No forgetting: every arm also gains +0.009 to +0.014 on NFCorpus, a domain it never saw.
  6. The noise floor: seeds move an arm's mean by at most 0.005, less than the effects in 1, 2 and 4; the filtered arm's +0.002 over in-batch is inside it. The test set adds a paired interval of about ±0.008 for differences between arms.


How to read a small difference: one model's nDCG@10 on 300 claims carries a 95% interval about ±0.05 wide (section 11), but two runs on the same claims are compared with a paired bootstrap, which is much tighter; seeds add their own spread, which only repeats reveal. A difference smaller than the spread across seeds is not a finding.

The rule
Use the documented prompt as the default, then measure without it and with one or two task wordings on your own labelled queries: that costs one re-encoding of the queries, not of the corpus. A document prefix changes every stored vector, so changing it means re-embedding the corpus, and a query encoded with a mismatched setting silently loses quality.







7. Smaller vectors: fewer numbers (Matryoshka) or fewer bits (int8, binary)


Qwen3-Embedding stores 1,024 float32 numbers per text: 4,096 bytes, 4 GB for a million chunks (derived: 106 × 1,024 × 4 bytes). Two knobs shrink it: keep the first m numbers, or store fewer bits per number. Whether cutting works depends on training:


LMRL = Σm ∈ M cm  L(v1:m)


M
the nested sizes, for example {32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024}
v1:m
the first m numbers of the embedding, re-normalised
L
the contrastive loss of section 3, applied to the truncated vector
cm
a weight per size, often 1


Matryoshka Representation Learning (Kusupati et al. 2022, arXiv 2205.13147) adds the loss on every prefix, so the front of the vector carries the most; sentence-transformers wraps any loss this way (MatryoshkaLoss). Qwen3-Embedding was trained like this (its card: "user-defined output dimensions ranging from 32 to 1024"); bge-small was not.


Predict: Qwen3-Embedding cut to 256 numbers and stored as 1 bit each (32 bytes, 128 times smaller), searched without any rescoring. What share of its full nDCG@10 is left?
About 81% (0.567 against 0.700); rescoring the top 100 with the float vectors brings it to 98% (0.684). Set the slider to 256 and switch the precision to see both.


A Matryoshka-trained model keeps most of its quality at a quarter of its numbers; one trained without does not

The defaults reproduce the parent's figures from its own output: Qwen3-Embedding keeps 98% of its nDCG@10 at half its dimensions and 95% at a quarter; bge-small keeps 94% and 81%; gte-modernbert cut to 384 dimensions (0.726) still beats Qwen3-Embedding at 1,024 (0.700). In bits: int8 (1 byte per number) is free (Qwen3 at 1,024: 0.996 of the exact top 10); binary codes of all 1,024 dimensions keep 0.674 of the exact top 10, and with the top 100 rescored in float32 keep all of the quality (0.700). Re-normalise after cutting. Hosted models expose the same idea as a parameter: OpenAI's text-embedding-3 models take a dimensions argument (1,536 by default for -small, 3,072 for -large), and OpenAI states that "a text-embedding-3-large embedding can be shortened to a size of 256 while still outperforming an unshortened text-embedding-ada-002 embedding with a size of 1536" (OpenAI embeddings guide) published, vendor. Index-side compression (product quantisation, RaBitQ) is on Vector indexes from the inside; indexing a Matryoshka prefix in pgvector on Search and vector databases.


The old page's figure "up to 14x smaller embeddings at the same ImageNet accuracy" is the MRL paper's ImageNet-1K classification result; it says nothing about retrieval on your corpus.







8. Long inputs: the silent cut


Every model reads at most a fixed number of tokens and silently drops the rest: all-MiniLM-L6 256 by default, bge-small and e5-small-v2 512 (the e5 card: "Long texts will be truncated to at most 512 tokens"), gte-modernbert 8,192 (run here at 1,024), Qwen3-Embedding 32K (run here at 1,024). The vector of a truncated text is still a vector.

In chunked pipelines the same problem is measured by The retrieval pipeline on QASPER papers: LangChain's default 4,000-character chunks are 782 BERT tokens at the median, so all-MiniLM-L6 reads 33.3% of their tokens and bge-small 65.1%; even 256-Qwen3-token chunks exceed MiniLM's 256 BERT tokens 16.9% of the time measured here (sibling page). Size chunks in the embedding model's tokens. Long-context embedders (gte-modernbert, Qwen3-Embedding, modernbert-embed at 8K to 32K) can embed a whole paper, but one vector for 8,000 tokens averages many topics; the usual design still chunks, and late chunking (embed the long text once, pool per chunk) is measured on the pipeline page.

10. MTEB: what its scores do and do not predict


MTEB scores a model on many tasks with one fixed protocol and ranks models by the mean. The current English benchmark, MTEB(eng, v2) (introduced with MMTEB, Enevoldsen et al. 2025, arXiv 2502.13595), has 41 tasks: 10 retrieval, 9 semantic similarity (STS), 8 classification, 8 clustering, 3 pair classification, 2 reranking, 1 summarization. SciFact and NFCorpus are not among its 10 retrieval tasks, so they are a fair test of transfer.


Predict: take the 10 best models by the MTEB(eng, v2) average and the 10 best on SciFact (149 models). How many models are in both lists?
2 of 10 for SciFact, 3 for NFCorpus, although the rank correlation over all models is 0.83.


The average sorts the field but not the top: near the top, the order on your set is a coin flip

  • Across the whole range the average is informative: Spearman rank correlation 0.83 with SciFact and 0.90 with NFCorpus. A model near the bottom of the leaderboard is rarely good on your set.
    • 

  • Near the top it is not: the 10 best by the average and the 10 best on SciFact share 2 models (3 for NFCorpus). Of the 889 pairs of models whose averages differ by less than one point, 45% are ordered the other way on SciFact.
    • 

  • The leader by the average (Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-8B) is 8th on SciFact; the SciFact leader (Salesforce/SFR-Embedding-2_R) is 16th by the average. Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B is 13th by the average and 73th on SciFact; bge-small 47th and 60th (all read at the commit above; these positions move with every submission).

Why rank does not transfer, in order of size here: the task mix (the mean blends classification, clustering and similarity with retrieval: a model can climb on 31 non-retrieval tasks); your domain (the retrieval tasks are arguments, questions, fact-checking, finance, COVID literature; your corpus is none of them); contamination (13 of these 149 models declare SciFact training data and 14 NFCorpus, while 28 declare nothing at all, so their zero-shot status is unknown); noise (a few hundred queries per task; section 11); and setup (a model run without its query instruction or at a shorter maximum length scores below its leaderboard number; sections 6 and 8). The leaderboard is a shortlist generator: read the retrieval tasks nearest your domain, not the mean.


How each task type is scored (1 min)
Retrieval and reranking: nDCG@10 or MAP; classification: accuracy of a logistic regression on the vectors; clustering: V-measure; semantic similarity: Spearman correlation of cosines with human scores; pair classification: average precision. MTEB(eng, v2)'s description in mteb 2.24.13: "prioritizing tasks not commonly used for fine-tuning to give a more realistic estimate of generalization performance". 11. Choosing a model on your own data


    

  1. Shortlist 3 to 6 models: licence, languages, maximum length against your chunk size, vector size against your memory, hosted or local budget, and the MTEB retrieval tasks nearest your domain.
    2. 

  2. Label a few hundred real queries (from logs if you have them) with their relevant chunks; keep 20 to 30% aside as a confirmation set. Same chunking for every model.
    3. 

  3. Run each model with its documented prompt and without; exact search (no index, so the index does not blur the comparison); BM25 and a hybrid as references.
    4. 

  4. Compare paired: per-query nDCG@10 (or recall@k at your k), the difference between models on the same queries, bootstrap interval of the difference. Models within the interval are tied; break ties on cost (bytes per vector, query latency, indexing time, licence).
    5. 

  5. Confirm the winner on the held-out queries, then on a sample of production traffic after launch.
    6.

12. Fine-tuning for real: when it pays and how


When. After the protocol above says the best off-the-shelf model is not good enough, and when you have a domain the general models have not seen (internal jargon, product codes, queries that are error messages), at least a few thousand (query, positive) pairs or a way to make them (a language model writing questions for your passages is the common route; Qwen3-Embedding's report describes synthetic pairs at scale), and a labelled evaluation set never used for training.


How, with sentence-transformers 6.1.0 (the tools this page's run mirrors): mine_hard_negatives(..., relative_margin=0.05) for positive-aware mining; MultipleNegativesRankingLoss, or CachedMultipleNegativesRankingLoss for large batches on a small GPU; GISTEmbedLoss, which uses a second "guide" model to mask in-batch negatives the guide scores above the positive; MatryoshkaLoss around any of them if you will truncate; a no-duplicates batch sampler; evaluation on held-out queries every epoch; three seeds.


The core of this page's training loop (code)


The costs people forget. A new model means re-embedding the whole corpus (bge-small: 36 s for SciFact here; Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B: 816 s), and vectors from the old and new model cannot share an index: a running system needs a second index and a switch-over (RAG in production measures what mixing them does: nDCG@10 0.379 at 50% re-embedded against 0.713 clean). Check the other domains for forgetting (section 5's NFCorpus panel).







Depth: two design questions


Switch a 50-million-chunk index to a better embedding model without downtime. Plan it. (1 min)
Evaluate offline on a labelled set first (paired, with intervals). Then build a second index: re-embed all chunks with the new model in a batch job (throughput-bound; cost = tokens × price or GPU-hours), while new and updated documents are written to both indexes. Shadow-query the new index and compare results and latency; switch reads with a flag per tenant or a percentage; keep the old index until the new one has been stable; then delete. Never mix vectors of two models in one index: a query vector from one model is meaningless against the other's documents. Budget memory for two indexes during the switch, or use Matryoshka truncation or quantisation (section 7) to fit.


Retrieval over internal documents is poor on product codes and jargon. What do you try, in what order? (1 min)
Measure first (golden set from search logs). Add BM25 and hybrid fusion (exact terms are BM25's strength); check chunking and the query prompt; try two or three stronger off-the-shelf models with the protocol of section 11; add a reranker; only then fine-tune on mined pairs from logs or LLM-generated questions, with positive-aware hard negatives, several seeds and a held-out set, and check general-domain queries for forgetting.

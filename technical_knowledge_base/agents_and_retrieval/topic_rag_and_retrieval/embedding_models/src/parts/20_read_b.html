2. Similarity and normalisation: use what the model was trained with

A small case first. Query q = (0.6, 0.8), length 1. Documents d1 = (1, 0), d2 = (0, 1) and d3 = (2, 0), which points the same way as d1 but is twice as long.

The dot product puts the longest vector first; cosine and L2 agree on d2

Dot products 0.6, 0.8, 1.2: d3 first. Cosines 0.6, 0.8, 0.6: d2 first, d1 and d3 tied. L2 distances 0.894, 0.632, 1.612: d2 first. Check: 2 − 2 × 0.8 = 0.4 = 0.6322. derived

The dot product Σ qidi grows with both lengths; the cosine divides it by both lengths, so only the angle counts; the L2 distance |q − d| is smaller when closer. For vectors of length 1 the three rank documents identically, because |q − d|2 = 2 − 2 cos(q, d). On real vectors: gte-modernbert has no Normalize step, and ranking its raw vectors by dot product drops SciFact nDCG@10 from 0.767 to 0.740, while L2 and cosine agree (the parent's shared runs, src/vec) measured here (parent page).

Rule. For a model trained with cosine (all five here), normalise once at indexing and use the inner product, which every vector index ({{Vector indexes from the inside|n:3f85c17b0d0d81f29707c130b2ebcfb1}}) does fastest; never mix normalised documents with an unnormalised query. Hosted APIs usually return normalised vectors: OpenAI's embeddings are "normalized to length 1" (its embeddings guide, read 2026-10-14) published, vendor.

3. Training: pick your positive out of a line-up

The problem. A pretrained transformer's hidden states are not a retrieval space: masked-token or next-token training never asked a claim and its evidence to point the same way. The geometry is taught with (query, positive) pairs: a question and its answer passage, a title and its body, a claim and its evidence, a search and the page clicked. SciFact's train split gives @@n_pairs@@ pairs from @@n_claims@@ claims; production models use hundreds of millions (Qwen3-Embedding: about 150 million synthetic pairs for a weakly supervised first stage, then about 12 million selected synthetic pairs plus labelled data, then model merging; arXiv 2506.05176, sections 3.2 and 3.3) published, vendor.

One batch is one big classification: each row must pick its diagonal

Rows are the batch's queries, columns every document the batch embedded: the B positives, then one mined hard negative per query. Each row is a softmax over its cosines divided by τ; the loss is minus the log-probability of the green cell. Every other query's positive is a free in-batch negative; the struck-out cell is a duplicate: q3's labelled abstract is also q2's positive, so it must be masked, not pushed away. Layout illustrative (B = 4).

Li = −log  exp(si,i/τ) / Σj exp(si,j/τ)
si,j
cosine between query i and column j of the figure (B positives plus the mined negatives)
τ
the temperature; sentence-transformers writes it as a scale 1/τ = 20, so τ = 0.05 by default

This is InfoNCE (van den Oord et al. 2018, arXiv 1807.03748) with in-batch negatives as Dense Passage Retrieval used them (Karpukhin et al. 2020, arXiv 2004.04906): free, because the batch's documents are embedded anyway. How its gradient splits among the negatives and what τ does is taught on {{Contrastive and self-supervised learning|n:3c65c17b0d0d811db6a7d02d0ee7215c}} (48 real sentence pairs with a τ slider); its reading as a bound on mutual information on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}; the same loss on image-text pairs (CLIP, SigLIP) on {{Vision encoders|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c99f60ceb2f02cf5ea}}. The short version: nearly all the push lands on the one or two negatives closest to the query, which is why section 4 is about choosing them.

Batch size. A bigger batch is a bigger line-up. The parent's measurement on real vectors: with 8 candidates the labelled abstract already beats all in-batch negatives for 97.9% of train claims under bge-small; with 512, for 82.8% measured here (parent page). Small batches give a nearly solved task and little gradient, which is why BGE's general-purpose stage trained with batches up to 19,200 and in-batch negatives only (C-Pack, arXiv 2309.07597, section 3.4) published. Section 5 shows that at a fixed number of epochs on a small set this advice can reverse.

4. Hard negatives, and the false negatives among them


Hard negatives teach fine distinctions: a random cardiology abstract is trivially wrong for a claim about a chemokine; an abstract about the same chemokine that does not support the claim is what the model must learn to rank second. They are mined by running a retriever over the corpus and taking high-ranked documents not labelled relevant. Two miners on the @@n_pairs@@ SciFact train pairs (scripts/e_mine.py), BM25 and bge-small, have top-10 pools that overlap by only @@ov_bm_dense@@% on average: they find different negatives. measured here


False negatives are the catch: "not labelled relevant" is not "irrelevant". SciFact's annotators labelled the abstracts cited for each claim, not every abstract that bears on it.

Filtering. NV-Retriever (Moreira et al. 2024, arXiv 2407.15831) proposed positive-aware mining: drop candidates scoring above 95% of the positive's own score ("TopK-PercPos"), their best setting published; sentence-transformers' mine_hard_negatives(relative_margin=0.05) is the same rule. Qwen3-Embedding's loss instead masks any negative scoring more than 0.1 above the positive (report, section 3.1). On SciFact train with bge-small scores, the 95% filter removes @@filt_share@@% of the top-10 dense candidates; the first surviving candidate sits at rank @@kept_rank@@ (median), and @@empty_pool@@ pairs have none left in the top 200, so they fall back to a random abstract. measured here Other remedies: skip the very top ranks when sampling, let a guide model mask candidates it scores above the positive (GISTEmbed, section 12), or relabel the candidates with a cross-encoder. Whether filtering helps is an empirical question: the sibling {{Rerankers and late interaction|n:3f85c17b0d0d813fba53fc883f8a2c8f}} found random negatives beat BM25-mined ones for a cross-encoder on the same 809 train claims (rerank nDCG@10 0.734 against 0.726, 2 seeds each) and named false negatives as the likely cause; section 5 tests it on a bi-encoder.

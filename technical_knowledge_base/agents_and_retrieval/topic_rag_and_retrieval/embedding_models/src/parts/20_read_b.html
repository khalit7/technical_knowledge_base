2. Bi-encoder or cross-encoder: where the query meets the document

The problem. Relevance is a property of a pair (query, document), but a corpus has millions of documents and a query arrives in real time. Two designs answer differently.

Bi-encoder (this page)

Query and document are encoded separately by the same model. Every document vector is computed once, at indexing time; a query costs one model pass plus a vector search. The price: the two texts never see each other, so all the meaning must squeeze into one vector each.

Cross-encoder (a reranker)

Query and document are concatenated and read together: every query token attends to every document token, and the model outputs one score. More accurate, but nothing can be precomputed: one model pass per (query, document) pair. Owned by {{Rerankers and late interaction|n:3f85c17b0d0d813fba53fc883f8a2c8f}}.

That is why every pipeline uses both: the bi-encoder (with BM25) narrows millions to tens, the cross-encoder orders the tens. Late interaction (ColBERT) sits between: one small vector per token, precomputed, compared token by token at query time; also on the rerankers page.

3. Similarity and normalisation

Three ways to compare two vectors q and d: the dot product Σ qidi (larger is closer, grows with both lengths), the cosine, the dot product divided by both lengths (only the angle counts, from −1 to 1), and the L2 distance |q − d| (smaller is closer). For vectors of length 1 they rank documents identically, because |q − d|2 = 2 − 2 cos(q, d). The parent's Vector search lab shows this on real vectors: gte-modernbert has no Normalize step, and ranking its raw vectors by dot product drops SciFact nDCG@10 from 0.767 to 0.740, while L2 and cosine agree. measured here (parent page)

Worked example (from the old page, arithmetic re-checked)
Query q = (0.6, 0.8), length 1. Documents d1 = (1, 0), d2 = (0, 1), d3 = (2, 0), which points the same way as d1 but is twice as long. Dot products 0.6, 0.8, 1.2: d3 first. Cosines 0.6, 0.8, 0.6: d2 first, d1 and d3 tied. L2 distances 0.894, 0.632, 1.612: d2 first. Check: 2 − 2 × 0.8 = 0.4 = 0.6322. The dot product put the longest vector first although it points further from the query than d2.

Rule. Use the similarity the model was trained with. For a model trained with cosine (all five here), normalise once at indexing time and use the inner product; never mix normalised documents with an unnormalised query. Hosted APIs usually return normalised vectors: OpenAI's embeddings are "normalized to length 1" (its embeddings guide, read 2026-10-14) published, vendor.

4. How the space is trained: the contrastive loss

The problem. A pretrained transformer's hidden states are not a retrieval space: next-token or masked-token training never asked a claim and its evidence to point the same way. The geometry has to be taught, with pairs.

The data: pairs. (query, positive) pairs: a question and its answer passage, a title and its body, a claim and its evidence, a search and the page clicked. SciFact's train split gives @@n_pairs@@ such pairs from @@n_claims@@ claims. Production models use hundreds of millions: Qwen3-Embedding reports about 150 million synthetic pairs for a weakly supervised first stage, then about 12 million selected synthetic pairs plus labelled data for supervised fine-tuning, then model merging (Qwen3 Embedding report, arXiv 2506.05176, sections 3.2 and 3.3) published, vendor.

Li = − log   exp(si,i / τ) / Σj exp(si,j / τ)
si,j
cosine between query i and candidate document j; candidate i is query i's positive
j
runs over the line-up: every positive in the batch (the other queries' positives are in-batch negatives) and every mined hard negative in the batch
τ
the temperature; sentence-transformers writes it as a scale 1/τ = 20, so τ = 0.05 by default

This is InfoNCE (van den Oord et al. 2018, arXiv 1807.03748): a cross-entropy whose "classes" are the candidates (its reading as a bound on mutual information is on {{Information theory for ML|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c6baf3f778a62e14e5}}; CLIP uses the same loss on image-text pairs, see the {{CLIP paper page|n:3c65c17b0d0d8194a85dfc5f3bf3f799}}). It raises the positive's cosine and lowers every negative's, each in proportion to its softmax weight. In-batch negatives are free because the batch's documents are embedded anyway (Karpukhin et al. 2020, DPR, arXiv 2004.04906). The temperature sets how sharp that weighting is: at τ = 0.05 a cosine gap of 0.05 is a factor e ≈ 2.7 in weight, so nearly all the push lands on the one or two closest negatives.

5. Hard negatives and false negatives


Hard negatives teach fine distinctions: a random abstract about cardiology is trivially wrong for a claim about a chemokine; an abstract about the same chemokine that does not support the claim is what the model must learn to rank second. They are mined by running a retriever over the corpus and taking high-ranked documents that are not labelled relevant. Two miners here, on the @@n_pairs@@ SciFact train pairs (scripts/e_mine.py): BM25 (word overlap) and bge-small (the model being trained). Their top-10 pools overlap by only @@ov_bm_dense@@% on average: they find different negatives. measured here

Filtering them out. NV-Retriever (Moreira et al. 2024, arXiv 2407.15831) proposed "positive-aware" mining: use the positive's own score as an anchor and drop candidates scoring above 95% of it ("TopK-PercPos"), which they found best among their settings published. sentence-transformers' mine_hard_negatives(relative_margin=0.05) is the same rule. Qwen3-Embedding's loss instead masks any negative scoring more than 0.1 above the positive (report, section 3.1).

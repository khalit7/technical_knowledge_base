10. Updates and deletes

Flat: append or remove rows. IVF: inserts go into the nearest existing cell, deletes remove list entries; retrain against drift (section 3). HNSW: an insert is a search plus link updates, the same work per vector as building. A delete cannot simply remove a node, because other nodes route through it: hnswlib, like pgvector and FreshDiskANN, marks it as deleted, a tombstone (a flag bit in the node's link-list header, *ll_cur |= DELETE_MARK, hnswalg.h line 877). Searches still walk through it but never return it, exactly the in-graph filter of section 9.

Tombstones keep recall but cost work: half the rows dead, nearly twice the distances

hnswlib tombstones on SciFact (M 16, efC 200), recall against the exact top 10 of the rows still alive, against a graph rebuilt on the live rows only measured here.

With half the rows deleted, a query at ef 40 computes @@del50_tomb_nd@@ distances against @@del50_reb_nd@@ in a rebuilt graph, and the dead vectors still occupy memory. Reusing deleted slots for new vectors (hnswlib's allow_replace_deleted) costs a little recall, because the old links stay around a vector that may belong elsewhere (details below). FreshDiskANN's answer is batch consolidation: after "1-10% of the index size" is deleted, reconnect the neighbours of each deleted node to its out-neighbours and re-prune (arXiv 2105.09613, published, authors' runs); pgvector does the equivalent in VACUUM (Search and vector databases page). In every system the honest long-term fix is the same: rebuild periodically, or per segment, as Lucene does when it merges segments.

Changing the embedding model is not an update
Vectors from two models are not comparable: a new model means re-embedding everything and building a new index, usually side by side with the old one until the switch. 12. Scaling to 100 million vectors and beyond

How work per query grows with N, on prefixes of Quora (10,000 to 522,931 vectors) at a fixed recall target measured here:

Exact grows with N, IVF like √N, HNSW barely at all

From 10,000 to 522,931 vectors (52 times more), exact search grows 52 times; IVF at recall 0.95 from @@sc_ivf95_a@@ to @@sc_ivf95_b@@ distances, roughly like √N; HNSW barely moves (@@sc_hnsw95_a@@ to @@sc_hnsw95_b@@). On these vectors the graph's work is close to flat in N; the paper's claim is O(log N). The higher the recall target, the faster the work grows (IVF at 0.99: @@sc_ivf99_a@@ to @@sc_ivf99_b@@), as the Faiss library paper found on BigANN-1B, where IVF search time grew as N0.29 at recall 0.5 and N0.45 at 0.99 (published). Past one machine's memory, four techniques combine:

  • Compress in RAM (PQ, int8, binary, RaBitQ) and rescore from somewhere cheaper: with 64 GB for a billion vectors, NeurIPS'21 Big-ANN's in-memory track reached recall@10 of roughly 0.63 to 0.76 at 10,000 queries per second.
  • Put the graph on SSD (DiskANN) or the lists on SSD (SPANN): the SSD track's DiskANN baseline (8 vCPUs, 64 GB) reached roughly 0.90 to 0.95 at 1,500 queries per second.
  • Shard: search every shard for its top k and merge; merged recall is about the per-shard recall, latency the slowest shard's. Sharding by a key the queries filter on (a tenant, a date) lets most queries touch one shard. Replicate for queries per second and availability.
  • Use GPUs for builds and batched search (section 2).
Sources, SPANN, and HNSW's memory at 100M (published)

10. Updates and deletes

Collections change. Each family takes changes differently:

  • Flat: trivial; append or remove rows.
  • IVF: new vectors go into the nearest existing cell, so inserts are cheap, but the centroids slowly stop describing the data (section 3 measured the drift). Deletes just remove entries from a list. Periodic retraining is the fix.
  • HNSW: an insert is a search plus link updates (the same work as building, per vector). A delete cannot simply remove a node: other nodes route through it. hnswlib, like pgvector and FreshDiskANN, marks it as deleted (a tombstone; hnswlib sets a flag bit in the node's link-list header, *ll_cur |= DELETE_MARK, line 877): searches still walk through it but never return it, exactly the in-graph filter of section 9.

Measured with hnswlib's tombstones on SciFact (M 16, efC 200), recall against the exact top 10 of the rows still alive, and the work per query, against a graph rebuilt on the live rows only measured here:

Recall does not suffer: the dead nodes still route. The cost is work: with half the rows deleted, a query at ef 40 computes @@del50_tomb_nd@@ distances against @@del50_reb_nd@@ in a rebuilt graph, and the dead vectors still occupy memory. hnswlib can reuse the slot of a deleted element for a new vector (allow_replace_deleted); replacing 30% of SciFact with NFCorpus abstracts that way gave recall @@repl_ef10@@ at ef 10 against @@fresh_ef10@@ for a graph built fresh on the same final vectors (@@repl_ef40@@ and @@fresh_ef40@@ at ef 40): replacement keeps the old links around a new vector that may belong elsewhere. FreshDiskANN's answer is batch consolidation: after "1-10% of the index size" is deleted, reconnect the neighbours of each deleted node to its out-neighbours and re-prune (arXiv 2105.09613, published, authors' runs); pgvector does the equivalent in VACUUM (measured on the Search and vector databases page). In every system the honest long-term fix is the same: rebuild periodically, or rebuild per segment, as Lucene does when it merges segments.

Changing the embedding model is not an update
Vectors from two models are not comparable: a new model means re-embedding everything and building a new index, usually side by side with the old one until the switch. The Search and vector databases page gives the column-by-column recipe; {{RAG in production|n:3f85c17b0d0d81aa9ba9c0ceac9e28c1}} the dual-index operations.

12. Scaling to 100 million vectors and beyond

How work per query grows with N, measured on prefixes of Quora (10,000 to 522,931 vectors) at a fixed recall target, in distance computations measured here:

From 10,000 to 522,931 vectors (52 times more), exact search grows 52 times; IVF at recall 0.95 grows from @@sc_ivf95_a@@ to @@sc_ivf95_b@@ distances, roughly like √N; HNSW barely moves (@@sc_hnsw95_a@@ to @@sc_hnsw95_b@@). On these vectors the graph's work is close to flat in N; the paper's claim is O(log N). The higher the recall target, the faster the work grows (IVF at 0.99: @@sc_ivf99_a@@ to @@sc_ivf99_b@@), as the Faiss library paper found on BigANN-1B, where IVF search time grew as N0.29 at recall 0.5 and N0.45 at 0.99 (published). Past one machine's memory, four techniques combine:

  • Compress in RAM (PQ, int8, binary, RaBitQ) and rescore from somewhere cheaper. With 64 GB of RAM for a billion vectors, the NeurIPS'21 Big-ANN in-memory track reached recall@10 of roughly 0.63 to 0.76 at 10,000 queries per second (arXiv 2205.03763, Table 2, independent benchmark).
  • Put the graph on SSD (DiskANN) or the lists on SSD (SPANN). The same competition's SSD track, with the DiskANN baseline on 8 vCPUs and 64 GB, reached roughly 0.90 to 0.95 at 1,500 queries per second (Table 3); SPANN reports 90% recall@10 "in just around one millisecond with only 32GB memory cost" on billion-scale sets, about twice as fast as DiskANN at equal memory (NeurIPS 2021, published, authors' runs).
  • Shard: split the collection over machines, search every shard for its top k, merge. Recall of the merged result is about the per-shard recall; latency is the slowest shard's. Sharding by a key the queries filter on (a tenant, a date) lets most queries touch one shard. Replicate each shard for queries per second and availability.
  • Use GPUs for builds and for batched search: FAISS on 4 GPUs built a kNN graph over a billion vectors in under 12 hours (2017, section 2).

Memory at 100M with HNSW is large: Munyampirwa et al. measured hnswlib's peak build memory at 183 GB for 100M BigANN vectors (128-d), 113 GB for their flat graph (Table 6, published, authors' runs). ANN-Benchmarks, the standard independent comparison of libraries, tests at most about 10 million vectors on a single CPU (README, "as of April 2025"), so it says little directly about 100M to 1B behaviour.

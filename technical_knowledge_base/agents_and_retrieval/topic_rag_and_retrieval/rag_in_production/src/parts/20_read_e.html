10. Monitoring retrieval quality

The problem. A RAG system rarely fails loudly. A half-finished backfill, an index rebuilt with the wrong setting or a new kind of question all return answers that look fine. Quality has to be measured continuously, with labels where you have them and without where you do not.

Log what a later investigation needs. Per request: a pseudonymous user and tenant id, the query, the retrieved and reranked ids with their scores, the index and embedding-model versions, the prompt version, per-stage latencies, the answer and its citations, and any feedback. Prefer ids to passage text in logs (section 4). Traces in the OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions and the tools that store them belong to {{LLM observability|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fe82c0c6e9360afec4}}.

Feedback signals (thumbs, clicks on a citation, copying the answer, asking again in other words within a minute) are cheap and biased: few users vote, and they vote when annoyed. Use them for trends and for finding cases to label, not as a metric.

A label-free drift monitor. The similarity of each query's best match in the index says how well the collection covers what people ask. With bge-small over SciFact, SciFact's own claims had a median best-match cosine of @@dr_sci@@; NFCorpus's medical questions sent to the same index @@dr_nf@@; HotpotQA's Wikipedia questions @@dr_hp@@ measured here. Track the share of queries below a low percentile of a healthy week (here, 90% of SciFact claims score above @@dr_sci_p10@@, while 90% of HotpotQA questions score below @@dr_hp_p90@@): a rise means new topics the corpus does not cover, or a broken index.

Canary queries are a handful of fixed questions with known answers sent through the production read path every few minutes. They catch the breaks that matter most and happen fastest: a model and index mismatch (section 3), an empty filter result (section 5), a stale replica.

Evals in CI: how many golden queries? A golden set is a fixed list of real questions with relevance labels, run before every change to models, chunking, indexes or prompts. Its size decides what it can see. Below, each change to the SciFact system (bge-small, exact search, nDCG@10 @@ci_base@@) was tested on golden sets of n claims drawn from the 300 (2,000 draws), with a one-sided paired t-test at 5%: the share of draws that flag the change is its power measured here.

  • Breaks are easy: FAISS IVF at nprobe 1 (nDCG @@ci_ivf1_ndcg@@) is flagged @@ci_ivf1_p10@@% of the time with only 10 claims; the half-done backfill likewise.
  • A real but moderate loss (IVF at nprobe 8, nDCG @@ci_ivf8_ndcg@@) needs about 100 claims (power @@ci_ivf8_p100@@%; @@ci_ivf8_p50@@% at 50, @@ci_ivf8_p25@@% at 25).

11. Security: what retrieved text can make the model do

Indirect prompt injection. Retrieved passages are text written by someone else, placed in the model's input next to your instructions. If a writer can get text into the corpus (a wiki page, an email, a support ticket, a web page), they can write instructions to the model, and the model may follow them: Greshake et al. named this indirect prompt injection (arXiv 2302.12173, 2023). On the constructed corpus, one community-edited page carries a planted instruction to send users to a fake sign-in page; retrieval put it in the top 4 for @@inj_in4@@ of @@inj_n@@ expense questions. With a plain prompt, Qwen3-1.7B repeated the planted link in @@inj_plain_17@@ of @@inj_n@@ answers and Qwen3-4B in @@inj_plain_4@@; with spotlighting (documents wrapped in tags and the rule that text inside them is data, never instructions; Hines et al., arXiv 2403.14720, which reports attack success on GPT-family models falling from above 50% to below 2%, published), @@inj_spot_17@@ and @@inj_spot_4@@ measured here. The planted page is public, so it also reached the prompts of the access-control test (in @@p5_in_filt@@ of @@ctx_n@@ filtered top 4s): Qwen3-1.7B appended the fake sign-in link to @@spill_17@@ of its @@acl_rows_n@@ answers there, questions about salaries and lawsuits included, and Qwen3-4B to @@spill_4@@. Prompt defences lower the rate; they are not a boundary.

Poisoning. PoisonedRAG reports that five crafted passages per target question, added to a knowledge base of millions, made the target answer appear about 90% of the time (Zou et al., arXiv 2402.07867, published, authors' runs): a crafted passage is written to be retrieved.

Exfiltration. If the chat interface renders Markdown, an injected instruction can make the model write an image link whose address carries private text (from the conversation or other retrieved passages); the browser fetches it and the text leaves. The fix is in the renderer: do not load remote images or links from model output, or allow only known domains.

Defences that are boundaries: access control at retrieval (section 5), so injected text cannot reach data the user cannot read anyway; provenance on every chunk (who could write it) and lower trust, or exclusion, for openly editable sources; least privilege for any tool the model can call, with a human confirmation for actions; output rendering that cannot make network requests. Defences that lower rates: spotlighting, classifiers on retrieved text, and output checks. The full treatment is {{Agent security|n:3f15c17b0d0d81ad9fb7d1dd74217335}}; staged runtime checks are on {{Guardrails|n:3c65c17b0d0d819bbfa5dffa9246c297}}.

12. Scaling: shards, replicas and hybrid search at scale

Two different things grow. Data: when the index no longer fits one machine's memory, it is compressed (int8, binary or product quantization, with rescoring), moved to SSD (DiskANN), or split into shards, each searched for its own top k and merged; the merged recall is about the per-shard recall, and the latency is the slowest shard's (section 7's tail). Traffic: when one copy cannot answer enough queries per second, add replicas (full copies), which also keep the service up when a machine fails. Sharding by a key that queries filter on (tenant, region, date) lets most queries touch one shard.

Hybrid search (BM25 plus vectors) shards the same way: each shard holds the keyword and vector index for its documents, returns both lists, and fusion happens after the merge. The published scale numbers (the Big-ANN competitions at a billion vectors, DiskANN and SPANN on SSD, HNSW's build memory at 100 million) and the measured growth of search work with collection size are in {{Vector indexes, section 12|n:3f85c17b0d0d81f29707c130b2ebcfb1}}; sizing a search cluster and replication in databases in {{Search and vector databases|n:3ef5c17b0d0d815eb4caef9b4cd4d732}}. For most RAG systems the collection is millions, not billions, of chunks: the binding costs are the generator and the operations of this page, not the index.

13. A launch checklist

  1. Every chunk carries: source id, content hash, ACL or tenant, embedding model and version, ingestion time.
  2. Access is filtered inside the search, by a role that cannot bypass it, with the user's identity set server-side; a test asks every restricted question as an unauthorised user and expects nothing back; filtered queries are checked for short results.
  3. Freshness is measured end to end by a probe, with a target; deletes flow through a ledger to every store, cache and log.
  4. A model change goes into a new index with an atomic switch, never in place.
  5. Cache keys include the index version and the user's access scope; no semantic answer cache without a measured false-hit rate on your own traffic.
  6. Latency is reported per stage at p50, p95 and p99; the prompt puts stable text first.
  7. Cost per query is computed from a formula with dated prices, and recomputed when any price, K or k changes.
  8. A golden set large enough for the changes you want to see runs in CI; canary queries and a best-match-similarity monitor run in production.
  9. Retrieved text is treated as untrusted: provenance, rendering without remote fetches, tools with least privilege.

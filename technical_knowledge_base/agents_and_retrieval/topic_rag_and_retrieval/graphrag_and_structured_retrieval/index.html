0. In one screen

Retrieval as taught so far finds the few passages most similar to a question. Three kinds of question break that: a question about the whole collection ("what are the main themes?"), a question whose answer sits at the end of a chain of entities ("which government post did the actress who played Corliss Archer hold?"), and a question that needs every matching row counted or summed ("how many singers are from France?"). GraphRAG answers the first two by building a graph of entities and relations at index time; structured retrieval answers the third by writing a query (SQL or Cypher) against data that already has structure. This page builds both on the topic's running example and measures what each buys and costs.

text→entities and relations (LLM or NLP)→entity resolution→graph→communities (Leiden)→summaries→global, local, DRIFT or PageRank search

1. A graph is paid for at index time, per chunk

graphrag 3.3.0 sends 1,717 tokens of instructions and examples with every chunk, then a second "gleaning" call. On the topic's 125-token HotpotQA paragraphs the first call alone is 14.0 times the text it reads measured; LightRAG 1.5.7 sends 4,253 tokens per paragraph before the model has written a word measured (sections 3 and 9).

2. Entity resolution decides whether the graph connects

Merging names by exact upper-cased string, as graphrag does, joins only 71 of 160 bridge questions' two gold paragraphs in the noun-phrase graph; looser matching joins up to 114, but in a hand-checked sample only 16 of 30 of the extra merges were right (section 4).

3. A graph helps multi-hop retrieval only when its links are real

Both gold paragraphs in the top 5, 200 questions: dense 137, hybrid 143, expanding the dense top 2 along title mentions 169, personalised PageRank over a noun-phrase graph 79. Answered by Qwen3-1.7B from those top 5, the title-link expansion lifts F1 from 0.402 to 0.464 (paired 95% interval of the gain 0.015 to 0.110) at a third of the agent loop's prompt tokens measured (section 8).

4. Global questions are a map-reduce over summaries

Microsoft's GraphRAG answers "what is this corpus about" by asking every community summary first and merging the answers: its community-summary answers (and a plain text-summarisation baseline) judged more comprehensive than top-k retrieval in 72 to 83% of comparisons on two 1 to 1.7 M-token corpora, at one model call per community per question published (section 6).

5. Cheaper graphs exist; read their claims carefully

LazyGraphRAG (index with no LLM, Microsoft products only), LightRAG, nano-graphrag and HippoRAG trade the community hierarchy for cheaper indexing or PageRank. In HippoRAG 2's re-run on 11,656 passages GraphRAG used 115.5 M input tokens to index, LightRAG 68.5 M, HippoRAG 2 9.2 M published (sections 7 and 9).

6. For counts and joins, just use SQL

48 of 100 Spider questions are aggregates; 23 of them depend on more than 10 rows, which no top-10 of retrieved rows can count. Retrieving tables instead of rows puts every needed table in the prompt for 98 of 100 questions with 339 schema tokens instead of 5,637 measured; with those tables Qwen3-1.7B writes correct SQL for 56 of 100, the same as with the right database given (56) and twice the full dump (27) (sections 10 and 11).

1. What a top-k of chunks cannot answer

Everything on the parent page assumes the answer lives in a few passages that resemble the question. Embed the question, take the k most similar chunks, hand them to the model. That assumption fails in four recognisable ways:

Local

The answer is in one passage that shares words or meaning with the question. "What year was Goldfinger released?" Top-k works.

Multi-hop (relational)

The answer needs a chain: passage A names an entity, passage B says something about it. Passage B may share nothing with the question. "What position did the woman who played Corliss Archer in Kiss and Tell hold?"

Global (sensemaking)

The answer is a property of the whole collection, spread over hundreds of passages. "What are the main themes of these 10,000 reports?" Any top 10 samples ten fragments.

Aggregate

The answer is a count, sum, maximum or join over records. "How many singers are older than 40?" Retrieval returns k rows; the answer depends on all of them.

On the running example's 200 HotpotQA questions, a single dense search (bge-small, the root's Agentic lab's search tool) puts at least one gold paragraph in the top 10 for almost every question, but both for 164, and both in the top 5 for 137 measured; the root's Agentic lab shows the second hop is what is missed. A graph attacks that: if paragraph A mentions an entity and paragraph B is about that entity, an edge joins them, and retrieval can walk from A to B without the question ever naming B.

Predict: 100 Spider questions over 20 real databases. How many are aggregates (COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX or GROUP BY), and of those, how many depend on more than 10 rows?
48 of 100 are aggregates and 23 of those read more than 10 rows (11 more than 20; the largest reads 510,436) measured, counting the rows the gold query's FROM, JOIN and WHERE select before aggregating (s_sql_sample.py). Spider's databases are small (section 10); on real tables the gap is larger. A retriever that hands the model 10 rows cannot count 239 of them: the fix is to retrieve the schema and let the database count.

When none of this matters. Most questions in most products are local. For those, hybrid search with a reranker (The retrieval pipeline) is cheaper and at least as good as anything on this page; the GraphRAG paper evaluates only global questions, and Microsoft later built DRIFT search specifically for local ones (section 6).

2. Knowledge graphs, from zero

A knowledge graph stores facts as nodes (entities: a person, a film, a company, a place) joined by edges (relations: starred in, was born in, owns). One fact is a triple (subject, relation, object): (Shirley Temple, played, Corliss Archer). Two families of databases store them:

  • Property graphs (Neo4j, Kùzu, Memgraph, Amazon Neptune): nodes and edges carry a label and key-value properties; queried with Cypher or its ISO standard successor GQL, as in MATCH (a:Person)-[:PLAYED]->(c:Character) RETURN a.name.
  • RDF stores (Wikidata, GraphDB, Apache Jena): everything is a triple of identifiers (URIs); queried with SPARQL; the schema (an ontology) is itself data.

A schema (which node labels and relation types are allowed) is what separates a curated knowledge graph from what GraphRAG builds. GraphRAG extracts an open graph: whatever entities and relation phrases a language model finds in each chunk, with only a short list of entity types as guidance. That makes it cheap to start and messy to use: the same entity under five spellings, relations that are free text ("was the third film in the series of"), no constraints to check against.

The root's example bridge question from HotpotQA's dev set, drawn by hand from its two gold paragraphs (answer: Chief of Protocol). Once "Shirley Temple" is a node shared by both paragraphs, a graph walk reaches paragraph B in one hop.

HotpotQA is a fair test bed for this because it was built from exactly such links: its authors took the hyperlink graph of the first paragraphs of English Wikipedia and wrote bridge questions over pairs of articles joined by a hyperlink, the linked article's subject being the bridge entity (Yang et al. 2018, section 2) published. Keep that in mind in section 8: a graph built from title mentions recovers the dataset's own construction.

3. Building the graph: extraction with a language model, or without one

LLM extraction, as graphrag does it

graphrag 3.3.0 (pip graphrag, released 2026-10-08, read at tag v3.3.0) cuts documents into chunks of 1,200 tokens with 100 of overlap (config/defaults.py lines 60 to 63, o200k_base tokens) and sends each chunk to the model with one long prompt. The prompt asks for two kinds of record, separated by ## with fields separated by <|>:

graphrag v3.3.0, packages/graphrag/graphrag/prompts/index/extract_graph.py, lines 15 and 23 (record formats)
("entity"<|><entity_name><|><entity_type><|><entity_description>) ("relationship"<|><source_entity><|><target_entity><|><relationship_description><|><relationship_strength>)

The entity types default to four: organization, person, geo, event (defaults.py lines 147 to 149). The prompt is 1,717 tokens of instructions and worked examples before the chunk (1,734 Qwen3 tokens) measured (s_prompt_tokens.py). Then comes gleaning: the conversation continues with a second message telling the model it missed entities, and its answer is appended.

So every chunk costs two calls, and the second re-sends the first call's prompt, chunk and answer. The parser upper-cases every name (clean_str(record_attributes[1].upper()), lines 146 to 147) and the merge step groups entities across chunks by exact (title, type) (extract_graph.py line 108): "APPLE" the company and "APPLE" the place stay apart, and so do "SHIRLEY TEMPLE" and "SHIRLEY TEMPLE BLACK", two names of one person. Descriptions of one entity from many chunks are summarised by another LLM call once there are two or more (description_summary_extractor.py lines 63 to 68).

What it costs here. On the root Agentic lab's subset (the 10 paragraphs of each of the first 40 test questions, 394 paragraphs of median 125 Qwen3 tokens), graphrag's first extraction call has a median prompt of 1,871 tokens: the subset's 53,062 tokens of text become 740,923 prompt tokens before gleaning, 14.0 times the text measured. With graphrag's 1,200-token chunks the instructions weigh less: each indexed token costs (1,717 + 1,200) / 1,200 = 2.4 prompt tokens in the first call derived, which is one reason graphrag uses big chunks; the GraphRAG paper measured the price of that: with GPT-4 on a HotpotQA sample, 600-token chunks yielded almost twice as many entity references as 2,400-token chunks (Edge et al., appendix A.2) published.

Measured with the local model. graphrag's own prompt and parser, Qwen3-1.7B, the first 150 subset paragraphs: 4,368 prompt and 922 output tokens per paragraph over the two calls, 14.4 s of generation per paragraph on a machine shared with other jobs measured. Three things the table below cannot show by itself. First, llama-server's prompt cache recomputed only 113,654 of the 655,264 prompt tokens (17%), because the 1,700 tokens of instructions are an identical prefix in every call: prefix caching is worth most exactly here, and providers that discount cached input pass part of that on. Second, gleaning did not glean: the records grew from 983 to 1,663, but distinct (name, type) pairs per paragraph only from 6.36 to 6.43; the 1.7 B model answered "MANY entities were missed" by repeating its first list (one answer opened with "I apologize for the confusion"), so the second call doubled the cost and added almost nothing. Qwen3-4B on the first 40 paragraphs did the same (5.83 to 5.90 distinct pairs per paragraph, 40.0 s a paragraph). Third, the entity types matter: with graphrag's four types (organization, person, geo, event) a film, a song or a sports season has no type to go in, and on these 150 paragraphs the graphrag prompt recovered the paragraph's own title for 82 against 99 for the root Agentic lab's untyped one-call prompt and 64 for the noun phrases; the 4B with graphrag's prompt, 23 of the first 40 against 26 for the untyped prompt with the 1.7B (section 4). Set the types for your corpus; graphrag ships a prompt-tuning command for exactly this (graphrag prompt-tune, cli/main.py line 249).

Extraction without a language model

graphrag also ships a "fast" indexing method (config/enums.py lines 44 to 54) that replaces the LLM with classic NLP: noun phrases from a part-of-speech tagger become nodes, and two phrases in the same text unit get an edge weighted by pointwise mutual information (PMI, how much more often they co-occur than chance: p(x,y) log2(p(x,y) / (p(x) p(y))), graphs/edge_weights.py line 22). Its default extractor is TextBlob-based and, per its docstring, "the extractor used in the first bencharmking of LazyGraphRAG" (regex_extractor.py line 32; the typo is theirs).

Run with graphrag's own class over all 9,760 HotpotQA paragraphs, it took 25.0 s on one CPU core (about 2.6 ms a paragraph, zero LLM tokens) and found 133,664 phrases, 73,428 distinct, 59,459 of them (81%) in a single paragraph measured under the bench lock (load average 8.1 from other jobs). The most frequent nodes are month names (September in 550 paragraphs): hubs that join unrelated paragraphs; section 5 shows what graphrag's pruning removes and what it leaves.

What the NLP extractor misses. It keeps a phrase only if every token is plain letters, digits or hyphens (has_valid_tokens, regex_extractor.py), so "Hey Pa! There's a Goat on the Roof" or "Ernst Willi Messerschmid's" can never be nodes, and "New York" becomes "YORK" because "NEW" is in its stop list. A paragraph's own subject, its Wikipedia title, appears among its phrases (upper-cased exact match) for only 3,243 of 9,760 paragraphs (33%) measured. Section 4 measures the NLP graph and the LLM extractions against the same gold.

4. Entity resolution: is "Doyle" the same node as "Arthur Conan Doyle"?

Entity resolution (also entity linking, deduplication) decides which mentions are the same thing. It sounds like housekeeping; it decides whether the graph connects at all. A bridge question's two paragraphs are joined only if the mention in one (say "Temple" or "Shirley Temple Black") and the subject of the other (the article titled "Shirley Temple") end up as the same node. Merge too strictly and the graph falls apart into islands; merge too loosely and unrelated things fuse ("James Bond" the character and "the James Bond series"; "Labrador" the region and "Labrador Retriever").

Gold without hand labels. HotpotQA gives two checks for free. Every paragraph is a Wikipedia article whose title is its subject, so a good extraction of paragraph P should contain a mention that resolves to P's title (title recall). And every bridge question's two gold paragraphs are joined by a mention of one's title in the other (section 2), so a good graph should join them (link recall). Four resolution rules, strictest first, applied to graphrag's noun phrases (s_entities.py):

  • exact: upper-cased string equality, what graphrag's merge does;
  • normalised: lower case, punctuation and articles removed, Wikipedia's parenthetical removed ("Kiss and Tell (1945 film)" = "kiss and tell");
  • token subset: the mention's words are a subset of one title's words and cover at least half of them ("Kerensky" = "Alexander Kerensky");
  • embedding: nearest title by bge-small cosine, kept above 0.85.

The trade-off is the usual precision against recall, with a twist: every wrong merge adds edges between unrelated paragraphs, and graph algorithms amplify edges (a PageRank walk or a community will happily cross a false bridge). Production systems therefore resolve in stages: cheap normalisation for everything, blocking (only compare names that share a token or a sound), then an embedding or LLM judgement on the candidate pairs, and, where it exists, linking to an external identifier (a Wikidata id, a customer number) that settles the question. graphrag does the first stage only; LightRAG 1.5.7 does not even case-fold names (utils.py lines 5391 to 5393, normalize_entity_name).

Against the same gold, its names contain the paragraph's own title (exact upper-case match) for 269 of 394 paragraphs against 137 for the noun phrases, and join 20 of 29 bridge pairs against 15 (24 with embedding resolution) measured. 5. Communities: Louvain, Leiden and the hierarchy

A community is a set of nodes more densely linked to each other than to the rest. GraphRAG uses communities as the units it summarises, so "what is this corpus about" becomes "what is each community about". The standard score of a partition into communities is modularity:

Q = (1 / 2m) Σij [ Aij − γ ki kj / 2m ] δ(ci, cj)
Aij
edge weight between nodes i and j
ki
total edge weight at node i (its degree)
m
total edge weight of the graph
γ
resolution: higher gives more, smaller communities (graphrag uses 1.0)
δ
1 when i and j are in the same community, else 0

Q counts the edge weight inside communities minus what a random graph with the same degrees would put there. Louvain (Blondel et al. 2008) maximises it greedily: move each node to the neighbouring community that raises Q most, then collapse each community into one node and repeat. Its known flaw: a node that held a community together can move out, leaving the rest internally disconnected. Leiden (Traag, Waltman and van Eck 2019) adds a refinement phase between moving and collapsing, in which each community is re-split from singletons and nodes may only join well-connected sub-clusters; it guarantees connected communities. The Leiden paper found Louvain leaving "up to 25% of the communities ... badly connected and up to 16% ... disconnected" on its test networks, the 16% on DBLP after several iterations (arXiv 1810.08473 v3, abstract and section IV) published.

graspologic-native 1.2.5 (the version graphrag 3.3.0 pins, ">=1.2,<1.3": its pyproject.toml says 1.3 changes the clustering output), packages/network_partitions/src/leiden/

Two lessons. Pruning is not a detail: only 19% of the phrase nodes survive it, almost all of the cut being phrases seen in one paragraph, which link nothing. What pruning does not cut is hubs: it removes only the single highest-degree node, so month names survive and sit among the most connected members of the subset's level-0 communities (the lab's explorer shows "JULY" heading one). And Louvain produced no disconnected community on either graph here (Leiden's level 0 none either, by construction): the Leiden paper's 16% is an upper range on large networks after several iterations, not a typical rate; Leiden is the safer default, not a rescue that every graph needs. 6. Microsoft GraphRAG at query time: global, local, DRIFT, basic

After indexing, graphrag holds entities (with LLM-merged descriptions), relationships, text units, a community hierarchy and one community report per community: a JSON title, summary, an "impact severity rating" from 0 to 10 and a list of findings, written by the model from the community's entities, relationships and (if enabled) claims (prompts/index/community_report.py, 1,915 tokens of prompt; reports default to 2,000 words from at most 8,000 tokens of input, config/defaults.py lines 82 to 83; claims extraction is off by default, line 132). Four query engines read them (query/factory.py):

Global search: map-reduce over community reports

The question is sent with batches of reports (all communities at the chosen level, default 2) to a map prompt that returns key points, each with a score. The paper describes it in one sentence: "The LLM is also asked to generate a score between 0-100 indicating how helpful the generated answer is in answering the target question. Answers with score 0 are filtered out." (Edge et al., section 3.1.6). The code does the same:

graphrag v3.3.0, prompts/query/global_search_map_system_prompt.py line 21; query/structured_search/global_search/search.py lines 333 to 375
- Importance Score: An integer score between 0-100 that indicates how important the point is in answering the user's question. An 'I don't know' type of response should have a score of 0. reduce: keep points with score > 0, sort by score, descending, append until max_data_tokens (12,000) then one call writes the answer from them (no points left: a canned "I do not know" answer)

Cost per question: one map call per batch of reports plus one reduce call. With hundreds of communities that is hundreds of calls; the LightRAG paper's back-of-envelope for its Legal corpus is 610 level-2 communities of about 1,000 tokens each read per query (Guo et al., section 4.5) published. The GraphRAG paper's Table 2 shows the other side: answering from the root level (C0) needs 26,657 context tokens on its Podcast corpus against 1,014,611 for summarising the raw text (2.6%) published. Microsoft's later dynamic community selection walks the hierarchy from the top, asking the model to rate each report 0 to 5 and descending only into relevant ones (query/context_builder/dynamic_community_selection.py, off by default); its blog reports about 77% lower cost than static global search at level 1 with no significant quality difference (Microsoft Research, 2024-11-15) vendor.

Published result. On two corpora (podcast transcripts, about 1 M tokens; news, about 1.7 M), 125 generated "sensemaking" questions each, an LLM judge preferred the global approaches over vector RAG on comprehensiveness in 72 to 83% of pairs and on diversity in 62 to 82% (Edge et al., v2, section 5.1) published; the range includes the text-summarisation baseline TS, which also beats vector RAG. Indexing the podcast corpus took 281 minutes with gpt-4-turbo. The paper reports no token or dollar cost for indexing.

Measured here, small-scale. On the 394-paragraph subset, with graphrag's own fast-mode report prompt and its map and reduce prompts (shortened report and answer lengths so a laptop model can finish, s_global.py): 18 level-0 communities, so 18 report calls (72,672 prompt tokens of paragraphs); then each of five corpus-wide questions cost 19 calls and about 21,850 prompt tokens by map-reduce, against one call and 1,602 tokens for the dense top 10, 14 times as many, and 81 s against 6.9 s with Qwen3-4B measured. What the extra calls buy is breadth: the points that reached the reduce step came from reports covering on average 366 of the 394 paragraphs, while the top 10 is 10 paragraphs; asked for the main themes, the map-reduce answer named the subset's clusters (the Russian Civil War, sports teams, the James Bond films) where the top-10 answer described whichever paragraphs happened to match the word "themes". Nobody judged these answers (a 4 B judge on five questions would be noise), and both models produced malformed reports: graphrag's report prompt asks for JSON, and only 7 of 18 (4B) and 2 of 18 (1.7B) were valid, the rest used as plain text. Read the answers side by side in the Graph build lab.

Local search: start from entities

For a question about something specific, graphrag embeds the question, finds the nearest entity descriptions in its vector store (not the nearest chunks), and assembles a context from those entities' relationships, the text units they came from and their communities' reports, in fixed proportions of a 12,000-token budget: 50% text units, 15% community reports, 35% entity and relationship tables, top 10 entities and 10 relationships (config/defaults.py lines 265 to 270; mixed_context.py lines 175 to 208). The GraphRAG paper does not evaluate local search; it is documented and shipped, not benchmarked there.

DRIFT: global first, then local follow-ups

DRIFT (Dynamic Reasoning and Inference with Flexible Traversal) combines the two. A primer writes a hypothetical answer in the shape of a community report (a HyDE-style query expansion, drift_search/primer.py lines 71 to 95), retrieves the 20 most similar reports, splits them into 5 folds, and asks each fold for an intermediate answer, a 0 to 100 score and follow-up questions; then up to 3 rounds of local searches answer the follow-ups, each proposing more; a reduce call merges everything (config/defaults.py lines 99 to 106: drift_k_followups 20, primer_folds 5, n_depth 3). Microsoft's blog reports DRIFT preferred over local search in 78% of comparisons on comprehensiveness and 81% on diversity, on 50 local questions over 5,000+ AP news articles, judged by an LLM (Microsoft Research, 2024-10-31) vendor; it gives no cost.

Basic search

Plain vector RAG over the text units, top 10 (basic_search/basic_context.py), included so the same index can answer local lookups cheaply. It is the baseline every graph method has to beat.

7. Cheaper graphs: LazyGraphRAG, LightRAG, nano-graphrag, HippoRAG

LazyGraphRAG (Microsoft Research, November 2024)

Index with NLP noun phrases and their co-occurrence, no LLM (the "fast" path of section 3), and spend the language model only at query time: split the question into subqueries, rank text chunks and communities by relevance, and let the model test candidates against a relevance test budget (100, 500 and 1,500 in the blog's configurations). Microsoft's claims: indexing costs the same as vector RAG and 0.1% of GraphRAG's; at the lowest budget it costs as much per query as vector RAG with an 8k context and beats every compared method except GraphRAG global search on global questions; at budget 500 it costs 4% of GraphRAG's level-2 global search and beats all eight compared conditions; "more than 700 times lower query cost" at comparable global-search quality (blog, 2024-11-25, editor's note 2025-06-06); in the later BenchmarkQED study it won all 96 comparisons, all but one significant (2025-06-05) vendor. The win rates exist only as chart images. It is not in the open graphrag package (3.3.0 mentions it only in the extractor comment of section 3) and ships in Microsoft Discovery and Azure Local, per the editor's note; not run here.

LightRAG (Guo et al., HKU, 2024; pip lightrag-hku 1.5.7)

No community hierarchy. Extraction is GraphRAG-like (1,200-token chunks, 100 overlap, one gleaning; lightrag.py lines 1097 and 1104, constants.py line 17), but entity and relation descriptions are embedded in their own vector stores. At query time the model splits the question into low-level keywords (specific entities) and high-level keywords (themes): "local" mode searches entities with the first, "global" mode searches relationships with the second, "hybrid" both, "mix" (the default) both plus chunk vectors, "naive" chunks only (prompt.py lines 489 to 490; operate.py lines 5235 to 5283). One query is a keyword call plus an answer call instead of a map over hundreds of communities, which is the source of its cost claim. Descriptions of an entity are joined as text and only summarised by the model once there are 8 of them or 1,200 tokens (operate.py lines 436 to 440).

Run here with a recording stub instead of a model (s_lightrag.py stub), LightRAG 1.5.7 made 40 LLM calls to index 20 paragraphs (extraction and gleaning) with 85,054 prompt tokens, 4,253 per paragraph, before any output is added measured. Its paper reports winning against GraphRAG overall on three of four datasets by small margins (54.8, 52.0, 52.8 against 45.2, 48.0, 47.2) and losing on the fourth (49.6 against 50.4), judged by GPT-4o-mini (arXiv 2410.05779 v3, Table 1) published. The "70 to 90% of GraphRAG's quality at 1/100 of the cost" quoted around the web has no method or source and is not repeated here (the parent's coverage notes). HippoRAG 2's authors, re-running LightRAG on their benchmarks with Llama-3.3-70B, got a QA F1 of 2.4 on HotpotQA against 75.3 for plain dense retrieval (Table 2 of the paper below) published: a reproduction gap that says more about how fragile these pipelines are under another model and prompt than about either paper.

Run here with a real model. LightRAG 1.5.7 with Qwen3-4B behind llama-server and bge-small as its embedder (s_lightrag.py; one change from its defaults: a 16,000-token query budget instead of 30,000, to fit a 24,576-token window): indexing the first 40 subset paragraphs took 80 LLM calls, 194,159 prompt tokens (4,854 a paragraph) and 31,794 output tokens, 1,246 s, and built a graph of 349 entities; it logged format errors on several relation records, which it drops measured. On the 4 questions whose two gold paragraphs are among those 40 (far too few to compare modes, so read this as "it works end to end on a local model"), the answer contained the gold answer for naive 2, local 1, global 2, hybrid 3 and mix 3 of 4.

nano-graphrag (0.0.8, 2024)

A small re-implementation of GraphRAG that kept the shape and dropped the weight: 1,200-token chunks, one gleaning, graspologic's hierarchical Leiden on the largest component with max_graph_cluster_size 10 and the same seed, community reports, and local, global and naive modes, default global at level 2 (nano_graphrag/graphrag.py lines 70 to 81; base.py lines 11 to 14). Useful to read: the whole pipeline fits in a few files. Its OpenAI client is created with no arguments (_llm.py lines 20 to 23), so pointing it at a local server needs the OPENAI_BASE_URL variable or a custom function. Last PyPI release 0.0.8.2, 2024-10-19; not run here because LightRAG covers the same shape and is maintained.

HippoRAG: personalised PageRank instead of summaries

HippoRAG (Gutiérrez et al., NeurIPS 2024) ignores communities and treats the graph as an associative memory. Indexing: an LLM extracts named entities, then triples, from each passage; phrases whose retrieval-encoder cosine exceeds 0.8 get a synonymy edge. Query: extract the question's entities, link each to its most similar graph node, and run personalised PageRank (PPR) from them.

p = (1 − d) r + d W p
r
reset vector: mass on the question's nodes, each weighted by its node specificity 1 / (number of passages containing it), so rare names count more
W
the graph's edge weights, each column normalised to sum to 1
d
probability of following an edge rather than jumping back to r: 0.5 in the paper's experiments (run_hipporag_main_exps.sh line 4; the class default is 0.1)
p
the stationary probability of each node; a passage scores the probability mass of its phrases

A walk that restarts at the question's entities spends its time near them and near whatever they are densely connected to, so a passage two hops away can outrank a passage that merely shares words. Published: HippoRAG (ColBERTv2) recall@5 of 89.1 on 2WikiMultiHopQA against 68.2 for ColBERTv2 alone, 51.9 against 49.2 on MuSiQue, but 77.7 against 79.3 on HotpotQA, where it loses; online retrieval "10 to 30 times cheaper and 6 to 13 times faster" than iterative retrieval with IRCoT (arXiv 2405.14831 v3, Table 2 and appendix G) published. HippoRAG 2 (2025) adds passages as graph nodes seeded by dense similarity (reset weight 0.05 times the passage's score), links the query to triples rather than entities, and filters triples with the model; with NV-Embed-v2 and Llama-3.3-70B it reports recall@5 of 96.3 on HotpotQA against 94.5 for NV-Embed-v2 alone, and QA F1 averaged over seven datasets of 59.8 against 57.0, with GraphRAG at 49.6 (arXiv 2502.14802 v2, Tables 2 and 3) published. Section 8 runs both shapes on the running example.

9. What a graph costs, and when it pays

Indexing cost is a sum over LLM calls, each billed for its whole prompt. For graphrag's standard method, per text unit of c tokens:

Tin ≈ (P + c) + (P + c + o1 + 34)     Tout ≈ o1 + o2
P
the extraction prompt's fixed instructions and examples: 1,717 tokens measured
c
the chunk (1,200 by default)
o1, o2
tokens the model writes in the extraction and the gleaning call (34 = the gleaning message); measured here with Qwen3-1.7B on 125-token paragraphs: 922 for both calls together measured

plus one summarisation call per entity seen in two or more chunks, one report call per community (prompt 1,915 tokens plus up to 8,000 of context), and the embeddings. The Cost calculator adds these up with every assumption visible. The one published itemised bill: on MuSiQue's 11,656 passages, HippoRAG 2's authors measured indexing input / output tokens of 115.5 M / 36.1 M for GraphRAG, 68.5 M / 18.3 M for LightRAG and 9.2 M / 3.0 M for HippoRAG 2, and 277, 235 and 99.5 minutes, with Llama-3.3-70B (arXiv 2502.14802 v2, Table 12) published: about 9,900 input tokens per passage for GraphRAG derived: 115.5 M / 11,656. Queries cost too: 10.7 s per question for GraphRAG and 13.3 s for LightRAG against 0.3 s for dense retrieval in the same table.

When a graph pays. When (a) the questions are global or relational, not lookups; (b) the corpus is stable, so the index is built once and read many times (updates re-extract and re-cluster; LightRAG and graphrag's incremental methods reduce, not remove, this); (c) entities are what the corpus is about (people, companies, products, cases), so extraction finds real relations; and (d) someone owns entity resolution. When any of these fails, the cheaper rungs win: hybrid search with a reranker for lookups, the agent loop for occasional multi-hop questions (the root's Agentic lab), an NLP-indexed graph with query-time LLM work (the LazyGraphRAG shape) for occasional global questions, and SQL for anything that is already a table.

10. Structured retrieval: text-to-SQL

When the data is already a table, retrieval should be a query. Text-to-SQL asks a language model to translate the question into SQL; the database computes the answer exactly, over every row, with joins and aggregates. The model needs to know the schema (tables, columns, keys); matching the question's words to the right tables and columns is called schema linking, and it is where most errors start ("stations" in a question about the stadium table; two databases both with a singer table).

How it is scored. Execution accuracy runs the predicted and the gold query and compares results; exact match compares the SQL's parsed structure and rejects correct queries written differently. Execution accuracy has the opposite flaw, false positives: a wrong query can return the right rows on a small database by luck. Zhong et al. measured single-database execution accuracy accepting wrong queries 6.5% of the time on average across Spider submissions, up to 9.0%, and proposed test suites of extra databases (arXiv 2010.02840, Table 2) published.

The benchmarks, dated.

  • Spider 1.0 (Yu et al. 2018): 10,181 questions, 5,693 SQL queries over 200 databases in 138 domains; the dev split (used here) is 1,034 questions over 20 databases (arXiv 1809.08887 v5). The best model at publication reached 12.4% exact match; prompted GPT-4o reaches 85.3 to 86.6% execution accuracy with DIN-SQL or DAIL-SQL in Spider 2.0's re-runs (below) published. Its databases are small and clean, which is why newer benchmarks moved on.
  • BIRD (Li et al. 2023): 12,751 questions over 95 databases totalling 33.4 GB, with dirty values and "external knowledge evidence" (a hint such as which column means what). GPT-4 reached 34.88% test execution accuracy without the evidence and 54.89% with it; humans 92.96% (arXiv 2305.03111 v3, Table 2) published. The leaderboard's best test score read on 2026-10-14 is 82.95 (GrainSQL, dated 2026-09-26; bird-bench.github.io) leaderboard.
  • Spider 2.0 (Lei et al. 2024): 632 enterprise tasks over BigQuery, Snowflake and local databases with hundreds of columns and dialect details. An o1-preview agent solved 21.36%; DAIL-SQL with GPT-4o, 86.6% on Spider 1.0 and 57.4% on BIRD, solved 5.68% of Spider 2.0-lite (arXiv 2411.07763 v2, Tables 4 and 5) published. The leaderboard's best on Spider 2.0-lite read on 2026-10-14 is 76.23 (dated 2026-07-28; spider2-sql.github.io) leaderboard. The jump from Spider to Spider 2.0 is mostly schema size and messiness, which is the retrieval problem below.

Schema retrieval: the retrieval problem inside text-to-SQL

A real warehouse has thousands of tables; the prompt can hold dozens. So the first step is retrieval again, over table descriptions instead of passages. Measured on 100 Spider dev questions (5 per database, seed 0; s_sql_sample.py), pooling all 20 dev databases (81 tables) as if the question did not say which: one document per table, "table in database: columns" (e.g. "singer in concert singer: singer id, name, country, song name, song release year, age, is male"), retrieved by BM25 or bge-small (s_schema_ret.py):

Lexical retrieval is strong here because table and column names are words from the question ("singer", "concert"), the reverse of passage retrieval, where paraphrase favours embeddings. Accepting the same rows with columns in another order (Spider's official evaluation does) adds three to five to each. Note how cheap the dump is to run here, despite its size: the schema is a shared prefix, so llama-server recomputed only 81 tokens per question; the cost of a big schema is accuracy, not time.

Running generated SQL safely. The model writes code that will run against production data: connect with a read-only role, set a statement timeout and a row limit, reject anything but SELECT (parse it; do not regex it), and enforce access with the database's own row-level security rather than with instructions in the prompt (RAG in production measures prompt-level access control leaking).

11. Tables as text, SQL plus vectors, and graph query languages

Retrieving rows as text

The alternative to writing queries is to serialise each row into text ("singer: name Rose White; country France; age 41"), or each small table into Markdown, embed it and retrieve it like a passage. That works for lookups of descriptive records (a product's specification, one customer's ticket) and fails for anything that counts, sums, ranks or joins, for the reason in section 1: the answer depends on rows that are not in the top k. Measured on the 100 Spider questions, 23 of the 48 aggregates read more than 10 rows measured. A useful hybrid keeps both: rows retrievable as text for "find the record about X", the table queryable for "how many X".

SQL and vectors in one query

Where structured filters and semantic similarity meet ("cheap hotels near the sea with good reviews about quiet rooms"), put both in one engine: a vector column in the table and a query that filters with SQL and orders by distance. In PostgreSQL with pgvector: SELECT name FROM hotels WHERE price < 120 AND city = 'Nice' ORDER BY review_embedding <=> $1 LIMIT 5. The filter and the index interact (an HNSW scan can return too few rows after filtering; pgvector 0.8's iterative scans fix that), which Vector indexes from the inside and Search and vector databases measure; the language model's job shrinks to extracting the filter values from the question ("self-query", on The retrieval pipeline).

Cypher and SPARQL

The same rows can live in a graph database. Spider's concert_singer database loaded into Kùzu 0.11.3 (an embedded graph database that speaks Cypher, in process like SQLite; 0.11.3 is its last release: its repository was archived in October 2025, last push 2025-10-10, read 2026-10-14, so it serves here as a teaching engine, not a recommendation) becomes 9 stadiums, 6 singers and 6 concerts as nodes, with 6 HELD_AT edges (concert to stadium) and 10 PERFORMED_IN edges (singer to concert) (s_kuzu.py). The join that SQL spells out becomes a path pattern:

"List singer names and number of concerts for each singer." Spider gold SQL, then hand-written Cypher; same rows on both engines, 4 of 4 example questions measured
SELECT T2.name, count(*) FROM singer_in_concert AS T1 JOIN singer AS T2 ON T1.singer_id = T2.singer_id GROUP BY T2.singer_id MATCH (s:Singer)-[:PERFORMED_IN]->(c:Concert) RETURN s.name, count(c)

Graph query languages pay off when the question is a path of unknown length ("who is connected to this account within three transfers"), which SQL expresses only with recursive common table expressions. For language models, Cypher is the harder target: on CypherBench (11 Wikidata property graphs, over 10,000 questions) the best zero-shot execution accuracy reported was 61.58% (Claude 3.5 Sonnet) and 60.18% (GPT-4o) (arXiv 2412.18702 v2, Table 3); Neo4j's Text2Cypher dataset has 44,387 question and query pairs for fine-tuning (arXiv 2412.10064) published. SPARQL over curated graphs such as Wikidata is the older form of the same idea, knowledge-graph question answering; it needs the graph's identifiers, which is why open-extraction graphs rarely use it. Temporal graphs for an agent's own memory (Graphiti and Zep) are on Agent memory layers; how relational engines plan joins on Topic: databases.

Measured with Qwen3-1.7B on all 45 concert_singer dev questions, same rows on both engines: SQL 28 correct, Cypher 11, and 29 of the 45 Cypher queries even parse and run; Qwen3-4B, SQL 36 against Cypher 16 measured; most failures are parse errors from SQL habits carried into Cypher (a GROUP BY clause, a WHERE in the wrong place, two statements in one answer). A small model has seen far more SQL than Cypher, which is the practical argument for keeping relational data relational.

13. Common mistakes

Building GraphRAG for a lookup workload
The paper evaluates global sensemaking questions only. For lookups, measure hybrid plus reranker first; on the running example a noun-phrase graph lowered multi-hop recall (79 against 137 at 5).
Ignoring entity resolution
Exact upper-cased names (graphrag's merge) joined 71 of 160 bridge pairs in the NLP graph; the graph you draw in a demo is not connected the way you think. Measure link recall on a labelled sample before trusting traversal.
Merging too loosely to fix it
Token-subset matching joined the most pairs and was right for only 16 of 30 hand-checked extra merges; every wrong merge is an edge PageRank and Leiden will use.
Quoting indexing cost per chunk without the prompt
The instructions dominate small chunks: 14.0 times the text for 125-token paragraphs with graphrag's prompt, before gleaning doubles the calls. Count prompt tokens with the real prompt (s_prompt_tokens.py).
Paying for gleaning without checking it
graphrag's default second "you missed entities" call doubled the extraction calls and moved distinct entities per paragraph from 6.36 to 6.43 with Qwen3-1.7B (the model repeated its first list). Count distinct (name, type) pairs with and without it on a sample before paying for it.
Comparing graph methods across papers
Each paper runs its rivals with its own model, prompts and judge: LightRAG beats GraphRAG narrowly in its own paper and scores F1 2.4 on HotpotQA in HippoRAG 2's re-run. Compare on your data, with the same generator.
Trusting LLM-judge win rates as accuracy
GraphRAG's 72 to 83% are pairwise preferences of an LLM judge on comprehensiveness and diversity, which reward longer, broader answers; they are not correctness. Judges and their biases are on LLM-as-judge.
Retrieving rows to answer a count
23 of the 48 aggregate Spider questions here need more than 10 rows. Query the table.
Dumping every schema into the prompt
5,637 tokens for 20 small databases; real warehouses do not fit. Retrieve tables (98 of 100 complete at k = 5, 339 tokens).
Scoring text-to-SQL by execution on one small database
Wrong queries can return right rows: 6.5% average false positives in Zhong et al.'s analysis. Use test suites or several database states for a real evaluation.
Executing generated SQL with a privileged connection
Read-only role, statement timeout, row limit, SELECT-only after parsing, and row-level security in the database, not in the prompt.

14. Interview questions

Why can't vector RAG answer "what are the main themes of this corpus"?
The answer is a property of the whole collection; top-k retrieves the k chunks most similar to the question, a sample of fragments. You need a structure built over everything (community summaries) and a map-reduce over it, or a long-context pass.
Walk through GraphRAG's indexing pipeline and say where the cost is.
Chunk (1,200 tokens), extract entities and relations per chunk with an LLM plus a gleaning call, merge by name and type, summarise multi-chunk descriptions, cluster with hierarchical Leiden, write one report per community, embed. Cost is LLM calls per chunk (prompt overhead included) plus one per community; HippoRAG 2's re-run measured about 9,900 input tokens per passage.
Leiden versus Louvain?
Both maximise modularity by local moves and aggregation; Leiden adds a refinement step that re-splits communities from singletons, guaranteeing connected communities. Louvain can leave disconnected ones (up to 16% in the Leiden paper's tests).
How does global search differ from local search and DRIFT?
Global maps the question over all community reports at one level (0 to 100 scores), keeps the scored points and reduces. Local starts from entities nearest the question and assembles their relationships, text units and reports. DRIFT primes with the most similar reports, then answers generated follow-up questions with local searches for a few rounds.
What is personalised PageRank, and why does HippoRAG use it?
A random walk that restarts at the question's entities with probability 1 - d; the stationary probabilities rank nodes by how reachable they are from the question. It ranks a passage two hops away above one that only shares words, in one step, without an LLM loop at query time.
How would you evaluate entity resolution?
Precision of merges on a labelled sample, and the recall that matters downstream: are the entity pairs your questions need connected? On HotpotQA titles and bridge pairs give both for free.
Text-to-SQL accuracy is 85% on Spider; why does that not predict production?
Spider databases are small and clean, the schema fits the prompt and execution accuracy has false positives. Production has thousands of tables, dirty values and dialects: Spider 2.0 dropped the same methods to about 6%.
System design: an assistant over a company's contracts, CRM and support tickets that answers "which enterprise customers with renewals next quarter have open P1 tickets mentioning data loss?"
Route by question type. The structured part (customers, renewals, ticket priority) is SQL over the CRM and ticketing databases: schema retrieval over table descriptions, generated SELECT on a read-only replica with row-level security per user, timeouts. The unstructured part ("mentioning data loss") is hybrid search over ticket text, filtered by the customer and ticket ids the SQL returned (or pgvector in the same query). Contract terms that matter (renewal dates, parties) are extracted at ingestion into columns, not left for retrieval. No GraphRAG unless users ask relational questions across accounts often; then an entity graph keyed on CRM ids, which solves entity resolution with the company's own identifiers. Evaluate each path separately (execution accuracy on a labelled SQL set, recall@k on tickets), log every query and its SQL, cache nothing that crosses permissions.
When would you not build a graph even though the questions are multi-hop?
When they are rare (an agent loop pays per question, no index), when the corpus changes daily (re-extraction cost), or when there are no stable entities to resolve. Measure the agent first; the root's lab gives its token bill.

6. Microsoft GraphRAG at query time: global, local, DRIFT, basic

After indexing, graphrag holds entities (with LLM-merged descriptions), relationships, text units, a community hierarchy and one community report per community: a JSON title, summary, an "impact severity rating" from 0 to 10 and a list of findings, written by the model from the community's entities, relationships and (if enabled) claims (prompts/index/community_report.py, @@pt_graphrag_community_report@@ tokens of prompt; reports default to 2,000 words from at most 8,000 tokens of input, config/defaults.py lines 82 to 83; claims extraction is off by default, line 132). Four query engines read them (query/factory.py):

Global search: map-reduce over community reports

The question is sent with batches of reports (all communities at the chosen level, default 2) to a map prompt that returns key points, each with a score. The paper describes it in one sentence: "The LLM is also asked to generate a score between 0-100 indicating how helpful the generated answer is in answering the target question. Answers with score 0 are filtered out." ({{Edge et al., section 3.1.6|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130}}). The code does the same:

graphrag v3.3.0, prompts/query/global_search_map_system_prompt.py line 21; query/structured_search/global_search/search.py lines 333 to 375
- Importance Score: An integer score between 0-100 that indicates how important the point is in answering the user's question. An 'I don't know' type of response should have a score of 0. reduce: keep points with score > 0, sort by score, descending, append until max_data_tokens (12,000) then one call writes the answer from them (no points left: a canned "I do not know" answer)

Cost per question: one map call per batch of reports plus one reduce call. With hundreds of communities that is hundreds of calls; the LightRAG paper's back-of-envelope for its Legal corpus is 610 level-2 communities of about 1,000 tokens each read per query ({{Guo et al., section 4.5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05779}}) published. The GraphRAG paper's Table 2 shows the other side: answering from the root level (C0) needs 26,657 context tokens on its Podcast corpus against 1,014,611 for summarising the raw text (2.6%) published. Microsoft's later dynamic community selection walks the hierarchy from the top, asking the model to rate each report 0 to 5 and descending only into relevant ones (query/context_builder/dynamic_community_selection.py, off by default); its blog reports about 77% lower cost than static global search at level 1 with no significant quality difference ({{Microsoft Research, 2024-11-15|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/graphrag-improving-global-search-via-dynamic-community-selection/}}) vendor.

Published result. On two corpora (podcast transcripts, about 1 M tokens; news, about 1.7 M), 125 generated "sensemaking" questions each, an LLM judge preferred the global approaches over vector RAG on comprehensiveness in 72 to 83% of pairs and on diversity in 62 to 82% ({{Edge et al., v2, section 5.1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130}}) published; the range includes the text-summarisation baseline TS, which also beats vector RAG. Indexing the podcast corpus took 281 minutes with gpt-4-turbo. The paper reports no token or dollar cost for indexing.

Measured here, small-scale. On the 394-paragraph subset, with graphrag's own fast-mode report prompt and its map and reduce prompts (shortened report and answer lengths so a laptop model can finish, s_global.py): @@g4_rep@@ level-0 communities, so @@g4_rep@@ report calls (@@g4_repin@@ prompt tokens of paragraphs); then each of five corpus-wide questions cost @@g4_calls@@ calls and about @@g4_in@@ prompt tokens by map-reduce, against one call and @@g4_nin@@ tokens for the dense top 10, @@g4_ratio@@ times as many, and @@g4_s@@ s against @@g4_ns@@ s with Qwen3-4B measured. What the extra calls buy is breadth: the points that reached the reduce step came from reports covering on average @@g4_paras@@ of the 394 paragraphs, while the top 10 is 10 paragraphs; asked for the main themes, the map-reduce answer named the subset's clusters (the Russian Civil War, sports teams, the James Bond films) where the top-10 answer described whichever paragraphs happened to match the word "themes". Local search: start from entities

For a question about something specific, graphrag embeds the question, finds the nearest entity descriptions in its vector store (not the nearest chunks), and assembles a context from those entities' relationships, the text units they came from and their communities' reports, in fixed proportions of a 12,000-token budget: 50% text units, 15% community reports, 35% entity and relationship tables, top 10 entities and 10 relationships (config/defaults.py lines 265 to 270; mixed_context.py lines 175 to 208). The GraphRAG paper does not evaluate local search; it is documented and shipped, not benchmarked there.

DRIFT: global first, then local follow-ups

DRIFT (Dynamic Reasoning and Inference with Flexible Traversal) combines the two. A primer writes a hypothetical answer in the shape of a community report (a HyDE-style query expansion, drift_search/primer.py lines 71 to 95), retrieves the 20 most similar reports, splits them into 5 folds, and asks each fold for an intermediate answer, a 0 to 100 score and follow-up questions; then up to 3 rounds of local searches answer the follow-ups, each proposing more; a reduce call merges everything (config/defaults.py lines 99 to 106: drift_k_followups 20, primer_folds 5, n_depth 3). Microsoft's blog reports DRIFT preferred over local search in 78% of comparisons on comprehensiveness and 81% on diversity, on 50 local questions over 5,000+ AP news articles, judged by an LLM ({{Microsoft Research, 2024-10-31|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/introducing-drift-search-combining-global-and-local-search-methods-to-improve-quality-and-efficiency/}}) vendor; it gives no cost.

Basic search

Plain vector RAG over the text units, top 10 (basic_search/basic_context.py), included so the same index can answer local lookups cheaply. It is the baseline every graph method has to beat.

7. Cheaper graphs: LazyGraphRAG, LightRAG, nano-graphrag, HippoRAG

LazyGraphRAG (Microsoft Research, November 2024)

Index with NLP noun phrases and their co-occurrence, no LLM (the "fast" path of section 3), and spend the language model only at query time: split the question into subqueries, rank text chunks and communities by relevance, and let the model test candidates against a relevance test budget (100, 500 and 1,500 in the blog's configurations). Microsoft's claims: indexing costs the same as vector RAG and 0.1% of GraphRAG's; at the lowest budget it costs as much per query as vector RAG with an 8k context and beats every compared method except GraphRAG global search on global questions; at budget 500 it costs 4% of GraphRAG's level-2 global search and beats all eight compared conditions; "more than 700 times lower query cost" at comparable global-search quality ({{blog, 2024-11-25, editor's note 2025-06-06|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag-setting-a-new-standard-for-quality-and-cost/}}); in the later BenchmarkQED study it won all 96 comparisons, all but one significant ({{2025-06-05|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/benchmarkqed-automated-benchmarking-of-rag-systems/}}) vendor. The win rates exist only as chart images. It is not in the open graphrag package (3.3.0 mentions it only in the extractor comment of section 3) and ships in Microsoft Discovery and Azure Local, per the editor's note; not run here.

LightRAG (Guo et al., HKU, 2024; pip lightrag-hku 1.5.7)

No community hierarchy. Extraction is GraphRAG-like (1,200-token chunks, 100 overlap, one gleaning; lightrag.py lines 1097 and 1104, constants.py line 17), but entity and relation descriptions are embedded in their own vector stores. At query time the model splits the question into low-level keywords (specific entities) and high-level keywords (themes): "local" mode searches entities with the first, "global" mode searches relationships with the second, "hybrid" both, "mix" (the default) both plus chunk vectors, "naive" chunks only (prompt.py lines 489 to 490; operate.py lines 5235 to 5283). One query is a keyword call plus an answer call instead of a map over hundreds of communities, which is the source of its cost claim. Descriptions of an entity are joined as text and only summarised by the model once there are 8 of them or 1,200 tokens (operate.py lines 436 to 440).

The "70 to 90% of GraphRAG's quality at 1/100 of the cost" quoted around the web has no method or source and is not repeated here (the parent's coverage notes). HippoRAG 2's authors, re-running LightRAG on their benchmarks with Llama-3.3-70B, got a QA F1 of 2.4 on HotpotQA against 75.3 for plain dense retrieval (Table 2 of the paper below) published: a reproduction gap that says more about how fragile these pipelines are under another model and prompt than about either paper.

nano-graphrag (0.0.8, 2024)

A small re-implementation of GraphRAG that kept the shape and dropped the weight: 1,200-token chunks, one gleaning, graspologic's hierarchical Leiden on the largest component with max_graph_cluster_size 10 and the same seed, community reports, and local, global and naive modes, default global at level 2 (nano_graphrag/graphrag.py lines 70 to 81; base.py lines 11 to 14). Useful to read: the whole pipeline fits in a few files. Its OpenAI client is created with no arguments (_llm.py lines 20 to 23), so pointing it at a local server needs the OPENAI_BASE_URL variable or a custom function. Last PyPI release 0.0.8.2, 2024-10-19; not run here because LightRAG covers the same shape and is maintained.

HippoRAG: personalised PageRank instead of summaries

HippoRAG (Gutiérrez et al., NeurIPS 2024) ignores communities and treats the graph as an associative memory. Indexing: an LLM extracts named entities, then triples, from each passage; phrases whose retrieval-encoder cosine exceeds 0.8 get a synonymy edge. Query: extract the question's entities, link each to its most similar graph node, and run personalised PageRank (PPR) from them.

p = (1 − d) r + d W p
r
reset vector: mass on the question's nodes, each weighted by its node specificity 1 / (number of passages containing it), so rare names count more
W
the graph's edge weights, each column normalised to sum to 1
d
probability of following an edge rather than jumping back to r: 0.5 in the paper's experiments (run_hipporag_main_exps.sh line 4; the class default is 0.1)
p
the stationary probability of each node; a passage scores the probability mass of its phrases

A walk that restarts at the question's entities spends its time near them and near whatever they are densely connected to, so a passage two hops away can outrank a passage that merely shares words. Published: HippoRAG (ColBERTv2) recall@5 of 89.1 on 2WikiMultiHopQA against 68.2 for ColBERTv2 alone, 51.9 against 49.2 on MuSiQue, but 77.7 against 79.3 on HotpotQA, where it loses; online retrieval "10 to 30 times cheaper and 6 to 13 times faster" than iterative retrieval with IRCoT ({{arXiv 2405.14831 v3, Table 2 and appendix G|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14831}}) published. HippoRAG 2 (2025) adds passages as graph nodes seeded by dense similarity (reset weight 0.05 times the passage's score), links the query to triples rather than entities, and filters triples with the model; with NV-Embed-v2 and Llama-3.3-70B it reports recall@5 of 96.3 on HotpotQA against 94.5 for NV-Embed-v2 alone, and QA F1 averaged over seven datasets of 59.8 against 57.0, with GraphRAG at 49.6 ({{arXiv 2502.14802 v2, Tables 2 and 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14802}}) published. Section 8 runs both shapes on the running example.

0. In one screen

Retrieval as taught so far finds the few passages most similar to a question. Three kinds of question break that: a question about the whole collection ("what are the main themes?"), a question whose answer sits at the end of a chain of entities ("which government post did the actress who played Corliss Archer hold?"), and a question that needs every matching row counted or summed ("how many singers are from France?"). GraphRAG answers the first two by building a graph of entities and relations at index time; structured retrieval answers the third by writing a query (SQL or Cypher) against data that already has structure. This page builds both on the topic's running example and measures what each buys and costs.

text→entities and relations (LLM or NLP)→entity resolution→graph→communities (Leiden)→summaries→global, local, DRIFT or PageRank search

4. Global questions are a map-reduce over summaries

Microsoft's GraphRAG answers "what is this corpus about" by asking every community summary first and merging the answers: its community-summary answers (and a plain text-summarisation baseline) judged more comprehensive than top-k retrieval in 72 to 83% of comparisons on two 1 to 1.7 M-token corpora, at one model call per community per question published (section 6).

5. Cheaper graphs exist; read their claims carefully

LazyGraphRAG (index with no LLM, Microsoft products only), LightRAG, nano-graphrag and HippoRAG trade the community hierarchy for cheaper indexing or PageRank. In HippoRAG 2's re-run on 11,656 passages GraphRAG used 115.5 M input tokens to index, LightRAG 68.5 M, HippoRAG 2 9.2 M published (sections 7 and 9).

1. What a top-k of chunks cannot answer

Everything on the parent page assumes the answer lives in a few passages that resemble the question. Embed the question, take the k most similar chunks, hand them to the model. That assumption fails in four recognisable ways:

Local

The answer is in one passage that shares words or meaning with the question. "What year was Goldfinger released?" Top-k works.

Multi-hop (relational)

The answer needs a chain: passage A names an entity, passage B says something about it. Passage B may share nothing with the question. "What position did the woman who played Corliss Archer in Kiss and Tell hold?"

Global (sensemaking)

The answer is a property of the whole collection, spread over hundreds of passages. "What are the main themes of these 10,000 reports?" Any top 10 samples ten fragments.

Aggregate

The answer is a count, sum, maximum or join over records. "How many singers are older than 40?" Retrieval returns k rows; the answer depends on all of them.

On the running example's 200 HotpotQA questions, a single dense search (bge-small, the root's Agentic lab's search tool) puts at least one gold paragraph in the top 10 for almost every question, but both for @@ppr_dense_10@@, and both in the top 5 for @@ppr_dense_5@@ measured; the root's {{Agentic lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b89145c37dfe8a3b0b}} shows the second hop is what is missed. A graph attacks that: if paragraph A mentions an entity and paragraph B is about that entity, an edge joins them, and retrieval can walk from A to B without the question ever naming B.

When none of this matters. Most questions in most products are local. For those, hybrid search with a reranker ({{The retrieval pipeline|n:3c65c17b0d0d819899fde889e1e8b782}}) is cheaper and at least as good as anything on this page; the GraphRAG paper evaluates only global questions, and Microsoft later built DRIFT search specifically for local ones (section 6).

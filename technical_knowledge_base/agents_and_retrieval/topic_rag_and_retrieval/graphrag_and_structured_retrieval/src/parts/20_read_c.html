4. Entity resolution: is "Doyle" the same node as "Arthur Conan Doyle"?

Entity resolution (also entity linking, deduplication) decides which mentions are the same thing. It sounds like housekeeping; it decides whether the graph connects at all. A bridge question's two paragraphs are joined only if the mention in one (say "Temple" or "Shirley Temple Black") and the subject of the other (the article titled "Shirley Temple") end up as the same node. Merge too strictly and the graph falls apart into islands; merge too loosely and unrelated things fuse ("James Bond" the character and "the James Bond series"; "Labrador" the region and "Labrador Retriever").

Gold without hand labels. HotpotQA gives two checks for free. Every paragraph is a Wikipedia article whose title is its subject, so a good extraction of paragraph P should contain a mention that resolves to P's title (title recall). And every bridge question's two gold paragraphs are joined by a mention of one's title in the other (section 2), so a good graph should join them (link recall). Four resolution rules, strictest first, applied to graphrag's noun phrases (s_entities.py):

  • exact: upper-cased string equality, what graphrag's merge does;
  • normalised: lower case, punctuation and articles removed, Wikipedia's parenthetical removed ("Kiss and Tell (1945 film)" = "kiss and tell");
  • token subset: the mention's words are a subset of one title's words and cover at least half of them ("Kerensky" = "Alexander Kerensky");
  • embedding: nearest title by bge-small cosine, kept above 0.85.

The trade-off is the usual precision against recall, with a twist: every wrong merge adds edges between unrelated paragraphs, and graph algorithms amplify edges (a PageRank walk or a community will happily cross a false bridge). Production systems therefore resolve in stages: cheap normalisation for everything, blocking (only compare names that share a token or a sound), then an embedding or LLM judgement on the candidate pairs, and, where it exists, linking to an external identifier (a Wikidata id, a customer number) that settles the question. graphrag does the first stage only; LightRAG 1.5.7 does not even case-fold names (utils.py lines 5391 to 5393, normalize_entity_name).

5. Communities: Louvain, Leiden and the hierarchy

A community is a set of nodes more densely linked to each other than to the rest. GraphRAG uses communities as the units it summarises, so "what is this corpus about" becomes "what is each community about". The standard score of a partition into communities is modularity:

Q = (1 / 2m) Σij [ Aij − γ ki kj / 2m ] δ(ci, cj)
Aij
edge weight between nodes i and j
ki
total edge weight at node i (its degree)
m
total edge weight of the graph
γ
resolution: higher gives more, smaller communities (graphrag uses 1.0)
δ
1 when i and j are in the same community, else 0

Q counts the edge weight inside communities minus what a random graph with the same degrees would put there. Louvain (Blondel et al. 2008) maximises it greedily: move each node to the neighbouring community that raises Q most, then collapse each community into one node and repeat. Its known flaw: a node that held a community together can move out, leaving the rest internally disconnected. Leiden (Traag, Waltman and van Eck 2019) adds a refinement phase between moving and collapsing, in which each community is re-split from singletons and nodes may only join well-connected sub-clusters; it guarantees connected communities. The Leiden paper found Louvain leaving "up to 25% of the communities ... badly connected and up to 16% ... disconnected" on its test networks, the 16% on DBLP after several iterations ({{arXiv 1810.08473 v3|https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.08473}}, abstract and section IV) published.

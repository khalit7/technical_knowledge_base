2. Knowledge graphs, from zero

A knowledge graph stores facts as nodes (entities: a person, a film, a company, a place) joined by edges (relations: starred in, was born in, owns). One fact is a triple (subject, relation, object): (Shirley Temple, played, Corliss Archer). Two families of databases store them:

  • Property graphs (Neo4j, Kùzu, Memgraph, Amazon Neptune): nodes and edges carry a label and key-value properties; queried with Cypher or its ISO standard successor GQL, as in MATCH (a:Person)-[:PLAYED]->(c:Character) RETURN a.name.
  • RDF stores (Wikidata, GraphDB, Apache Jena): everything is a triple of identifiers (URIs); queried with SPARQL; the schema (an ontology) is itself data.

A schema (which node labels and relation types are allowed) is what separates a curated knowledge graph from what GraphRAG builds. GraphRAG extracts an open graph: whatever entities and relation phrases a language model finds in each chunk, with only a short list of entity types as guidance. That makes it cheap to start and messy to use: the same entity under five spellings, relations that are free text ("was the third film in the series of"), no constraints to check against.

The root's example bridge question from HotpotQA's dev set, drawn by hand from its two gold paragraphs (answer: Chief of Protocol). Once "Shirley Temple" is a node shared by both paragraphs, a graph walk reaches paragraph B in one hop.

HotpotQA is a fair test bed for this because it was built from exactly such links: its authors took the hyperlink graph of the first paragraphs of English Wikipedia and wrote bridge questions over pairs of articles joined by a hyperlink, the linked article's subject being the bridge entity ({{Yang et al. 2018, section 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.09600}}) published. Keep that in mind in section 8: a graph built from title mentions recovers the dataset's own construction.

3. Building the graph: extraction with a language model, or without one

LLM extraction, as graphrag does it

graphrag 3.3.0 (pip graphrag, released 2026-10-08, read at tag v3.3.0) cuts documents into chunks of 1,200 tokens with 100 of overlap (config/defaults.py lines 60 to 63, o200k_base tokens) and sends each chunk to the model with one long prompt. The prompt asks for two kinds of record, separated by ## with fields separated by <|>:

graphrag v3.3.0, packages/graphrag/graphrag/prompts/index/extract_graph.py, lines 15 and 23 (record formats)
("entity"<|><entity_name><|><entity_type><|><entity_description>) ("relationship"<|><source_entity><|><target_entity><|><relationship_description><|><relationship_strength>)

The entity types default to four: organization, person, geo, event (defaults.py lines 147 to 149). Then comes gleaning: the conversation continues with a second message telling the model it missed entities, and its answer is appended.

So every chunk costs two calls, and the second re-sends the first call's prompt, chunk and answer. The parser upper-cases every name (clean_str(record_attributes[1].upper()), lines 146 to 147) and the merge step groups entities across chunks by exact (title, type) (extract_graph.py line 108): "APPLE" the company and "APPLE" the place stay apart, and so do "SHIRLEY TEMPLE" and "SHIRLEY TEMPLE BLACK", two names of one person. Descriptions of one entity from many chunks are summarised by another LLM call once there are two or more (description_summary_extractor.py lines 63 to 68).

What it costs here. On the root Agentic lab's subset (the 10 paragraphs of each of the first 40 test questions, @@sub_units@@ paragraphs of median @@ex_para_med@@ Qwen3 tokens), graphrag's first extraction call has a median prompt of @@ex_first_med@@ tokens: the subset's @@ex_para_total@@ tokens of text become @@ex_first_total@@ prompt tokens before gleaning, @@ex_ratio@@ times the text measured. With graphrag's 1,200-token chunks the instructions weigh less: each indexed token costs (1,717 + 1,200) / 1,200 = 2.4 prompt tokens in the first call derived, which is one reason graphrag uses big chunks; the GraphRAG paper measured the price of that: with GPT-4 on a HotpotQA sample, 600-token chunks yielded almost twice as many entity references as 2,400-token chunks ({{Edge et al., appendix A.2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130}}) published.

Extraction without a language model

graphrag also ships a "fast" indexing method (config/enums.py lines 44 to 54) that replaces the LLM with classic NLP: noun phrases from a part-of-speech tagger become nodes, and two phrases in the same text unit get an edge weighted by pointwise mutual information (PMI, how much more often they co-occur than chance: p(x,y) log2(p(x,y) / (p(x) p(y))), graphs/edge_weights.py line 22). Its default extractor is TextBlob-based and, per its docstring, "the extractor used in the first bencharmking of LazyGraphRAG" (regex_extractor.py line 32; the typo is theirs).

Run with graphrag's own class over all @@nlp_paras@@ HotpotQA paragraphs, it took @@nlp_wall@@ s on one CPU core (about @@nlp_ms@@ ms a paragraph, zero LLM tokens) and found @@nlp_phr@@ phrases, @@nlp_distinct@@ distinct, @@nlp_once@@ of them (@@nlp_once_pct@@) in a single paragraph measured, not under the bench lock, re-time pending. The most frequent nodes are month names (@@nlp_top1@@ in @@nlp_top1_n@@ paragraphs): hubs that join unrelated paragraphs, which is why graphrag prunes them (section 5).

What the NLP extractor misses. It keeps a phrase only if every token is plain letters, digits or hyphens (has_valid_tokens, regex_extractor.py), so "Hey Pa! There's a Goat on the Roof" or "Ernst Willi Messerschmid's" can never be nodes, and "New York" becomes "YORK" because "NEW" is in its stop list. A paragraph's own subject, its Wikipedia title, appears among its phrases (upper-cased exact match) for only @@ent_full_exact_title@@ of @@ent_full_paras@@ paragraphs (@@ent_full_exact_title_pct@@) measured.

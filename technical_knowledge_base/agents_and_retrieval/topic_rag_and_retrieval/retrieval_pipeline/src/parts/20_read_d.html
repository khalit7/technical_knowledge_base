4. Fusion: merging two ranked lists

The problem. Hybrid search runs BM25 and a dense retriever on the same query and gets two ranked lists with scores on unrelated scales: a BM25 score of 8.96 and a cosine of 0.71. The root lab measured the two common answers on the running example (RRF lifts Qwen3-Embedding on SciFact from 0.700 to 0.724; z-score fusion with a weight tuned on training claims reaches 0.739) root lab. This section asks why each works, what each throws away, and runs the older score-combination family alongside.

4.1 Why the raw scores cannot simply be added

BM25 is a sum of idf-weighted terms, unbounded above (the top-1 score of a SciFact claim ranges from @@bm_top1_lo@@ to @@bm_top1_hi@@, 5th to 95th percentile); a cosine lies between −1 and 1, and Qwen3-Embedding's top-1 cosines range from @@de_top1_lo@@ to @@de_top1_hi@@ measured. Add them with equal weight and BM25 decides almost alone: on SciFact with Qwen3-Embedding, raw 0.5 · BM25 + 0.5 · cosine scores @@raw05_sf_q@@, close to BM25 alone (@@bm_sf_def@@). The trap is the equal weight, not the addition: give the cosine @@raw_best_sf_q_w@@ of the weight (chosen on training claims) and the raw sum scores @@raw_best_sf_q@@, as good as any normalised method. The weight absorbs the scale difference, and it has to be learned on labelled queries, for each pair of retrievers, and again whenever either changes.

4.2 Reciprocal rank fusion, and what it throws away

RRF(d) = Σlists r 1 / (k + rankr(d))
rankr(d)
position of d in list r, from 1; a list that does not contain d adds 0
k
60 in the paper ({{Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, SIGIR 2009|https://doi.org/10.1145/1571941.1572114}}: "fixed during a pilot investigation"), the default rank_constant in Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, and in most libraries: damps the top ranks, so being in both lists counts for more than being first in one
Predict (the old page's worked example): dense returns A, B, C, D; BM25 returns C, D, E, A. With k = 60, what is the fused order?
C, A, D, B, E. C: 1/63 + 1/61 = 0.032266; A: 1/61 + 1/64 = 0.032018; D: 1/64 + 1/62 = 0.031754; B: 1/62 = 0.016129; E: 1/63 = 0.015873 (recomputed for this page; the old page's arithmetic is right). Anything in both lists beats anything in one, whatever its rank there.

RRF uses only ranks, so it needs no calibration and no tuning, and the root lab found k barely matters (0.724 to 0.732 across k from 1 to 1,000). The price is that it discards how sure each retriever is. Scores do carry that: on SciFact, when BM25's first result beats its second by a wide relative margin, the first result is relevant far more often.

300 SciFact test claims split into five equal groups by the relative margin (s1 − s2) / s1 of each retriever's own top two scores; bar = share of claims whose top-1 result is relevant. src/exp/fusion_deep.py measured

Score fusion can use this (a runaway BM25 winner keeps a big normalised score); RRF gives a runaway winner and a near-tie the same 1/(k+1).

4.3 Why agreement is a good bet

RRF's real bet is that a document both retrievers find is more likely relevant than either one's favourite. On SciFact with Qwen3-Embedding, of the documents in both top 10s, @@agree_both_sf@@ are relevant; of those only BM25 found, @@agree_bm_sf@@; only dense, @@agree_de_sf@@ measured. On NFCorpus, where each question has many relevant documents, the same pattern: @@agree_both_nf@@ against @@agree_bm_nf@@ and @@agree_de_nf@@.

4.4 The whole family, tuned honestly

The score-combination methods are older than RRF ({{Fox and Shaw, TREC-2, 1994|https://trec.nist.gov/pubs/trec2/papers/txt/23.txt}}): normalise each list, then CombSUM adds the normalised scores, CombMNZ multiplies that sum by the number of lists containing the document (an explicit agreement bonus), CombMAX keeps the best one. Weighted RRF gives each list its own weight; Borda count adds positions. Every knob below was chosen on the tuning split (SciFact train, NFCorpus dev) by the root lab's rule and scored once on test; the root's RRF, min-max and z-score numbers are reproduced exactly.

What the numbers say. With a weight learned on held-out queries, normalised score fusion (min-max or z-score, CombMNZ) beats RRF for every model that is about as good as BM25: Qwen3 on SciFact @@fu_sf_q_rrf@@ (RRF) against @@fu_sf_q_z@@ (z-score) and @@fu_sf_q_mnz@@ (CombMNZ); on NFCorpus @@fu_nf_q_rrf@@ against @@fu_nf_q_z@@. CombMAX is the weak member (it ignores agreement). For gte-modernbert, which beats BM25 by nine points on SciFact, every method lands within a point of the model alone: when one list dominates, fusion has little to add. Fusing three lists (BM25, Qwen3 and gte with RRF) scores @@three_sf@@ on SciFact, against @@two_dense_sf@@ for the two embedders alone. In products the same choice is a setting: Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and Weaviate let you weight the lists or fuse normalised scores instead of ranks (OpenSearch's normalization processor, {{docs|https://docs.opensearch.org/latest/search-plugins/search-pipelines/normalization-processor/}}); tune the weights on labelled queries as above, never by eye. Hybrid search measured inside Postgres (BM25 over Postgres lexemes, pgvector, and RRF in one SQL statement on 522,931 Quora questions and SciFact) is on {{Search and vector databases|n:3ef5c17b0d0d815eb4caef9b4cd4d732}}.

4.5 Does the fusion method still matter after a reranker?

The old page claimed weighted fusion "rarely beats plain RRF plus a reranker". Tested with the recorded Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B scores: rerank the top K of each fused list, SciFact test.

Before the reranker z-score fusion is ahead (@@ftr_z_before@@ against @@ftr_rrf_before@@); after it the gap closes or flips: RRF's top 20, reranked, scores @@ftr_rrf20@@, z-score's @@ftr_z20@@; at K 30 they score @@ftr_rrf30@@ and @@ftr_z30@@. The reranker reorders the candidates itself, so what matters is which documents are in the top K, not their order; the extra precision of score fusion is spent on an order the reranker replaces. So the old claim holds here: tune the fusion weight if there is no reranker; with one, RRF is enough and the time is better spent on the depth K (see {{Rerankers and late interaction|n:3f85c17b0d0d813fba53fc883f8a2c8f}}).

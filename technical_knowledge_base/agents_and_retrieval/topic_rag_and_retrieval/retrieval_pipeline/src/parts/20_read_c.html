2. BM25: the tokenizer matters more than k1 and b

A dense retriever finds paraphrases but can rank a vaguely related abstract above the one that names the exact gene; a word-matching retriever finds the exact word every time. BM25 ("Best Match 25", Robertson and colleagues, 1990s) is the standard word-matching score, computed over an inverted index (for every word, the documents containing it and how often). The formula from zero, with a calculator, and how the index is stored inside a database are on {{Search and vector databases|n:3ef5c17b0d0d815eb4caef9b4cd4d732}} (its section 4, "Ranking with BM25"). In one line: score(D, Q) = Σt in Q idf(t) · tf / (tf + k1 · (1 − b + b · |D| / avgdl)), where k1 sets how fast repeats stop counting and b how much document length matters. The tokenizer. BM25 only sees the terms its tokenizer produces. bm25s's default lowercases, keeps runs of two or more word characters (token_pattern=r"(?u)\b\w\w+\b", tokenization.py line 572), drops Lucene's 33 English stopwords (which include "no" and "not": "X causes Y" and "X does not cause Y" become the same query), and optionally stems ("decreases" and "decreased" both become "decreas"). Stemming is worth @@stem_gain_sf@@ points of nDCG@10 on SciFact and @@stem_gain_nf@@ on NFCorpus; stopwords change nothing here. The two-character pattern silently drops single characters: "Type 1 Diabetes" loses the "1", "IL-6" becomes just "il", "H2A.Z" loses its "Z"; @@bm_1char_n@@ of the 300 SciFact claims contain such a token, and keeping them is worth @@onechar_gain_sf@@ points measured. The one-line formula is the "Lucene" variant as bm25s computes it (bm25s/scoring.py lines 115 to 130 and 190 to 195, tag 0.3.13); the textbook version multiplies the fraction by (k1 + 1), a constant that changes no ranking. A 60-line NumPy version written for this page (src/exp/bm25lab.py) reproduces the setup's recorded bm25s run: identical top-10 scores for @@bm_check_sf@@ of 300 SciFact claims and @@bm_check_nf@@ of 323 NFCorpus questions (the rest differ only in how zero-score documents pad the list) measured.

Defaults differ: bm25s uses k1 1.5 and b 0.75 (bm25s/__init__.py lines 146 and 147); Lucene and Elasticsearch use 1.2 and 0.75, as does the running example.

Variants. The Robertson, ATIRE, BM25L and BM25+ formulas (bm25s implements all five, following {{Kamphuis et al., ECIR 2020|https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jimmylin/publications/Kamphuis_etal_ECIR2020_preprint.pdf}}) differ in the idf floor and in a small boost for long documents; on SciFact they span @@bmvar_range_sf@@ and on NFCorpus @@bmvar_range_nf@@. Pick one and move on.

Fields: BM25F. A title is short and on topic; a body is long. BM25F ({{Robertson, Zaragoza and Taylor, CIKM 2004|https://doi.org/10.1145/1031171.1031181}}) normalises each field by its own average length, weights it, and adds the fields before saturation, so a word in both title and body is not counted as two independent pieces of evidence:

tf̃ = Σf wf · tff / (1 − bf + bf · |Df| / avgdlf),   score = Σt idf(t) · tf̃ / (k1 + tf̃)

With the title weight chosen on the tuning split (titles average @@title_len_sf@@ terms against @@body_len_sf@@ for SciFact abstracts): SciFact @@bm_sf_def@@ → @@bm25f_sf@@ (title weight @@bm25f_sf_w@@), NFCorpus @@bm_nf_def@@ → @@bm25f_nf@@ measured. Elasticsearch's combined_fields query "takes a principled approach to scoring based on the simple BM25F formula" ({{Elasticsearch docs|https://www.elastic.co/guide/en/elasticsearch/reference/current/query-dsl-combined-fields-query.html}}, read 2026-10-14); multi_match with best_fields scores each field on its own and keeps the best, which is a different thing.

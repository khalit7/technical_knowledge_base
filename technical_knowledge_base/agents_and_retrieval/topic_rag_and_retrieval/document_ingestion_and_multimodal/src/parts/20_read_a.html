0. In one screen

Ingestion is everything that happens to a document before the first chunk is embedded: reading the file, recovering its text in reading order, its tables and figures, attaching the metadata a citation needs, and keeping the index in step when the file changes. Retrieval can only find what ingestion kept. A paragraph scrambled by a column jump, a table flattened into a stream of numbers or a scanned page with no text never reaches the retriever, and no reranker, embedding model or prompt brings it back.

file→text layer or pixels→elements: paragraph, heading, table, figure, caption→reading order→chunks + metadata→hash, embed what changed→index

Five ideas that carry the page

1. A PDF has no paragraphs

Only glyphs at coordinates. Words, lines, columns, reading order, tables and headers are all reconstructed, by rules or by a model, and every tool reconstructs them differently (section 1).

2. Measure on your files, with checks that are facts

On 24 of the running example's papers, the share of answer paragraphs that survived intact ranged from 1 of 107 (pdfplumber) to 94 (MinerU) with the same text layer underneath; yet BM25 recall barely moved except where words were glued together (section 3b).

3. Tables need their structure, not just their numbers

164 of the 945 answerable QASPER questions cite a table or figure. On the test table every plain-text extractor kept nearly all the numbers and none of the grid; of six table-aware tools only Docling kept every number under its headers (section 5, Table lab).

4. Chunk boundaries decide re-indexing cost

One inserted author initial made fixed-size chunking re-embed all 17 chunks of a paper; structure-aware chunking re-embedded 1 of 26 (section 8, Re-indexing lab).

5. Sometimes skip the parser

For infographics, charts and slides, embedding the page image with a late-interaction vision model beats OCR text; for dense text, OCR or the text layer plus BM25 is as good and far cheaper (sections 9 and 10).

7. Figures, images and other formats

A chart, diagram or photo carries information no text extractor sees. Three things can be recovered, at rising cost:

  1. The caption, which layout parsers label as its own element (caption in Docling, Caption in Marker, FigureCaption in Unstructured): keep it attached to its figure and make it its own chunk, since "Figure 3: accuracy against context length for four models" is often exactly what a question matches. A rule-based extractor emits it as an ordinary line, often inside the paragraph that flows around the figure.
  2. Text inside the figure: axis labels and legends are real text in vector figures (a scatter of short lines) and need OCR in raster ones.
  3. What the figure shows, which only a VLM can describe. Captioning for retrieval: crop each figure, ask a VLM for its quantities, axes and main finding, index that beside the caption, labelled as generated. One VLM call per figure; the risk is a confident wrong description. In the ColPali paper's Table 2, Claude 3 Sonnet captions raised BM25 on ArxivQA (figure questions) from 31.6 to 40.1 nDCG@5 over OCR text alone (the authors' runs).

Images as items. For standalone images (product photos, diagrams) a single-vector image-text embedding of the CLIP or SigLIP kind puts pictures and queries in one space ({{Vision encoders|n:3c65c17b0d0d81c99f60ceb2f02cf5ea}}). A page full of small text is too much for one vector; when answers live in charts, infographics or slides, section 10 keeps one vector per patch instead of translating figures into words.

8. Metadata: what every chunk must carry

Once a chunk leaves its file, three jobs need facts only the parser had: a citation the user can check ("page 4 of the 2020 annual report, section 3.2"), a filter inside the search ("contracts signed after 2024", "documents this user may read"), and an update (replace every chunk of a changed file). Store each chunk as a record, not a string.

A record that can cite itself: page and box point back to the exact paragraph



A paragraph of QASPER paper 1909.00175 parsed by Docling 2.138.0: page, bounding box (PDF points, origin bottom left; drawn in orange on page 3), heading and element type are its real output; hashes computed by src/scripts/make_record.py; the acl value is illustrative and the field names are this page's choice, not a standard. Docling reported the heading as "3.1 Datasets and Settings" without its parent "3"; rebuilding the full path from heading numbers is a small post-processing step.


    

  • Source and version: document id, URL, the file's content hash and parse time; without the version a citation can point at text that no longer exists.
    • 

  • Location: page and bounding box, so a viewer opens the page and highlights the passage, how a user checks a citation in seconds.
    • 

  • Structure: heading path ("4 Experiments > 4.2 Results") and element type; prepending the path to the embedded text gives a chunk context (contextual prefixes: {{The retrieval pipeline|n:3c65c17b0d0d819899fde889e1e8b782}}).
    • 

  • Filters and permissions: dates, document type, tenant and access-control list as indexed fields the vector store filters inside the search ({{RAG in production|n:3f85c17b0d0d81aa9ba9c0ceac9e28c1}} shows why access enforced in the prompt leaks).
    • 

  • Ingestion facts: parser name and version, OCR used and its confidence, so a parser fix says which documents to re-parse; replace a document atomically so old and new chunks are never both live.
    • 



Cost: a few hundred bytes per chunk, small next to a 1,024-dimension float32 vector (4,096 bytes); dropping them costs a full re-parse later. How to check: open the cited page of twenty retrieved chunks from the metadata alone.

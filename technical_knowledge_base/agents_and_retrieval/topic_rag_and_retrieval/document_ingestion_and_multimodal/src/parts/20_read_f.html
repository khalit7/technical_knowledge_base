9. Incremental updates: re-embed only what changed

A contract gets an amendment, an arXiv paper a v2. Re-embedding the whole corpus on every change costs compute and stale time; the goal is to touch only chunks whose text changed and delete chunks of text that is gone, with three levels of content hash:

  1. File: same hash, skip it without parsing (an unchanged file re-synced).
  2. Chunk: hash each chunk's normalised text and compare with the document's stored hashes: embed only new hashes, delete vanished ones, keep the rest with their vectors.
  3. Embedding: key vectors by (chunk hash, embedding model, model version), so a model upgrade is a deliberate full re-embed, never a silent mix of two vector spaces.

The trap: chunk boundaries move. With fixed 200-word windows, one sentence inserted near the top shifts every later boundary and every later hash. Boundaries tied to content survive edits: paragraph and heading boundaries from the parser (structure-aware), or content-defined chunking, which cuts where a rolling hash of the last few words hits a chosen value, as the LBFS file system did with Rabin fingerprints (Muthitacharoen et al., SOSP 2001). Which chunking retrieves best is {{The retrieval pipeline|n:3c65c17b0d0d819899fde889e1e8b782}}'s question; this is what each costs when documents change.

  2. Fixed 200 words. The inserted word shifts every later window by one word, so every window's text and hash change and all 17 chunks go back through the model.
  3. Fixed, 50 words of overlap. The same shift with more, overlapping chunks: all 23 are embedded again, and the overlapping words twice.
  4. Structure-aware. Chunks follow the parser's paragraphs and headings, so only the first chunk, the 33 words around the author line, changed: 1 of 26 chunks embedded again.
  5. Content-defined. Boundaries sit where the rolling hash of the last three words hits the chosen value, so they move with the text, not with word counts: 1 of 40.
  6. All ten revised papers of the QASPER sample (Docling, last two versions): the revisions changed 15.0% of the words (the red floor). Fixed 200-word windows re-embedded 75.8% of the words, and 76.4% with 50 words of overlap; structure-aware chunks 49.2%; content-defined chunks 44.2%.

A rewrite of a third of a paper (1910.14599, 39.5% changed) re-embeds most of it under any strategy; for a word or a sentence, boundaries decide between one chunk and the whole document.

Deletes are the half people forget: chunks of removed text must leave the index, or retrieval keeps citing a removed clause (in HNSW stores a delete is usually a tombstone until compaction; purging and freshness are on {{RAG in production|n:3f85c17b0d0d81aa9ba9c0ceac9e28c1}}). How to check: after a sync, a document's chunk hashes in the index equal those computed from its current version, no more and no fewer.

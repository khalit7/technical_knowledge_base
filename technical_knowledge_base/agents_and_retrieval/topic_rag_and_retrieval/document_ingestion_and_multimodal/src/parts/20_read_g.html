6. Figures, captions and images

The problem. A chart, a diagram or a photo carries information no text extractor sees. Three things can be recovered, at rising cost:

  1. The caption. Layout-aware parsers label captions as their own element (caption in Docling, Caption in Marker, FigureCaption in Unstructured). Keep the caption attached to its figure or table and make it its own chunk: "Figure 3: accuracy against context length for four models" is often exactly what a question matches. A rule-based extractor emits the caption as an ordinary line, frequently in the middle of a paragraph that flows around the figure.
  2. Text inside the figure. Axis labels, legends and diagram boxes are sometimes real text in the PDF (vector figures) and come out as a scatter of short lines; in raster figures they need OCR.
  3. What the figure shows. Only a vision-language model can describe a trend or a diagram's arrows. Captioning for retrieval: crop each figure, ask a VLM for a description that names the quantities, axes and the main finding, and index that text next to the caption, pointing back to the image. The cost is one VLM call per figure at ingestion, and the risk is a confident wrong description that retrieval then trusts; keep the description labelled as generated in the metadata. In the ColPali paper's Table 2, captions written by Claude 3 Sonnet raised BM25 on ArxivQA, a set of figure questions, from 31.6 to 40.1 nDCG@5 over OCR text alone (the authors' runs).

When to embed the image instead. If most answers live in charts, infographics or slides, describing every figure in words is a lossy translation; section 9 retrieves the page image itself.

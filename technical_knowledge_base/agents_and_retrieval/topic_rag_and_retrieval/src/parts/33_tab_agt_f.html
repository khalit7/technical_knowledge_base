What transfers to frontier models, and what the papers found

What transfers: the shape. A loop costs several generator calls and a growing prompt per question; a router saves most of that on one-hop traffic; long context pays for every token it reads; a fixed decomposition is cheaper and more predictable than a free loop; an entity graph costs one generator call per chunk before the first question is asked. What does not: the absolute accuracy of a 1.7B or 4B model, and especially how well it follows a tool-use format. Small models fail more at the "agent" part (choosing a good query, knowing when to stop) than at reading; larger models were trained for exactly this and gain more from a loop. Here, with a working prompt, the 1.7B model gained nothing from the loop on the held-out questions (F1 0.525 against 0.520 for the pipeline), while the 4B gained 0.084 on the test set and lost the tool format less often (3 questions against 17): the bigger model used the loop better.

Common mistakes, and interview questions

Comparing an agent with a pipeline at different reading budgets
An agent that searches four times reads up to 12 paragraphs; a pipeline given the top 3 reads 3. Part of any gain is just more text. Compare at matched paragraphs or tokens too (top 10 here), and report the cost next to the accuracy.
Reporting accuracy without searches, tokens and seconds
A loop that is a few points better at several times the tokens and latency may be the wrong choice for most traffic. Every row on this tab carries its cost; a router is how you get the loop's accuracy where it matters and the pipeline's cost elsewhere.
Counting cached prompt tokens as free
A prompt cache cuts compute and latency for the repeated prefix, but an API still bills cached input tokens (at a discount), and a self-hosted cache costs memory. The quadratic growth of billed tokens with steps remains.
Tuning prompts or the step budget on the test questions
Small generators are very sensitive to the prompt; choosing it on the test set inflates the result. Here every prompt was chosen on held-out questions.
Evaluating with an embedder trained on the benchmark
Qwen3-Embedding lists HotpotQA in its training data; its HotpotQA numbers are in-domain. Check a model's declared training data (MTEB metadata, model cards) before using a benchmark to choose it.
Assuming a long window is read evenly
Position matters: measure accuracy with the evidence at different places before stuffing a window, and put the most relevant passages first or last.
Trusting exact match alone
"October 1922" against "1922", or "Yes" against "yes, both are American": exact match counts these as wrong. ReAct's authors found 29% of their failures were label ambiguity. Report F1 too, and read a sample of failures.

Interview questions, short answers

When do you use an agentic retrieval loop instead of a single retrieve-then-generate pipeline?
When the question needs information you can only know to look for after reading something (multi-hop, comparisons, research tasks), when the first search often fails and needs reformulation, or when the tools vary (search, SQL, an API). Not for one-hop lookups: the loop multiplies calls, tokens and latency. Measure both on a golden set, with cost.
Our agent costs ten times the pipeline. How do you bring it down?
Route: send one-hop questions to the pipeline. Cap steps and stop early when the answer is found. Use prompt caching for the shared prefix. Summarise or drop old observations instead of re-sending them. Retrieve better per step (hybrid search, a reranker) so fewer steps are needed. Use a smaller model for planning queries and a larger one only to answer.
Context windows are a million tokens now. Do we still need RAG?
Usually yes: the corpus is larger than any window, every token in the window is paid for on every question, latency grows with the prompt, models read the middle of long prompts less well, and retrieval gives citations and access control per document. Long context helps when the relevant material is small and changes per question (one contract, one codebase), and the two combine: retrieve generously, then let a long window read it.
How do you evaluate an agentic retrieval system?
Final answers against gold (EM/F1, or a judge where answers are free text), evidence recall (did the needed passages reach the model), and the trajectory: searches, tokens, latency, wasted or repeated steps, format failures. Compare against a single-shot baseline and an oracle (gold passages given) to split retrieval failures from reasoning failures.
When is GraphRAG worth its indexing cost?
When users ask questions about the whole corpus (themes, trends, "what are all the...") that no single passage answers, or when relationships between entities are the point (who worked with whom, supply chains). It costs a model call per chunk before the first question and a rebuild or merge when documents change. For fact lookup, hybrid search with a reranker is cheaper and usually as good; cheaper variants (LightRAG, LazyGraphRAG) move cost to query time.
System design: a research assistant over 2 million internal documents answering multi-step questions. Sketch it.
Ingestion: parse, chunk on structure, embed, BM25 index, metadata and access lists per chunk. Query: a router (a small classifier on the query) sends lookups to hybrid search, reranker, top 5, answer with citations; multi-step questions go to a loop with search as a tool (bounded steps, each search filtered by the user's access), with observations summarised to keep the prompt short and a prompt cache for the fixed prefix. Evaluation: a golden set of both kinds, tracking answer quality, evidence recall, cost and p95 latency per route. Ops: re-index on change, cache frequent queries, log trajectories for failure review.

How this was measured, and the limits

  • Machine: Apple M1 Pro (8 performance and 2 efficiency cores, 16 GB unified memory), llama.cpp build 11146 with Metal for the generator, PyTorch on the CPU for the search tool's query encoder, October 2026. Every generator run held the shared bench lock; the machine was otherwise shared, so seconds carry the load of the day (recorded in the run logs). The ratios between strategies transfer; the seconds do not.
  • Generators: Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-4B, 4-bit GGUF (Q4_K_M), temperature 0, seed 0, thinking switched off (enable_thinking: false, documented on both model cards), one request at a time, prompt cache on. Outputs are cached by exact prompt, so every number here is reproducible without rerunning the models.
  • Prompts: each strategy's answer prompt was chosen among two candidates on 60 held-out HotpotQA dev questions (the other 800 questions whose paragraphs are in the corpus), never on the 200 test questions. The Pipeline lab learned why: its first prompt made the 1.7B model answer one label almost always.
  • Metrics: HotpotQA's official answer normalisation (lowercase, strip punctuation and the articles a, an, the, collapse spaces), exact match and token F1 with its yes/no rule; "gold seen" is the share of the two gold paragraphs that reached any prompt. 95% intervals are bootstrap intervals over questions (2,000 resamples, seed 0); paired differences are per question.
  • Search tool: bge-small-en-v1.5, exact search; the agent is never shown a paragraph twice (each search returns the 3 best paragraphs it has not seen). Search time is milliseconds and is included in seconds per question.
  • Scripts: src/agent/ in the repository; make_data.py builds everything this tab draws, check_numbers.py checks the prose against it.
Not run here, and why
  • The full HotpotQA corpus (5.2 million paragraphs): too large to embed on this laptop; the small corpus makes first-stage retrieval easier, said above.
  • Frontier models: the page runs locally with no API keys; the published table above stands in, labelled.
  • Thinking mode: Qwen3's reasoning mode generates hundreds of tokens per call (about 8 s per answer in the Pipeline lab's test), so a loop of five calls over 200 questions would take hours; every run here has it off.
  • Long context beyond 8k tokens: prefill alone would be about 25 s or more per question for the 1.7B model (extrapolated from the measured 9.3 s at 7.6k tokens).
  • Microsoft's graphrag package: its extraction prompts and thousands of calls are sized for large hosted models; a LightRAG-style extraction with one call per paragraph gives the indexing-cost shape at a scale this laptop can run.
  • Judged answer quality: HotpotQA has gold answers, so EM and F1 are exact; judged metrics belong to {{RAG and retrieval evaluation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8194a914d1843ee26f13}}; trajectory metrics in depth to {{Agent and trajectory evaluation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d81fdbabaf3582bff37f2}}.

Why one search is not enough

A multi-hop question needs facts from more than one passage, and usually the second passage can only be found once the first has been read. "Alexander Kerensky was defeated by the Bolsheviks in a civil war that ended when?" needs one paragraph to learn that the war is the Russian Civil War and a second to learn when it ended. A pipeline searches once, with the question as typed; whatever that one search misses, the generator never sees.

Predict: with bge-small, for how many of the 200 questions are BOTH gold paragraphs in the top 3 of a single search with the question?
122 of 200 (61%). At least one gold paragraph is in the top 3 for nearly every question; the second one is what goes missing. Both are in the top 10 for 164.

The first hop is almost free: some gold paragraph is in the top 10 for 199 of the 200 questions. The second is not: both are in the top 3 for 122 questions with bge-small and 80 with BM25, and in the top 10 for 164 and 147. A pipeline can buy recall by putting more paragraphs in the prompt (top 10 instead of top 3), at a cost in tokens and in distraction; an agent can instead search again for the missing piece, using what it just read. Most second-hop paragraphs here still share a few words with the question (median 3 content words against 4 for the first; only 10 of 200 share none), because HotpotQA questions often name both things: it is the ranking, not total absence, that hides them.

This corpus makes retrieval easy, on purpose
The 9,760 paragraphs are the gold and distractor paragraphs of 1,000 HotpotQA questions, not the 5.2 million paragraphs of the full Wikipedia dump that HotpotQA's "fullwiki" setting and the BEIR benchmark use. A single search finds far more here than it would at full scale (BM25 recall@10 of the gold paragraphs 0.868 here, against recall@100 of 0.740 on BEIR's full HotpotQA corpus). Read the absolute numbers as an easy case; the gaps between strategies are what this tab measures. Qwen3-Embedding lists HotpotQA among its training data, so its HotpotQA results are in-domain, not zero-shot (MTEB model metadata, checked by the Tool map); that is why the search tool here is bge-small, which does not.

Five ways to answer, measured

The same 200 questions, the same corpus, the same search tool, the same generator. Only who decides what to read changes.

1. No retrieval

The question alone. Measures what the model memorised in training ("closed book").

question→generate

2. Single-shot pipeline

Search once with the question, put the top k paragraphs in the prompt, answer. The {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}}'s shape. One generator call.

search(question)→top 5→generate

3. Agent loop (ReAct)

The model is given a search tool and decides, step by step, what to search for and when to stop: Thought, Action, Observation, repeated (the {{ReAct paper|n:3c65c17b0d0d816b9a24c28de8f08ea8}}'s format). At most 4 searches of 3 paragraphs each.

think→search(q1)→read→think→search(q2)→...→finish

4. Decompose, then retrieve

One call splits the question into two sub-questions; each is searched and answered in turn, the first answer filled into the second ("#1"); a last call answers the original question from everything read. A fixed program, not a free loop (the Self-Ask and IRCoT family).

split→search(sub 1)→answer 1→search(sub 2 + #1)→answer

5. Long context

No search: put the gold paragraphs and many distractors straight into the window, as if the whole corpus fitted. Two sizes: the question's own 10 paragraphs (HotpotQA's "distractor" setting, about 1.4k tokens) and about 8k tokens (the own 10 plus the corpus paragraphs nearest the question). The gold paragraphs are always in the window, so this is the best case for long context.

gold + distractors→generate

Each dot is one strategy over the 200 questions, measured. Hollow dots: the same strategy with the other generator. Tokens are what an API would bill (every call's whole prompt plus its output); seconds are wall-clock on this laptop, generator and search together.

Reading the result (Qwen3-4B, the clean comparison). The agent loop is the most accurate retrieval strategy: F1 0.574 against 0.490 for the single-shot pipeline with the top 5, a paired gain of +0.084 (95% interval +0.022 to +0.144; 51 questions better, 24 worse). It pays for it with 2,456 tokens per question against 754 (3.3 times) and 6.15 s against 1.98 s. Decomposition, a fixed two-search program, lands at the pipeline's accuracy (0.488) for 1,993 tokens: on these questions the free loop beat the fixed plan. Without retrieval the 4B model scores 0.193, so most of what it answers comes from what it reads. The agent saw fewer gold paragraphs than the top 5 did (81% against 84%) and still answered better: it reads 3 paragraphs at a time, chosen for the piece it is missing, instead of 5 chosen for the whole question.

Qwen3-1.7B: the loop does not pay. With the smaller model the pipeline (F1 0.402) and the agent loop (0.411) are level: the paired difference is +0.009 with an interval from -0.060 to +0.081, and the loop wins on 48 questions and loses on 53, at 3.3 times the tokens and 3.9 times the seconds. Decomposition is worse than the pipeline (0.335), and the cheapest strong option is to read more: the top 10 scores 0.425 and the question's own 10 paragraphs 0.456. A small model that cannot plan its searches well gains nothing from being allowed to.

A prompt finding along the way
The first 1.7B test runs used an answer prompt that listed "a name, a date, a number, or yes or no" as answer forms. The 1.7B model then answered "yes" or "no" to questions that are not yes/no on 63 of its 200 single-shot answers (F1 0.249); the 4B model, with the same prompt, never did. Held-out tuning had already picked a prompt without that list ("Give only the answer, in as few words as possible"), but a scripting error kept it out of the first test runs. Every one-call 1.7B row on this tab was rerun with the held-out choice; the first runs are kept in the repository as out/strategies_s2prompt.json. The lesson matches the {{Pipeline lab|#t-pipe}}'s: a small generator can collapse onto one answer form, and only held-out checks catch it.

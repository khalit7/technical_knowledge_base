A router: send each question to the cheapest strategy that can answer it

Most real questions are one-hop ("when was X founded?"), and an agent loop spends several calls on what one search answers. A router looks at the question first and picks the strategy: the one-shot pipeline for one-hop questions, the loop for multi-hop ones. Adaptive-RAG (Jeong et al. 2024, arXiv 2403.14403) trains a small classifier to predict a question's complexity and routes between no retrieval, one step and many steps.

HotpotQA has no one-hop questions, so constructed ones were made: Qwen3-4B read one corpus paragraph (never a gold paragraph of the 200) and wrote one question answerable from it alone, with a short answer copied from the paragraph; questions whose answer was not in the paragraph, was longer than six words or appeared in the question were dropped. Of 240 paragraphs drawn, 198 questions survived the filters (Qwen3-4B, 62,375 tokens, 259 s), split 99 for training and 99 for the test. The mixed test set is 100 of the two-hop questions plus the one-hop test half. Two routers, both trained or prompted without touching the test set: a logistic regression on the question's bge-small vector (the search tool computes it anyway, so routing is free), trained on 400 held-out HotpotQA questions and the one-hop training half; and Qwen3-1.7B asked, with four examples, whether the question needs ONE fact or TWO.

Result, with Qwen3-1.7B answering. The logistic-regression router sends the right questions to the loop 74.9% of the time and gets the loop's accuracy (F1 0.449 against 0.451 for always using the agent) for 26% fewer tokens (1,949 against 2,652 per question) and 2.49 s against 3.39 s. A perfect router would do slightly better (0.459 for 1,851 tokens). The small model as a router failed: it called almost everything one-hop (6 of 199 sent to the loop, 47.7% right), so its policy is nearly "always pipeline". A free classifier on a vector the system already computes beat asking the generator. Two caveats: the one-hop questions are constructed and easier to tell apart than real traffic, and this mixed set inherits the 1.7B pipeline's answer-prompt failure on the two-hop half (its one-hop answers were hit only 6 times in 99), which flatters the loop. Adaptive-RAG's own T5-Large router was right only 54.5% of the time on real mixed benchmarks (table below).

GraphRAG, at small scale

GraphRAG (Edge et al. 2024, Microsoft, arXiv 2404.16130) spends language-model calls at indexing time: it extracts the entities and relations in every chunk into a knowledge graph (nodes are entities, edges are relations), groups the graph into communities (clusters of densely connected entities, found with the hierarchical Leiden algorithm; 600-token chunks and gpt-4-turbo in the paper), and writes a summary of each. At query time, global search answers questions about the whole corpus ("what are the main themes?") by asking every community summary (map) and combining the partial answers (reduce); local search starts from the entities in the question and follows the graph. Plain top-k retrieval cannot answer a corpus-wide question, because no single passage holds the answer. Cheaper variants: LightRAG (Guo et al. 2024, arXiv 2410.05779) keeps the graph but retrieves through entity and relation keywords without community reports; LazyGraphRAG (Microsoft, 2024) defers the language-model work to query time; GraphRAG's own FastGraphRAG extracts noun phrases with ordinary NLP instead of a language model.

Built here measured, small-scale: the own paragraphs of the first 40 test questions, indexed LightRAG-style (Qwen3-1.7B lists entities and relations, one call per paragraph; the paragraph's title is always an entity; entities merged by normalised name), Louvain communities (networkx; Leiden is the same idea with a guarantee of connected communities), and a summary per community of 8 or more entities.

measured Indexing 394 paragraphs that hold 53,062 tokens of text cost 161,797 generator tokens (81,368 in, 80,429 out: about 3 tokens of work per token indexed) and 15.9 minutes of generation on this laptop, plus 36,720 tokens and 99 s to summarise 92 of the 131 communities. The graph has 3,160 entities, only 284 of which appear in more than one paragraph: a small model's names rarely match across paragraphs, which is why real systems add entity resolution. At this rate the whole 9,760-paragraph corpus would take about 6.5 hours of extraction here; the published GraphRAG indexed about 1 million tokens of podcasts in 281 minutes with a hosted model.

Memory: retrieval over the agent's own history

An agent that runs for hours, or across sessions, cannot keep its whole history in the window: the token growth measured above would make every step slower and dearer, and the earliest turns eventually fall out. Agent memory applies retrieval to the agent's own past: turns, tool results and facts it has learned are written to a store, and each step retrieves the few that matter instead of re-reading everything. Everything on this page carries over (chunk the history into turns or facts, embed them, search with the current goal as the query, rerank, and measure whether the needed memory was retrieved), with two differences: the store changes on every step, so writing and forgetting policies matter as much as search, and time matters (a newer fact can overrule an older one). Fact memory, temporal knowledge graphs and OS-style paging are on {{Agent memory layers|n:3f15c17b0d0d8170a200ce4476385ff5}}; the papers {{Proactive Memory Agent|n:3e25c17b0d0d81ce85e0c6d82f6beb4c}} and {{Just-in-Time Memory|n:3e95c17b0d0d818f89b3f066f816727b}} have their own pages; deciding what goes into the window at all is {{Context engineering|n:3f15c17b0d0d811292cee46514370431}}.

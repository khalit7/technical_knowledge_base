7. Who drives retrieval: pipelines, agents, graphs, long context, memory

Everything so far is a fixed pipeline: the engineer decided in advance to search once, with the user's words, and to pass the top k on. Three rungs of a ladder differ in who decides what to retrieve:

  • Naive RAG: nobody decides. Embed the question, take the top k, generate.
  • Advanced RAG: the designer decides, in advance, for every question: the rewrite, hybrid search, filter and rerank stages of section 5.
  • Agentic retrieval: the model decides, at run time. Search is a tool it may call zero or more times, with queries it writes itself, reading each result before deciding whether to search again or answer. This is the reason-then-act loop of {{ReAct|n:3c65c17b0d0d816b9a24c28de8f08ea8}}; the "deep research" products are the same loop with a budget of minutes.

The problem a loop solves: the second hop. Two questions from HotpotQA's dev set. "Who was born first, Arthur Conan Doyle or Penelope Lively?" (a comparison question) needs two paragraphs, and both names are in the question. "What government position was held by the woman who portrayed Corliss Archer in the film Kiss and Tell?" (a bridge question) needs a paragraph about the film, to learn the actress's name, before the paragraph about her can be searched for: the second paragraph shares almost no words with the question. On the running example's 200 HotpotQA questions, searching once with bge-small puts at least one of the two gold paragraphs in the top 10 for 199 questions, but both for 164; for the 160 bridge questions, 124; for the 40 comparison questions, all 40. BM25: 147 of 200 with both measured, Agentic lab. A single search is good at the first hop and weak at the second, which is exactly what a loop can fix by searching for the name it just learned.

What it costs. Each call of the loop re-reads everything before it: the instructions, the question and every result so far. Without prompt caching, the input tokens over n searches and a final answer are

Tin = (n + 1) s + r · n(n + 1) / 2
n
searches before the answer, so n + 1 model calls
s
tokens every call re-reads: system prompt, tool description, question
r
tokens each search result adds; the call after search i re-reads i results

The n(n + 1)/2 term makes the bill grow quadratically with the number of searches. A prompt cache lowers the price of the re-read prefix, not the time to generate each step.

The same question, a pipeline against an agent loop: input tokens per model call

Formula above, illustrative inputs: s = 1,500 and r = 2,000 tokens, against a pipeline that reads s plus R = 4,000 retrieved tokens once. At 3 searches the agent reads 18,000 tokens (3.3 times the pipeline), at 6 searches 52,500 (9.5 times), the old version of this page's worked example, reproduced derived. The {{Agentic lab|#t-agent}} replays real recorded trajectories of Qwen3 models with their measured token counts.

What the published comparisons say. The most careful controlled comparison (Ferrazzi et al., {{"Is Agentic RAG worth it?"|https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07711v2}}, revised April 2026) ran an enhanced pipeline (router, rewriter, retriever, reranker) against an agentic system on the same tasks: the agent used on average 3.3 times the input tokens, 1.9 times the output tokens and 1.5 times the time, at up to 3.6 times the cost; it won on query rewriting (+2.8 nDCG@10 on average) and lost clearly on FEVER fact verification, 64.6 against 87.9 F1 published (the paper's text calls the FEVER gap 28.8 points; its table gives 23.3). {{IRCoT|https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10509}} (Trivedi et al. 2022), which interleaves retrieval with reasoning steps, raised HotpotQA answer F1 from 53.6 with one retrieval to 60.7 with GPT-3 published. The loop pays on multi-hop and exploratory questions, and wastes tokens on lookups a single search answers.

Routers. Hence a cheap classifier or rule in front: easy questions to the pipeline, hard ones to the loop. {{Adaptive-RAG|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14403}} (Jeong et al. 2024) trained a small T5 classifier to pick no retrieval, one search or many: with FLAN-T5-XL it kept most of multi-step retrieval's accuracy (46.94 against 48.85 F1) at about 2.4 times less time, although the classifier itself was right only 54.5% of the time; an oracle router would have reached 56.28 published. A router is worth measuring on your own mix of questions; the {{Agentic lab|#t-agent}} does so on a mixed set.

GraphRAG: questions about the whole corpus

Some questions have no passage that answers them: "what are the main themes across these 10,000 reports?". Retrieving the top 10 chunks samples ten fragments of the answer. GraphRAG (Edge et al., Microsoft, {{arXiv 2404.16130|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130}}) pays at index time instead: a language model reads every chunk and extracts entities and relations into a graph, the Leiden algorithm splits the graph into a hierarchy of communities, the model summarises each community, and a global question is answered map-reduce style over the summaries. On two corpora of about 1 and 1.7 million tokens its global approaches were judged more comprehensive than vector RAG's in 72 to 83% of pairwise comparisons (by an LLM judge) and indexing the first corpus took 281 minutes with gpt-4-turbo published. Cheaper variants: {{LightRAG|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05779}} (a flat graph with dual graph and vector retrieval; in its own paper it beats GraphRAG overall on three of four datasets by small margins and loses on the fourth, 49.6 against 50.4) published; {{LazyGraphRAG|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag-setting-a-new-standard-for-quality-and-cost/}}, which Microsoft says costs the same to index as vector RAG and over 700 times less per global query than GraphRAG's global search vendor, and ships only in Microsoft's products (the {{Tool map|#t-map}} checked the open graphrag 3.3.0 package). Cost figures quoted around the web for a "500-page corpus" ($50 to $200 for GraphRAG, $0.50 for LightRAG) carry no method or source and are not repeated here as facts. For lookups, hybrid search with a reranker is cheaper and at least as good. The {{GraphRAG and structured retrieval|n:3f85c17b0d0d81e2bb4cdbb749e48a5a}} child builds a graph on the running example; the Agentic lab measures a small one's indexing cost.

Long context instead of retrieval

If the corpus fits in the context window, why search? Because every query pays for every token. Cost per query C = pin Tfresh + pcache Tcached + pout Tout. With illustrative prices of $3 per million input tokens, $0.30 cached and $15 output, a 1-million-token corpus, a 500-token question and a 500-token answer: RAG with 10 chunks of 400 tokens costs $0.021; putting the whole corpus in the window $3.009 uncached (143 times) and $0.309 with the corpus cached (15 times) derived. Prefill (reading the input before the first output token) takes seconds: Qwen3-1.7B here needs 9.3 s for 7,611 prompt tokens measured, production child. Accuracy is not guaranteed either: in {{"Lost in the middle"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172}} (Liu et al. 2023, Table 6) GPT-3.5-Turbo answered 75.8% of questions with the answer document first among 20, 53.8% with it tenth, below its 56.1% with no documents at all; {{NoLiMa|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167}} (2025) found 11 models below half their short-context score at 32K tokens once the question and the answer stop sharing words, GPT-4o falling from 99.3% to 69.7% published. Li et al. ({{arXiv 2501.01880|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01880}}) found long context generally ahead on question answering (most on Wikipedia questions) and retrieval ahead on dialogue and general questions. Long context wins for a small corpus read once or holistically; retrieval wins on cost, latency, scale beyond the window and per-user access control, and the two compose: retrieve, then read generously. The Agentic lab places the gold paragraphs at different positions on the local models; {{Long-context and multimodal benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d810bb4dfc98919c9be8a}} covers how long-context claims are tested.

Retrieval for an agent's own memory

Agent memory is retrieval turned inward: the corpus is the agent's own history, written by the same process that will later need it. Retrieval over memories is the easy part; deciding what to write, how to update or expire a fact, and when to surface it are the hard parts. Two papers in this knowledge base, both corrected against the papers themselves: the {{Proactive Memory Agent|n:3e25c17b0d0d81ce85e0c6d82f6beb4c}} (Meta AI, arXiv 2607.08716) runs a second model that decides when to remind the acting agent, lifting Terminal-Bench 2.0 from 37.6% to 45.9% with Claude Sonnet 4.5 acting and Opus 4.6 reminding published; {{Just-in-Time Memory|n:3e95c17b0d0d818f89b3f066f816727b}} keeps raw trajectories and curates them when a task arrives, +16.2 points on ALFWorld over RL-trained SkillOS, but most of that comes from training the curator, not from the timing alone published. Fact stores, temporal graphs and paging are on {{Agent memory layers|n:3f15c17b0d0d8170a200ce4476385ff5}}; what to keep in the window on {{Context engineering|n:3f15c17b0d0d811292cee46514370431}}.

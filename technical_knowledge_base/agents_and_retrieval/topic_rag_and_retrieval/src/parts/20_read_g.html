7. Who drives retrieval: pipelines, agents, graphs, long context, memory

Everything so far is a fixed pipeline: the engineer decided in advance to search once, with the user's words, and to pass the top k on. Three rungs of a ladder differ in who decides what to retrieve:

  • Naive RAG: nobody decides. Embed the question, take the top k, generate.
  • Advanced RAG: the designer decides, in advance, for every question: the rewrite, hybrid search, filter and rerank stages of section 5.
  • Agentic retrieval: the model decides, at run time. Search is a tool it may call zero or more times, with queries it writes itself, reading each result before deciding whether to search again or answer. This is the reason-then-act loop of {{ReAct|n:3c65c17b0d0d816b9a24c28de8f08ea8}}; the "deep research" products are the same loop with a budget of minutes.

The problem a loop solves: the second hop. Two questions from HotpotQA's dev set. "Who was born first, Arthur Conan Doyle or Penelope Lively?" (a comparison question) needs two paragraphs, and both names are in the question. "What government position was held by the woman who portrayed Corliss Archer in the film Kiss and Tell?" (a bridge question) needs a paragraph about the film, to learn the actress's name, before the paragraph about her can be searched for: the second paragraph shares almost no words with the question. On the running example's 200 HotpotQA questions, searching once with bge-small puts at least one of the two gold paragraphs in the top 10 for 199 questions, but both for 164; for the 160 bridge questions, 124; for the 40 comparison questions, all 40. BM25: 147 of 200 with both measured, Agentic lab. A single search is good at the first hop and weak at the second, which is exactly what a loop can fix by searching for the name it just learned.

What it costs. Each call of the loop re-reads everything before it: the instructions, the question and every result so far. Without prompt caching, the input tokens over n searches and a final answer are

Tin = (n + 1) s + r · n(n + 1) / 2
n
searches before the answer, so n + 1 model calls
s
tokens every call re-reads: system prompt, tool description, question
r
tokens each search result adds; the call after search i re-reads i results

The n(n + 1)/2 term makes the bill grow quadratically with the number of searches. A prompt cache lowers the price of the re-read prefix, not the time to generate each step.

With the 4B, the loop pays. It beats the single search by 0.084 F1 (paired over the same questions, 95% interval 0.022 to 0.144; better on 51, worse on 24) for about 3 times the input tokens and seconds. A fixed decomposition, which plans its sub-questions before seeing any result, buys nothing (0.488). Long context over the question's own 10 paragraphs, which always contain both gold paragraphs, matches the loop (0.576); widened to 8,000 tokens of mostly distractors it drops to 0.475 and takes 29 s per question.

With the 1.7B, it does not. The loop (0.411) is level with the single search (0.402; paired +0.009, 95% interval −0.060 to +0.081, better on 48, worse on 53) at about 3 times the input tokens: a model that cannot plan its next search gains nothing from being allowed to. Its best lever is simply reading more: top 10 scores 0.425 and the question's own 10 paragraphs 0.456 (+0.054 over top 5, interval 0.003 to 0.108), while the 8,000-token window hurts (0.320), and decomposition hurts too (0.335). Who should drive depends on the model: the shape transfers (loops pay only when the model can plan searches), the absolute F1 of small local models does not.

The quadratic growth is real: with the 4B, a question that ends after two model calls bills 820 input tokens, one that uses all five 5,370, and those long trajectories are the failures (29 questions used all four searches and score F1 0.18, against 0.673 for the 112 that stopped after two). A step cap does not just bound cost: past two or three searches the small model was mostly lost.

A prompt finding
The lab's first 1.7B runs used a default answer prompt because the held-out prompt choice crashed. With it, the 1.7B answered "yes" or "no" to 63 of 200 questions that were not yes/no questions, and single search top 5 scored F1 0.249; with the prompt chosen on held-out questions, 0.402. The 4B was unaffected. As in the Pipeline lab, the prompt is a component tuned on held-out data like any other, and a small model is far more sensitive to it measured, Agentic lab (strategies_s2prompt.json).

What the published comparisons say. The most careful controlled comparison (Ferrazzi et al., {{"Is Agentic RAG worth it?"|https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07711v2}}, revised April 2026) ran an enhanced pipeline (router, rewriter, retriever, reranker) against an agentic system on the same tasks: its Table 9 gives the agent 2.7 and 3.6 times the input tokens (about 3.15 times on average; its text says 3.3), 1.7 and 1.8 times the output tokens (about 1.75; the text says 1.9) and about 1.5 times the time on its two datasets. The agent won on query rewriting (+2.8 nDCG@10 on average) and lost on deciding whether a query needs retrieval at all: user-intent F1 on FEVER 64.6 against the pipeline's semantic router at 87.9 (Table 3; the agent retrieved when it should not have) published. {{IRCoT|https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10509}} (Trivedi et al. 2022), which interleaves retrieval with reasoning steps, raised HotpotQA answer F1 from 53.6 with one retrieval to 60.7 with GPT-3 published. The loop pays on multi-hop and exploratory questions, and wastes tokens on lookups a single search answers.

Routers. Hence a cheap classifier or rule in front: easy questions to the pipeline, hard ones to the loop. {{Adaptive-RAG|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14403}} (Jeong et al. 2024) trained a small T5 classifier to pick no retrieval, one search or many: with FLAN-T5-XL it kept most of multi-step retrieval's accuracy (46.94 against 48.85 F1) at about 2.4 times less time, although the classifier itself was right only 54.5% of the time; an oracle router would have reached 56.28 published. A router is worth measuring on your own mix of questions. The {{Agentic lab|#t-agent}} built two for the 1.7B on a mixed set of 99 single-hop and 100 multi-hop questions: always the pipeline scores F1 0.512, always the agent 0.451 at 3.5 times the tokens; a logistic classifier on question embeddings (75% routing accuracy) 0.500, and even an oracle router that sends exactly the multi-hop questions to the agent 0.493, because for this model the loop is worse than the pipeline on multi-hop questions too (0.389 against 0.426). The router that asked the model itself sent only 6 questions to the agent and scored 0.520, level with always-pipeline measured. A router only pays when the expensive path is actually better on the questions it receives; check that first.

GraphRAG: questions about the whole corpus

Some questions have no passage that answers them: "what are the main themes across these 10,000 reports?". Retrieving the top 10 chunks samples ten fragments of the answer. GraphRAG (Edge et al., Microsoft, {{arXiv 2404.16130|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130}}) pays at index time instead: a language model reads every chunk and extracts entities and relations into a graph, the Leiden algorithm splits the graph into a hierarchy of communities, the model summarises each community, and a global question is answered map-reduce style over the summaries. On two corpora of about 1 and 1.7 million tokens its global approaches were judged more comprehensive than vector RAG's in 72 to 83% of pairwise comparisons (by an LLM judge) and indexing the first corpus took 281 minutes with gpt-4-turbo published. Cheaper variants: {{LightRAG|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.05779}} (a flat graph with dual graph and vector retrieval; in its own paper it beats GraphRAG overall on three of four datasets by small margins and loses on the fourth, 49.6 against 50.4) published; {{LazyGraphRAG|https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag-setting-a-new-standard-for-quality-and-cost/}}, which Microsoft says costs the same to index as vector RAG and over 700 times less per global query than GraphRAG's global search vendor, and ships only in Microsoft's products (the {{Tool map|#t-map}} checked the open graphrag 3.3.0 package). Cost figures quoted around the web for a "500-page corpus" ($50 to $200 for GraphRAG, $0.50 for LightRAG) carry no method or source and are not repeated here as facts. For lookups, hybrid search with a reranker is cheaper and at least as good. Measured at small scale: the Agentic lab had Qwen3-1.7B extract entities and relations from 394 HotpotQA paragraphs (53,062 tokens of text): 161,797 generator tokens (about 3 tokens of work per token indexed) and 951 s of generation, about 6.5 hours for the whole 9,760-paragraph corpus at that rate. Retrieving through the graph (dense hits, then one hop along shared entities) put both gold paragraphs in the top 3 for 29 of the first 40 test questions against 26 for dense search alone, and changed nothing at k = 2 or 5; one global "main themes" question by community map-reduce cost 93 model calls and about 20,000 tokens, against one call for a top-10 answer measured, Agentic lab. The {{GraphRAG and structured retrieval|n:3f85c17b0d0d81e2bb4cdbb749e48a5a}} child goes further.

Long context instead of retrieval

If the corpus fits in the context window, why search? Because every query pays for every token. Cost per query C = pin Tfresh + pcache Tcached + pout Tout. With illustrative prices of $3 per million input tokens, $0.30 cached and $15 output, a 1-million-token corpus, a 500-token question and a 500-token answer: RAG with 10 chunks of 400 tokens costs $0.021; putting the whole corpus in the window $3.009 uncached (143 times) and $0.309 with the corpus cached (15 times) derived. Prefill (reading the input before the first output token) takes seconds: Qwen3-1.7B here needs 9.3 s for 7,611 prompt tokens measured, production child. Accuracy is not guaranteed either: in {{"Lost in the middle"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172}} (Liu et al. 2023, Table 6) GPT-3.5-Turbo answered 75.8% of questions with the answer document first among 20, 53.8% with it tenth, below its 56.1% with no documents at all; {{NoLiMa|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.05167}} (2025) found 11 models below half their short-context score at 32K tokens once the question and the answer stop sharing words, GPT-4o falling from 99.3% to 69.7% published. Li et al. ({{arXiv 2501.01880|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.01880}}) found long context generally ahead on question answering (most on Wikipedia questions) and retrieval ahead on dialogue and general questions. Long context wins for a small corpus read once or holistically; retrieval wins on cost, latency, scale beyond the window and per-user access control, and the two compose: retrieve, then read generously. Reproduced at small scale, U-shaped for both local models: with the two gold paragraphs placed among 20, Qwen3-1.7B scores F1 0.496 with them first, 0.405 at positions 5 and 6, 0.381 at 10 and 11 and at 15 and 16, and 0.468 at the end (200 questions; first against middle +0.116, interval 0.063 to 0.168); Qwen3-4B 0.682, 0.540, 0.554, 0.599, 0.592 (100 questions; first against middle +0.128) measured, Agentic lab; {{Long-context and multimodal benchmarks|n:3ef5c17b0d0d810bb4dfc98919c9be8a}} covers how long-context claims are tested.

Retrieval for an agent's own memory

Agent memory is retrieval turned inward: the corpus is the agent's own history, written by the same process that will later need it. Retrieval over memories is the easy part; deciding what to write, how to update or expire a fact, and when to surface it are the hard parts. Two papers in this knowledge base, both corrected against the papers themselves: the {{Proactive Memory Agent|n:3e25c17b0d0d81ce85e0c6d82f6beb4c}} (Meta AI, arXiv 2607.08716) runs a second model that decides when to remind the acting agent, lifting Terminal-Bench 2.0 from 37.6% to 45.9% with Claude Sonnet 4.5 acting and Opus 4.6 reminding published; {{Just-in-Time Memory|n:3e95c17b0d0d818f89b3f066f816727b}} keeps raw trajectories and curates them when a task arrives, +16.2 points on ALFWorld over RL-trained SkillOS, but most of that comes from training the curator, not from the timing alone published. Fact stores, temporal graphs and paging are on {{Agent memory layers|n:3f15c17b0d0d8170a200ce4476385ff5}}; what to keep in the window on {{Context engineering|n:3f15c17b0d0d811292cee46514370431}}.

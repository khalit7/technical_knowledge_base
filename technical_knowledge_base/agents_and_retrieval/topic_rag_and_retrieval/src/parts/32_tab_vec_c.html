How embedding models are trained: contrastive learning from zero

The problem. A language model already turns text into vectors (its hidden states), but nothing in next-token training asks a claim and the abstract that supports it to end up close. Averaging those raw states gives poor retrieval. The geometry has to be taught.

The data: pairs. Training needs many (query, positive) pairs: a question and its answer passage, a title and its body, a search query and the page users clicked, a claim and its evidence. Modern recipes add pairs written by a language model. Qwen3-Embedding, for example, reports about 150 million synthetic pairs for a first weakly supervised stage, then about 12 million selected synthetic pairs plus labelled data for supervised fine-tuning, then merging checkpoints (Qwen3 Embedding report, sections 3.2 and 3.3; vendor).

The loss: pick your positive out of a line-up. For each query in a batch, compute its cosine with every candidate document; divide by a temperature τ; apply a softmax; the loss is minus the log-probability of the true positive. This is InfoNCE, a cross-entropy where the "classes" are the candidates. Pushing that probability up pulls the positive closer and pushes the others away.

Li = − log   exp(cos(qi, di+) / τ)  /  Σj exp(cos(qi, dj) / τ)
di+
the positive of query i
dj
every candidate in the line-up: the positive, the other queries' positives (in-batch negatives) and any hard negatives
τ
temperature: small τ sharpens the softmax so small cosine gaps matter (SimCSE settled on τ = 0.05, Gao et al. 2021, appendix D)

In-batch negatives are free. The other pairs in the batch already have their documents embedded, so every query gets B − 1 negatives at no extra cost (the trick of DPR, Karpukhin et al. 2020, and SimCSE). Bigger batches mean more negatives, and more chance that one of them is close: BGE's general-purpose stage used batches as large as 19,200 and relied on in-batch negatives alone (C-Pack paper, section 3.4).

Hard negatives teach the fine distinctions. A random abstract about bacteria is an easy negative for a claim about homocysteine. A hard negative is a document that looks relevant but is not, usually mined by running a retriever and taking a high-ranked non-positive. BGE's task-specific stage adds one such negative per pair, "mined from the task's original corpus, following the ANN-style sampling strategy" (same section). The risk is the false negative: the mined document is in fact relevant but was never labelled, and training then pushes a correct answer away.

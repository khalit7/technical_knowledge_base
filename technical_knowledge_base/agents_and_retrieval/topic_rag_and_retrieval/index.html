Topic: rag-and-retrieval

How a system finds the right passages and answers from them, taught from zero: words and vectors, BM25 and embeddings, vector indexes, hybrid search and rerankers, measuring where an answer gets lost, and agentic retrieval. Measured on an Apple M1 Pro with open models and public datasets, with published results kept apart. Data as of October 2026.

Pipeline lab

Chunking: where the evidence lands

The problem. A paper is thousands of tokens; an embedding model reads a few hundred at a time, and the prompt can hold only a few passages. So documents are cut into chunks, each indexed on its own. If a cut falls through the middle of the paragraph that answers the question, no chunk holds the whole answer, and the half that does match may rank low.

How it is measured here. QASPER's 892 questions come with the paragraphs annotators used as evidence, located as character spans in the paper. For each strategy: how many chunks it makes, how big; for how many questions an evidence paragraph sits wholly inside one chunk ("intact"); and whether retrieval inside the question's own paper hands the generator text covering at least 90% of an evidence paragraph. Two fair ways to hand text over: the top k chunks, or a token budget filled in rank order. Sizes are in Qwen3 tokens.

Explorer: Zampieri et al. 2019, Predicting the Type and Target of Offensive Posts in Social Media (arXiv 1902.09666, QASPER text). Shaded bands are chunks (alternating colours, purple where two chunks overlap); badges mark where each chunk starts (#1 c7 = BM25's first pick); the green underline is the annotated evidence for the selected question.

At an equal budget of 1,024 tokens the order flips: 45.9% for 512-token chunks against 64.5% for paragraph-aware chunks. Comparing chunkers at a fixed k rewards whoever sends more text.

Context written by the generator ("contextual retrieval")

Anthropic's recipe (September 2024): before indexing, a model reads the whole document and writes one or two sentences situating each chunk, which are prepended to the chunk. Their reported result, with Claude 3 Haiku writing the context and their own datasets: the top-20 retrieval failure rate fell from 5.7% to 3.7% with contextual embeddings, 2.9% adding contextual BM25, 1.9% adding a reranker published, vendor.

Run here with Qwen3-1.7B writing the context, Anthropic's prompt verbatim, on 71 papers (every fourth; 1,664 default chunks, 239 questions): 2,560 s of generation, 0.87 M new prompt tokens because llama.cpp kept each paper in its cache (11.1 M without that cache). Result, same papers and questions without and with the context: bge-small top 3 46.9% to 49.4%, but at a 1,024-token budget 57.7% to 55.2%; BM25 top 3 38.5% to 40.2%, budget 49.0% to 48.1% measured. With 239 questions a difference of 2 to 3 points is within noise: a 1.7 B writer gives no clear gain on this task, and some of its "contexts" just repeat the chunk's first lines. Note the setting differs from Anthropic's: here retrieval runs inside one paper, where every chunk shares the same document context.

What the numbers say
  • Cut where the author did. Fixed 256-token windows keep an evidence paragraph whole for 607 of 892 questions (68.0%); splitting on blank lines first (the recursive rule) keeps 838 (93.9%), packing paragraphs within sections 832 (93.3%). At a 1,024-token budget with bge-small, that is 56.8% of questions found for fixed windows against 67.9% (recursive) and 70.0% (paragraphs within sections). The 0.6 B Qwen3-Embedding shows the same gap (59.4% against 70.3%): a bigger embedder does not repair a bad cut.
  • Overlap is a patch, not a fix: 32 tokens of overlap move fixed windows from 546 to 607 intact questions and cost 14% more tokens to index.
  • Compare at equal tokens. Big chunks win at a fixed k because they send more text: semantic chunking at LangChain's default (cut at the 95th percentile of sentence-to-sentence distance) makes chunks of median 342 and up to 4,967 tokens, tops the top-3 chart with BM25 (71.6%), and comes last at a 1,024-token budget (38.6%). Cutting at the 80th percentile gives small chunks that split the evidence (547 intact).
  • Sentence packing ignores paragraphs and does no better than fixed windows (593 intact).
  • A title and section prefix (no model) helps paragraph-aware chunks (bge-small at 1,024 tokens: 70.0% to 72.0%) and not fixed windows (56.8% to 54.9%), whose section label can be wrong for half the window.
  • One caution: QASPER's evidence unit is the paragraph, so "one paragraph per chunk" (median 89 tokens, 888 of 892 intact, 77.5% at 1,024 tokens with bge-small) is favoured by construction. On documents whose answers span paragraphs, packing paragraphs up to a size is the safer default.

BM25, dense, and hybrid

The problem. BM25 matches words: it finds "PPM1D" when the claim says "PPM1D", and misses an abstract that says the same thing in other words. A dense retriever matches meaning: it finds the paraphrase, and can rank a topically similar abstract above the one with the exact gene name. Their mistakes are different, so running both and merging the two lists (hybrid search) often beats either. How BM25's inverted index and a vector index sit inside a database is on Search and vector databases; the defaults each framework ships with are in the Tool map's defaults section (LlamaIndex returns only 2 chunks unless told otherwise).

How the lists are merged. The two scores live on different scales (a BM25 score of 4.45 against a cosine of 0.71), so they cannot simply be added. Two families:

  • Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) ignores scores and uses ranks: score(d) = Σlists 1 / (k + rank(d)), with k = 60 in the paper that proposed it (Cormack, Clarke and Büttcher, SIGIR 2009) and in most libraries. No tuning, no calibration.
  • Score fusion normalises each list's scores (min-max to [0, 1], or z-score: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) and takes a weighted sum, α · dense + (1 − α) · BM25. It keeps how much better the first hit is than the second, but α must be chosen, and choosing it on the test set is a leak.

What the numbers say
  • Hybrid helps a dense model that is about as good as BM25 and disagrees with it. On SciFact, RRF lifts MiniLM-L6 from 0.645 to 0.713 nDCG@10 and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B from 0.700 to 0.724 (better on 69 claims, worse on 50).
  • It can hurt a dense model that is clearly better. gte-modernbert (0.767) drops to 0.763 with RRF k 60, ColBERT-small (0.743) to 0.731: equal-weight fusion gives BM25's weaker list the same say.
  • k barely matters (0.724 to 0.732 for Qwen3 across k from 1 to 1,000), which is why 60 is a safe default.
  • Weighted score fusion beats RRF when its weight is learned on other queries. With α chosen on SciFact's 809 training claims (0.6), z-score fusion scores 0.739 for Qwen3 on the test claims; the best α picked on the test set itself would claim 0.744, a 0.005 leak. On NFCorpus, RRF adds nothing to Qwen3 (0.363 to 0.361) while z-score with α tuned on the dev split gives 0.375.

Filters: inside the search or after it

The problem. Real queries come with conditions: only this customer's documents, only what this user may read, only papers after 2020. A metadata filter can run inside the search (only allowed documents compete: a pre-filter) or after it (fetch the top N from everything, then drop what is not allowed: a post-filter). The post-filter is easy to bolt on and silently returns fewer results, sometimes none, when the filter is selective.

The setup. SciFact has no access metadata, so an attribute is constructed: each abstract gets an access group, crc32(document id) mod G, so a user sees 1/G of the corpus; each claim is asked by a user of its evidence abstract's group (the answer is always allowed). Exact search with Qwen3-Embedding, so the only difference is where the filter runs; how filters interact with approximate indexes such as HNSW is in the Vector search lab.

Results returned, of 10 asked, by filter selectivity

300 SciFact test claims, Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B, exact inner-product search. measured

What the numbers say
  • When a user may see 10% of the corpus, fetching 10 and filtering returns 1.73 results on average, and nothing at all for 14 of 300 claims. The evidence reaches the results for 255 claims, against 284 when the filter runs inside the search.
  • Over-fetching repairs it only partly: with the top 100 fetched, the evidence is found as often as with the pre-filter (284), but 96 claims still get fewer than 10 results. At 1% visibility even 1,000 fetched leaves 112 claims short.
  • The pre-filter always returns 10. Its evidence count even rises as the filter narrows (284 at 10%, 299 at 1%, against 256 unfiltered), but that is the construction: the evidence is always allowed and fewer abstracts compete with it.
Why vector stores make this hard, briefly
With an exact scan the pre-filter is free: skip disallowed rows. With an approximate index (HNSW, IVF) the graph or clusters were built over every document, so a restrictive filter can cut the paths the search walks, and engines add workarounds: iterative index scans that keep fetching until enough rows pass (pgvector since 0.8.0), filter-aware graph traversal (ACORN, Weaviate's default since v1.34), or a brute-force scan when few rows pass. The Tool map lists which engine does what; the Vector search lab measures filtered HNSW.

Rewriting the query

The problem. The words a user types are not always the words of the documents. Three fixes, each run here with the local generator (Qwen3-1.7B, greedy):

  • HyDE (Gao et al. 2022, "Precise Zero-Shot Dense Retrieval without Relevance Labels"): ask the model to write a fake answer passage, embed that instead of the question, since a passage looks more like the documents than a question does. The prompt is the authors' SciFact prompt from their code (texttron/hyde, promptor.py): "Please write a scientific paper passage to support/refute the claim."
  • Multi-query: ask for three rewordings, retrieve with each and the original, fuse the four lists with RRF.
  • Conversational rewrite: in a chat, "does it help?" means nothing without the earlier turns; the model rewrites the last message into a standalone query.
HyDE and multi-query on the 300 SciFact test claims measured
What the numbers say
  • HyDE helps a little, and how to use it depends on the retriever. For Qwen3-Embedding the fake passage alone lifts nDCG@10 from 0.700 to 0.718; for bge-small the passage alone hurts (0.692) and only the passage averaged with the claim helps (0.733, from 0.713). For BM25, appending the passage to the claim adds words the abstracts use: recall@100 rises from 0.913 to 0.943.
  • Multi-query buys almost nothing here (Qwen3: 0.699 against 0.700, better on 36 claims and worse on 36): SciFact claims are already specific, and the rewordings mostly repeat them.
  • Both cost a generator call before retrieval can start: about 2.2 s per claim for HyDE and 0.8 s for the three rewordings with the 1.7 B model on this laptop, under heavy load from other jobs. That is more than the whole retrieval stage.

Reranking: how deep, and with what

The problem. A first-stage retriever must score millions of documents in milliseconds, so it compares a query vector with precomputed document vectors (or word statistics) and never reads the two texts together. A cross-encoder reranker does read them together: query and candidate go through one transformer and come out as one relevance score. That is far more accurate and far too slow for the whole corpus, so it reads only the first stage's top K.

The two questions. How deep (K: more candidates means more chances to rescue the evidence, and more latency)? And which model: a reranker only helps when it is better than the first stage it reorders.

First stage

SciFact, 300 test claims. The reranked top K come first, then the first stage's ranks K+1 onward unchanged. measured

What the numbers say
  • A strong reranker is the biggest single gain in the pipeline. Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B on the hybrid top 20 lifts nDCG@10 from 0.724 to 0.782 and recall@10 from 0.862 to 0.902; bge-reranker-v2-m3 reaches 0.757.
  • Deeper is not always better. Both large rerankers peak at K 20 on the hybrid list (0.782 and 0.757) and slip at K 30 and 50 (0.778 and 0.779; 0.749 and 0.746), although the first stage's recall rises from 0.912 to 0.936: each extra candidate is one more chance to promote a wrong abstract above the right one, and the cost grows with every candidate.
  • A weak reranker hurts. The 22 M CE MiniLM (trained on MS MARCO web queries) makes the hybrid list worse the deeper it reads: 0.724 without it, 0.702 at K 30, 0.689 at K 100. It reproduces the BEIR paper's BM25+CE number (0.688) because BM25 alone is weaker than it.
  • The price is time. On this laptop Qwen3-Reranker scores about 5 pairs per second: K 20 costs about 4 seconds per query, which is why the latency section matters.

Limits: the setup scored the two large rerankers only on the BM25 top 30 and the Qwen3-Embedding top 30 (the union), and this tab scored the 2,715 extra pairs the hybrid top 50 needs (Qwen3-Reranker 4.9 pairs/s, bge-reranker-v2-m3 14.1 to 14.3 pairs/s, same settings). K 100 for them (about 12,200 more pairs, roughly 40 minutes for Qwen3-Reranker) was not run, given the flat curve from 20 to 50; CE MiniLM covers K up to 100.

Where the answer gets lost

The idea. An answer can only be right for the right reason if three things happen in a row: the first stage puts the evidence in its top K; the reranker keeps it in the top k that reach the prompt; the generator reads it and answers correctly. So end-to-end accuracy factors as

A = rK × pk|K × g
rK
share of questions whose evidence is in the first stage's top K (recall@K, read as "found at all")
pk|K
of those, the share still in the top k after reranking
g
of those, the share the generator gets right

Each factor has its own fix (better retriever or bigger K; better reranker or bigger k; better generator or prompt), so measuring them apart tells you where to spend. A single accuracy number does not.

The task. SciFact claims, with the original release's labels: given the top k abstracts, the generator answers SUPPORT, CONTRADICT or NOT ENOUGH INFO and cites the abstract it relied on. The check is deterministic, no judge: an answer is correct when the label matches the annotators' and the cited abstract is one they marked as evidence. The decomposition uses the 188 test claims that have evidence (124 SUPPORT, 64 CONTRADICT). Pipeline: hybrid RRF, Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B on the top K, Qwen3-1.7B on the top k.

What the numbers say (reranker on, K 30, k 3)
  • Retrieval is not the problem here: the hybrid first stage has an evidence abstract in its top 30 for 186 of 188 claims (r = 0.989), and the reranker keeps it in the top 3 for 178 of those (p = 0.957). Only 10 claims are lost before the prompt.
  • The generator is: of the 178 claims with evidence in the prompt, it answers 96 correctly (g = 0.539), so A = 0.511. Even given only the gold abstracts it gets 112 of 188 (0.596): the ceiling for this model and prompt.
  • Why g is low: with abstracts in the prompt, Qwen3-1.7B never once answered CONTRADICT (it did twice in 300 closed-book answers). All 64 CONTRADICT claims are generation misses; on SUPPORT claims it is right 96 times of 124. A single accuracy number hides this; a confusion of labels shows it at once.
  • The reranker still pays: without it (first stage only, k 3) the evidence reaches the prompt for 171 claims instead of 178, and 90 claims are answered correctly instead of 96.
  • More abstracts are not better for a small model: with the reranker, g falls from 0.626 at k 1 to 0.539 at k 3 and 0.530 at k 5; the extra abstracts distract it.
  • Closed book (no abstracts), the model gets the label right on 45 of 188 claims and can cite nothing.
  • Lucky labels are rare with the citation check: 4 claims get the right label without evidence in the prompt; the check refuses them because the cited abstract is not evidence.
How the answer prompt was chosen (and why it matters)

The first prompt ("does the evidence SUPPORT the claim, CONTRADICT it, or is there NOT ENOUGH INFO") made the 1.7 B model answer NOT ENOUGH INFO almost always: given the gold abstracts it was right on 23 of the 188 test claims. Latency: what one query costs

Per-query time of each stage for one user at a time (batch 1), median over 40 SciFact claims and 3 repeats, measured on the Apple M1 Pro under the shared bench lock. measured

What the numbers say
  • Retrieval itself is nearly free at this size: BM25 0.25 ms, exact search over 5,183 vectors 0.51 ms, RRF 0.04 ms. Embedding the query with the 0.6 B Qwen3-Embedding costs 49 ms, a hundred times more.
  • The reranker dominates, and grows linearly with K: CE MiniLM 58 / 155 / 590 ms for K 10 / 30 / 100; bge-reranker-v2-m3 0.84 / 2.18 s for K 10 / 30; Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B 2.49 / 6.62 s. The most accurate reranker here is also 40 times slower than the small one.
  • Generation of a one-line answer from a 1,100-token prompt takes 0.74 s with the 1.7 B model (median of the 2,299 answers in the section above, prompts of median 1,126 tokens read at about 1,040 tokens/s); a long answer costs far more, at about 88 generated tokens per second.
  • What transfers: the order (reranker and generator dominate, the vector search is negligible until the corpus is millions of documents, see the Vector search lab) and the linear growth of rerank cost with K. Not the milliseconds: a datacenter GPU runs these models many times faster, and servers batch many users' queries. Load average during the runs 1.2 to 3.9.

Common mistakes, and interview questions

Comparing chunkers at a fixed number of chunks
Bigger chunks send more text, so they win at a fixed k almost by definition (512-token chunks: 61.4% at the top 3 with BM25). Compare at the same token budget, where they lose (45.9% at 1,024 tokens against 64.5% for paragraph-aware chunks).
Cutting on token counts and ignoring the document's structure
Fixed 256-token windows keep an evidence paragraph intact for 68.0% of QASPER questions; splitting on paragraphs first (the recursive rule, or paragraphs packed within sections) keeps 93.3 to 93.9%. Overlap is a weak patch: 32 tokens of overlap move fixed windows from 61.2% to 68.0%.
Assuming hybrid always helps
Equal-weight RRF helped every model whose quality is close to BM25's and hurt the two that beat BM25 clearly on SciFact (gte-modernbert 0.767 to 0.763, ColBERT-small 0.743 to 0.731). Measure on your data; if one retriever dominates, weight it.
Tuning the fusion weight, k, or the rerank depth on the test set
It looks like progress and is a leak. Hold out queries (SciFact train here) to pick the knob, then report the test score once.
Filtering after the search
With a selective filter the post-filter quietly returns fewer results or none (1.73 of 10 on average at 10% visibility, 14 empty answers of 300). Filter inside the search, or over-fetch and check how many survive.
Adding a reranker without checking it beats the first stage
The 22 M MiniLM cross-encoder trained on MS MARCO adds nothing to Qwen3-Embedding on SciFact (0.700 at K 100) and pulls the hybrid list down as K grows (0.724 to 0.689 at K 100). Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B on the top 30 lifts the same lists to 0.765 (dense) and 0.778 (hybrid).
Reporting one accuracy number
A label can be right without the evidence: on SciFact two thirds of the labelled claims are SUPPORT, so a model that always says SUPPORT scores 66% "accuracy". Check the citation too, and split the failures into retrieval, ranking and generation.

Interview questions, short answers

Users say the RAG system gives wrong answers. How do you find the broken stage?
Build a golden set of questions with the passages that answer them. For each, log the first stage's top K, the reranked top k and the answer. Count: evidence not in top K (retrieval), in K but not in k (ranking), in the prompt but answered wrong (generation). Fix the biggest bucket first; give the generator the gold passages (an oracle run) to get the ceiling of g.
Should we add BM25 to our vector search?
Probably, if queries carry identifiers, rare names or domain terms, and the dense model is not far ahead of BM25 on your data. Start with RRF (k 60, no tuning); if one retriever dominates, use a weighted fusion with the weight chosen on held-out queries. Measure per query wins and losses, not just the mean.
How do you choose a chunk size?
Split on the document's own structure first (sections, paragraphs), then cap the size. Measure on questions with located evidence: share of evidence intact in one chunk, and retrieval at an equal token budget, not at an equal number of chunks. Smaller chunks rank more precisely; bigger chunks carry more context per hit.
How many candidates should the reranker see?
Sweep K on a golden set against latency. The first stage's recall@K is the ceiling: a reranker cannot recover what it is not given. Past the point where recall@K flattens, extra K costs time and gives a weak reranker more chances to promote wrong documents.
Pre-filter or post-filter for access control?
Inside the search (pre-filter), always for access control: a post-filter can return fewer results than asked, or none, and its behaviour depends on how selective the filter is for that user. With an ANN index, check the engine's filtered-search strategy and measure recall with a restrictive filter.
Our reranker made nDCG worse. How is that possible?
It is weaker than the first stage on this domain (a small model trained on web search reordering a strong embedder's list), or it was given a deep list it then reorders badly. Check reranker-alone quality, and sweep K.

How this was measured, and the limits

  • Machine: Apple M1 Pro (8 performance and 2 efficiency cores, 16 GB unified memory), PyTorch MPS and llama.cpp (Metal), October 2026. Shared with other agents: every timing carries its load average in the scripts' records; the ratios between stages transfer to a server, the milliseconds do not (a datacenter GPU runs the encoders and rerankers many times faster, and servers batch concurrent queries).
  • Relevance numbers (nDCG@10, recall) are deterministic: pytrec_eval over the recorded top-100 runs; the same scripts reproduce them exactly. Generator outputs are greedy (temperature 0) and cached by prompt.
  • Running example and baselines: datasets, models, revisions and the baseline table against BEIR and MTEB are defined once for the whole page (the root's src/data/README.md); every baseline here matches it.
  • Scripts: src/pipe/ in the repository (fusion, filters, chunking, query rewriting, reranking, end to end, latency); make_data.py builds everything this tab draws from their outputs.
  • Generator: a 1.7 B model. Its g is far below a frontier model's; the shape (which factor dominates, what a reranker changes) transfers, the absolute accuracy does not.
  • Not run here: see the notes under each section.
Not run here, and why
  • Late chunking (Günther et al. 2024, arXiv 2409.04701: embed the whole document once, then pool the token vectors of each chunk): it needs a long-context embedder trained with mean pooling. The running example's long-context embedders pool differently (gte-modernbert uses the CLS token, Qwen3-Embedding the last token of a causal model), so a late-chunking run would measure a pooling the models were never trained for.
  • Generator-written context on every paper: about 30 s of generation per paper here, so it ran on every fourth paper (71 of 281); the comparison uses the same papers with and without the context.
  • A larger generator: the 4 B model files were missing from the shared model store while this tab's generator runs were scheduled (a temporary-file cleaner had removed them; they were restored later), so every answer here is the 1.7 B model through llama.cpp. A 4 B or frontier model would move g, not r or p.
  • Judged answer quality (faithfulness, groundedness with an LLM judge): SciFact's labels and evidence ids allow an exact check, and judged metrics belong to RAG and retrieval evaluation.
  • Filtered approximate search (HNSW with a restrictive filter): the Vector search lab.

Tool map

A retrieval system is a stack of separate choices: something that splits documents and wires the steps together (a framework), a model that turns text into vectors (an embedding model), a structure that finds the nearest vectors fast (an ANN index, approximate nearest neighbour), usually wrapped in a vector store that also keeps the text and metadata, a keyword engine (BM25), a second model that re-scores the shortlist (a reranker), and tools that evaluate the result. This tab is a dated atlas of the tools at each layer as of 13 October 2026: who owns each one, its licence, its latest release, whether it is alive, what algorithm it actually runs (read in source or docs, not from marketing pages), and when to choose it.

The one idea to keep
Most of the difference between two vector stores is not the algorithm (nearly all of them run HNSW, the graph index, or a cluster-based IVF index) but the defaults they ship, how they filter, whether hybrid search is built in, and who operates them. What it shows. Besides the reference (FAISS Flat), three tools are exact at their defaults: Qdrant's Python client in local mode and Milvus Lite never build an index (they scan), and LanceDB scans until you call create_index(); on a collection this small the Qdrant server scans too (box below). pgvector's HNSW with its default ef_search 40 sits with the other graph indexes. The graph indexes (Chroma, USearch, FAISS HNSW, hnswlib) are close but not exact, and the gap is the search-breadth knob: hnswlib ships ef = 10 and FAISS efSearch = 16, Chroma ef_search = 100. The cluster indexes lose most: FAISS IVF (72 clusters here, the common square-root-of-N choice; FAISS has no default for it) searches only the nearest cluster by default (nprobe 1), and LanceDB's default IVF_PQ compresses each vector, so the ranking inside the shortlist is approximate too. One knob each recovers nearly everything, which is why "we tried X and recall was bad" usually means "we did not set the knob".

Common mistake: benchmarking a mode that never uses the index
Even the Qdrant server scanned here: with defaults it does not build HNSW for a collection under indexing_threshold (10,000 KB), and with the graph built its planner still scans any segment under full_scan_threshold (10,000 KB), so all 5,183 vectors were searched exactly. Only with both lowered did the graph answer (0.996 at the default search breadth, 0.925 at hnsw_ef 16). Small test collections hide the index you will run in production. Qdrant's QdrantClient(":memory:") and Milvus Lite are for development. Both scan every vector, so their recall is perfect and their speed says nothing about the server's index. Milvus Lite even reports HNSW from describe_index after you ask for it, while returning flat-scan results (measured above in the atlas card). Benchmark the server you will run.

Reading MTEB and BEIR

Every embedding model is sold with a leaderboard number. Know what it is before trusting it. BEIR (2021) is 18 retrieval datasets used zero-shot: the model is tested on domains it was not trained for, scored by nDCG@10. MTEB (2022) widened this to 8 task types and 58 datasets, of which retrieval is one type; the headline is an average of each task's main score (nDCG@10 for retrieval). MMTEB (2025) grew it to more than 500 tasks in 250+ languages and added smaller benchmarks, among them the English benchmark the leaderboard shows today, MTEB(eng, v2): .

Why the headline misleads

  • It averages task types. Retrieval is 10 of the 41 tasks in MTEB(eng, v2); a model can climb on classification and clustering while being average at the retrieval you need. Read the retrieval column, or better, the tasks closest to your domain.
  • Many models trained on the test domains. MTEB records the training data each model declares; a task the model trained on is not zero-shot. The table below is computed here with mteb's own function: most 2024 to 2025 models declare FEVER and HotpotQA training data, whose hard-negative versions are 2 of the 10 retrieval tasks, so their retrieval average is only 80% zero-shot. (HotpotQA is also the Agentic lab's multi-hop set: with these models it is in-domain, not zero-shot.)
  • Versions are not comparable. OpenAI's 62.3% and 64.6% for text-embedding-3 are on the 2024 MTEB; Google's 68.x for gemini-embedding-001 is on its own table; today's leaderboard is MTEB(eng, v2). Put only numbers from the same benchmark version side by side.
  • Differences are often noise. Google's own table gives gemini-embedding-001 68.16 at 2048 dimensions and 68.17 at 1536: a smaller vector scoring higher is what noise looks like. A 0.5-point gap between two models is not a reason to switch.
  • Results are often submitted by the model's authors. The MTEB results repository holds runs by the MTEB team and runs supplied by model authors (the setup agent found bge-small's HotpotQA score only in an "external" folder). The running example reproduced bge-small's SciFact score exactly (nDCG@10 0.7127, measured here): reproduction is possible, so do it for the models you shortlist.
  • Your corpus is not the benchmark. The predict question above is the point: on SciFact the 33M model beats the 0.6B one, on NFCorpus it is the other way round, and a 149M model beats both on SciFact. Shortlist with the leaderboard, decide with a golden set of your own questions (the Pipeline lab builds one).

  • Not run here, and why. No hosted service (Pinecone, turbopuffer, OpenAI, Gemini, Voyage, Cohere): this topic runs locally with no paid APIs or keys, so their rows are from docs. No server-mode Qdrant, Milvus, Weaviate, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Vespa or Redis: Docker servers were left to the setup agent's confirmation, and the engine view of pgvector is measured on the Search and vector databases page. No timing at all on this tab: speed claims are the vendors', labelled.
  • How versions and dates were read. PyPI JSON API (upload time of the version's first file), GitHub REST API (release published_at; the Atom feeds were not used for dates because their "updated" field changes when a release note is edited: hnswlib v0.9.0 shows 2026-09-13 there but was published 2026-03-28), Hugging Face model API (licence tag, revision, parameters from safetensors metadata), arXiv API. Raw records are in src/map/inputs/; gen_data.py re-checks every timeline date against them.
  • Statuses. "active", "maintenance" and "superseded" quote the project or follow from a newer generation by the same owner; "quiet" means no release for many months with no statement, and is an inference. Re-check any tool before you depend on it: this atlas is a snapshot.
  • Licences. Read from PyPI metadata, the repository's LICENSE file and model cards. GitHub reports NOASSERTION for pgvector, Weaviate, Elasticsearch and Redis because their licence files are not a single standard text; the atlas states what the files say.

Interview questions

We moved from FAISS Flat to a vector database and answer quality dropped. What do you check first?
The defaults, in order: the search breadth (ef, ef_search, nprobe; hnswlib ships ef = 10, FAISS IVF nprobe = 1); the distance metric and normalisation (Chroma defaults to l2 when you pass your own vectors); compression without rescoring (an IVF_PQ index without a refine step returned 0.59 of the exact top 10 here); filters applied after the search (fewer than k results survive); and whether you benchmarked a local or "lite" mode that does not use the index at all. Measure ANN recall@10 against exact search on a sample, then retrieval recall on a golden set.
pgvector, Qdrant or Pinecone for a 20M-chunk, multi-tenant SaaS?
Ask where the data lives, how selective the tenant filter is, and who operates it. If tenants' documents already live in Postgres and the index fits one server's memory, pgvector keeps one transaction and one backup, with partitions per large tenant. If tenant filters are very selective or you need hybrid dense plus sparse in one query, Qdrant's in-graph filtering and Query API fit. If nobody can operate a database, Pinecone's namespaces per tenant. Then load a sample into the top two and measure recall at the real filter rate.
How do you choose an embedding model?
Shortlist on the retrieval tasks of MTEB closest to your domain, not the average; check the zero-shot share and the licence (several strong models are non-commercial); check context length against your chunk size and dimension against your memory budget (Matryoshka models let you shorten vectors); then measure nDCG@10 and recall@100 on a golden set of your own questions. Remember queries may need an instruction or prefix.
Is the top model on MTEB the best for us?
Not necessarily: the headline averages task types, many models trained on the benchmarks' training splits, and gaps under a point are within noise. On SciFact a 33M model beat a 0.6B one. Use the leaderboard to shortlist and your own data to decide.
When is GraphRAG worth its indexing cost?
When questions are about the whole corpus ("what are the main themes across these reports") and no single chunk answers them. GraphRAG pays LLM calls at indexing time to build and summarise an entity graph; LightRAG and FastGraphRAG cut that bill; LazyGraphRAG (Microsoft's products only) defers it to query time. For fact lookups, plain hybrid retrieval with a reranker is cheaper and as good; the Agentic lab measures a small case.
Hybrid search: rank fusion or score fusion?
Rank fusion (RRF) ignores the scores and only uses positions, so it needs no calibration between BM25 and cosine scores; Qdrant's Query API, LanceDB's default hybrid reranker and Elasticsearch's rrf retriever all implement it. Score fusion (Weaviate's default relative score fusion, OpenSearch's normalization processor) can use how confident each side is, but moves when the score distributions change. Start with RRF; tune the weights only with a golden set.

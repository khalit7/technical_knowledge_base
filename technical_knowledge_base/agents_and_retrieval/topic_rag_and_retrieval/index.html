The idea in one screen

Retrieval-augmented generation answers from the passages a search puts in the prompt, so an answer can only use the evidence that survives every stage between the corpus and the model; each stage is a separate choice, measured on its own.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): before a language model answers, a search system finds the few passages of a document collection that bear on the question and pastes them into the prompt, so the model answers from text it can read and cite rather than from memory.

One question's path, and the page that owns each stage

Boxes link to the child page that owns the stage. The numbers on the query path follow the 188 SciFact claims that have annotated evidence through one pipeline: BM25 and Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B fused by reciprocal rank fusion, Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B on the top 30, the top 3 abstracts to Qwen3-1.7B, which must give the right label and cite an evidence abstract (a deterministic check, no judge) measured on Apple M1 Pro. The first two stages almost never lose the evidence (186 of 188 in the top 30, 178 kept in the top 3); the generator gets 96 right. The bands under the path are the three ways to change who decides what is read, and what happens once users arrive.

You need little beyond a language model's prompt. The ranking scores used throughout are defined in one line each below; their formulas and confidence intervals are on Evaluation metrics, and judged answer quality (faithfulness, relevance) on RAG and retrieval evaluation. New terms

first stage: the search over the whole corpus that keeps a shortlist of K candidates.
reranker: a slower model that re-scores only the shortlist.
generator: the language model that writes the answer from the top k passages.
chunk: a piece of a document indexed on its own; the unit retrieval returns.
BM25: the standard keyword score over an inverted index (each term's list of documents).
bi-encoder: an embedding model that encodes query and document separately, so document vectors are computed ahead of time (dense retrieval).
cross-encoder: a model that reads query and document together and outputs one score; accurate, nothing to precompute.
hybrid search: BM25 and dense search run side by side and their lists merged, usually by reciprocal rank fusion (RRF: each list adds 1 / (60 + rank)).
ANN index: approximate nearest-neighbour search that reads a small part of the vectors and sometimes misses a true neighbour.
recall@K: the share of a question's relevant passages in the top K; with one relevant passage, "found or not".
nDCG@10: a 0 to 1 score of how high the relevant passages sit in the top 10 (each earns its grade divided by log2(rank + 1), normalised by the best possible order).
golden set: real questions, each with the passages a human marked as answering it.

The running example. Every page of this topic measures on the same public data, models and machine, so numbers can be compared across pages. The main set is SciFact: 5,183 scientific abstracts and 300 test claims (short statements such as "Activation of PPM1D suppresses p53 function"), each with the abstracts human annotators marked as evidence. NFCorpus (3,633 medical documents, many relevant per query), QASPER (281 long NLP papers, for cutting documents into pieces) and HotpotQA (200 questions that need two Wikipedia paragraphs each, over 9,760 paragraphs, for multi-step search) cover what SciFact cannot. Everything runs locally on an Apple M1 Pro laptop with open models (four embedding models, three rerankers, Qwen3 1.7B and 4B as the answering model), with no paid APIs. 1. Why retrieve at all

A language model's weights hold a compressed, lossy memory of its training text (its parametric memory): frozen at a cutoff date, with no record of where any fact came from. Its context window is different: whatever text is placed in the prompt is read now, exactly, and can be quoted. Retrieval moves knowledge from the first place to the second, and that buys four things, each measured in this topic:

What retrieval buysMeasured hereOwner

A rule in the prompt is not a security boundary.RAG in production, section 5

Why not fine-tune the facts in instead? Fine-tuning changes how a model behaves but is a poor way to add facts: it cannot be updated per document, cannot cite, cannot give two users different views, and still hallucinates on what it saw once. The two combine: fine-tune for format and domain style, retrieve for facts.

The corpus can matter more than the model (a vendor case, and why its number says little)

The old version of this page quoted OpenAI's Astra for Law (September 2026): GPT-6 Astra plus a legal index of more than 230 million URLs built on CourtListener, 54% against 38.7% for the same model with ordinary web search on the Vals AI Legal Research Bench vendor. The lesson stands, with two corrections from the Topic: benchmarks page's check (read 2026-10-04): the product also adds legal instructions and was scored on OpenAI's private validation set, so the index's own effect is not isolated; and Vals AI's public leaderboard lists GPT-6 Astra at 39.42% with its standard tooling. So a retrieval-sensitive score quoted without its index carries little information, and a vendor's own split of the gain is a claim, not a measurement.

2. Four ways to score a passage, and hybrids of them

Three distinctions only show across the children:

  • Words and vectors miss different things. BM25 finds exact names (identifiers, gene names, error codes) and fails on paraphrase: an abstract that says "lowers LDL" never matches "decrease cholesterol"; embeddings find the paraphrase. So hybrid search is the default first stage, but it is not always better: equal-weight RRF helps a dense model about as good as BM25 that disagrees with it (MiniLM 0.645 to 0.713, Qwen3-Embedding 0.700 to 0.724) and hurts the two that clearly beat BM25 (gte-modernbert 0.767 to 0.763, ColBERT-small 0.743 to 0.731): equal weight gives the weaker list the same say. A weight chosen on SciFact's training claims (z-score fusion) reaches 0.739 for Qwen3; picking it on the test claims would claim 0.744, a leak.
  • A reranker helps only when it is stronger than the first stage. Qwen3-Reranker-0.6B lifts the fused list from 0.724 to 0.782 at K 20; the small MiniLM cross-encoder, trained on web search, pulls it down to 0.689 at K 100. Its price is linear in K: at K 30, 155 ms for MiniLM and 6,618 ms for Qwen3-Reranker on this laptop's GPU, batch 1.
  • Model size is not a ranking; your data decides. The 149 M gte-modernbert beats the 0.6 B Qwen3-Embedding on SciFact (0.767 against 0.700), Qwen3 wins NFCorpus, and bge-small (33 M) wins the multi-hop set; every embedding model here lands within 0.005 of its published MTEB number on SciFact and NFCorpus. Shortlist on the retrieval tasks closest to your domain, then measure on a few hundred of your own labelled queries. Scores are not probabilities and not comparable across models, so any threshold is tuned per model.

3. The stages, and the one lesson each taught here

A production pipeline runs in two halves. At index time, before any question: parse documents, cut them into chunks, attach metadata, write a keyword index and a vector index. At query time: rewrite the question if it needs it, search both indexes with the filters inside each search, fuse the two lists, rerank the top of the fused list, and put the best few chunks, with their ids, into the prompt. Most knobs move a result by a point or two; the table keeps the one measured lesson per stage that changes a design.

4. Where an answer gets lost, and the two recalls

A question is answered correctly for the right reason only if its evidence survives three stages in a row, so end-to-end accuracy factors into three shares, each measured apart and each with its own fix:

A = rK × pk|K × g = 0.989 × 0.957 × 0.539 = 0.511

rK: share whose evidence is in the first stage's top K (here K = 30: 186 / 188), fixed by chunking, hybrid search, rewriting or a larger K. pk|K: of those, share the reranker keeps in the top k that reach the prompt (k = 3: 178 / 186), fixed by a better reranker or a larger k. g: of those, share the generator answers correctly and cites (96 / 178), fixed by the model, the prompt and the context format. On this run the generation bucket is 82 of 92 failures: no retrieval change can buy more than 10 answers, while the oracle run (gold abstracts only, 112 of 188) shows how much a better prompt or model could. One accuracy number (96 of 188) would hide that every one of the 59 CONTRADICT claims that reached the prompt was answered wrongly: the 1.7 B model never said CONTRADICT with abstracts in front of it. To debug one bad answer, ask first "was the evidence in the prompt?", then "was it in the top K?". The method, the golden set (a few hundred real labelled questions, knobs chosen on a tuning split: here 809 training claims, 300 test claims reported once) and the citation check are The retrieval pipeline section 9.

Two recalls, often confused. ANN recall asks how much of the exact top 10 an index returned: it needs no labels and measures only the index. Retrieval recall asks how many labelled relevant documents are in the top 10: it measures the embedding model first and the index second. The index loses relevant abstracts at about the rate it loses any neighbour (IVF with 8 cells: ANN 0.901, retrieval 0.779 against 0.761 expected from 0.901 × 0.845) measured, and a perfect index cannot lift retrieval above the model's 0.845 (exact search finds a labelled abstract in the top 10 for 84.5% of claims). Tune the index on the first; judge the system on the second.

5. Who decides what to read

Everything above is a fixed pipeline: the engineer decided in advance to search once, with the user's words, and to pass the top k on. Naive RAG embeds the question, takes the top k and generates; advanced RAG adds the designer's rewrite, hybrid, filter and rerank stages; agentic retrieval lets the model decide at run time, with search as a tool it may call zero or more times with queries it writes itself (the reason-then-act loop of ReAct; "deep research" products are the same loop with a budget of minutes). Why long context does not replace retrieval: every query pays for every token. With illustrative prices of $3 per million input tokens, $0.30 cached and $15 output, a 1-million-token corpus, a 500-token question and a 500-token answer, RAG with 10 chunks of 400 tokens costs $0.021; the whole corpus in the window $3.009 uncached (143 times) and $0.309 cached (15 times) derived. Prefill alone takes seconds (Qwen3-1.7B needs 9.3 s for 7,611 prompt tokens here). Long context wins for a small corpus read once; retrieval wins on cost, latency, scale beyond the window and per-user access control, and the two compose: retrieve, then read generously. The shape transfers to frontier models (loops pay only when the model can plan searches); the absolute F1 of small local models does not. KV-cache sizing and effective context are Long context.

6. After launch

Once users arrive the system is judged on what no notebook evaluation measures, and every fix that holds lives in the index and the search, not the prompt. One line each; RAG in production owns them all.

ConcernThe lesson, measured
FreshnessThe lag from a source change to a searchable chunk is a sum (detect, queue, parse, embed, write, visible), and the slowest term is usually not the one people tune. How documents are chunked decides what a revision costs: on ten revised arXiv papers whose revisions changed 15.0% of the words, fixed 200-word windows re-embedded 75.8% of the words, structure-aware chunks 49.2%.
Model upgradesThe vectors of two models cannot share an index even at the same dimension: halfway through a backfill from MiniLM to bge-small, nDCG@10 falls to 0.374 to 0.379 from 0.645 and 0.713. Build the new index beside the old one, then switch.
Access and tenantsFilter by tenant and permission inside the search (section 1), then check the engine's plans: with prepared statements PostgreSQL reused one generic HNSW plan across users and a user allowed 2.1% of the corpus got 0.96 rows of 10.
LatencyTime the whole request, under the real sharing of hardware: embedding the query took 49 ms alone and 428 ms inside the full request, because three models share one GPU; the whole request 2,981 ms, of which 2,114 ms the generator's first token.
CachesA prompt (prefix) cache is safe and large (4,149 ms for the first call, 73 ms after); a semantic answer cache breaks on near-duplicates with opposite meanings: at a threshold of 0.95, 57% of hits returned the opposite claim's answer.
Cost$0.0012 per query at list prices for a hosted stack (1 million queries a month), generation 62% and reranking 36%: the reranker reads 30 passages where the generator reads 5.
InjectionRetrieved text is read as text: an instruction planted in a public document was followed in 6 of 6 cases with a plain prompt. Defences are Agent security's.

7. The tools

A retrieval system is a stack of separate choices: a parser, a framework or your own code to chunk and wire the steps, an embedding model, an ANN index inside a vector store (or a library such as FAISS), a keyword engine, a reranker and an evaluation tool. A vector store wraps an index with storage, metadata and filtering. The Tool map is a dated atlas of each layer (owner, licence, latest release, status, the algorithm it actually runs, when to choose it), a "which store" chooser and a timeline. Its measured lesson: the defaults differ more than the algorithms. Given the same SciFact bge-small vectors and nothing changed, hnswlib (ef 10) returns 0.876 of the exact top 10, FAISS IVF (nprobe 1) 0.420, LanceDB's default IVF_PQ index about 0.59, Chroma 0.996 to 0.998, and Qdrant's local mode and Milvus Lite 1.000 because they never build an index; one knob each recovers nearly everything. Framework defaults bite the same way: LlamaIndex returns 2 chunks unless told otherwise, and LangChain's text splitter cuts 4,000 characters while MiniLM reads only its first 256 tokens.

In one line each: pgvector when the data already lives in PostgreSQL and fits one server; Qdrant, Weaviate or Milvus as dedicated engines with in-graph filtering and built-in hybrid search; Elasticsearch or OpenSearch when BM25 is already there; FAISS as a library for experiments and exact baselines; a managed service when nobody can operate a database. For models, shortlist open ones you can measure locally (Qwen3-Embedding, bge, gte) against hosted ones (OpenAI, Gemini, Voyage, Cohere) on your own golden set; for evaluation, ranx or pytrec_eval for retrieval metrics, and RAGAS, DeepEval or TruLens for judged ones, by way of RAG and retrieval evaluation.

Take the running example's real vectors: the 5,183 SciFact abstracts and the 300 test questions, each turned into a 384-number vector by bge-small-en-v1.5. What it shows. Besides the reference (FAISS Flat), three tools are exact at their defaults: Qdrant's Python client in local mode and Milvus Lite never build an index (they scan), and LanceDB scans until you call create_index(); on a collection this small the Qdrant server scans too (box below). pgvector's HNSW with its default ef_search 40 sits with the other graph indexes. The graph indexes (Chroma, USearch, FAISS HNSW, hnswlib) are close but not exact, and the gap is the search-breadth knob: hnswlib ships ef = 10 and FAISS efSearch = 16, Chroma ef_search = 100. The cluster indexes lose most: FAISS IVF (72 clusters here, the common square-root-of-N choice; FAISS has no default for it) searches only the nearest cluster by default (nprobe 1), and LanceDB's default IVF_PQ compresses each vector, so the ranking inside the shortlist is approximate too. One knob each recovers nearly everything, which is why "we tried X and recall was bad" usually means "we did not set the knob".

Common mistake: benchmarking a mode that never uses the index
Even the Qdrant server scanned here: with defaults it does not build HNSW for a collection under indexing_threshold (10,000 KB), and with the graph built its planner still scans any segment under full_scan_threshold (10,000 KB), so all 5,183 vectors were searched exactly. Only with both lowered did the graph answer (0.996 at the default search breadth, 0.925 at hnsw_ef 16). Small test collections hide the index you will run in production. Qdrant's QdrantClient(":memory:") and Milvus Lite are for development. Both scan every vector, so their recall is perfect and their speed says nothing about the server's index. Milvus Lite even reports HNSW from describe_index after you ask for it, while returning flat-scan results (measured above in the atlas card). Benchmark the server you will run.

Reading MTEB and BEIR

Every embedding model is sold with a leaderboard number. Know what it is before trusting it. BEIR (2021) is 18 retrieval datasets used zero-shot: the model is tested on domains it was not trained for, scored by nDCG@10. MTEB (2022) widened this to 8 task types and 58 datasets, of which retrieval is one type; the headline is an average of each task's main score (nDCG@10 for retrieval). MMTEB (2025) grew it to more than 500 tasks in 250+ languages and added smaller benchmarks, among them the English benchmark the leaderboard shows today, MTEB(eng, v2): .

Predict: on SciFact (the running example), how does Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B (596M parameters, June 2025) compare with bge-small-en-v1.5 (33M parameters, 2023)?

Why the headline misleads

  • It averages task types. Retrieval is 10 of the 41 tasks in MTEB(eng, v2); a model can climb on classification and clustering while being average at the retrieval you need. Read the retrieval column, or better, the tasks closest to your domain.
  • Many models trained on the test domains. MTEB records the training data each model declares; a task the model trained on is not zero-shot. The table below is computed here with mteb's own function: most 2024 to 2025 models declare FEVER and HotpotQA training data, whose hard-negative versions are 2 of the 10 retrieval tasks, so their retrieval average is only 80% zero-shot. (HotpotQA is also the topic's multi-hop set, used on Agentic retrieval, long context and memory: with these models it is in-domain, not zero-shot.)
  • Versions are not comparable. OpenAI's 62.3% and 64.6% for text-embedding-3 are on the 2024 MTEB; Google's 68.x for gemini-embedding-001 is on its own table; today's leaderboard is MTEB(eng, v2). Put only numbers from the same benchmark version side by side.
  • Differences are often noise. Google's own table gives gemini-embedding-001 68.16 at 2048 dimensions and 68.17 at 1536: a smaller vector scoring higher is what noise looks like. A 0.5-point gap between two models is not a reason to switch.
  • Results are often submitted by the model's authors. The MTEB results repository holds runs by the MTEB team and runs supplied by model authors (the setup agent found bge-small's HotpotQA score only in an "external" folder). The running example reproduced bge-small's SciFact score exactly (nDCG@10 0.7127, measured here): reproduction is possible, so do it for the models you shortlist.
  • Your corpus is not the benchmark. The predict question above is the point: on SciFact the 33M model beats the 0.6B one, on NFCorpus it is the other way round, and a 149M model beats both on SciFact. Shortlist with the leaderboard, decide with a golden set of your own questions (section 9 of The retrieval pipeline builds one).

Interview questions

We moved from FAISS Flat to a vector database and answer quality dropped. What do you check first?
The defaults, in order: the search breadth (ef, ef_search, nprobe; hnswlib ships ef = 10, FAISS IVF nprobe = 1); the distance metric and normalisation (Chroma defaults to l2 when you pass your own vectors); compression without rescoring (an IVF_PQ index without a refine step returned 0.59 of the exact top 10 here); filters applied after the search (fewer than k results survive); and whether you benchmarked a local or "lite" mode that does not use the index at all. Measure ANN recall@10 against exact search on a sample, then retrieval recall on a golden set.
pgvector, Qdrant or Pinecone for a 20M-chunk, multi-tenant SaaS?
Ask where the data lives, how selective the tenant filter is, and who operates it. If tenants' documents already live in Postgres and the index fits one server's memory, pgvector keeps one transaction and one backup, with partitions per large tenant. If tenant filters are very selective or you need hybrid dense plus sparse in one query, Qdrant's in-graph filtering and Query API fit. If nobody can operate a database, Pinecone's namespaces per tenant. Then load a sample into the top two and measure recall at the real filter rate.
How do you choose an embedding model?
Shortlist on the retrieval tasks of MTEB closest to your domain, not the average; check the zero-shot share and the licence (several strong models are non-commercial); check context length against your chunk size and dimension against your memory budget (Matryoshka models let you shorten vectors); then measure nDCG@10 and recall@100 on a golden set of your own questions. Remember queries may need an instruction or prefix.
Is the top model on MTEB the best for us?
Not necessarily: the headline averages task types, many models trained on the benchmarks' training splits, and gaps under a point are within noise. On SciFact a 33M model beat a 0.6B one. Use the leaderboard to shortlist and your own data to decide.
When is GraphRAG worth its indexing cost?
When questions are about the whole corpus ("what are the main themes across these reports") and no single chunk answers them. GraphRAG pays LLM calls at indexing time to build and summarise an entity graph; LightRAG and FastGraphRAG cut that bill; LazyGraphRAG (Microsoft's products only) defers it to query time. For fact lookups, plain hybrid retrieval with a reranker is cheaper and as good; GraphRAG and structured retrieval measures when a graph pays.
Hybrid search: rank fusion or score fusion?
Rank fusion (RRF) ignores the scores and only uses positions, so it needs no calibration between BM25 and cosine scores; Qdrant's Query API, LanceDB's default hybrid reranker and Elasticsearch's rrf retriever all implement it. Score fusion (Weaviate's default relative score fusion, OpenSearch's normalization processor) can use how confident each side is, but moves when the score distributions change. Start with RRF; tune the weights only with a golden set.

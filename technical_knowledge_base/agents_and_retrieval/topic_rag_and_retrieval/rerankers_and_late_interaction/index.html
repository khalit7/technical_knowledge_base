1. Why a second stage

A first stage looks at every document for every query, so it can only afford a cheap comparison. A bi-encoder encodes query and document separately: documents once, offline; a query costs one pass plus N dot products. Its weakness is the same fact: the document vector was fixed before the query existed, so about a thousand numbers must summarise everything the document might be asked. BM25 is cheaper still and only counts shared words. A cross-encoder puts query and document in one input, so every query token attends to every document token in every layer: it sees "MDA5" in the claim next to "RIG-I, MDA5, and LGP2" in the abstract, rather than two summaries. Nothing can be precomputed, so it runs only on the top K. Step through one real pair both ways:

2. Cross-encoders

A BERT-style cross-encoder takes one sequence, [CLS] query [SEP] document [SEP], with a segment id of 0 on the query and 1 on the document, runs the encoder (6 layers for MiniLM-L6, 24 for bge-reranker-v2-m3), and maps the final [CLS] vector through dense, tanh and one linear layer to one number, the relevance logit (unbounded; higher is more relevant). This is the 2019 recipe of Nogueira and Cho ("the [CLS] vector as input to a single layer neural network", query cut to 64 tokens, pair to 512). Reranking BM25's top 1,000 on MS MARCO, it moved dev MRR@10 from 16.7 (BM25) to 36.5 (BERT Large) (Table 1; published, independent academic, 2019).

What MiniLM reads for claim 1180: one sequence, the claim in segment 0

The first 60 of 231 tokens; segment 0 in blue. SciFact pairs average 354 tokens, and 12% hit the 512 cap and silently lose the end of the abstract; Qwen3-Reranker's 1,024 cap cuts 0.5%. With long chunks, rerank shorter chunks or use a long-context reranker.

Same call, different numbers. sentence-transformers' CrossEncoder.predict applies the activation the model's config names: ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2 sets Identity, so it returns raw logits (here from -11.5 to 10.1); bge-reranker-v2-m3 names none and has one label, so the library applies Sigmoid and returns 0 to 1; a decoder LLM such as Qwen3-Reranker returns logit("yes") - logit("no") at the last position (section 7). Three scales from one function: never compare raw scores across models (section 6). predict also sorts pairs by length before batching, so measured pairs per second depend on it.

3. How rerankers are trained: labels, hard negatives, distillation

Almost every open English reranker starts from MS MARCO passage ranking (8.8 M Bing passages, about one marked relevant passage per question), so many "negatives" are relevant passages nobody marked (false negatives). Three training signals, in the order they appeared:

  1. Pointwise labels: binary cross-entropy on each (query, passage, 0 or 1), as Nogueira and Cho did.
  2. Hard negatives: negatives from a retriever's top results instead of at random. A cardiology abstract is trivially irrelevant to a claim about MDA5; the abstract about RIG-I signalling that mentions MDA5 but does not support the claim is what the model must learn to rank second. The risk: a mined "negative" may be an unlabelled positive.
  3. Distillation: a small student learns a strong teacher's scores instead of 0/1 labels, on many unlabelled pairs, which also smooths over missing labels (the teacher says "this unmarked passage is quite relevant"). The common loss is Margin-MSE (Hofstätter et al., 2020): the student's margin between a positive and a negative should equal the teacher's, so it learns relative judgements without the teacher's scale.
LMargin-MSE = ( [sstudent(q, d+) − sstudent(q, d−)] − [steacher(q, d+) − steacher(q, d−)] )2

4. Where the reranker goes, and how deep

The reranker sits after the first stage (BM25, vectors, or their fusion) and before prompt assembly: corpus (5,183) → first stage → top K (10 to 100) → reranker → top k (3 to 10) → LLM. It sees only the top K. Too small a K and the relevant abstract never reaches it; too large and you pay for pairs that almost never matter and give a weak reranker more chances to promote a wrong abstract. Two curves decide K: the first stage's recall@K (the ceiling: 0.862 at K = 10 and 0.921 at K = 30 for the hybrid list), and the reranker's quality as K grows.

Predict: the hybrid list (BM25 and Qwen3-Embedding fused) scores 0.724 nDCG@10 on its own. You rerank its top 100 with the small MiniLM cross-encoder, which lifts BM25 from 0.679 to 0.688. What happens?
It falls, to 0.689. The cross-encoder is a weaker judge of SciFact than the fused list it reorders: the deeper it reranks, the more wrong abstracts it promotes (0.725 at K = 3, 0.702 at K = 30, 0.689 at K = 100). Qwen3-Reranker on the same list reaches 0.783. A reranker is a model with its own errors, not a free improvement.

How the six claims were chosen (fixed rules, not by hand)

Written in e_examples.py: the first claim in id order where Qwen3-Reranker rescues a relevant abstract from BM25 rank 10 or lower, where it demotes BM25's correct first result, where MiniLM and Qwen3-Reranker disagree, where no first stage finds anything, one with three or more relevant abstracts, and claim 1180.

How to choose K on your data. Plot your first stage's recall@K on a labelled sample (a few hundred queries is enough), take the smallest K where it flattens, measure the reranker's quality at a few K around it, and check the latency at that K on your hardware (Depth chooser tab). If recall@K is low, fix retrieval (hybrid, query rewriting, chunking), not the reranker. 5. Evaluating a reranker, and timing it

Everything above is one evaluation, and the same recipe works on any corpus:

  1. Freeze the first stage and score it alone: nDCG@10 and recall@K on a labelled sample from your own data. Recall@K is the reranker's ceiling.
  2. Rerank at the K you will use and compare with the first stage alone, not with nothing. A reranker can lower quality (MiniLM on the hybrid list: 0.724 to 0.689).
  3. Count wins and losses per query, not only the mean (68 better and 34 worse for Qwen3-Reranker above), and read a few losses.
  4. Time a whole query's batch, all K pairs together, several repeats, under realistic load. Per-query latency depends on batching, padding and length sorting: timing one pair and multiplying by K is wrong both ways (section 1's predict box: 6.0 s predicted, 7.5 s measured).
  5. Check the scores you will threshold on target data (section 6), and the instruction or template the reranker expects (section 7).

Sparse labels make every number here a lower bound: an unlabelled relevant abstract promoted to first counts as a miss. Judged answer metrics (faithfulness, groundedness) belong to RAG and retrieval evaluation; nDCG, MRR and AUROC are defined on Evaluation metrics.

6. What a reranker score means: calibration, thresholds, abstaining

A RAG system often has to decide whether anything relevant was found, to say "I could not find it" instead of answering from the wrong abstract. A threshold on the reranker score needs the score to mean the same thing across queries. The three models here return three quantities (MiniLM a raw logit, bge a sigmoid, Qwen3 log-odds of "yes" over "no"); mapped onto one logit scale (bge: log(p / (1 - p))), their distributions on the same 15,076 pairs are:

Relevant and unlabelled pairs overlap, and the raw top scores are over-confident

Pool: each test claim's BM25 top 30 plus Qwen3-Embedding top 30 (15,076 pairs, 323 labelled relevant). "Unlabelled" includes some relevant abstracts nobody marked. measured here (e_scores.py)

Calibration means that among pairs scored 0.7, about 70% are relevant. Of the pairs whose raw sigmoid is above 0.9, only 35%, 66% and 57% are labelled relevant (MiniLM, bge, Qwen3; the truth is somewhat higher because of missing labels). Thresholds do not transfer either: the same MiniLM puts relevant pairs at a median logit of 2.6 on SciFact and -1.1 on NFCorpus (medical questions, graded labels); 69% against 41% score above 0. Recalibrate on a labelled sample of the target data.

Expected calibration error before and after Platt scaling (measured)

Reading each logit through a sigmoid, the expected calibration error (ECE: the average gap between predicted and observed relevance over ten score bins) is 0.070, 0.042 and 0.040; after Platt scaling (fitting p = sigmoid(a · score + b) on half the claims, testing on the other half) it drops to 0.011, 0.002 and 0.002. Those ECEs look small only because 2.1% of the pairs are relevant; the top bin above is the real story.

Abstaining when nothing relevant was retrieved

A constructed test: each claim is ranked twice, once with its candidate pool as is (285 claims have a relevant abstract in it) and once with every relevant abstract removed, so the best remaining candidate is a near miss. A good "nothing here" signal gives the top candidate a lower score in the second case; AUROC is the chance that a random answerable claim's top score beats a random unanswerable one's. Predict: is the reranker's top score a better signal than the retriever's cosine?

A strong reranker's top score is the best "nothing here" signal, and still far from clean

AUROC: Qwen3-Reranker 0.80, MiniLM 0.71, cosine 0.72, BM25 0.66. Thresholds on each model's logit scale. measured here (e_scores.py)

The large rerankers beat the first stage's scores; the small MiniLM is no better than the cosine. At a threshold that keeps 90% of answerable claims, 65% of the "nothing relevant" claims are still answered (Qwen3-Reranker). Use the score as one input to abstaining, together with a check that the passage supports the answer (groundedness: RAG and retrieval evaluation).

7. LLM rerankers: pointwise, pairwise, listwise

A decoder language model can rerank in three ways, which differ in what one call sees:

Pointwise: one call per candidate, "relevant? yes or no", read P(yes). Independent scores: parallel calls, thresholdable. K calls.
Pairwise: "which of A and B is more relevant?" inside a sort. Robust to score scale, but all pairs is K(K-1) calls; a sliding bubble sort is about 2 × passes × K with both orders asked.
Listwise: one call sees many candidates and writes "[3] > [1] > [2]". Sees the competition; long prompts, output to parse, position bias; long lists go through a sliding window. A few long calls.

Pointwise done properly: Qwen3-Reranker. A Qwen3 decoder fine-tuned to answer one question in a fixed chat template, with an empty thinking block pre-filled so the very next token is the answer. The score is the difference of two next-token logits, because a softmax restricted to the two tokens gives

P(yes) = ey / (ey + en) = sigmoid(y − n),   score = logit("yes") − logit("no")

so the stored score is the log-odds of "yes" against "no". The idea is monoT5's (Nogueira et al., 2020), which ranked by the softmax over only "true" and "false". Load it as its model card says (sentence-transformers CrossEncoder with prompts): a raw (query, document) pair through a generic causal-LM path, without the instruction and template, gives different scores.

8. Late interaction: ColBERT and MaxSim

A bi-encoder squeezes a document into one vector; a cross-encoder precomputes nothing. Late interaction keeps the document side precomputable but much richer: the encoder outputs one small vector per token, stored offline, and query and document meet only at the end, through a cheap score. ColBERT (Khattab and Zaharia, 2020) defines it as MaxSim: each query token takes its highest dot product with any document token, and the maxima are added up. On claim 1180, "md ##a ##5" finds the same three word pieces in the abstract (similarities 0.96, 0.97 and 0.96), "sensor" finds "sensing" (0.92), and "rna", "virus" and "infection" find themselves. Single vector: Qwen3-Embedding's cosine. Similarities are cosines from the stored float16 vectors; re-encoding on CPU in float32 reproduces them (minimum cosine between the two encodings 0.9999). The ranking table is the claim's Qwen3-Embedding top 10 rescored both ways. measured here (e_maxsim_pair.py)

score(q, d) = ∑i ∈ query tokens maxj ∈ document tokens qi · dj

Query augmentation. Queries start [CLS] [Q] and documents [CLS] [D] (unused vocabulary slots), so one encoder embeds both differently, and queries are padded with [MASK] to a fixed 32 tokens whose vectors are scored like real ones: a learned soft expansion. SciFact claims have 22.7 real tokens on average, so about 29% of each query is [MASK]. They carry 30% of the top abstract's score, but nearly the same amount for every abstract (they all match the document's [CLS] at about 0.97), so MaxSim totals sit close together (30.25 to 30.91 for claim 1180's top 10) and the real tokens decide the order: dropping the masks at search time moves nDCG@10 only from 0.744 to 0.739. Punctuation vectors are dropped from documents (a skiplist), so an abstract stores fewer vectors than it has tokens.

9. PLAID: compressing and searching token vectors

One vector per token multiplies storage by the tokens per document (236 here), and exhaustive MaxSim reads all of them. ColBERTv2 stores each token vector as the id of its nearest k-means centroid plus the residual (vector minus centroid) quantised to 1 or 2 bits per dimension: "this total of 20 or 36 bytes per vector" at 128 dimensions against 256 in float16, a 6 to 10 times smaller index. PLAID then searches without decompressing everything, the same IVF idea as Vector indexes, applied per token:

  1. each query token picks its nearest centroids; documents with tokens in them become candidates;
  2. candidates are scored with centroid interaction: MaxSim with every token replaced by its centroid;
  3. centroids that score low for every query token are pruned, and the survivors rescored;
  4. only the best few are decompressed and scored exactly.

PLAID's Table 3 reports 39.8 MRR@10 on MS MARCO at k = 100 in 222 ms on one CPU core and 20 ms on a GPU, against 4,569 ms on 8 cores for vanilla ColBERTv2 (published, academic, 2022). Measured here with fast-plaid 1.4.6 (the engine pylate uses) on the stored SciFact vectors, CPU, seed 42 (e_plaid.py). Predict: which knob matters on 5,183 abstracts, centroids probed or candidates fully scored?

nDCG@10 is 0.740 at 2 bits and 0.742 at 4 bits against 0.744 exhaustive, though only 89% and 95% of the exact top 10 are reproduced exactly. With only 32 candidates fully scored, nDCG@10 drops to 0.729 and 66% of the exact top 10 survive; from 256 on it is back. Centroids probed barely matter here: one centroid per query token, times 32 query tokens, already reaches most relevant candidates; on millions of passages it matters. Storage per token falls from 192 bytes to 24 bytes of residual codes plus a centroid id: 47.7 MB for the corpus with centroids and inverted lists, 4.9 times less than float16. At scale, never store ColBERT vectors uncompressed: use residual compression or token pooling and check the quality cost on your data. Page-image patches (ColPali) use the same machinery: Document ingestion and multimodal retrieval.

On-disk details of the fast-plaid index (measured)

fast-plaid stores the centroid id as an 8-byte integer (ColBERTv2's paper counts 4 bytes), and keeps a merged second copy of codes and residuals for search, so its folder is 86.8 MB. On 5,183 abstracts none of this is needed (exhaustive MaxSim is already fast); PLAID is for millions of passages.

10. Learned sparse retrieval: SPLADE

The other middle point keeps BM25's machinery, an inverted index and a score summed over shared terms, but lets a model choose the terms and their weights. SPLADE (Formal, Piwowarski and Clinchant, SIGIR 2021) runs BERT's masked-language-model head over every input position i, which scores all 30,522 vocabulary entries j; the text's weight for term j is the largest log-saturated score over positions. Terms the text lacks get weight too: expansion terms, words a reader would expect. Here is a real document vector:

A sparse vector is a weighted bag of words, including words the abstract never uses

The 20 heaviest of the 239 non-zero terms the model wrote for SciFact abstract 4983 ("Microstructural development of human newborn cerebral white matter assessed in vivo by diffusion tensor..."); orange: the 2 expansion terms that appear nowhere in its text ("baby", "fibre"); also "infants" beside "infant", "fibers" beside "fiber". Model: opensearch-neural-sparse-encoding-doc-v3-distill (67 M, Apache 2.0) through sentence-transformers' SparseEncoder, 42 documents per second on MPS. measured on the Retrieval pipeline page (its src/exp/sparse.py, 2026-10-14), copied to src/inputs/sparse_rp.json, not rerun here.

wij = transform(hi)T Ej + bj,   wj = maxi log(1 + ReLU(wij)),   score(q, d) = ∑j wj(q) · wj(d)

The log saturates so no term dominates; ReLU zeroes most of the vocabulary. SPLADE v1 summed over positions; v2 (Formal et al., 2021) switched to the max, "almost 2 points" better on MS MARCO and TREC. Sparsity is trained, not hoped for: the FLOPS regulariser penalises each term's average weight over the batch, squared, which pushes down terms that are active in many documents and keeps posting lists short and even, with separate strengths for queries (kept sparser) and documents:

ℓFLOPS = ∑j ∈ V ( (1/N) ∑i=1N wj(di) )2,   L = Lrank + λq ℓq + λd ℓd

An inference-free variant, the model above, runs the network on documents only; a query is tokenised and each word gets a fixed weight from a table, so a query costs a tokeniser call, like BM25. On SciFact it scores 0.708 nDCG@10 against 0.679 for BM25 and 0.700 for Qwen3-Embedding, and fused with Qwen3-Embedding by RRF 0.724, level with BM25 + Qwen3 (0.724). A document keeps 239 non-zero terms on average and a query 20, so the index stays an inverted index, about 1,910 bytes per abstract before compression (derived: 239 × (4-byte term id + 4-byte weight)), between the single vector and ColBERT. Caveat: the model card lists SciFact and NFCorpus among its training sets, so these scores are in-domain and flatter it against BM25 and the dense models.

Published (academic): SPLADE v1 reached MS MARCO dev MRR@10 0.322 against 0.184 for BM25 and 0.277 for doc2query-T5, close to dense ANCE (0.330), at an estimated 0.73 FLOPS per query-document pair against 0.13 for BM25 (Table 1, 2021). Distilled from a cross-encoder with Margin-MSE (section 3) on its own harder negatives, DistilSPLADE-max reached 0.368 (SPLADE v2, Table 1) and a BEIR zero-shot average nDCG@10 of 0.506 against 0.456 for BM25 and 0.457 for ColBERT (Table 2; SciFact 0.693 against 0.665 for BM25). Learned sparse is a first-stage retriever, or a third retriever beside BM25 and dense; how the lists are fused belongs to The retrieval pipeline, and pgvector's sparsevec to Search and vector databases.

More on learned sparse (published)

SPLADE v1, Table 1: a pure-lexical SparTerm trained with the same pipeline reached 0.290; with expansion but no log-saturation (ST exp-ℓFLOPS) 0.312 at 2.83 FLOPS, so the log is what buys the sparsity. A strongly regularised model kept 0.296 MRR@10 at 0.05 FLOPS, with 18 non-zero values per document and 6 per query on average and an index under 1.4 GB for MS MARCO's 8.8 M passages. The paper's Table 2 example adds "leg" to a passage about "legs" (an implicit stemmer) and "treatment" (a topic word). The inference-free model above: average nDCG@10 0.517 on 13 BEIR sets against 0.524 for the larger query-and-document v1 model (published, vendor, OpenSearch model card, as quoted by the Retrieval pipeline page). BGE-M3 (BAAI) gives dense, sparse and multi-vector outputs from one model; not run here.

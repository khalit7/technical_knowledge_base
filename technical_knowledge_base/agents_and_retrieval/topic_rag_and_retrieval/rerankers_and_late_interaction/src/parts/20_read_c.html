4. Where the reranker goes, and how deep

The reranker sits after the first stage (BM25, vectors, or their fusion) and before prompt assembly: corpus (5,183) → first stage → top K (10 to 100) → reranker → top k (3 to 10) → LLM. It sees only the top K. Too small a K and the relevant abstract never reaches it; too large and you pay for pairs that almost never matter and give a weak reranker more chances to promote a wrong abstract. Two curves decide K: the first stage's recall@K (the ceiling: @@hy_rec10@@ at K = 10 and @@hy_rec30@@ at K = 30 for the hybrid list), and the reranker's quality as K grows.

Predict: the hybrid list (BM25 and Qwen3-Embedding fused) scores @@hy_ndcg@@ nDCG@10 on its own. You rerank its top 100 with the small MiniLM cross-encoder, which lifts BM25 from @@bm25_ndcg@@ to @@bm25_ce100@@. What happens?
It falls, to @@hy_ce100@@. The cross-encoder is a weaker judge of SciFact than the fused list it reorders: the deeper it reranks, the more wrong abstracts it promotes (@@hy_ce_k3@@ at K = 3, @@hy_ce_k30@@ at K = 30, @@hy_ce100@@ at K = 100). Qwen3-Reranker on the same list reaches @@hy_qw_best@@. A reranker is a model with its own errors, not a free improvement.

How the six claims were chosen (fixed rules, not by hand)

Written in e_examples.py: the first claim in id order where Qwen3-Reranker rescues a relevant abstract from BM25 rank 10 or lower, where it demotes BM25's correct first result, where MiniLM and Qwen3-Reranker disagree, where no first stage finds anything, one with three or more relevant abstracts, and claim 1180.

How to choose K on your data. Plot your first stage's recall@K on a labelled sample (a few hundred queries is enough), take the smallest K where it flattens, measure the reranker's quality at a few K around it, and check the latency at that K on your hardware ({{Depth chooser|#t-rrdepth}} tab). If recall@K is low, fix retrieval (hybrid, query rewriting, chunking), not the reranker. The root's {{Pipeline lab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b89145c37dfe8a3b0b}} shows the same sweep inside the whole pipeline, with generation.

5. Evaluating a reranker, and timing it

Everything above is one evaluation, and the same recipe works on any corpus:

  1. Freeze the first stage and score it alone: nDCG@10 and recall@K on a labelled sample from your own data. Recall@K is the reranker's ceiling.
  2. Rerank at the K you will use and compare with the first stage alone, not with nothing. A reranker can lower quality (MiniLM on the hybrid list: @@hy_ndcg@@ to @@hy_ce100@@).
  3. Count wins and losses per query, not only the mean (@@win_qw@@ better and @@loss_qw@@ worse for Qwen3-Reranker above), and read a few losses.
  4. Time a whole query's batch, all K pairs together, several repeats, under realistic load. Per-query latency depends on batching, padding and length sorting: timing one pair and multiplying by K is wrong both ways (section 1's predict box: @@qw30_pred_s@@ s predicted, @@t_qw30_s@@ s measured).
  5. Check the scores you will threshold on target data (section 6), and the instruction or template the reranker expects (section 7).

Sparse labels make every number here a lower bound: an unlabelled relevant abstract promoted to first counts as a miss. Judged answer metrics (faithfulness, groundedness) belong to {{RAG and retrieval evaluation|n:3ef5c17b0d0d8194a914d1843ee26f13}}; nDCG, MRR and AUROC are defined on {{Evaluation metrics|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a29acef722e842a29b}}.

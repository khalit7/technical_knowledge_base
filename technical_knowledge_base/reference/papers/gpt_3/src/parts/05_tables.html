Results, task by task

Nine groups of tasks, each in zero-, one- and few-shot, all eight sizes (Table H.1 above). "SOTA" here is almost always a fine-tuned model, so a near miss is a different kind of system coming close.

  • Language modelling and completion ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}): new state of the art on PTB, perplexity 20.5 against 35.8 zero-shot ({{Table 3.1|ax:S3.T1}}); PTB was kept because it predates the modern internet, while the four Wikipedia benchmarks and the One Billion Word benchmark were dropped as contained in the training data. LAMBADA (predict a paragraph's last word): 76.2% zero-shot, 8 points above the previous best, and 86.4% few-shot, 18.4 above it ({{Table 3.2|ax:S3.T2}}). The few-shot gain is a prompt-format trick: a fill-in-the-blank format ("Alice was friends with Bob. Alice went to visit her friend ___. → Bob") tells the model a single word is wanted, which a plain language model cannot know; it costs the smallest model almost 20 points and helps 175B by 10, and in one-shot it always does worse than zero-shot. GPT-3 2.7B few-shot already beats the 17B Turing-NLG ({{Figure 3.2|ax:S3.F2}}). HellaSwag 79.3% few-shot (fine-tuned SOTA 85.6, humans 95.6); StoryCloze 87.7% (K = 70), 4.1 below the fine-tuned best.
  • Closed-book QA ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}, {{Table 3.3|ax:S3.T3}}): TriviaQA 64.3% zero-shot, 68.0% one-shot, 71.2% few-shot (test server). Zero-shot already beats fine-tuned closed-book T5-11B by 14.2 points and T5-11B+SSM by 3.8; one-shot matches the fine-tuned open-domain RAG system that retrieves over a 15.3B-parameter dense index of 21M documents; few-shot is 3.2 above it. WebQuestions 41.5% few-shot against 44.7 for T5+SSM, a big zero-to-few jump suggesting its question style is out of distribution. Natural Questions 29.9% few-shot against 36.6 for closed-book T5+SSM and 44.5 for open-domain RAG; its fine-grained Wikipedia questions "could be testing the limits of GPT-3's capacity". On all three the score rises smoothly with size, "model capacity translates directly to more 'knowledge'".
  • Translation ({{§3.3|ax:S3.SS3}}, {{Table 3.4|ax:S3.T4}}): never trained for, with 7% non-English data. Averaged over six directions, one example adds 7.6 BLEU over zero-shot and 64 examples another 4.1. Few-shot into English beats the best prior unsupervised NMT (XLM, MASS, mBART) by 5.4 BLEU on average: Fr→En 39.2, De→En 40.6, Ro→En 39.5. Out of English it is weaker, and En→Ro (21.0) is "over 10 BLEU worse", perhaps because the BPE was built for English. The Fr→En and De→En scores beat the best supervised results the authors found, which they suspect are not true state of the art.
  • Winograd-style ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}, {{Table 3.5|ax:S3.T5}}): Winograd (273 schemas) 88.3, 89.7, 88.6, "no clear in-context learning", marked with an asterisk for contamination; the adversarial Winogrande does show it: 70.2, 73.2, 77.7 against fine-tuned RoBERTa 79, SOTA 84.6 and humans 94.0.
  • Common sense ({{§3.5|ax:S3.SS5}}, {{Table 3.6|ax:S3.T6}}): PIQA 82.8% few-shot on the test server, above the fine-tuned 79.4 (asterisked for contamination); ARC Challenge 51.5% and Easy 70.1%, 27.0 and 21.9 points below UnifiedQA; OpenBookQA 57.6 to 65.4 from zero to few-shot, still over 20 points short. "Mixed results", with small and inconsistent in-context gains except on OpenBookQA.
  • Reading comprehension ({{§3.6|ax:S3.SS6}}, {{Table 3.7|ax:S3.T7}}): best on CoQA, 85.0 F1 few-shot, within 3 points of humans; worst on QuAC, 44.3 F1, "13 F1 below an ELMo baseline"; DROP 36.5 F1, above the original fine-tuned BERT baseline; SQuAD 2.0 69.8 F1, almost 10 above zero-shot; RACE 46.8% (high school) and 58.1% (middle school), competitive only with the earliest contextual-representation work.
  • SuperGLUE ({{§3.7|ax:S3.SS7}}, {{Table 3.8|ax:S3.T8}}): 71.8 few-shot on the test set with 32 examples per task (256 in all) and no gradient updates, above fine-tuned BERT-Large's 69.0 and far below the fine-tuned SOTA of 89.0 (all three averages recompute from the table's columns). COPA (92.0) and ReCoRD (91.1 F1) come near the state of the art; GPT-3 beats BERT-Large on four of the eight tasks. Sweeping K, "less than eight total examples per task" pass BERT-Large overall ({{Figure 3.8|ax:S3.F8}}); the BERT++ to BERT-Large difference (630K against 125K fine-tuning examples) roughly equals GPT-3's difference between one and eight examples in context. WiC sits at chance, 49.4%, after "a number of different phrasings".
  • NLI ({{§3.8|ax:S3.SS8}}): on RTE only 175B is convincingly above chance, matching a fine-tuned BERT-Large few-shot; on adversarial ANLI every smaller model sits at chance (about 33%) and 175B "shows signs of life" on Round 3, 40.2% few-shot, closing "almost half the gap from random chance to SOTA" ({{Figure 3.9|ax:S3.F9}}).

The consistent weak spot is comparing two pieces of text: whether a word means the same in two sentences (WiC), whether one sentence implies another (ANLI, RTE, CB), plus QuAC, RACE and DROP ({{§3.7|ax:S3.SS7}}). The authors suspect the unidirectional decoder ({{Limitations|tab:t-read:s-lim}}).

Synthetic and qualitative tasks in detail

Tasks "designed especially to probe in-context learning", unlikely to appear verbatim in the training data ({{§3.9|ax:S3.SS9}}).

Arithmetic ({{§3.9.1|ax:S3.SS9.SSS1}}): ten tests of 2,000 random problems each, asked as "Q: What is 48 plus 76? A:", the answer generated exactly. Few-shot 175B gets 2-digit addition 100.0%, subtraction 98.9%, 3-digit addition 80.4% (the text says 80.2) and subtraction 94.2%, 4-digit about 25 to 27%, 5-digit 9 to 10%, 2-digit multiplication 29.2% and one-digit composite operations 21.3% ({{Table 3.9|ax:S3.T9}}). Small models fail at all of them.

Is it memorised? Searching the training data for the test problems as "<NUM1> + <NUM2> =" and "<NUM1> plus <NUM2>" found 17 of 2,000 addition problems (0.85%, printed 0.8%) and 2 of 2,000 subtraction problems (0.1%); and wrong answers often forget to carry a 1, which "suggest[s] it is actually attempting to perform the relevant computation rather than memorizing a table". The released problem sets ({{openai/gpt-3|https://github.com/openai/gpt-3}}) check out (all 6,000 answers in three sets are correct), with one difference: every one-digit composite problem puts the parentheses around the first two numbers, "(9 + 8) * 2", not the last two as §3.9.1 says.

Word manipulation ({{§3.9.2|ax:S3.SS9.SSS2}}, {{Table 3.10|ax:S3.T10}}): 10,000 frequent words of 5 to 14 letters, K = 100. Few-shot 175B: random insertion removed 67.2%, cycled letters 37.9%, anagrams keeping the first and last two letters 39.7%, keeping only the first and last 15.1%, reversed words 0.44% ("none of the models can reverse the letters"). One-shot halves these and zero-shot rarely solves any, which the authors read as learning at test time. The tasks are hard for a BPE model, which sees about 0.7 words per token and must pull tokens apart into letters. (The text's 66.9%, 38.6% and 40.2% are the Table C.1 run with another draw of examples, not Table 3.10.)

SAT analogies ({{§3.9.3|ax:S3.SS9.SSS3}}): 374 problems; 65.2% few-shot, 59.1% one-shot, 53.7% zero-shot against a 57% average for college applicants and 20% for guessing.

News articles ({{§3.9.4|ax:S3.SS9.SSS4}}, {{Appendix E|ax:A5}}): conditioned on three previous articles plus a real title and subtitle from newser.com, each model wrote completions of 25 articles; about 80 US participants per model judged real against generated. Mean accuracy at spotting the model's article fell from 86% for a deliberately bad control model to 76% for GPT-3 Small and 52% for 175B (95% interval 49 to 54%), "barely above chance" ({{Table 3.11|ax:S3.T11}}). For about 500-word Reuters articles it was again 52% against 88% for the control ({{Table 3.12|ax:S3.T12}}). Factual errors, repetition and odd phrasing are the usual tells; on the hardest article only 12% of non-neutral judgements were right ({{Figure 3.14|ax:S3.F14}}), on the easiest 61%. Participants came through Positly from Mechanical Turk, paid $12 for an estimated hour.

Novel words and grammar ({{§3.9.5|ax:S3.SS9.SSS5}}, {{§3.9.6|ax:S3.SS9.SSS6}}): given the definition of a made-up word ("A 'Gigamuru' is a type of Japanese musical instrument"), GPT-3 used it plausibly in a sentence, even conjugating "screeg" as "screeghed"; given "Poor English input / Good English output" pairs it corrected grammar, once also deleting "cheap" and changing the meaning. Both are qualitative, one sitting, nothing omitted.

How the data was filtered (Appendix A)

From {{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}} and {{Appendix A|ax:A1}}:

  • Filter by a quality classifier. A logistic regression on Spark's tokenizer and HashingTF features, trained with curated corpora (WebText, Wikipedia, the web books corpus) as positives and raw Common Crawl as negatives. A document is kept if np.random.pareto(α) > 1 − document_score, with α = 9 chosen to match the classifier's scores on WebText, so mostly high scores but some out-of-distribution documents survive. 41 monthly shards from 2016 to 2019: 45 TB of compressed plaintext became 570 GB, "roughly equivalent to 400 billion" tokens.
  • Fuzzy deduplication at the document level, within and across datasets (Spark MinHashLSH, 10 hashes); WebText was also removed from Common Crawl. Together about 10% smaller.
  • Add curated corpora: WebText2 (WebText scraped over a longer period), two internet book corpora (Books1, Books2) and English Wikipedia.

The training recipe (Appendix B)

Recipe ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}): Adam with β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, ε = 10⁻⁸; gradient norm clipped at 1.0; linear warmup over the first 375 million tokens, then cosine decay to 10% of the peak over 260 billion tokens, held at 10% after that; batch size ramped linearly from 32k tokens to full over the first 4 to 12 billion; weight decay 0.1; data sampled without replacement until an epoch boundary. Every sequence is a full 2,048 tokens, with short documents packed together and separated only by an end-of-text token. To fit in memory the model is split "within each matrix multiply and ... across the layers" (both width and depth, the first as in {{Megatron-LM|n:3c65c17b0d0d8133ae92fbe90a1f308e}}, the second pipeline-style), on V100 GPUs in "a high-bandwidth cluster provided by Microsoft" ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}).

The six flagged groups, case by case

From {{§4|ax:S4}}:

  • Reading comprehension (QuAC, SQuAD 2.0, DROP): over 90% flagged, but in every overlap inspected the source passage was in the training data and the question and answer were not.
  • German translation: 25% of WMT16 De-En flagged, an effect of 1 to 2 BLEU; matches were monolingual news snippets, not sentence pairs.
  • Reversed words and anagrams: the flags were palindromes and trivial cases ("kayak = kayak"); removing them makes the set harder, a spurious drop.
  • PIQA: 29% flagged, 3 points lower (4% relative) on the clean subset; a 25 times smaller model shows the same drop, so probably bias in which examples were copied, not memorisation. Asterisked.
  • Winograd: 45% flagged, 2.6% lower; 132 schemas really were in the training data, in another format. Asterisked.
  • Language modelling: the four Wikipedia benchmarks and the Children's Book Test were almost entirely in the training data and are not reported.

Broader impacts in detail

Misuse ({{§6.1|ax:S6.SS1}}): any harm that relies on writing text (misinformation, spam, phishing, fraudulent essays, social-engineering pretexts) gets cheaper as text quality rises, and the news result above is called "a concerning milestone". Monitoring forums since GPT-2's release found discussion but "no successful deployments", and threat analysts saw no change in advanced persistent threat operations; the authors expect interest to rise once models are "sufficiently consistent and steerable".

Bias ({{§6.2|ax:S6.SS2}}): 83% of 388 occupations were more likely followed by a male word than a female one in "The {occupation} was a"; the average log ratio of female to male probability was −1.11 for that prompt, −2.14 for "The competent {occupation} was a" and −1.15 for "incompetent". On Winogender pronoun resolution 175B was the most accurate (64.17%) and the only model more accurate for female than male occupation pronouns (81.7% against 76.7%). Generated descriptions of women leaned on appearance ("beautiful" 158 co-occurrences, "gorgeous" 28); "Asian" had consistently high sentiment and "Black" consistently low (lowest in 5 of 7 models); Islam co-occurred with "violent", "terrorism" and "terrorist" more than other religions. The authors call the analysis preliminary and "subjective signposting".

Energy ({{§6.3|ax:S6.SS3}}): training took several thousand petaflop/s-days against tens for the 1.5B GPT-2, but once trained, generating 100 pages costs "on the order of 0.4 kW-hr, or only a few cents in energy costs".

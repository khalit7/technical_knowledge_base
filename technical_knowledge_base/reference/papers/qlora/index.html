Problem

§1, Figure 1

Finetuning is the most effective way to improve an LLM and to add or remove behaviours, but regular 16-bit finetuning of LLaMA 65B "requires more than 780 GB of GPU memory" (§1): a multi-node cluster. Quantisation methods of the time (LLM.int8(), GPTQ, SmoothQuant and the k-bit inference scaling work) shrink a model for inference, but "break down during training" (§1, citing Wortsman et al. 2023). LoRA (our LoRA page) cuts the trainable parameters, yet the frozen base still sits in GPU memory in 16 bits.

The question: can a model be finetuned while its weights are stored in 4 bits, at 33B to 65B on one GPU, without losing 16-bit task performance?

Full 16-bit finetuning of LLaMA 65B with Adam. Roughly how much GPU memory, before activations?

LLaMA 65B has 65,285,660,672 parameters (recounted from its configuration: 80 layers, width 8,192, MLP width 22,016, vocabulary 32,000). The paper gives no accounting for its 780 GB; the one that lands on it is 12 bytes per parameter: BF16 weights (2) and gradients (2) plus Adam's two moments in FP32 (8), which is 783 GB. With FP32 master weights as well (16 bytes, the ZeRO accounting, ZeRO page) it is 1,045 GB. QLoRA's version of the same model is under 48 GB. Idea: store in 4 bits, compute in 16

§3, Eq. 5 and 6

QLoRA backpropagates gradients through a frozen, 4-bit quantised pretrained model into LoRA adapters (Abstract). It has one storage data type (usually 4-bit NormalFloat) and one computation data type (BFloat16): whenever a weight tensor is used, it is dequantised to BF16 and the matrix multiplication runs in 16 bits (§3). For one linear layer with one adapter:

YBF16 = XBF16 doubleDequant(c1FP32, c2k-bit, WNF4) + XBF16L1BF16L2BF16
Equation 5 (§3); doubleDequant first rebuilds the block constants from their 8-bit codes, then the weights from their 4-bit codes (Eq. 6, §3). NF4 for W with blocks of 64, an 8-bit type for c2 with blocks of 256. L1, L2 are LoRA's two thin matrices with a scalar s (Eq. 3, §2), written BA on the LoRA page.

Only the adapters get weight gradients (∂E/∂Li); the 4-bit weights are never updated. Computing those gradients still needs ∂X/∂W, which goes through the same dequantisation to BF16 in the backward pass (§3). Everything new in the paper serves this one equation: a 4-bit type precise enough that the frozen base loses little (NF4), constants small enough that 4 bits really means about 4 (double quantisation), and a guard against the memory spikes that would otherwise end a single-GPU run (paged optimizers). The fourth ingredient is not new but is tuned: LoRA on every linear layer (below).

Blockwise k-bit quantisation (§2, Eq. 1 and 2): to use the whole range of a low-bit type, a tensor is rescaled by its absolute maximum, XInt8 = round(127 / absmax(X) · X), and the scale c is stored so that dequantising divides by it again. One outlier would waste most of the bins, so the flattened tensor is cut into blocks of B values, each with its own constant (§2). 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4)

§3, Eq. 4, Appendix E and F

Quantile quantisation puts the bin edges at the quantiles of the data, so every bin receives an equal number of values: information-theoretically optimal, but estimating quantiles is expensive, and fast approximations (SRAM quantiles) err most on outliers, which are often the most important values (§3). NF4's way round it: pretrained weights are roughly zero-centred normal with some standard deviation σ (Appendix F), so once each block is scaled into [−1, 1] every tensor has the same quantiles, and they can be computed once, exactly, from N(0, 1) (§3). The recipe: (1) estimate the 2k + 1 quantiles of N(0, 1); (2) normalise them into [−1, 1]; (3) normalise each input block into [−1, 1] by absmax. A symmetric construction has no exact zero, which matters for padding and other zeros, so NF4 is asymmetric: quantiles of two ranges, 2k−1 for the negative half and 2k−1 + 1 for the positive half, merged, with the duplicate zero removed. The result has an equal expected number of values in each bin; its 16 values are printed in Appendix E.

Where the 16 values really come from

Eq. 4 defines each value as the mean of two neighbouring quantiles, qi = ½(QX(i/(2k+1)) + QX((i+1)/(2k+1))) (Eq. 4). Read literally it cannot produce Appendix E: its first and last terms are the quantiles at 0 and 1, which are infinite. The released code does something simpler: bitsandbytes' create_normal_map takes the normal quantiles at evenly spaced probabilities from 0.5 up to an offset of 0.9677083 (8 positive values, 7 negative, one zero) and divides by the largest (bitsandbytes functional.py). That reproduces Appendix E to 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ (recomputed below). The offset equals 1 − (1/64 + 1/60) to all seven printed digits, the mean of 1 − 1/32 and 1 − 1/30 (our observation; the paper and code do not say where it comes from). Quantise LLaMA-7B weights with NF4 in blocks of 64. Do the 16 values each get 1/16 of the weights, as "equal expected number in each bin" suggests?

Close, not equal. Each block is divided by its own maximum, so in every block one weight lands exactly on ±1 (1 in 64, 1.6% of all weights, against the 6.25% "fair share"), and the rest follow the distribution of a normal sample divided by its own maximum, which is not N(0, 1) scaled by a fixed amount. Davis Yoshida made the same point two weeks after the paper: the distribution being quantised depends on the block size, so NF4 "can't quite be" optimal (Yoshida 2023). Measured on a whole LLaMA-7B matrix (layer 15's value projection, 16.8 million weights), NF4's bin entropy is 3.88 bits of a possible 4, against 3.71 for FP4 and 3.49 for Int4: the closest to equal of the types, not equal. Are LLaMA's weights normal? Appendix F tests it: Shapiro-Wilk on the weights of each hidden unit of LLaMA 7B finds 7.5% of neurons non-normal at the 5% level, "about 2.5% more than the expected false-positive rate" (Appendix F). We reran the test on whole matrices (each output unit's 4,096 or 11,008 incoming weights): in the middle of the network it lands close to the paper (6.5% of the rows of layer 15's value projection, 5.9% of its MLP up projection), but the first layer's query projection rejects normality for 93% of its rows and the last layer's down projection for 76%; both are heavy-tailed (excess kurtosis 9.3 and 7.1). "Weights are normal" holds for the bulk of the network, not its ends (whole-matrix results).

Double quantisation

§3

Small blocks are needed for precise 4-bit quantisation, but each block's FP32 constant costs 32/64 = 0.5 bits per parameter at block size 64. Double quantisation (DQ) quantises the constants themselves: the FP32 constants c2 of the first quantisation become inputs to a second one with 8-bit floats and blocks of 256, giving 8-bit constants and one FP32 second-level constant c1 per 256 blocks. The constants are all positive, so their mean is subtracted first to use a symmetric code. The cost drops to 8/64 + 32/(64 · 256) = 0.127 bits, saving 0.373 bits per parameter, "approximately 3 GB for a 65B model" (§1, §3). No degradation is observed for the 8-bit second step, in line with Dettmers and Zettlemoyer (§3).

Defaults reproduce §3's 0.5, 0.127 and 0.373 bits and §1's "about 3 GB" (3.04 GB over all 65,285,660,672 parameters) independently, from the formula. The error line is measured: the first 512 block constants of the selected real LLaMA-7B matrix, double-quantised as the released code does it.

The code's "8-bit float" is not FP8

§3 says the constants are quantised to 8-bit floats. The released bitsandbytes quantises them with its default 8-bit dynamic map (the dynamic tree quantisation of the 8-bit optimizers paper), as far back as version 0.39.1, a month after the paper; the FP8 call is commented out (0.39.1 functional.py). The memory arithmetic is the same (8 bits either way), and on real LLaMA weights the dynamic map is the more precise of the two: mean relative error of the rebuilt constants 0.17% against 0.34% for an E4M3 grid, and both leave the weights' quantisation error within 1% of plain NF4's (measured).

Paged optimizers

§3, §4, Appendix G

Paged optimizers allocate the optimizer states in NVIDIA unified memory, which transfers pages between CPU and GPU automatically, "like regular memory paging between CPU RAM and the disk": when the GPU runs out of memory the states are evicted to CPU RAM, and they are paged back when the optimizer update needs them (§3). The spikes they absorb come from gradient checkpointing on mini-batches with long sequences (§1). The paper calls them critical for 33B and 65B on one 24 or 48 GB GPU but gives no hard measurements, because paging only happens on rare long mini-batches; it reports only that at batch size 16 on a 48 GB GPU, 65B trains as fast with paged as with regular optimizers, and leaves the slowdown to future work (§4). Appendix G adds that 33B "does not quite fit" a 24 GB GPU without them (Appendix G). The animation is that situation: the static memory is the paper's own Figure 6 labels, and the sequence of batches and their activation memory are illustrative.

Static memory is Figure 6's printed labels (model, adapters, optimizer; 65B: 37,074, 1,440 and 5,760 MB; 33B: 19,302, 877.5 and 3,510 MB), with the weight gradients equal to the adapters (BF16; Figure 6 draws that segment the same height) (Appendix G, Figure 6). Activations are an illustrative formula, not the paper's: gradient checkpointing keeps each layer's input (2 bytes × width × layers per token) plus one layer's attention probabilities while it is recomputed (3 × heads × tokens² × 2 bytes). The paper gives no per-length figures and paging time is not modelled. On 33B the static parts alone exceed 24 GB, which is Appendix G's "does not quite fit".

LoRA on every layer

§2, §4, Figure 2, Appendix A

QLoRA keeps LoRA's mechanics (the LoRA page trains one live) and changes how much of it to use. Its argument starts from memory: LoRA's parameters are not where finetuning memory goes. For LLaMA 7B on FLAN v2 at batch size 1, with LoRA weights the usual 0.2% of the model, the LoRA input gradients take 567 MB while the LoRA parameters take only 26 MB; gradient checkpointing brings the input gradients down to about 18 MB per sequence, still more than all the LoRA weights, and the 4-bit base model takes 5,048 MB (§2). So "aggressively reducing the amount of LoRA parameter yields only minor memory benefits", and adapters can go everywhere.

LLaMA 7B finetuned on Alpaca with 4-bit QLoRA. Which LoRA choice decides whether it matches a well-tuned 16-bit full finetune?

The layers. The default of adapting only the attention query and value projections does not match full finetuning at scale; LoRA on all linear layers of the transformer block does, and "other LoRA hyperparameters, such as the projection dimension r, do not affect performance" (§4, Appendix A). The points below are the paper's own Figures 2 and 4, read from the vector graphics of the arXiv source (positions, not eyeballed curves):

The hyperparameter search behind it (Appendix A)

Appendix A's search covered LoRA dropout {0.0, 0.05, 0.1}, r {8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256} and layers {key+query, all attention, all FFN, all layers, attention + FFN output}, with α fixed and the learning rate searched, "since LoRA α is always proportional to the learning rate". Dropout 0.05 helps 7B and 13B but not 33B and 65B; r is unrelated to final performance when every layer is adapted (Appendix A.1, Figure 4). The full-finetuning baseline was tuned too: Stanford Alpaca's default hyperparameters are undertuned, so the authors searched learning rates from 1e-6 to 5e-5 and batch sizes 8 to 128 for a robust 16-bit baseline (§4). For Super-NaturalInstructions they used r = 16 for the small, medium and large T5 models, r = 64 for XL and XXL, α = 64 and no dropout (A.2).

Later work agrees on the direction, not the order: Thinking Machines' "LoRA Without Regret" (2025) found MLP-only about as good as MLP plus attention, where QLoRA found MLP plus attention ahead of MLP, ahead of attention (LoRA Without Regret, beyond the paper).

Does 4-bit QLoRA match 16-bit?

§4, Tables 2 to 4, Figure 3

Setup. Three architectures: GLUE with RoBERTa-large (encoder), Super-NaturalInstructions with T5 80M to 11B (encoder-decoder), and 5-shot MMLU after finetuning LLaMA 7B to 65B on FLAN v2 and Alpaca (decoder). QLoRA is compared with 16-bit adapter finetuning and, up to 3B, with full finetuning. Separately, NF4 is compared with other 4-bit types the way Dettmers and Zettlemoyer did it: zero-shot accuracy and perplexity right after quantisation, with no finetuning, for OPT, LLaMA, BLOOM and Pythia from 125M to 13B (§4, the text says 65B in one place, §4).

NF4 beats FP4 and Int4 as a storage type. Mean Pile Common Crawl perplexity across those models: Int4 34.34, FP4 (E2M1) 31.07, FP4 (E3M0) 29.48, NF4 + DQ 27.41 (Table 2). On LLaMA, mean zero-shot accuracy over Winogrande, HellaSwag, PiQA, Arc-Easy and Arc-Challenge is higher for NF4 than FP4 at every size; DQ only adds minor gains but lets the footprint be tuned to fit 33B and 65B into 24 and 48 GB (Figure 3; an image without printed values, so it is not redrawn here).

Adapters recover what quantisation loses. On GLUE and Super-NaturalInstructions, 16-bit, 8-bit and 4-bit adapter finetuning replicate the 16-bit fully finetuned baseline, which "suggests that the performance lost due to the imprecise quantization can be fully recovered through adapter finetuning after quantization" (Table 3). Full finetuning at and beyond 11B needs more than one server, so for LLaMA 7B to 65B the comparison is 4-bit QLoRA against 16-bit LoRA: NF4 with DQ recovers 16-bit LoRA's MMLU (53.1 against 53.0 on average), while FP4 lags by about one point (52.2) (Table 4).

The section's summary: 4-bit QLoRA with NF4 matches 16-bit full finetuning and 16-bit LoRA on academic benchmarks, NF4 beats FP4, and DQ does not hurt; and, in line with the k-bit inference work, for a fixed finetuning and inference budget it pays to increase the parameters while decreasing their precision. Since no degradation against full finetuning was seen with 4 bits, where the precision trade-off lies for QLoRA is left open (§4 Summary).

Guanaco: instruction tuning at 7B to 65B

§5.1, §5.3, Appendix B

With 4-bit finetuning cheap, the authors trained more than 1,000 models to study instruction tuning at scales impossible with regular finetuning (§1). Eight datasets, all trained the same way: the details are in the box.

Datasets, training setup and hyperparameters (§5.1, Appendix B)

Data: eight datasets, crowd-sourced (OASST1, HH-RLHF), distilled from instruction-tuned models (Alpaca, Self-Instruct, Unnatural Instructions), corpus aggregations (FLAN v2) and hybrids (Chip2, Longform) (§5.1; sizes in Appendix B.1: OASST1 161,443 messages in 66,497 conversations across 35 languages, of which the top reply at each level of each tree gives 9,209 examples; HH-RLHF 160,800; Self-Instruct 82,612; Alpaca 51,942; Unnatural Instructions 240,670; Longform 23,700; Chip2 210,289; FLAN v2 1,836 tasks and over 15M examples, subsampled). Training: cross-entropy only, no reinforcement learning, even where human preferences exist; on the response only where instruction and response are separable (for OASST1 and HH-RLHF, the top response at every level and the whole conversation including instructions); always NF4 + DQ with paged optimizers (§5.1). Hyperparameters found at 7B generalise, except that 33B and 65B halve the learning rate and double the batch size; r = 64, α = 16, adapters on all linear layers, Adam β2 0.999, max grad norm 0.3, LoRA dropout 0.1 up to 13B and 0.05 above, a constant learning rate, group-by-length batching, MMLU 5-shot dev for validation (Appendix B.2, Table 9). Training only on the target beats training on instruction plus target for MMLU, 38.6 against 37.5 on average over four datasets at 7B (Table 10).

Guanaco is LLaMA finetuned with QLoRA on OASST1. On the Vicuna benchmark scored against ChatGPT by GPT-4, Guanaco 65B reaches 99.3% of ChatGPT after 24 hours on one professional GPU, 33B reaches 97.8% in under 12 hours on a 24 GB consumer GPU, and "the 7B model (5 GB)" scores 87.0% (Table 6, §5.3). Guanaco is the only top model not trained on proprietary data (OASST1's guidelines forbid GPT outputs); the next best open-data model, HH-RLHF, scores 30 points lower (§5.3). No overlapping prompts were found between OASST1 and the Vicuna prompts by fuzzy matching and manual inspection (§6.2).

Correction: "beats a 26 GB Alpaca by more than 20 points"

The introduction says the 7B Guanaco (5 GB) "outperforms a 26 GB Alpaca model by more than 20 percentage points" (§1). Table 6 has no 26 GB Alpaca: its Alpaca 13B is a 4-bit QLoRA model at 10 GB, and Guanaco 7B leads it by 87.0 − 69.4 = 17.6 points; §5.3 itself says "nearly 20". Over Alpaca 7B the lead is 22.6 points. The old summary repeated the introduction's version.

Data quality beats data size. OASST1's 9k examples beat FLAN v2's 450k (subsampled) for chat (§1). Subsampling Chip2, FLAN v2 and Unnatural Instructions to 50k, 100k and 150k examples and training 1 to 3 epochs moves MMLU by 0.0 to 0.5 points, while the gap between datasets is 1.5 to 8.0 points, "up to 40x larger" (Appendix B.4, Table 11). And benchmarks pull apart: FLAN v2 is the best MMLU dataset at every size and the worst on Vicuna, so "strong MMLU performance does not imply strong chatbot performance (as measured by Vicuna or OA benchmarks) and vice versa" (§5.3, Table 5). At 33B and 65B, every finetuning dataset except FLAN v2 leaves MMLU below the untuned LLaMA (65B: 63.4 untuned, Guanaco 62.2).

How the chatbots were judged

§5.2, Tables 1, 6, 7, Appendix C and D

Benchmarks. The Vicuna prompts (80, unmodified) and the OASST1 validation set (all 953 user messages, with earlier turns as context), the "Vicuna" and "OA" benchmarks; generations use nucleus sampling, p = 0.9, temperature 0.7 (§5.2). Relative scores: GPT-4 sees ChatGPT's answer and the system's, scores each out of ten with an explanation, and the system's total is reported as a percentage of ChatGPT's (it can exceed 100%). GPT-4 favours whichever answer comes first, so the paper reports both orders and their mean (§5.2). Pairwise tournaments: GPT-4 picks the better of two answers or declares a tie, for all ordered pairs of systems on both benchmarks; humans on Amazon Mechanical Turk do the same on Vicuna, two annotators per comparison against ChatGPT and three per pairwise comparison, with the same wording GPT-4 got (§5.2, Appendix C). Elo: each comparison is a match, starting from 1,000 with K = 32; an upset moves the ratings a lot, an expected result a little; the matches are replayed in 10,000 random orders and averaged (§5.2). The paper's example: 1,100 against 1,000 means an expected win rate of about 65% (64.0% by the formula), and each 10 points is about 1.5% of win rate near even odds (Table 7).

Results. GPT-4-judged Elo on Vicuna: GPT-4 1,348, Guanaco 65B 1,022, Guanaco 33B 992, Vicuna 13B 974, ChatGPT 966, Guanaco 13B 916, Bard 902, Guanaco 7B 879, each "± 1" (Table 1). With human raters, Guanaco 65B and 33B have an expected win probability against GPT-4 of 30% (29.3% and 27.7% by the formula), "the highest reported to date" (§5.3). Table 7 says Guanaco 65B and 33B beat every model but GPT-4 on Vicuna and OA, but its own OA column puts ChatGPT (1,015) second, ahead of both (1,008 and 1,002), and the human column ranks Guanaco 7B third, ahead of 33B; the paper notes that Vicuna favours open models and the larger OA benchmark favours ChatGPT (Table 7, §5.3). The pairwise GPT-4 judgments, averaged over both orders, are transitive and give a complete ordering: Guanaco 65B, 33B, Vicuna, ChatGPT, Bard, Guanaco 13B, 7B (Appendix D, Tables 12 and 13).

GPT-4 against humans. Rankings agree moderately at the system level, Kendall τ = 0.43 and Spearman r = 0.55, with the strongest disagreement on Guanaco 7B; per example, agreement between GPT-4 and the human majority is weak, Fleiss κ = 0.25, and humans agree with each other only moderately, κ = 0.42, less so between two strong systems; the authors themselves disagreed on many of the ChatGPT against Guanaco 65B preferences (§5.3, §6.2). GPT-4 rates its own answers far above what humans do, Elo 1,348 against 1,176, which the paper calls "an additional 20% probability of winning against an opponent" (§6.2); by the Elo formula that holds only against opponents rated about 1,100 to 1,420 (against ChatGPT's human rating of 916 it is 10.6%).

Table 1 prints Guanaco 65B at 1,022 ± 1 (95% interval). The 80 prompts are a sample. If they were redrawn, how far could that Elo move?

Considerations, limitations and impact

§6.2, §8, §9, Table 8
  • Evaluation biases. Strong order effects (GPT-4 scores the first answer higher), weak example-level GPT-4 to human agreement, and GPT-4's preference for its own outputs; benchmark validity is always in question, and "certain benchmarks can steer the community" (§6.2, §8).
  • Data. OASST1 and the OA benchmark are multilingual; whether that explains the larger gap between Vicuna 13B (English only) and Guanaco 33B and 65B on OA is left open, as are the trade-offs of cross-entropy against RLHF (§6.2).
  • Not shown. That QLoRA matches full 16-bit finetuning at 33B and 65B (too expensive); other benchmarks (BigBench, RAFT, HELM); other bit widths such as 3-bit bases, or other adapters; the authors speculate that 3-bit GPTQ plus LoRA might also recover 16-bit performance (§8).
  • Bias. On CrowS-Pairs, Guanaco 65B averages 43.5 against 66.6 for LLaMA 65B, 67.2 for GPT-3 and 69.5 for OPT-175B, suggesting OASST1 finetuning reduces the base model's bias; other kinds of bias are not assessed (Table 8). The caption says Guanaco "follows the biased pattern of the LLaMA base model", the text says it is less biased. The three baseline averages are weighted by category size, Guanaco's is a plain mean (weighted it is 44.7, checked). CrowS-Pairs' unbiased score is 50 (Nangia et al. 2020), so values well below 50 mean a preference for the anti-stereotypical sentence, not the absence of bias.
  • Impact. 33B on one consumer GPU and 65B on one professional GPU, "an equalizing factor" between large corporations and small teams; finetuning on phones, with an estimate that an iPhone 12 Plus could finetune 3 million tokens per night while charging (no method given; the phone model does not exist under that name, iPhone 12 and 12 Pro Max do); privacy-preserving use; and the dual-use risk of finetuning (§9).

How much of this to believe

§4, §5, Tables 3 to 7

What holds up.

  • NF4 as a data type. Its values reproduce exactly from the released code; on real LLaMA-7B matrices it has the lowest round-to-nearest error of the 4-bit types the paper tests (relative squared error 0.0085 against 0.0117 for Int4 and 0.0150 for FP4 in a middle layer), and on four small open models we reproduce Table 2's conclusion that NF4 gives the lowest perplexity after quantisation (quantiser tab). Later practice confirmed it as the 4-bit type of choice for QLoRA.
  • The memory arithmetic. 0.5 → 0.127 bits, about 3 GB on 65B, and more than 780 GB for 16-bit finetuning all reproduce from the formula and the parameter count (the last under one stated accounting). The method shipped and runs: the 65B-on-48-GB claim has been repeated by every QLoRA user since.
  • The Elo rankings come from released judgments and are reproducible: we get the OA column within 2 points and the same ranks on Vicuna (tournament tab). Table 6's order columns reproduce to the printed decimal.
  • Honesty in the text. The paper reports both answer orders, warns that Table 6's intervals overlap, recommends Elo over absolute scores, reports weak example-level agreement, and states that 33B and 65B were never compared with full finetuning. That candour counts in its favour.

What does not, or is not like for like.

  • "Matches 16-bit full finetuning" is shown only up to 3B. At 7B to 65B the comparison is with 16-bit LoRA, and the abstract's "preserving full 16-bit finetuning task performance" for 65B is an extrapolation the paper itself flags (§8). At the one size above 3B where full finetuning was run (T5-11B), every adapter method, 16-bit LoRA included, is 1.1 to 1.3 RougeL below it (Table 3).
  • Single runs, no error bars in Tables 2 to 5. Table 4's headline, NF4 + DQ 53.1 against BF16 53.0, does not survive recomputation: the eight printed cells average 52.99 for NF4 + DQ and 53.04 for BF16, a difference of 0.05 points in the other direction, far inside any plausible run-to-run noise. "FP4 is consistently one point behind" holds on average (0.8), not cell by cell (FP4 is ahead in 2 of 8 cells). Figure 2's seeds are the exception: four seeds per adapter setting, and the spread (about 0.1 RougeL) is far smaller than the gap between attention-only and all-layer adapters (about 1.1).
  • The 99.3% and the Elo ± 1. 99.3% is GPT-4 scoring 80 prompts; it is 96.7% or 101.9% depending on which answer GPT-4 reads first, and its interval (printed ± 4.4; ± 3.5 by our bootstrap over prompts) includes ChatGPT, Vicuna and Bard levels. The Elo "95% confidence intervals" of ± 1 measure only the order in which matches are replayed; resampling the prompts gives intervals of about ± 40 Elo, so Guanaco 33B, Vicuna 13B and ChatGPT are not separable on Vicuna, and GPT-4's own column, the one Table 1 reports, is GPT-4 judging its own answers.
  • Self-built evaluation, GPT-4 as judge. The tournaments are the authors' own construction; the human study is 80 prompts with 3 crowd workers each; the order effect is large (in the released Vicuna pairwise judgments, the first answer wins 2,512 times and the second 1,355, and 26% of decided verdicts flip when the order is swapped).
  • Inconsistencies. Table 6's duplicated row; Table 1 and Table 7 disagree on Guanaco 13B (916 against 913) and on Guanaco 7B's size (6 GB against 5 GB); the Figure 6 labels are not internally derivable from the stated r = 64 recipe (every adapter label is exactly 0.90 of the recount); §2 gives the 7B base as 5,048 MB, Figure 6 as 5,046 MB; Table 5's Alpaca and FLAN rows differ from Table 4's NF4 + DQ cells for the same models in 4 of 8 cases with no explanation; the CrowS-Pairs averages mix weighting; the "26 GB Alpaca" and the 1833 factorisation are wrong (all checks).
  • Paged optimizers are unmeasured. No ablation, no memory trace, one throughput statement; their necessity rests on Figure 6's 24.7 GB for 33B.

Verdict. Believe the method and the memory claims: NF4 storage plus BF16 compute plus all-layer LoRA does let a 65B model be finetuned on one 48 GB GPU with little or no loss against 16-bit LoRA, and NF4 is the best 4-bit type among those tested. Treat "matches full 16-bit finetuning" as shown at 3B and below, and treat the chatbot results (99.3% of ChatGPT, the Elo ranking) as a rough ordering with wide error bars, built on GPT-4 as a judge with a strong order effect.

What it takes to use this

  • Code and licence. artidoro/qlora (MIT) holds the training script, the Guanaco scripts and all generations and ratings; the kernels are in bitsandbytes (MIT). The Guanaco adapters were released for 7B to 65B, but they need the original LLaMA weights and follow LLaMA's research licence (README). In Hugging Face transformers, QLoRA is BitsAndBytesConfig(load_in_4bit=True, bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4", bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True, bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16) plus a PEFT LoraConfig; note that the library defaults are "fp4" and no double quantisation, so NF4 must be asked for (transformers docs, HF blog).
  • Hardware. One 48 GB GPU for 65B, one 24 GB GPU (with paging) for 33B; 24 hours for Guanaco 65B and under 12 for 33B on OASST1 (§1, Figure 6). The Guanaco 65B script uses batch 1 with 16 accumulation steps, 1,875 steps, r = 64, α = 16, learning rate 1e-4, gradient checkpointing and 3% warm-up (finetune_guanaco_65b.sh).
  • Costs. Dequantising every weight on every pass costs time: in Raschka's measured comparison, QLoRA used 14.18 GB against 21.33 GB for 16-bit LoRA, and took 2.79 h against 1.85 h (he reports "a 39% increase in runtime"; those two times are 51% apart) (Raschka). The repository lists 4-bit inference as slow and FP16 compute as unstable (about 80% of 7B runs completed) (Known issues).
  • When it pays. When the model would not otherwise fit, or fits only across GPUs. When it does fit in 16-bit, plain LoRA trains faster. For serving, the adapter can be merged into a 16-bit copy or kept beside the 4-bit base.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

QLoRA moved 33B to 65B adaptation from cluster budgets to one GPU, and its large ablation gave the field a default recipe: NF4, double quantisation, adapters on all linear layers, paged optimizers. It shipped directly into bitsandbytes and Hugging Face transformers and PEFT, published the day after the paper (HF blog, 24 May 2023), and the main open finetuning frameworks list QLoRA as a standard mode: LLaMA-Factory added it on 3 June 2023 (README), and Axolotl, torchtune and Unsloth all support it (Axolotl, torchtune, Unsloth). It also normalised the idea that a small, high-quality dataset can produce a near state-of-the-art chatbot, and was an early systematic use of GPT-4 as a judge with an honest look at its limits; the position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases it noted were studied systematically weeks later in MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al. 2023).

What followed refined rather than replaced it: AbnormalFloat (AF4), a code fitted to absmax blocks (Yoshida 2023); quantisation-aware initialisation and adaptation, LoftQ, which jointly quantises the model and initialises LoRA to close the gap, especially at 2 bits (LoftQ), and QA-LoRA, whose adapters merge into a quantised model (QA-LoRA); fused kernels (Unsloth); and better post-training quantisation for inference. NF4 remains the type recommended for QLoRA, though not the library default (see above).

The chatbot results rest on judgments the authors released: every GPT-4 verdict on the Vicuna and OA benchmarks and every Mechanical Turk vote (artidoro/qlora/eval).

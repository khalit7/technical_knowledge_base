Results: ten benchmarks, two sizes

[[S5|§5]]

The suite ({{§5|ax:S5}}) has ten benchmarks in three groups. Agentic, end-to-end execution: QwenClawBench (realistic OpenClaw tasks), WorkBuddy Bench (multi-domain workplace scenarios), PinchBench (standardised OpenClaw workflows), VitaBench (multi-turn daily-life service scenarios). Agentic, tool use and interactive tasks: BFCL v4 (function calling and agentic tool use) and τ²-Bench (user, agent and tool interaction in the Airline, Retail and Telecom domains). Coding: HumanEval and LiveCodeBench v6. Instruction following: IFEval (verifiable instructions) and IFBench (unseen constraints). Only text interfaces are evaluated.

Baselines. 4B track: Qwen3.5-4B (the base), Spark-X2.5-4B, Gemma-4-E4B-it, Nanbeige-4.2-3B, Agents-A1-4B. 9B track: Qwen3.5-9B (the base), Granite-4.2-8B, Ornith-1.5-9B, Gemma-4-12B-it, and Muse-Glimmer-30B as a larger contextual reference. Every model the authors ran used the same benchmark-specific harness, tools, context limits and interaction budgets, its official chat template and its own recommended sampling settings; numbers taken from a model's own report carry a star (one: Nanbeige's LiveCodeBench).

Settings. NeoHorse-1 and its Qwen3.5 bases follow the Qwen3.5 recommendations: temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, min-p 0, presence penalty 1.5, repetition penalty 1.0, thinking mode on (enable_thinking and force_nonempty_content true), served with SGLang v0.5.17. Maximum output is 51,200 tokens for IFEval, IFBench, HumanEval and LiveCodeBench v6 and 32,768 for the rest. QwenClawBench and PinchBench run in the OpenSquilla harness, WorkBuddy in its native harness, VitaBench in its official framework with DeepSeek-V4-Flash as user simulator and judge (the recommended models are no longer available), BFCL v4 in native function-calling mode, τ²-Bench in its official framework. Runs: three for QwenClawBench, WorkBuddy and τ²-Bench (mean reported); one for PinchBench and VitaBench; the official protocol for the rest ({{§5|ax:S5}}).

What the paper reads from this ({{§5|ax:S5}}): at 4B, NeoHorse-1 beats its base on every benchmark, most on harness-based agent tasks and coding; at 9B it improves "on most", again concentrated on harness tasks, tool interaction and selected coding, while instruction following "remains largely stable, with one metric showing a minor decrease". The 9B beats the 4B on all ten, by more on harness tasks, function calling and hard coding, little on instruction following, which the authors read as scale paying off where a task needs sustained state tracking, tools and revision after feedback. The post-trained 4B closes 89% of the macro-average gap to the base 9B. On how many of the ten benchmarks does it actually beat the base 9B?

That is still the economically interesting claim: post-training buys a large fraction of a size step, since a 4B model is cheaper to serve than a 9B one. What the paper cannot say is how much of that fraction the routing-derived curriculum buys, as opposed to the data, the distillation or simply more training (see {{How much to believe|tab:t-read:s-trust}}).

How big is the noise?

The paper reports no error bars. Five of the benchmarks give their size away: every printed score is a whole number of items out of a fixed count (HumanEval 164 problems, LiveCodeBench v6 175, IFBench 300, IFEval 541, VitaBench 400), checked across all twelve models in {{the tables tab|tab:t-tables:se}}. That turns gains into item counts and gives a binomial standard error for each (treating the two models' runs as independent, which overstates the error a little if they fail on the same items, and ignoring the extra noise of sampling at temperature 1.0):

z is the gain divided by its standard error; |z| above about 2 is unlikely to be noise. At 9B, LiveCodeBench and IFBench are identical to the item (114 of 175 and 199 of 300) before and after training, which the paper does not comment on.

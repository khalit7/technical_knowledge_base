How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • The models got better, and not by noise. At 4B every one of the ten benchmarks went up; if gains and losses were coin flips that would happen with probability 2 × 0.510 ≈ 0.002. The two largest item-level gains are well clear of sampling error: HumanEval +16 of 164 problems (z ≈ 3.3) and VitaBench +42 of 400 tasks (z ≈ 3.4; one run). At 9B VitaBench is +44 (z ≈ 3.2) and HumanEval +9 (z ≈ 2.4) ({{error bars above|tab:t-read:seT}}).
  • The comparison with the base is fair in setup. Each model the authors ran used the same benchmark harness, tools, context limits and budgets, its own chat template and recommended sampling; three of the long agentic benchmarks were averaged over three runs; the training corpus was screened against the evaluation items ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}, {{§5|ax:S5}}).
  • The numbers are internally consistent. All twelve printed averages are the plain mean of their ten columns, Figure 1's 72 bar labels match the tables, and Figure 7's baseline is exactly the base model's five-benchmark mean (45 checks in {{the tables tab|tab:t-tables:checks}}).
  • The weights are public under Apache 2.0, so anyone can rerun the ten benchmarks.

What does not.

  • Nothing isolates the paper's own ideas. There is no run with the curriculum shuffled instead of routing-ordered, none comparing the hard and soft scores of Eq. 2 (the report never says which one trained the released models), none with SFT alone against SFT plus on-policy distillation, and none with allocation off. Table 3 holds the curriculum fixed and changes the data. So the headline gains show that this post-training recipe on this company's traffic helps a 4B and a 9B model; they do not show that routing signals are a better curriculum than any other ordering.
  • The one controlled comparison is framed generously. The "+6.26 over public data" is the gap between two trained runs; against the untrained base the public-data run is −4.99 and the harness run +1.26 ({{Table 3|ax:S5.T3}}, {{Figure 7|ax:S5.F7}}). A public set that makes the model worse than no training is a weak baseline: Toucan is synthetic MCP data that was not built for this serialisation, and the budgets are "closely matched", not equal.
  • The controlled runs are much smaller than the release. The data-scaling sweep stops at about 10 million unique supervised tokens (decoded from Figure 7) and reaches 71.45; the released 4B scores 74.39 on the same five benchmarks. 58% of its gain over the base is outside every controlled experiment.
  • Home-harness advantage. QwenClawBench and PinchBench are run inside OpenSquilla, the authors' own harness. The paper names OpenSquilla among the harnesses whose traffic became training data ({{§1|ax:S1}}), so NeoHorse-1 was trained on that harness's prompts, tools and message format; its base and the other baselines were not. Decontamination removes overlapping items, not familiarity with the harness. That does not make the gains fake, but some of the +6 points on those two may be format fit rather than general agent skill.
  • Single runs where noise is largest. PinchBench and VitaBench are one run each at temperature 1.0, and no spread is given for the three-run benchmarks. VitaBench uses DeepSeek-V4-Flash as user simulator and judge because the recommended models are gone, so its scores are not comparable with other published VitaBench numbers.
  • No gain on instruction following or LiveCodeBench at 9B. LiveCodeBench and IFBench are identical to the item before and after training (114 of 175, 199 of 300) and IFEval drops 2 prompts; the 9B's 3.44-point macro gain comes from the agentic six (+4.88 on average) and HumanEval.
  • Not reproducible from the report. Corpus size is given only as 105 to 106 trajectories; the teacher, the models in the pool, the reserve fraction, learning rates, epochs and compute are not stated, and the data is private user traffic.
  • The loop is a design. Evaluation, selection and update ran once, so nothing here measures self-improvement across iterations. The authors say this plainly: the results are "an initial attempt at recursive self-improvement rather than a definitive demonstration", and whether gains "can continue to accumulate across successive iterations remains to be tested" ({{§6|ax:S6}}). That candour counts in their favour.
  • Small inconsistency. The arXiv listing's abstract says "eleven benchmarks"; the HTML abstract, §6, the tables and the GitHub README say ten.

Verdict
A credible engineering report on a useful pair of small agent models, with released weights and gains that survive a noise check. As evidence for routing-guided curricula, on-policy distillation or harness-mediated self-improvement it is not yet anything: those parts are described, not tested, and the only controlled experiment is easy to over-read.

What it takes to use this

  • The models: Text only (the Qwen3.5 vision weights are removed), BF16 safetensors plus GGUF quantisations, 262,144-token context natively; the README gives SGLang 0.5.17 and vLLM recipes with the Qwen3 reasoning parser ({{GitHub|https://github.com/TokenRhythm/NeoHorse}}, {{model card|https://huggingface.co/TokenRhythm/NeoHorse-1-4B}}).
  • Which one: in its track the 4B has the best average, but not everywhere: Nanbeige-4.2-3B is ahead on BFCL, HumanEval and (by its own report) LiveCodeBench, Spark-X2.5-4B on IFBench and IFEval, Agents-A1-4B on VitaBench. Its clearest leads are PinchBench, WorkBuddy Bench, QwenClawBench and τ²-Bench. The 9B adds most on harness tasks and little on instruction following.
  • The recipe: nothing of it is released: no training code, curation pipeline, judge prompts, curriculum scores or data. What is open is the harness and a cold-start router: {{OpenSquilla|https://github.com/TokenRhythm/opensquilla}} (Apache 2.0) with the LightGBM ranker described in {{Agentic Routing|https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11399}} (§3.2 there). To copy the idea you need your own production traffic through a routing harness, a structural validator, a judge model for the six dimensions, a teacher for on-policy distillation and an evaluation suite stratified by the same attributes.
  • When it pays: if you already serve agents through a harness with a model pool, you are sitting on the data this recipe uses, and the router's tier scores cost nothing extra to log. Keep the three routing fields separate (predicted, decided, served), as the paper does. If you have no traffic, the paper's own Table 3 suggests a public synthetic substitute can make a small model worse, not better.

Why it matters beyond the paper

This is the first published instance of the loop the harness-scaling line in this knowledge base has been circling since 2026-08-31 (see {{Topic: agentic-harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d881a8fe98b4cff7bb}}): harness and model improving each other, with the harness supplying the training signal rather than merely consuming the weights. {{Prime Agent|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81579a02f0431f24c6cb}} found that rich harnesses offer affordances models are not trained to use; {{JIT-Agent|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81148227fbd67dc4b3ee}} trained a model to emit harnesses but in a language far poorer than a production one; {{Terminal-Universe|n:3d45c17b0d0d814abc07fde424e09a31}} supplied environments and {{Repo-To-Skill|n:3d45c17b0d0d818bacfeda8a40caddb2}} supplied skills. NeoHorse-1 runs one pass of the loop (the old summary said it "runs the loop"), at 4B and 9B rather than at frontier scale, using routing decisions as the curriculum signal. The honest reading is that this is a demonstration of the mechanism and not evidence that it scales, or even, yet, that the routing signal is what helped.

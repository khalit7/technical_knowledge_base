Problem

§1 Introduction

In 2021 the best vision systems were trained to predict a fixed set of categories: the 1,000 ImageNet classes, or 18,291 for the largest weakly supervised work. Any concept outside the list needs new labelled data and a new output head; and the static softmax classifier has no way to produce a new answer (§1).

NLP had already left this behind. Task-agnostic pre-training on raw web text, with "text-to-text" as the interface, let GPT-3 transfer zero-shot to new tasks with little or no task-specific data (§1). Vision still pre-trained on crowd-labelled ImageNet. Learning images from text was not new (Mori et al. 1999 to VirTex, ICMLM and ConVIRT in 2020), but these were proofs of concept trained for accelerator days on one to two hundred thousand images, while the weakly supervised models that worked trained for accelerator years on millions to billions (§1). The best zero-shot result, Visual N-Grams, reached 11.5% on ImageNet, against 88.4% for the state of the art and 50% for classic pre-deep-learning methods (§1).

The question: does supervision from natural language, at web scale, give visual representations that transfer, and can it replace the static softmax head with classifiers written in words?

Idea

§2.1, Figure 1

Train an image encoder and a text encoder together so that each image lands next to its own caption in one shared embedding space. Training never asks the model to name anything; it asks which caption, out of a batch, belongs to which image. At test time, the text encoder turns class names into a classifier: write each class as a sentence, embed it, and pick the sentence closest to the image (Figure 1).

The authors call the family "learning from natural language supervision": earlier papers used the same idea but described it as unsupervised, self-supervised, weakly supervised or supervised (§2.1). Its two advantages: text needs no labelling format, so it scales by collecting what is already on the internet; and unlike self-supervised learning, it connects the representation to language, which is what makes zero-shot transfer possible (§2.1).

Data: 400 million pairs from the web

§2.2

Existing image-text datasets were too small or too noisy. MS-COCO and Visual Genome have about 100,000 photos each. YFCC100M has 100 million, but its metadata is sparse: titles such as "20160716_113957.JPG" and descriptions of camera settings. Keeping only English natural-language titles or descriptions shrinks it six times, to 15 million, about the size of ImageNet (§2.2).

So the authors built WIT (WebImageText): 400 million (image, text) pairs from public internet sources. To cover many visual concepts, they searched for pairs whose text contains one of 500,000 queries: every word that occurs at least 100 times in English Wikipedia, plus high-PMI bigrams, the names of Wikipedia articles above a search volume, and all WordNet synsets not already included. Results are roughly class-balanced by keeping at most 20,000 pairs per query. The total word count is similar to GPT-2's WebText (§2.2, footnote 1). WIT was never released (model card).

The objective: match pairs, do not write captions

§2.3, Figures 2 and 3

Training cost decided the method. The best vision systems of the day used 19 GPU-years (ResNeXt101-32x48d) or 33 TPUv3 core-years (Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2) to learn just 1,000 classes, so learning an open set of concepts from text "seems daunting" (§2.3). The first attempt, like VirTex, trained an image CNN and a text Transformer from scratch to generate each image's caption. It scaled badly.

Three objectives, the same ResNet-50 image encoder, the same data. Which reaches a given zero-shot ImageNet accuracy with the fewest training images?

The captioning Transformer, already twice the compute of its ResNet-50 image encoder, learns ImageNet classes three times slower than a model that predicts a bag of words of the same text; switching that bag of words from prediction to a contrastive objective gives another four times (Figure 2, §2.3). The two arrows in Figure 2 join the 400M, 134M and 33M images-processed ticks: 400 / 134 = and 134 / 33 = , so about × in all.

Both predictive objectives try to reproduce the exact words that come with an image, and there are very many ways to describe, comment on or caption the same picture. Recent work had found contrastive objectives learn better representations than their predictive equivalents, and generative image models need an order of magnitude more compute than contrastive ones for the same quality; so the authors tried the "potentially easier proxy task" of predicting only which text as a whole goes with which image (§2.3).

The toy curves are measured: four objectives trained on this page's toy web of 120,000 pairs, one run each (details in Run a tiny CLIP). The paper's curves are not redrawn: the method forbids reading values off a plot, and Figure 2 prints none.

Given a batch of N pairs, CLIP predicts which of the N × N possible pairings actually occurred. Both encoders map into the shared space through a linear projection; the embeddings are L2-normalised; their cosine similarities, scaled by a learned temperature, form an N × N matrix of logits; and a symmetric cross-entropy asks each image to pick its own caption and each caption its own image, averaging the two losses. The N real pairs are pushed together and the N² − N wrong ones apart (§2.3). This is the multi-class N-pair loss of Sohn (2016), popularised as InfoNCE by Oord et al. (2018) and applied to medical image-text pairs by ConVIRT (§2.3).

Five simplifications against ConVIRT (§2.3)


Because the dataset is so large, overfitting is not a worry and the recipe is simpler than ConVIRT's (§2.3):


    

  • From scratch. Neither encoder is initialised from ImageNet or from a pre-trained language model.
    • 

  • Linear projections. No non-linear projection head (Bachman et al., then SimCLR); the authors saw no difference in training efficiency and speculate the non-linear head is co-adapted with image-only self-supervised methods.
    • 

  • No text sampling. ConVIRT's text transformation tu, which samples one sentence, is removed, since many WIT texts are a single sentence.
    • 

  • One augmentation. A random square crop from resized images.
    • 

  • Learned temperature, so it need not be tuned as a hyperparameter.
    • 








Encoders and training

§2.4, §2.5, Appendix F

Image encoders. Two families (§2.4). A ResNet-50 modified with the ResNet-D improvements, antialiased rect-2 blur pooling, and attention pooling in place of global average pooling: one layer of Transformer-style multi-head QKV attention whose query is the global average-pooled representation. And the Vision Transformer, followed closely, with one extra LayerNorm on the combined patch and position embeddings before the Transformer and a slightly different initialisation (the ViT page explains the architecture).


Text encoder. A GPT-2-style Transformer: 63M parameters, 12 layers, 512 wide, 8 heads, over a lower-cased byte-pair encoding with a 49,152 vocabulary and sequences capped at 76 tokens. The text is bracketed by [SOS] and [EOS]; the top layer's activation at [EOS], layer-normalised and linearly projected, is the text feature. Self-attention is masked (causal), to keep open initialising from a language model or adding a language-modelling loss, which the paper leaves to future work (§2.4). Recounted: 12 blocks plus embeddings give  with that vocabulary and  with Table 18's 49,408; both round to 63M (tables tab).


Scaling. ResNets grow EfficientNet-style, but with compute split equally across width, depth and resolution rather than a tuned ratio; the text encoder only grows in width, in proportion to the ResNet's width, because performance was less sensitive to text capacity (§2.4).


Training in one line: eight models (five ResNets, three ViTs), 32 epochs, a batch of 32,768, 12 to 18 days on 256 to 592 V100s for the largest; "CLIP" in the results means ViT-L/14 fine-tuned one more epoch at 336 pixels.


The training details (§2.5, Appendix F)


    

  • Eight models: ResNet-50, ResNet-101, RN50x4, RN50x16 and RN50x64 (about 4, 16 and 64 times a ResNet-50's compute), and ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14.
    • 

  • 32 epochs; Adam with decoupled weight decay (0.2) on everything that is not a gain or bias; cosine learning-rate decay after 2,000 warm-up iterations. Hyperparameters were tuned by grid search, random search and hand on a ResNet-50 trained for one epoch, then adapted heuristically for the larger models.
    • 

  • A minibatch of 32,768. Mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, half-precision Adam statistics and half-precision stochastically rounded text-encoder weights save memory; each GPU computes only the similarities its local batch needs.
    • 

  • RN50x64 took 18 days on 592 V100s ( GPU-days); ViT-L/14 took 12 days on 256 (). ViT-L/14 then trained one more epoch at 336 pixels, as FixRes does: ViT-L/14@336px, the model the paper calls "CLIP" unless it says otherwise.

Zero-shot transfer: a classifier written in words

§3.1.1 to §3.1.3

In computer vision "zero-shot" usually means recognising unseen object categories. The paper uses it more broadly: transfer to unseen datasets, as a proxy for unseen tasks, and as a measure of what a model has learned to do rather than of its representation (§3.1.1). On datasets such as CIFAR-10, whose "real" task is unclear, it is closer to a test of robustness to distribution shift. The inspiration is GPT-1 and GPT-2's zero-shot task learning (§3.1.1).


The recipe reuses the training task. Embed the image; embed every class name with the text encoder; take cosine similarities, scale by the temperature and apply a softmax. That is a multinomial logistic regression with L2-normalised inputs and weights, no bias and temperature scaling, in which the text encoder is a hypernetwork producing the classifier's weights from text. The classifier is computed once per dataset and cached, so its cost is amortised over every prediction. Seen this way, every training step optimises a randomly made proxy dataset with 32,768 classes, one example each (§3.1.2).

Against Visual N-Grams, the only prior zero-shot result on standard datasets: 11.5% to 76.2% on ImageNet (top-5 95%, matching Inception-V4), 72.4% to 98.4% on aYahoo (a  cut in errors; the paper says 95%), 23.0% to 58.5% on SUN (Table 1). Prompt engineering and ensembling

§3.1.4, Figure 4

Datasets treat class names as an afterthought: most store numeric ids and a file mapping them to English, and Flowers102 and GTSRB did not appear to ship that mapping at all (§3.1.4; Alec "learned much more about flower species and German traffic signs ... than he originally anticipated", footnote 2). Two problems follow:


    

  • Polysemy. A bare name has no context: ImageNet has both construction cranes and cranes that fly, and in Oxford-IIIT Pets "boxer" is a dog breed that a text encoder could read as an athlete.
    • 

  • Distribution gap. In WIT the text is rarely a single word, so "A photo of a {label}." is a better default; it alone adds 1.3% on ImageNet.
    • 



Task-specific prompts help further: "A photo of a {label}, a type of pet." on Oxford-IIIT Pets, the type of food on Food101 and of aircraft on FGVC Aircraft, quotes around the text or number for OCR, and "a satellite photo of a {label}." for satellite images. Ensembling many prompts ("A photo of a big {label}", "A photo of a small {label}") is done in embedding space, not probability space, so the averaged text embeddings are cached and the ensemble costs the same as one classifier. On ImageNet 80 prompts add another 3.5%, so engineering plus ensembling add almost 5% (§3.1.4). Across 36 datasets the gain is almost 5 points on average, about what four times the compute buys with bare names (Figure 4).

Representation learning: linear probes

§3.2, Figures 10 to 12

The usual test of a representation: a linear classifier on frozen features, preferred to fine-tuning because fine-tuning can mask failures, because it mirrors the zero-shot classifier, and because 66 models on 27 datasets is already 1,782 evaluations (§3.2). The probe is scikit-learn's L-BFGS logistic regression, its L2 strength searched over 10⁻⁶ to 10⁶ on validation splits (Appendix A.3).

CLIP scales well: RN50x64 slightly beats the best existing model, Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2, on score and compute. CLIP ViTs are about 3× more compute-efficient than CLIP ResNets, echoing the ViT paper. The best model, ViT-L/14@336px, beats the best existing model by 2.6% on average (Figure 10; recomputed  points).

Robustness to natural distribution shift

§3.3, Figures 13 to 15

ImageNet models "exceeded human performance" on its test set in 2015 yet keep failing on new benchmarks, plausibly because they exploit correlations that hold only in their training distribution. Taori et al. (2020) measured them on 7 natural shifts (ImageNetV2, ImageNet Sketch, Youtube-BB, ImageNet-Vid, ObjectNet, ImageNet Adversarial, ImageNet Rendition): shifted accuracy rises predictably with ImageNet accuracy, linearly in logit space. Effective robustness is accuracy under shift above that trend; relative robustness is any improvement under shift. A ResNet-101 makes 5 times as many mistakes on these shifts as on ImageNet's validation set (§3.3).


A ResNet-101 trained on ImageNet and zero-shot CLIP both score 76.2% on ImageNet. On ImageNet-A, adversarially filtered natural images, the ResNet-101 scores 2.7%. CLIP?








77.1%, as high as its ImageNet score (Figure 13). The figure's own table, redrawn:




Every zero-shot CLIP model improves effective robustness by a large amount, shrinking the gap between ImageNet accuracy and accuracy under shift by up to 75% (Figure 13). Zero-shot CLIP improves the state of the art on 5 of the 7 shifts, most on ImageNet-Vid and Youtube-BB (its flexible classes) and on ImageNet-R (creative content in its data) (Appendix E.5). Note one mismatch: Figure 13 prints 77.1 for ImageNet-A, Table 16 prints 77.2.




Is training on ImageNet the cause? A zero-shot model cannot exploit correlations specific to a distribution it never trained on (though it can exploit ones shared between pre-training and test, footnote 4). The authors test it from the other side: adapting CLIP to ImageNet with an L2-regularised logistic regression adds 9.2% on ImageNet, to 85.4%, tying the 2018 state of the art and worth "roughly 3 years" of progress, yet average accuracy under shift slightly decreases (Figure 14). Only ImageNetV2, built the way ImageNet was, gains; ImageNet-R loses 4.7, ObjectNet 3.8, Sketch 2.8, ImageNet-A 1.9; Youtube-BB and ImageNet-Vid barely move (§3.3).


Custom classifiers, and few-shot in between (Figures 14 and 15)


Custom classifiers are a robustness tool too. Youtube-BB and ImageNet-Vid use super-classes of ImageNet's, which an ImageNet model must handle by max-pooling sub-classes (its "person" pools baseball player, bridegroom and scuba diver). CLIP just embeds each dataset's own class names: average effective robustness rises 5%, concentrated on a few datasets, and even ObjectNet gains 2.3% from its creators' names (§3.3).


Few-shot sits in between. On the best CLIP's features, 0-, 1-, 2-, 4- up to 128-shot and fully supervised classifiers show effective robustness that fades as more ImageNet data is used, mostly though not entirely gone when fully supervised; at equal ImageNet accuracy zero-shot is more robust than few-shot. "High effective robustness seems to result from minimizing the amount of distribution specific training data", at a cost in dataset-specific accuracy (Figure 15). The authors stop short of a cause: the large, diverse data or language supervision could also explain it, and they ask the authors of the JFT and Instagram models to run the same test (§3.3).

Humans, data overlap, and bias

§4, §5, §7

Against humans on Oxford-IIIT Pets (§4)


Five people labelled each of the 3,669 test images of Oxford-IIIT Pets with one of 37 cat and dog breeds (or "I don't know"), with no examples, then with one and two examples per breed, and no internet search (§4). High accuracy on STL-10 (94%) and on attention-check images (97 to 100%) suggests the workers were trying.




Humans go from 54% to 76% with a single example per class, and a second adds almost nothing; the gain is almost entirely on images they were unsure of, so humans "know what they don't know". CLIP's few-shot methods do the opposite: its accuracy drops from zero-shot to a linear probe with one example. The authors read this as room for algorithms that use prior knowledge the way people do (Table 2, §4). The hardest categories for CLIP are also hardest for humans, probably because of label noise and out-of-distribution images (Figure 16).




Did the evaluation sets leak into the 400 million pairs? (§5)

  1.     2. 

  2. Zero-shot RN50x64 accuracy on All minus Clean is the inflation due to contamination.
    3. 

  3. A one-sided binomial test of Overlap against Clean, and 99.5% Clopper-Pearson intervals.
    4. 



Of 35 datasets, 9 have no detected overlap (MNIST, CLEVR and GTSRB are synthetic or specialised; ObjectNet and Hateful Memes postdate WIT). Median overlap is 2.2%, mean 3.2%. Overall accuracy moves by more than 0.1% on only 7 datasets, 2 of them significant after Bonferroni correction; the largest gain is 0.6% on Birdsnap (12.1% overlap). Country211 overlaps most, 21.5%, because it was built from YFCC100M, part of WIT, yet gains only 0.2%: the captions rarely mention the location (§5, Figure 17). Caveats the authors give: the detector's recall cannot be checked over 400M images, and Overlap and Clean may differ in difficulty (Kinetics-700's "overlaps" are mostly black transition frames, hence an apparent 20% drop). Mahajan et al. and Kolesnikov et al. found similar rates and small effects, and Kolesnikov et al. found de-duplicating first made little difference (§5).




Bias, surveillance and class design (§7)


Because anyone can "roll their own classifier" by writing classes, how classes are designed shapes both accuracy and harm (§7). The probes, which the authors call preliminary:


    

  • FairFace. A linear probe on CLIP beats FairFace's own model and an Instagram-pretrained ResNeXt on most race, gender and age tests; gender accuracy is above 95% for every race group (Table 3, Table 5). The authors stress that benchmark accuracy is not fairness, and that higher accuracy on under-represented groups can be used to justify deployments that harm them (§7.1).
    • 

  • Denigration. With "animal", "gorilla", "chimpanzee", "orangutan", "thief", "criminal" and "suspicious person" added to the classes, 4.9% of 10,000 FairFace images (CI 4.6 to 5.4%) were classified as non-human; images of Black people about 14% (12.6 to 16.4%), all other groups under 8%; people aged 0 to 20 most often, at 14%. 16.5% of men against 9.8% of women landed in crime classes, about 18% of under-20s. Adding a "child" class drastically reduced both for under-20s (Table 6, Table 7).
    • 

  • Members of Congress. 100% gender accuracy; but at a 0.5% label threshold "nanny" and "housekeeper" appear for women and "prisoner" and "mobster" for men; with the labels of commercial vision APIs, hair and appearance words go to women, "executive" and "doctor" to men, much as Schwemmer et al. found for Google Cloud Vision (Figure 18).
    • 

  • Surveillance. On 515 CCTV images from 12 sequences, coarse scene classification is 91.8% top-1, falling to 51.1% when one "close" caption is added (it picks the close one 40.7% of the time); detecting small objects is near random. Zero-shot celebrity identification: 59.2% top-1 among 100 names, 43.3% among 1,000 (Table 8). The authors judge the appeal for surveillance low where supervised systems exist, but note CLIP lowers the skill needed for niche uses (§7.2). OpenAI's model card puts any deployed use, and surveillance and facial recognition in all cases, out of scope (model card).
    • 








Limitations the authors list

§6

    

  • Far from the state of the art. Zero-shot CLIP is on average only competitive with a linear probe on ResNet-50, itself well below the best results; the authors estimate about 1,000× more compute would be needed to reach the state of the art with this recipe, "infeasible to train with current hardware", so efficiency has to improve.
    • 

  • Weak tasks. Fine-grained classes (car models, flower species, aircraft variants), abstract or systematic tasks such as counting, and novel tasks like distance to the nearest car can be near chance.
    • 

  • Truly out-of-distribution data. 88% on MNIST handwritten digits, beaten by logistic regression on raw pixels; retrieval finds almost nothing MNIST-like in WIT. CLIP sidesteps brittle generalisation by hoping everything is in distribution, an assumption MNIST breaks.
    • 

  • Fixed choices. It can only choose among the concepts it is given, unlike a captioner; joint contrastive and generative training is suggested as a fix.
    • 

  • Data efficiency. 12.8 billion images over 32 epochs would take 405 years at one per second (recomputed: 400M × 32 ÷ seconds per year =  years).
    • 

  • Methodology. Full validation sets were queried repeatedly during development, "unrealistic for true zero-shot scenarios"; and the 27-dataset suite is "somewhat haphazardly assembled" and "undeniably co-adapted with the development and capabilities of CLIP". A benchmark designed for zero-shot transfer would help.
    • 

  • Social biases from uncurated web pairs (section 7), and concepts that are hard to specify in text; the zero-to-few-shot drop is the opposite of human learning (§6).
    • 






Why it matters beyond the paper


CLIP made images and text directly comparable in one space, where a classifier is just text. That made classification open-vocabulary, brought prompt engineering into vision and recentred robustness evaluation on zero-shot transfer. It is in most of today's multimodal stack:


    

  • Vision towers for language models. LLaVA connects "the pre-trained CLIP visual encoder ViT-L/14" to an LLM (LLaVA); InternVL scales the vision side to 6 billion parameters, aligned "using web-scale image-text data" (InternVL); Qwen-VL adds a visual receptor to its language model (Qwen-VL). The old version of this page also traced "the lineage behind frontier multimodal models" to CLIP towers; closed models do not document their encoders, so that is unconfirmed.
    • 

  • Generation. Stable Diffusion conditions its U-Net on "the (non-pooled) text embeddings of a CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder" (CompVis README) through cross-attention layers (LDM; see the Latent Diffusion page); DALL-E 2 (unCLIP) generates a CLIP image embedding from a caption, then decodes it (unCLIP); CLIPScore rates captions without references (CLIPScore); CLIP-score guidance of generators is also common practice (not separately sourced here).
    • 

  • Data curation. LAION-400M and LAION-5B were filtered by CLIP similarity (LAION-400M, LAION-5B); DataComp and Data Filtering Networks made such filtering a research problem in itself, so CLIP-style models now curate the data their successors train on (DataComp, DFN).
    • 

  • Successors. ALIGN (Google, noisier data at a larger scale than "similar": 1.8B alt-text pairs, 4.5 times WIT, with minimal filtering; 76.4% zero-shot ImageNet, ALIGN), OpenCLIP's open reproductions and contrastive scaling laws (Cherti et al.), EVA-CLIP (EVA-CLIP), MetaCLIP (MetaCLIP), and the SigLIP line (2023), which replaces the batch softmax with a per-pair sigmoid to decouple the loss from the batch size (SigLIP); SigLIP 2 (2025) adds captioning and self-supervised losses; the old version of this page called it "today's default open vision tower", a claim about usage this page has not checked. Step through them in Then and now.
    • 

  • Retrieval. The paper anticipates image search "given text, or relevant text given an image" (§7); LAION-400M was released with "their CLIP embeddings and kNN indices that allow efficient similarity search" (LAION-400M). That cross-modal search became "a commodity capability in vector databases", as the old version of this page put it, is unconfirmed here.
    •

How much of this to believe


What holds up.


    

  • The headline numbers are reproducible by anyone, because the weights were released: 76.2% zero-shot ImageNet is a measurement on a 50,000-image test set (a binomial standard error of about √(0.76 × 0.24 / 50,000) ≈ 0.19 points), and open reproductions on public data reached and passed it: OpenCLIP's ViT-H/14 on LAION-2B scores 78.0% (OpenCLIP), DataComp-1B trains a ViT-L/14 to 79.2% with "the same training procedure and compute" (DataComp), and MetaCLIP's re-creation of WIT's curation beats WIT at ViT-B (70.8% against 68.3%) (MetaCLIP).
    • 

  • The linear-probe comparison is as fair as such studies get: one protocol, the same L2 search, every model's public weights, and the 12-dataset suite reported first to limit selection bias (§3.2). Recomputing from Tables 10 and 11 reproduces 16 of 27 wins (Figure 5), 21 of 27 (Figure 11), the 2.6-point margin, and a correlation of 0.81 against the printed 0.82 (tables tab).
    • 

  • Contamination is measured, not assumed, with significance tests, and is small (§5).
    • 

  • The authors state most weaknesses themselves: the Visual N-Grams comparison is "not a direct methods comparison", validation sets were queried during development, the 27-dataset suite is co-adapted with CLIP (§3.1.3, §6). That counts in their favour.
    • 



What to read with care.


    

  • "Contrastive is 4× more efficient" is one comparison at one scale. Figure 2 is single runs of a ResNet-50 up to 400M images processed (one of the 32 epochs), scored on zero-shot ImageNet, a metric on which contrastive models are known to scale better; it has no error bars. Later, with data, compute and capacity matched, captioners gave vision encoders "competitive with contrastively pretrained encoders" on classification and better on vision-language tasks, while still lagging on zero-shot classification with a gap that "becomes smaller with increasing scale" (CapPa, 2023).
    • 

  • "Robust because zero-shot" is the authors' hypothesis, and they say so. A controlled follow-up tested five causes (data size, training distribution, language at training, language at test, the contrastive loss) and found "the more diverse training distribution is the main cause", the others contributing little (Fang et al., 2022). The toy above shows the same split. The fine-tuning result also depends on the method: ensembling the weights of the zero-shot and fine-tuned models "provides large accuracy improvements under distribution shift, while preserving high accuracy on the target distribution" (WiSE-FT, 2021).
    • 

  • Prompt engineering may be partly tuned to the test. The paper does not say how the 80 ImageNet prompts and the per-dataset templates were chosen, and it says full validation sets were queried repeatedly during development (§6); some of the "almost 5 points" may be selection on the evaluation distribution.
    • 

  • "Matches ResNet-50" compares unlike things: zero-shot from the largest CLIP (ViT-L/14@336px, 12 days on 256 V100s plus a 336-pixel epoch) against the 2015 ResNet-50; at matched architecture CLIP's zero-shot RN50 scores 59.6% (Table 11). The paper says this; summaries often do not.
    • 

  • Single runs throughout. No seeds or intervals for the training runs; the authors note individual zero-shot evaluations are noisy and cannot separate run-to-run variance from real effects (Figure 9).
    • 

  • Small inconsistencies in the paper's own numbers: Figure 5 disagrees with Tables 10 and 11 on KITTI (−34.0 against −45.2) and UCF101 (+7.7 against +5.7); Figure 13 and Table 16 differ on ImageNet-A (77.1, 77.2); §2.4 gives a 49,152 vocabulary and Table 18 49,408; Table 9 lists Food-101 with 102 classes; the 3.8-point ObjectNet drop only matches Table 16 after removing the 2.3 points from ObjectNet's own class names (tables tab). None changes a conclusion.
    • 

  • Not a compositional model. Contrastive training on web data rewards bag-of-words shortcuts; CLIP-like models "blunder when linking objects to their attributes" and lack order sensitivity (Yuksekgonul et al., 2022). The toy shows a version of this: its bag-of-words text encoder beat its Transformer.
    • 



Verdict: believe the method and the measurements; they have been re-run more than almost any vision result. Treat the efficiency ratio as a 2021, ResNet-50-scale finding, and the robustness story as a statement about diverse data rather than about language or zero-shot inference.





What it takes to use this


    

  • Code and licence. openai/CLIP releases the model code and weights under the MIT licence; not the training code and not WIT, which OpenAI said it will not release (model card). OpenAI's model card scopes the models to research and calls any deployed use "currently out of scope". For training, OpenCLIP is the open implementation, with models trained on LAION and DataComp data.
    • 

  • Budget. The paper's largest runs took 3,072 (ViT-L/14) and 10,656 (RN50x64) V100-days at a batch of 32,768 (§2.5); the contrastive loss wants a big batch because the batch supplies the negatives, the reason SigLIP introduced a per-pair loss that "performs better at smaller batch sizes" (SigLIP). Most users never train one: they download a checkpoint.
    • 

  • When it pays off. Zero-shot or few-shot classification and retrieval over everyday web concepts; a frozen feature extractor; a filter for image-text data; a vision tower for a language model.
    • 

  • When it buys little. Specialised imagery (satellite, medical, traffic signs), counting and spatial relations, fine-grained categories rare on the web, and text in handwriting: there a supervised model or fine-tuning wins (section 6). Expect results to move with how classes are worded, and test the class design, as the bias probes show (§7.1).
    •

Then and now beyond the paper


CLIP's recipe has five parts: the data, the image encoder, the text encoder, the loss, and the batch that supplies the negatives. The step-through changes one part at a time, each with the paper that changed it, and keeps the zero-shot ImageNet number each paper reports. The numbers are not like for like (different encoders, data, compute, and some start from image towers pre-trained on labels); they show where the field went, not which change was worth how much.




One recipe card, changed one step at a time.
















The loss that replaced CLIP's: softmax against sigmoid, on one toy batch


CLIP's loss normalises each row and each column of the batch's similarity matrix, so every cell's loss depends on the whole row: the batch is the set of negatives, which is why it wants 32,768 pairs. SigLIP scores every cell on its own, as a yes or no: logits = t · cos + b, labels +1 on the diagonal and −1 elsewhere, loss −Σ log σ(label · logit) / n, with t and b learned and started at 10 and −10 so training starts near the prior that almost every pair is negative (SigLIP §3.2). It "requires just a single pass" and less memory, performs better at small batches, and saturates at about 32k (SigLIP).





The same eight pairs, the shipped toy CLIP's cosines.













The cosines come from the toy CLIP, which was trained with the softmax loss; the sigmoid side applies SigLIP's starting values (t = 10, b = −10) to them to show the arithmetic, not a model trained that way. What survived, and what changed


    

  • Survived: two encoders meeting in one space through a single dot product; zero-shot classifiers written as prompts; web-scale image-text pairs as the supervision; ViT image towers.
    • 

  • Changed: the data became the main research object (LAION, DataComp, DFN, MetaCLIP), and CLIP-style models now filter it; the softmax over the batch gave way to a per-pair sigmoid in the most used open towers; captioning came back as an extra loss (CoCa, SigLIP 2) after CapPa showed captioners scale "the same or better" for representations (CapPa); and the robustness CLIP was praised for was traced to diverse data (Fang et al.).
    • 

  • Where CLIP itself still runs: LLaVA's vision encoder is "the pre-trained CLIP visual encoder ViT-L/14" (LLaVA), and Stable Diffusion v1 conditions on "a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder" (CompVis).

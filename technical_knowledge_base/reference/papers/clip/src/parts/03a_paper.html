Problem

[[S1|§1 Introduction]]

In 2021 the best vision systems were trained to predict a fixed set of categories: the 1,000 ImageNet classes, or 18,291 for the largest weakly supervised work. Any concept outside the list needs new labelled data and a new output head; and the static softmax classifier has no way to produce a new answer ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

NLP had already left this behind. Task-agnostic pre-training on raw web text, with "text-to-text" as the interface, let GPT-3 transfer zero-shot to new tasks with little or no task-specific data ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Vision still pre-trained on crowd-labelled ImageNet. Learning images from text was not new (Mori et al. 1999 to VirTex, ICMLM and ConVIRT in 2020), but these were proofs of concept trained for accelerator days on one to two hundred thousand images, while the weakly supervised models that worked trained for accelerator years on millions to billions ({{§1|ax:S1}}). The best zero-shot result, Visual N-Grams, reached 11.5% on ImageNet, against 88.4% for the state of the art and 50% for classic pre-deep-learning methods ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

The question: does supervision from natural language, at web scale, give visual representations that transfer, and can it replace the static softmax head with classifiers written in words?

Idea

[[S2.SS1|§2.1, Figure 1]]

Train an image encoder and a text encoder together so that each image lands next to its own caption in one shared embedding space. Training never asks the model to name anything; it asks which caption, out of a batch, belongs to which image. At test time, the text encoder turns class names into a classifier: write each class as a sentence, embed it, and pick the sentence closest to the image ({{Figure 1|ax:S1.F1}}).

The authors call the family "learning from natural language supervision": earlier papers used the same idea but described it as unsupervised, self-supervised, weakly supervised or supervised ({{§2.1|ax:S2.SS1}}). Its two advantages: text needs no labelling format, so it scales by collecting what is already on the internet; and unlike self-supervised learning, it connects the representation to language, which is what makes zero-shot transfer possible ({{§2.1|ax:S2.SS1}}).

Data: 400 million pairs from the web

[[S2.SS2|§2.2]]

Existing image-text datasets were too small or too noisy. MS-COCO and Visual Genome have about 100,000 photos each. YFCC100M has 100 million, but its metadata is sparse: titles such as "20160716_113957.JPG" and descriptions of camera settings. Keeping only English natural-language titles or descriptions shrinks it six times, to 15 million, about the size of ImageNet ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}).

So the authors built WIT (WebImageText): 400 million (image, text) pairs from public internet sources. To cover many visual concepts, they searched for pairs whose text contains one of 500,000 queries: every word that occurs at least 100 times in English Wikipedia, plus high-PMI bigrams, the names of Wikipedia articles above a search volume, and all WordNet synsets not already included. Results are roughly class-balanced by keeping at most 20,000 pairs per query. The total word count is similar to GPT-2's WebText ({{§2.2, footnote 1|ax:S2.SS2}}). WIT was never released ({{model card|https://github.com/openai/CLIP/blob/main/model-card.md}}).

The objective: match pairs, do not write captions

[[S2.SS3|§2.3, Figures 2 and 3]]

Training cost decided the method. The best vision systems of the day used 19 GPU-years (ResNeXt101-32x48d) or 33 TPUv3 core-years (Noisy Student EfficientNet-L2) to learn just 1,000 classes, so learning an open set of concepts from text "seems daunting" ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}). The first attempt, like VirTex, trained an image CNN and a text Transformer from scratch to generate each image's caption. It scaled badly.

Three objectives, the same ResNet-50 image encoder, the same data. Which reaches a given zero-shot ImageNet accuracy with the fewest training images?

The captioning Transformer, already twice the compute of its ResNet-50 image encoder, learns ImageNet classes three times slower than a model that predicts a bag of words of the same text; switching that bag of words from prediction to a contrastive objective gives another four times ({{Figure 2|ax:S1.F2}}, {{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}). Both predictive objectives try to reproduce the exact words that come with an image, and there are very many ways to describe, comment on or caption the same picture. Recent work had found contrastive objectives learn better representations than their predictive equivalents, and generative image models need an order of magnitude more compute than contrastive ones for the same quality; so the authors tried the "potentially easier proxy task" of predicting only which text as a whole goes with which image ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}).

The toy curves are measured: four objectives trained on this page's toy web of 120,000 pairs, one run each (details in {{Run a tiny CLIP|tab:t-run}}). The paper's curves are not redrawn: the method forbids reading values off a plot, and Figure 2 prints none.

Given a batch of N pairs, CLIP predicts which of the N × N possible pairings actually occurred. Both encoders map into the shared space through a linear projection; the embeddings are L2-normalised; their cosine similarities, scaled by a learned temperature, form an N × N matrix of logits; and a symmetric cross-entropy asks each image to pick its own caption and each caption its own image, averaging the two losses. The N real pairs are pushed together and the N² − N wrong ones apart ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}). This is the multi-class N-pair loss of Sohn (2016), popularised as InfoNCE by Oord et al. (2018) and applied to medical image-text pairs by ConVIRT ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}).

Five simplifications against ConVIRT (§2.3)


Because the dataset is so large, overfitting is not a worry and the recipe is simpler than ConVIRT's ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}):


    

  • From scratch. Neither encoder is initialised from ImageNet or from a pre-trained language model.
    • 

  • Linear projections. No non-linear projection head (Bachman et al., then SimCLR); the authors saw no difference in training efficiency and speculate the non-linear head is co-adapted with image-only self-supervised methods.
    • 

  • No text sampling. ConVIRT's text transformation tu, which samples one sentence, is removed, since many WIT texts are a single sentence.
    • 

  • One augmentation. A random square crop from resized images.
    • 

  • Learned temperature, so it need not be tuned as a hyperparameter.
    • 








Encoders and training

[[S2.SS4|§2.4, §2.5, Appendix F]]

Image encoders. Two families ({{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}). A ResNet-50 modified with the ResNet-D improvements, antialiased rect-2 blur pooling, and attention pooling in place of global average pooling: one layer of Transformer-style multi-head QKV attention whose query is the global average-pooled representation. And the Vision Transformer, followed closely, with one extra LayerNorm on the combined patch and position embeddings before the Transformer and a slightly different initialisation (the {{ViT page|n:3c65c17b0d0d811fa231cfcde6c1954d}} explains the architecture).


Text encoder. A GPT-2-style Transformer: 63M parameters, 12 layers, 512 wide, 8 heads, over a lower-cased byte-pair encoding with a 49,152 vocabulary and sequences capped at 76 tokens. The text is bracketed by [SOS] and [EOS]; the top layer's activation at [EOS], layer-normalised and linearly projected, is the text feature. Self-attention is masked (causal), to keep open initialising from a language model or adding a language-modelling loss, which the paper leaves to future work ({{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}). Recounted: 12 blocks plus embeddings give  with that vocabulary and  with Table 18's 49,408; both round to 63M ({{tables tab|tab:t-tables:tbHp}}).


Scaling. ResNets grow EfficientNet-style, but with compute split equally across width, depth and resolution rather than a tuned ratio; the text encoder only grows in width, in proportion to the ResNet's width, because performance was less sensitive to text capacity ({{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}).


Training in one line: eight models (five ResNets, three ViTs), 32 epochs, a batch of 32,768, 12 to 18 days on 256 to 592 V100s for the largest; "CLIP" in the results means ViT-L/14 fine-tuned one more epoch at 336 pixels.


The training details (§2.5, Appendix F)


    

  • Eight models: ResNet-50, ResNet-101, RN50x4, RN50x16 and RN50x64 (about 4, 16 and 64 times a ResNet-50's compute), and ViT-B/32, ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/14.
    • 

  • 32 epochs; Adam with decoupled weight decay (0.2) on everything that is not a gain or bias; cosine learning-rate decay after 2,000 warm-up iterations. Hyperparameters were tuned by grid search, random search and hand on a ResNet-50 trained for one epoch, then adapted heuristically for the larger models.
    • 

  • A minibatch of 32,768. Mixed precision, gradient checkpointing, half-precision Adam statistics and half-precision stochastically rounded text-encoder weights save memory; each GPU computes only the similarities its local batch needs.
    • 

  • RN50x64 took 18 days on 592 V100s ( GPU-days); ViT-L/14 took 12 days on 256 (). ViT-L/14 then trained one more epoch at 336 pixels, as FixRes does: ViT-L/14@336px, the model the paper calls "CLIP" unless it says otherwise.

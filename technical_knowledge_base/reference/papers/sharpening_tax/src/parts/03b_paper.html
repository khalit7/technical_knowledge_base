The Sharpening Tax: one number for a whole curve

[[S4|§4, Eqs. 1 to 3, Proposition 1]]

Comparing two full pass@k curves for each of 42 cells does not scale, and a single endpoint (pass@128) hides whether a curve saturated after two attempts or kept climbing. The paper summarises the curve's shape with an area ({{§4|ax:S4}}). For a budget K:

Raw scalability   A(K) = Σk=1K−1 [pass@K − pass@k]   ({{Eq. 1|ax:S4.E1}})
Calibrated scalability   S(K) = A(K) / ((K − 1)(1 − pass@1)) ∈ [0, 1]   ({{Eq. 2|ax:S4.E2}})
Sharpening Tax   TaxX(K) = XBase(K) − XPost(K),   X ∈ {A, S}   ({{Eq. 3|ax:S4.E3}})

A is the area between the curve and its own ceiling: how much success the extra attempts bought. S divides by the room the first attempt left (1 − pass@1) and by the budget, so it lies between 0 and 1. A positive tax means post-training reduced what extra attempts are worth.

What the area counts ({{Proposition 1|ax:Thmtheorem1}}, proof in {{B.1|ax:A2.SS1}}). Let H be the attempt on which a random task is first solved (infinite if never). Then A(K) = E[(H − 1) · 1{H ≤ K}]: the expected number of failed attempts before a success that arrives within the budget. A task solved on the first try adds nothing; a task never solved adds nothing; a task first solved on attempt h adds h − 1. S is the same quantity among tasks that failed their first attempt, divided by K − 1. So a positive tax says the post-trained model's successes lean less on retries: its failures are recovered less often (lost coverage) or sooner (faster saturation).

The identity on recorded attempts, and Corollary 4

The identity also holds exactly for the estimates from 128 recorded attempts. {{Corollary 4|ax:Thmtheorem4}} splits lost coverage from faster saturation: A(K) = K[pass@K − mean of pass@1..K].

Where it is charged ({{Figure 6|ax:S4.F5}}, {{Table 7|ax:A4.T5}}). At K = 128, TaxS is positive in 36 of the 42 cells and TaxA in 39. For the largest models it is positive at almost every budget and is largest for gemma-4-31B. For the smallest it is often negative at small budgets (most visibly Ministral-3-3B: sharpening helps when compute is scarce) and rises with K. Raw TaxA grows almost monotonically with the budget; calibrated TaxS can rise or fall. Every one of the 84 tax values in Table 7 comes out of the released counts to the printed precision ({{replay them|tab:t-run}}).

The six cells with a negative TaxS(128)

Ministral-3-3B, Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B on BFCL, Ministral-3-3B on WebShop, and Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen3.5-35B-A3B on ACEBench, the last three within 0.03 of zero ({{Table 7|ax:A4.T5}}).

Note what the tax is and is not. It is not "the base model reaches more tasks". The base model's pass@128 is higher in 26 of the 42 cells, and the printed 95% interval is clear of zero in 18 of them; in 13 the post-trained model is clearly ahead, mostly on BFCL and for small models (Table 7). A post-trained model can reach more tasks and still pay a positive tax, because its curve saturates sooner: gemma-4-12B on BFCL covers 74.0% against 47.0% and still pays TaxS 0.075. The tax measures what extra attempts are worth, which is what "sharpening" claims to remove.

Is it useful as a diagnostic? ({{Figure 7|ax:S4.F6}}). Three claims, each a rank correlation over the 42 cells. (1) TaxS(8), from 8 attempts on half the tasks, predicts TaxS(32) on the other half with Spearman ρ = 0.85. (2) The tax at K = 8 rises with the consistency gap Δpass8: the more consistent the post-trained model became, the more coverage it gave up. (3) Of several cheap 8-attempt predictors, only the early tax predicts all three later metrics (TaxS(32), Δpass@32 and Δpass32) "with a strong statistical sign".

Checked here: over 200 random splits of the released counts the first correlation averages about 0.8, not 0.85; the paper's 0.85 is one split at the top of the range. The claim survives in direction and size.

How the 0.85 was checked, from the released counts
Drawing 8 random attempts per task on a random half of the tasks and computing TaxS(32) from all 128 attempts on the other half gives ρ = on average over 200 random splits (5th to 95th percentile ). The paper's 0.85 is one split, at the top of that range, and it equals what the two quantities give with no split at all (). The claim survives in direction and size; expect about 0.8 on held-out tasks rather than 0.85. The paper does not say how its 8 attempts were chosen (first 8 or random), so this check is independent but not identical.
A use: route each task to one model (Appendix D.4)

A use: route each task to one model ({{Appendix D.4|ax:A4.SS4}}, {{Figure 33|ax:A4.F32}}). Spend 32 pilot attempts on each model, estimate the tax per task, and give the rest of the budget to the base model where retries pay and to the post-trained one elsewhere. Averaged over the four largest backbones, its pass@128 beats the post-trained model on WebShop by up to 13.6 points, and it beats uniform random routing in all six panels. Its pass@1 sits between the two models', so it suits large budgets. Not run here: the pilot rollouts' cost is counted only as attempts on the other model, and per-task routing needs a verifier at test time.

Why sharpening charges a tax

[[S6|§6, Theorems 2 and 3]]

Treat each task as having a true per-attempt success probability p, with attempts independent. Then the task's contribution to A(K) is aK(p) = Σk<K [(1 − p)k − (1 − p)K], positive for every p strictly between 0 and 1 and zero at both ends (the chart above draws it). Post-training's polarisation moves tasks exactly toward those two zeros.

Post-training takes a task the base model solved with p = 0.3 and makes it solved every time (p = 1). Its pass@1 goes from 30% to 100%. What happens to its contribution to the raw tax, TaxA?

It raises it, by the task's whole retry value aK(0.3), the same as if the task had become unsolvable. That is {{Theorem 2|ax:Thmtheorem2}}: if post-training sends a fraction λ of tasks to 0 or 1, independently of their p, then TaxA(K) = λ · ABase(K), whichever extreme they reach. The calibrated tax keeps a second effect: SPost / SBase = (1 − λ)ε / ((1 − λ)ε + λ0), with ε the base model's first-attempt failure rate and λ0 the share sent to 0 ({{Eq. 6|ax:S6.E6}}). So pass@1 can rise (when λ0 < λε) while TaxS stays positive whenever any task is sent to 0. Below, on gemma-4-31B's real WebShop tasks: sharpen a share λ of the base model's tasks, a share λ0 of them to never-solved, and compare the measured tax with λ · ABase.

The theorem is a limiting model, not a fit: real post-training also moves tasks part of the way (and some the other way). The paper's empirical counterpart is {{Table 8|ax:A4.T6}}, which splits each TaxA(128) into retry value lost on tasks only the base model solves (positive in all 42 cells), value recovered on tasks only the post-trained model solves (large mainly for small models on BFCL and WebShop) and a change of curve shape on tasks both solve. All 294 numbers in it reproduce from the released counts. The tasks that pay most ({{Appendix D.3|ax:A4.SS3}}) look alike across families: the post-trained model is flat at 100% or 0%, and the base model succeeds perhaps once in 128 attempts.

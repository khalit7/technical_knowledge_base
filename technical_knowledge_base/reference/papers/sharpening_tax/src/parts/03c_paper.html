PTGS: heat the prompts the policy keeps failing

[[S5|§5, Eq. 4, Algorithm 1]]

The obvious fix, sampling the post-trained model hotter, trades the wrong way: in the paper's twelve panels a higher temperature usually raises pass@k at large budgets, but pass@1 never improves and sometimes clearly drops ({{Appendix C.4|ax:A3.SS4}}, {{Figure 25|ax:A3.F24}}).

Take a policy that RL has already sharpened and simply sample it hotter at test time. What happens?

Coverage comes back at the price of accuracy, one global knob moving along one trade-off. The paper shows it for the largest post-trained model of each family (Appendix C.4). Posterior-tempered group sampling (PTGS) changes only how training rollouts are sampled, prompt by prompt ({{§5|ax:S5}}, {{Algorithm 1|ax:A1.SS6}}). For each prompt x it keeps discounted counts of the successes and failures seen in that prompt's past rollout groups, with forgetting factor γ:

(s̃x, f̃x) ← (γs̃x + sx, γf̃x + n − sx)
posterior Beta(ax, bx), ax = 2p̃ + s̃x, bx = 2(1 − p̃) + f̃x
draw p̂x ~ Beta(ax, bx) (Thompson sampling),   Tx = τh(p̂x)   ({{Eq. 4|ax:S5.E4}})
h(p) = (p̃ − p)/p̃ if p ≤ p̃ (hard),   (p̃ − p)/(1 − p̃) if p > p̃ (easy)

p̃ is the target success rate and τ > 1 the spread: a prompt the policy keeps failing is sampled at up to τ (more exploration), one it has mastered at down to 1/τ (more exploitation), and one at the target at temperature 1. The prior has total weight 2 and is centred on p̃. The policy-gradient log-probabilities are computed at the same temperature the group was sampled at, so the update stays on-policy; nothing else in PPO or GRPO changes, and PTGS is switched off at test time. Why heating a hard prompt helps RL ({{Theorem 3|ax:Thmtheorem3}}, {{B.4|ax:A2.SS4}}). With n independent rollouts per group and success probability q, a group contains at least one success with probability In(q) = 1 − (1 − q)n, and is mixed (some succeed, some fail) with probability Jn(q) = 1 − qn − (1 − q)n. PPO can only reinforce a success if the group has one; GRPO's group-relative advantage is zero unless the group is mixed. In rises with q everywhere and Jn rises up to q = 1/2, which is why the target ramps toward 0.5. Cooling a mastered prompt (q ≥ 1/2) turns mixed groups into all-success groups, never into all-failure ones ({{Proposition 5|ax:Thmtheorem5}}). The theorem assumes that heating raises a hard prompt's success probability; the paper checks it indirectly by counting fewer zero-success groups under PTGS ({{Appendix F.1|ax:A6.SS1}}). The {{Train with PTGS|tab:t-toy}} tab shows one batch of eight prompts going through both samplers, including one where the assumption fails.

Setup ({{A.5|ax:A1.SS5}}). Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, fine-tuned in the {{RAGEN framework|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.20073}} on two grid games: Sokoban (6 × 6, one box) and slippery FrozenLake (each move goes the intended way with probability 0.8). PTGS improves pass@1 and pass@128 for both algorithms in both games and lowers TaxS(128) in all four comparisons ({{§5|ax:S5}}). On FrozenLake the two PPO runs end at the same validation success, but the policies differ: under fixed-temperature PPO the token entropy falls steadily toward zero, while under PTGS it stays several times higher and rises repeatedly as hard prompts are heated ({{Figure 9|ax:S5.F8}}, {{Appendix E.1|ax:A5.SS1}}). On Sokoban, PPO degrades after step 150 while PTGS keeps improving.

Is it just a hotter sampler? ({{Appendix E.3|ax:A5.SS3}}, {{Table 9|ax:A5.T7}}, {{Table 10|ax:A5.T8}}). No fixed training temperature from 0.5 to 1.5 improves pass@1 and pass@128 together, and top-p, top-k and min-p truncation barely move the baseline. PTGS has the best pass@128 in both games and a smaller tax than every fixed temperature of 1.0 or more. The paper words that last claim carefully: the fixed temperature of 0.5 pays a smaller tax than PTGS in both games (0.073 against 0.081 on Sokoban, 0.004 against 0.020 on FrozenLake), with lower pass@1. On FrozenLake, PTGS's consistency (pass128) is lower than the default's, 42.5 against 50.9, intervals overlapping (the paper notes it). In the paper's {{Figure 41|ax:A6.SS2}} comparison against PPO sampled at 1.3 or 1.5, the extra entropy of PTGS is not simply a higher temperature, and only PTGS's comes with higher coverage.

At test time, without training ({{Appendix E.4|ax:A5.SS4}}, {{Figure 37|ax:A5.F36}}): spend 128 pilot attempts per prompt, form the posterior, and sample 128 more at the PTGS temperature. On FrozenLake every PTGS setting lies beyond the global-temperature trade-off curve; on Sokoban only two settings beat every fixed temperature on both axes, by one or two of the 64 tasks.

Does the estimate track difficulty? ({{Appendix F.1|ax:A6.SS1}}). The posterior mean correlates with realised group success at Pearson 0.81 (Sokoban) and 0.71 (FrozenLake), and runs about 0.1 low, because the discounted counts remember the weaker earlier policy. Mean temperature stays above 1 on Sokoban and falls below 0.9 on FrozenLake late in training, with a spread of 0.2 to 0.3 across the prompts of one step: PTGS heats and cools at once rather than shifting one global temperature.

How much of this to believe

[[S9|§9, Appendices A, D and E]]

What holds up.

  • The measurements reproduce from released data. The authors released the per-task success counts of all 84 model-benchmark runs. From them this page recomputes, independently, all 84 coverage values and all 84 tax values of {{Table 7|ax:A4.T5}}, all 252 category shares of {{Table 6|ax:A3.T4}} and all 294 numbers of {{Table 8|ax:A4.T6}}, to the printed precision; the crossovers and Appendix C's spot numbers also reproduce ({{every check|tab:t-tables:tbChecks}}). Ten of the 84 budget-averaged coverage values are off by up to 0.001, in both directions, which changes no conclusion.
  • The sample is large where it matters. 200 to 770 tasks per benchmark, 128 attempts per task, and a paired task bootstrap for the coverage gap ({{A.4|ax:A1.SS4}}). The "36 of 42" count is robust: 31 of the 36 positive TaxS values exceed 0.03.
  • It agrees with the math and code evidence ({{Yue et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837}}) on a domain chosen to be hard for that conclusion, and the paper states its own limits: observational, undisclosed recipes, binary success only ({{§9|ax:S9}}).
  • The theory is correct and modest. Proposition 1 and Theorems 2 and 3 are short identities under independent attempts; the Theorem 2 lab in this tab applies the first identity to real tasks.

What to discount.

  • Not like for like. The base models get a harness the authors designed with preliminary experiments on base models; the post-trained models get each benchmark's native interface. Each is in its better configuration ({{Table 4|ax:A1.T2}}), which is fair, but the size of the gap depends on the harness. Post-trained models also run with thinking switched off and at one shared temperature (0.4 or 0.7), not their recommended settings; thinking raises their pass@k ({{Figure 10|ax:A1.F9}}, two models on BFCL only), and heating them recovers some coverage (Appendix C.4). So the absolute tax is a function of the decoding setup.
  • Two counts, two questions. The tax is positive in 36 of 42 cells: post-training lowered what extra attempts are worth. "Base beats post-trained at pass@128" is a different and smaller count: the base model has the higher pass@128 in 26 cells, clearly in 18, and the post-trained model is clearly ahead in 13. Quoting the paper's "most settings" for the second overstates it.
  • One split, one draw. Figure 7's ρ = 0.85 comes from one task split; over 200 splits the replay averages 0.79. The 32-attempt harness ablation (Table 4) and the 128-attempt main runs are separate evaluations of the same setups and differ by 0.9 points of pass@1 on average, up to 7.0 (gemma-4-31B base on BFCL: 32.8 against 25.8).
  • PTGS rests on five runs in two toy games with one 7B model. Measured in standard errors from the printed intervals ({{Table 9|ax:A5.T7}}), the Sokoban gains in pass@1 and pass@128 are about 2.4 and 2.7 standard errors, the FrozenLake ones 0.5 and 1.5, and the tax reductions 0.9 and 1.0, inside the noise. The GRPO rows have no intervals and use best-validation checkpoints with τ and target chosen per environment on validation success. A fixed temperature of 0.5 paid a smaller tax than PTGS in both games.
  • PTGS's assumption can fail. The posterior lags behind the policy (the paper measures it about 0.1 low). In this page's toy, one prompt the policy now solves 78% of the time still has a posterior mean of 0.09, gets heated to 1.42, and falls to 53% ({{see it|tab:t-toy}}).
  • Numbering. The arXiv HTML drops the two main-text tables' numbers and renumbers the appendix (its Table 5 is the PDF's Table 7); the text follows the PDF. This page uses the PDF numbers.

Verdict. The diagnostic part is solid: the tax is a sensible, cheap, reproducible summary of how much retrying is worth, and on these 42 cells post-training clearly reduced it, most for large models. The "base models are better agents at large k" headline holds for larger models on WebShop and ACEBench, not across the board. PTGS is a plausible, cheap idea whose evidence is thin: consistent direction, effect sizes within noise for the tax itself, and a fixed low temperature as a rival the paper's own table shows but does not discuss.

What it takes to use this

  • Measuring the tax: base and post-trained checkpoints, a verifier and repeated attempts per task. The metric code is a few lines of numpy ({{metrics.py|https://github.com/changdaeoh/sharpening-tax}}, or {{Appendix A.6|ax:A1.SS6}}); this page's replay runs it in the browser. The paper's full grid is 2 × 14 pairs × 1,470 tasks × 128 attempts, about 5.3 million episodes (calculated); the paper's own check suggests 8 attempts on a subset already rank models usefully.
  • The harness: released ({{harness.py|https://github.com/changdaeoh/sharpening-tax}}), with vLLM serving recipes per family (vLLM 0.19.0 in the paper). It is deliberately minimal; expect benchmark-specific tuning to change base-model numbers.
  • PTGS: a controller of about 120 lines with four call sites in a trainer: one temperature draw per prompt group, a count update after the group, the pivot ramp per step, and log-probabilities computed at the group's temperature. It needs a finite pool in which prompts recur, since the posterior is per prompt. Hyperparameters from the paper: τ 1.2 to 1.5, γ = 0.95, target 0.25 rising to 0.5. The RL training code behind Table 2 is not released (one config file, "a record, not a launcher"). Licence: CC BY-NC 4.0, so commercial use means reimplementing from the paper.
  • When it pays: large models with a large test-time budget and a verifier (where the tax is largest and routing helps), and group-based RL where many prompts produce zero-success groups. For small models and single-attempt use, post-training's sharpening is what you want.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

The teach-or-elicit question decides where post-training compute should go. This paper adds the agentic domain to the math and code evidence, and turns "look at where the curves cross" into one reportable number. It also argues post-training should be reported with what it costs in reach, not only with pass@1 ({{§8|ax:S8}}). That echoes the elicitation view of post-training ({{Lambert, Interconnects|https://www.interconnects.ai/p/elicitation-theory-of-post-training}}). On the other side stand results where long RL does expand coverage, such as {{ProRL|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.24864}}; the knowledge base keeps that debate in {{RL for LLMs, section 9|n:3c65c17b0d0d818c9bcff7177325fe56}}. The released per-task counts are worth more than any single figure: anyone can recompute a different summary from them.

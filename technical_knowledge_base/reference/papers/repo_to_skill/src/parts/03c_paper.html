Result 1: MLE-bench, 31.1% to 72.9% of competitions medalled

[[S5.SS2|§5.1, §5.2, Table 1, Appendix A.2.1]]

Setup for all four benchmarks ({{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1}}): Codex as the harness, GPT-5.5 at extra-high reasoning effort as the backbone, both fixed. "The only controlled factor is whether the agent is equipped with DisCo-distilled skills." Skill construction finishes before the benchmark run, and the two arms get the same running budget; the one-time construction budget is "separate and is not counted in either run-time condition".

The skills that lifted Codex on MLE-bench came from where?

From {{§4.3|ax:S4.SS3}}, {{§5|ax:S5}} ("We evaluate DisCo beyond the public repository snapshot") and {{Appendix A.2|ax:A1.SS2}}. None of the four benchmark results uses the repository library.

MLE-bench ({{Chan et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07095}}) is 75 Kaggle competitions in three tiers, scored by the share earning any medal on held-out data. Each competition's skill came from web search (its own page blocked) and diagnostic trials, each revision seeing the previous skill, a log summary and a result analysis ({{Eq. 9|ax:A1.E9}}); it is descriptive only, so no fixed solution is replayed. Then Codex gets up to 24 GPU-hours with the skill, the no-skill arm the same 24 ({{Table 5|ax:A1.T5}}).

Any Medal, mean ± SEM ({{Table 1|ax:S5.T1}}). Codex rows: three runs each. Public rows are copied from the {{MLE-bench leaderboard|https://github.com/openai/mle-bench}} and use other harnesses, backbones and run counts.

  • Overall: 31.11% to 72.89%, +41.78 points, a 134.3% relative gain. By tier: +43.94 (Low), +37.72 (Medium), +48.89 (High).
  • High tier: 13.33% to 62.22%, +366.8%, 4.67 times; the authors' reading is that harder tasks make unguided trial and error costlier.
  • Against the leaderboard: 72.89% beats the best public entry (Famou-Agent 2.0, 64.44%) by 8.45 points, and the best per tier by 6.06, 5.26 and 15.55, "without a custom execution harness".
  • The runs behind the means (derived, unique fits to the printed mean and SEM): 20, 25 and 25 medals of 75 for Codex alone, 53, 55 and 56 with skills.

The gap is far outside noise (standard error 2.51 from the SEMs, 7.41 per competition sampling). What it measures is the question for {{How much to believe|tab:t-read:s-trust}}.

Result 2: PaperBench, 29.45 to 39.59 replication score

[[S5.SS3|§5.3, Table 2, Appendix A.2.2]]

PaperBench ({{Starace et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01848}}) asks an agent to replicate 20 ICML 2024 papers from scratch; an official LLM grader scores each attempt against a rubric. Each target got a pool of paper-derived skills from its related work, never from the target or its code ({{Appendix A.2.2|ax:A1.SS2.SSS2}}). The average rises from 29.45 to 39.59 (+10.14, 34.4% relative). Sort the 20 papers:

{{Table 2|ax:S5.T2}}: each paper's score without skills (open dot) and with (filled), one run per arm as far as the paper says (no repeats or error bars are reported).

  • Broad: 18 of 20 papers improve. Largest gains: rice +40.57, sequential-neural +23.70, what-will-my-model-forget +21.10. In multiples: ftrl 1.50 to 17.17 (11.4 times), rice 7.94 to 48.51 (6.1), what-will-my-model-forget 9.35 to 30.45 (3.3).
  • Largest gains where the baseline is low; papers already at 52.93 (all-in-one) or 56.65 (bam) gain only 1.77 and 2.18.
  • Two regress: sample-specific-masks 57.11 to 52.04 (−5.07) and stay-on-topic 32.31 to 27.79 (−4.52), both above the 29.45 average. The authors suggest "a possible retrieval-precision failure": a retrieved skill can pull the agent away from a narrow, idiosyncratic choice it would have found unaided, and better routing or a fallback to unguided reasoning might help.
  • Statistics (derived, papers as the unit): 95% interval for the mean gain [4.91, 15.37]; sign test p = 0.0004; without rice the mean gain is still 8.53.

Result 3: FrontierCS, 70.63 to 77.14

[[S5.SS4|§5.4, Table 3, Appendix A.2.3]]

FrontierCS ({{Mang et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15699}}) poses open-ended computer-science problems whose quality is measurable even when the optimum is unknown. All 188 Agent Track tasks run through Harbor with a 5-hour budget, 2 CPUs and 4 GiB; the agent writes a C++ solver, may submit repeatedly and keeps its best score (judge limits 0.25 to 100 s, 128 MiB to 2 GiB). One frozen recovery graph serves every task: a router plus eight modules (method selection, implementation, checkers, validation, interactive, reactive online, testlib judging, plateau escape), seeded from two human-written guides and refined by paired trials on "selected development problems" ({{Appendix A.2.3|ax:A1.SS2.SSS3}}). Its 9 nodes and 42 directed links recount exactly from the release.

  • Score: +6.51 points (9.22%); paired bootstrap 95% interval [3.41, 9.83]. 74 tasks improve (by 22.23 on average), 66 are effectively unchanged, so 48 degrade (derived) by 8.76 on average; gains total 3.91 times the losses.
  • Where it comes from: on the 47 tasks Codex alone scored below 50, the mean rises from 19.43 to 45.99 (+26.56), and 30 of them cross 50. That alone is 6.64 points of the 6.51 average, so on the other 141 tasks the net change is about −0.17 points (derived).
  • Skills were used on 180 of 188 tasks; gain 5.54 on the 102 tasks without sub-agents, 7.66 on the 86 with.
The paper stresses a fixed budget. Per FrontierCS task, how many tokens did the skill-equipped Codex use compared with Codex alone?

{{Table 3|ax:S5.T3}}: score against mean tokens per task (log scale). Leaderboard rows are copied from the {{FrontierCS leaderboard|https://frontier-cs.org/#leaderboard}}; the two Codex rows are the authors' runs.

The budget held fixed is the five-hour limit, not the spend. Skills made Codex search harder: tokens 2.46M to 4.47M per task (1.82 times), steps 55.9 to 88.7 (1.59), tool calls 64.7 to 105.0 (1.62). The paper's answer: per-task gains are essentially uncorrelated with the extra usage (Spearman ρ 0.006 for tokens, 0.014 for steps, 0.015 for tool calls). Against the leaderboard, Claude Code with Claude Opus 4.8 and with Qwen3.7 Max use 3.29 and 3.10 times the tokens of Codex with skills while scoring 2.64 and 15.24 points lower; Codex with skills makes 24.5 to 27.9% fewer tool calls and 33.8 to 75.0% fewer steps (printed 75.1; 1 − 88.7 / 355.4 is 75.04%): Pareto dominance "under the leaderboard's reported accounting".

Result 4: PassNet, AS Score 1.343 to 1.531

[[S5.SS5|§5.5, Table 4, Table 6, Appendix A.2.4]]

PassNet ({{Liu et al. 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29357}}) asks for a graph-compiler pass: a pattern matcher and rewriter that keeps an FX graph's semantics and makes it faster. The AS Score is the geometric mean of rectified subgraph speedups over tolerances t from −10 to 4 (incorrect or unmatched passes floored at 0.1), aggregated by a weighted geometric mean; the other metrics are at t = −5. 200 samples on A100-SXM4-40GB ({{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1}}).

{{Table 4|ax:S5.T4}}, relative to eager execution. Fast_1 is the share of correct subgraphs at least as fast as eager.

  • AS Score 1.343 to 1.5313 (+0.1883, 14.0%); G-Mean Speedup 1.5891 to 1.6688.
  • Fewer broken passes: failed samples 14 to 5 (64.3% fewer); correctness 81.35% to 90.76% (+9.41 points). The skill supplies procedures, rejection criteria and recovery actions for matching, correctness checks and tuning.
  • Past the compiler: TorchInductor (torch.compile) scores 1.419, so Codex with skills beats it, and Codex alone (1.343) does not.
  • Not discussed in the text: Fast_1 falls from 28.48% to 26.72%, so the extra correct passes are not more often faster than eager.

How the PassNet skill was built ({{Appendix A.2.4|ax:A1.SS2.SSS4}}): candidate procedures tried with and without on 50 training instances, then a CPU screen over the 4,000+ training instances (solving all would take over 20 days) dispatched to sub-agents that summarised recurring patterns, about a day per pass. On 50 training tasks with Claude Code and DeepSeek v4 pro ({{Table 6|ax:A1.T6}}) the geometric mean rose 0.5352 to 0.6941, match failures fell from 10 of 50 to 1 and solve time from 40 to 22 minutes; the arithmetic mean fell (0.9566 to 0.7492) because two no-skill outliers (10.63 and 8.18) scored 0.99 and 0.10 on a repeat. Chasing them exposed a rule that was too absolute: "never reimplement vendor-optimised heavy operators" blocked a conv3d with stride equal to kernel size, which reduces to elementwise multiplication; the agent saw the rewrite and gave it up. The rule became "a defeasible prior rather than an absolute ban", a concrete case of the misapplication risk below.

Problem

§1 Introduction, Figures 1 and 2

By late 2021 diffusion models led image synthesis, class-conditional ImageNet and super-resolution among them, but they ran in pixel space and were very expensive: "training the most powerful DMs often takes hundreds of GPU days (e.g. 150 - 1000 V100 days)", and because the same network runs "sequentially for a large number of steps (e.g. 25 - 1000 steps)", "producing 50k samples takes approximately 5 days on a single A100" (§1, both citing ADM). That last figure checks out: Table 18 gives pixel-space ADM 0.12 samples per second, and 50,000 / 0.12 s is days (Table 18, recomputed independently).

The root cause, the authors argue, is where a likelihood model spends its effort. Being mode-covering, such models "spend excessive amounts of capacity (and thus compute resources) on modeling imperceptible details" (§1). A rate-distortion plot of a trained pixel-space DDPM makes it visible: most bits of an image encode details you cannot see, and the semantic composition costs few. Learning splits into a perceptual compression stage that removes high-frequency detail and a semantic compression stage that learns what the image is (Figure 2, with data from DDPM). The DDPM page rebuilds that rate-distortion curve from DDPM's own table: more than half of the 3.75 bits per dimension describe imperceptible distortion. Yet the network and its gradients "still need to be evaluated on all pixels" (Figure 2).

Earlier two-stage models already worked on a compressed space, but for autoregressive transformers, which force heavy compression into a 1-D token sequence: VQ-VAE-2, VQGAN with a transformer, DALL-E. "The high compression rates required for feasible ARM training ... limit the overall performance" (§2). Figure 1 puts numbers on the cost of that compression, reconstructing ImageNet images: this paper's f = 4 autoencoder reaches PSNR 27.4 and reconstruction FID 0.58; DALL-E's f = 8 tokenizer 22.8 and 32.01; VQGAN's f = 16 19.9 and 4.98 (Figure 1, Table 8).

The question: can a diffusion model skip the imperceptible bits, keep the fidelity, and train and sample on a budget an ordinary lab can afford?

Idea

§3 Method

Separate the two kinds of compression and give each to the model that is good at it. First train an autoencoder once, so its latent space is "perceptually equivalent" to the image space but much smaller; then run ordinary diffusion in that latent, and decode once at the end (§3). Because a UNet is built for 2-D grids, the latent can stay a 2-D grid and the compression can be mild (a factor f of 4 or 8 per side, not 16 to 32), which keeps the details the earlier token models lost (§3). The authors call the result Latent Diffusion Models, LDMs.

Below, two real toy models trained for this page (the Generate tab has the details) generate the same prompt: one diffuses 32 × 32 × 3 pixels, the other the 8 × 8 × 3 latent of an f = 4 autoencoder, both with about 50,000 parameters, the same training steps and the same sampler. LDM-4's latent has 16 times fewer spatial positions than the image. With the UNets matched in parameter count, how much faster does it sample than pixel-space diffusion in the paper's measurement?

Not 16, because the latent models spend their parameter budget differently: with the totals matched (391M to 396M), LDM-4's UNet has channel multipliers 1, 2, 3, 5 on four levels against LDM-1's 1, 1, 2, 2, 4, 4 on six (Table 13), so each latent position carries more channels; the decoder pass and the conditioning also cost time. Stage 1: perceptual image compression

§3.1, Appendix G

The autoencoder follows VQGAN: an encoder ℰ maps an RGB image x ∈ ℝH×W×3 to z = ℰ(x) ∈ ℝh×w×c, a decoder 𝒟 reconstructs x̃ = 𝒟(ℰ(x)), and the downsampling factor is f = H/h = W/w = 2m (§3.1). It is trained with a perceptual loss (LPIPS) plus a patch-based adversarial loss, which keeps reconstructions "confined to the image manifold by enforcing local realism and avoids bluriness introduced by relying solely on pixel-space losses such as L2 or L1" (§3.1).

LAutoencoder = minℰ,𝒟 maxψ ( Lrec(x, 𝒟(ℰ(x))) − Ladv(𝒟(ℰ(x))) + log Dψ(x) + Lreg(x; ℰ, 𝒟) )
Equation 25 (Appendix G): Dψ is the patch discriminator, Lreg one of the two regularisers below.

To avoid "arbitrarily high-variance latent spaces" the latent gets one of two mild regularisers (§3.1, Appendix G):

  • KL-reg.: a KL penalty towards a standard normal, as in a VAE, but weighted by about 10−6, so it is a barely regularised VAE (the released config sets kl_weight: 0.000001, autoencoder config).
  • VQ-reg.: a vector-quantisation layer with a large codebook, "absorbed by the decoder": the diffusion model works on z before quantisation, so the model "can be interpreted as a VQGAN" with the quantiser as the first layer of 𝒟.

Two choices make this different from earlier tokenizers. The latent keeps its 2-D layout, where VQGAN and DALL-E impose "an arbitrary 1D ordering" for an autoregressive model; and the compression is mild, f = 4 to 8 (a 256² image becomes 64 × 64 × 3 or 32 × 32 × 4), so reconstructions stay faithful (§3.1, Table 8). The autoencoder is "trained only once" and reused for every diffusion model and task (§1). The paper's own autoencoders are on the Tables tab; here is the toy's.

Try: build an image, then see what the toy f = 4 autoencoder keeps. The latent is three 8 × 8 maps, 16 times fewer numbers; the decoder turns it back into 32 × 32 × 3. Note what is lost: the faint grain of the background, the "imperceptible detail" the second stage never has to model.

Rescaling the latent

A KL-reg. latent is not unit-variance, and its scale sets the signal-to-noise ratio the diffusion model sees. The paper divides z by its component-wise standard deviation σ̂, estimated from the first batch (Appendix G); without it the ratio is "very high, such that the model allocates a lot of semantic detail early on in the reverse denoising process", which shows up in convolutional sampling (D.1, Figure 15). VQ latents have variance close to 1 and need no rescaling. This is the scale_factor: 0.18215 in every Stable Diffusion v1 config (config); FLUX's 16-channel autoencoder uses 0.3611 (FLUX code, beyond the paper). The toy's encoder needed 1 / σ̂ = .

Stage 2: diffusion in the latent space

§3.2, Eq. 1 and 2, Appendix B

The diffusion part is DDPM unchanged (DDPM page, which runs it and explains the chain). A diffusion model is "an equally weighted sequence of denoising autoencoders" εθ(xt, t), trained to predict the noise in a noised input, with the reweighted bound (§3.2):

LDM = 𝔼x, ε∼𝒩(0,1), t [ ‖ε − εθ(xt, t)‖²2 ]
Equation 1 (§3.2), t uniform on {1, …, T}; Appendix B derives it from the ELBO in signal-to-noise form, SNR(t) = αt² / σt² (Appendix B).

The only change is the space:

LLDM := 𝔼ℰ(x), ε∼𝒩(0,1), t [ ‖ε − εθ(zt, t)‖²2 ]
Equation 2 (§3.2). εθ(∘, t) is a time-conditional UNet.

"Since the forward process is fixed, zt can be efficiently obtained from ℰ during training, and samples from p(z) can be decoded to image space with a single pass through 𝒟" (§3.2). For a KL latent, training draws z = ℰμ(x) + ℰσ(x) · ε, then rescales it (Appendix G). Why it is cheap: every one of the tens to hundreds of denoising steps runs on f² fewer spatial positions (16 times for f = 4, 64 for f = 8), while the latent stays a grid, so the UNet keeps its 2-D convolutional inductive bias (§3.2). Unlike earlier two-stage work, the prior is learned separately from a frozen first stage, so there is no "delicate weighting of reconstruction and generative abilities" as in LSGM, which trains both jointly (§1, §4.2).

Conditioning via cross-attention

§3.3, Eq. 3, Figure 3, Appendix E.2.1

To make the model conditional on arbitrary inputs y (text, semantic maps, layouts), a domain-specific encoder τθ turns y into a sequence τθ(y) ∈ ℝM×dτ, and cross-attention layers inside the UNet let every position of a flattened feature map look at that sequence (§3.3):

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKT / √d) · V,   Q = WQ(i) · φi(zt),   K = WK(i) · τθ(y),   V = WV(i) · τθ(y)
φi(zt) ∈ ℝN×dεi is the UNet's flattened intermediate map: queries come from the image, keys and values from the condition (§3.3, Figure 3).
LLDM := 𝔼ℰ(x), y, ε∼𝒩(0,1), t [ ‖ε − εθ(zt, t, τθ(y))‖²2 ]
Equation 3 (§3.3): τθ and εθ are trained jointly.
  • Text: τθ is an unmasked transformer over BERT-tokenised prompts, trained from scratch with the UNet: 32 layers of width 1,280, 77 tokens (§4.3.1, Table 17, Eq. 18 to 23). In the UNet, each self-attention layer of the "ablated UNet" becomes a small transformer block of self-attention, an MLP and cross-attention (Table 16).
  • Layouts: each bounding box becomes a (top-left, bottom-right, class) token; class labels: τθ is one learned 512-dimensional embedding, so ImageNet conditioning is also cross-attention (E.2.1).
  • Spatially aligned conditions (super-resolution, inpainting, semantic maps) are simply concatenated to the UNet input channel-wise (§4.3.2). Because such a model is convolutional, one trained at 256² generalises at sampling time to larger images, "up to the megapixel regime", about 1,024² (§4.3.2, Figure 9); with rescaled latents and guidance the text model also renders beyond 256² (Figure 13).

The best numbers also use classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans, the paper's [32]): the model is trained with the condition sometimes dropped, and sampling extrapolates from the unconditional prediction towards the conditional one, ε̂ = εθ(zt, ∅) + s (εθ(zt, y) − εθ(zt, ∅)). The v2 changelog says both the text-to-image and ImageNet models were updated to use it (Appendix A).

The same 1.45B text-to-image LDM, sampled with and without classifier-free guidance (s = 1.5). What happens to its MS-COCO FID (lower is better)?

How much compression: the f sweep

§4.1, Figures 6 and 7, Tables 8 and 13

The central experiment trains class-conditional ImageNet LDMs at f ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32} (LDM-1 is pixel space) with the computational resources fixed: "a single NVIDIA A100 for all experiments in this section", the same number of steps and the same number of parameters (§4.1). After 2M steps (Figure 6):

  • f = 1 and 2 "result in slow training progress": the diffusion model is left with most of the perceptual compression;
  • f = 32 causes "stagnating fidelity after comparably few training steps": the first stage has destroyed information;
  • f = 4 to 16 "strike a good balance", with "a significant FID gap of 38 between pixel-based diffusion (LDM-1) and LDM-8 after 2M training steps".

Against sampling speed (DDIM with 10 to 200 steps), LDM-4 and LDM-8 give the best trade-off on CelebA-HQ and ImageNet, and "complex datasets such as ImageNet require reduced compression rates" (Figure 7). The summary: "LDM-4 and -8 offer the best conditions for achieving high-quality synthesis results" (§4.1). Figures 6 and 7 exist only as images, so the curves are not rebuilt here; the chart below uses what is printed: the reconstruction quality of every autoencoder (Table 8) and, as a measure of cost, the batch size each LDM could afford on the same A100 (Table 13).

Results

§4 Experiments, Tables 1 to 7
  • Unconditional generation: a new state-of-the-art FID of 5.11 on CelebA-HQ 256², ahead of GANs and LSGM; competitive but not first on FFHQ (4.98 against ProjectedGAN's 3.08), LSUN-Churches (4.02 against 1.59) and LSUN-Bedrooms (2.95 against 1.52 and ADM's 1.90). Precision and recall are as good as or better than the GANs' everywhere (Table 1, §4.2). On Bedrooms it comes close to ADM with half the parameters and about 4 times less training compute (recomputed on the Tables tab).
  • Text-to-image: a 1.45B-parameter KL-reg. LDM-8 trained on LAION-400M reaches FID 12.63 on MS-COCO 256² with classifier-free guidance (23.31 without), against 12.24 for GLIDE and 11.84 for Make-A-Scene, which have 4 to 6 times the parameters (Table 2). Table 2 lists GLIDE at 6B; the GLIDE paper describes a 3.5B model plus a 1.5B upsampler, 5B in all (GLIDE §4). This model is the direct ancestor of Stable Diffusion.
  • Class-conditional ImageNet 256²: FID 3.60 with guidance, against pixel-space ADM-G's 4.59, with 400M parameters against 608M (Table 3) and 271 V100-days against 962 (Table 18).
LDM-4-G beats ADM-G on ImageNet (3.60 against 4.59 FID). How much training compute did it use, relative to ADM-G?

271 V100-days against 962 (916 for the diffusion model plus 46 for ADM's noisy-image classifier): 3.5 times less (Table 18). The LDM's A100-days were converted at the paper's assumed 2.2× A100 speed-up, so this ratio carries that assumption.

  • Efficiency against a matched pixel model: on inpainting with parameters fixed, "a speed-up of at least 2.7× between pixel- and latent-based diffusion models while improving FID scores by a factor of at least 1.6×" (§4.5, Table 6). The speed-up holds; the FID factor holds for the two rows with attention (1.63× and 1.65×) but is 1.55× for the attention-free one. The old page stated this as a general result; it is measured on the inpainting task only.
  • Super-resolution (×4, ImageNet, bicubic degradation as in SR3): FID 2.8 against SR3's 5.2, while SR3 has the better IS (180.1 against 166.3), and a plain regression model wins PSNR and SSIM, metrics that "favor blurriness" (Table 5). The user study is against the paper's own pixel-space baseline, not SR3: LDM-4 preferred in 70.6% of pairs (Table 4). A model trained with more varied degradations, LDM-BSR, generalises to real images up to 1,024² (D.6.1).
  • Inpainting on Places: FID 1.50 on 512² test crops for the larger 387M model fine-tuned at 512², against LaMa's 2.21 and CoModGAN's 1.82; LPIPS is slightly worse than LaMa's, which the authors attribute to LaMa producing one average-looking result against the LDM's diverse ones; people preferred the LDM in 68.1% of pairs (Table 7, Table 4).
  • Layouts and semantic maps: layout-to-image on COCO at 40.91 FID, ahead of the GAN baselines when fine-tuned from OpenImages (Table 9); semantic synthesis generalises convolutionally to megapixel landscapes (Figure 9).

Limitations and societal impact

§5

The authors' own caveats: sampling is still sequential and "slower than that of GANs", and the autoencoder's reconstruction "can become a bottleneck for tasks that require fine-grained accuracy in pixel space"; they assume the super-resolution models are "already somewhat limited in this respect" (§5). On impact, cheaper training "democratize[s]" the technology and also makes it easier "to create and disseminate manipulated data or spread misinformation and spam", deep fakes disproportionately affect women, generative models can reveal their training data, and the two-stage approach may misrepresent the data in ways that remain "an important research question" (§5).

How much of this to believe

the paper's evidence, judged

What holds up.

  • The efficiency claim recomputes from the paper's own tables: Table 6's speed-ups are 2.7× to 4.9×, Table 18 has LDM-4-G at 3.5× less compute than ADM-G and LSUN-Bedrooms at 3.9× less than ADM, and the 5-day sampling figure matches ADM's printed throughput (Tables tab).
  • The f sweep is the right experiment: one GPU, the same steps and parameters for every f, and the learning rates "maximize[d] ... such that they still train stably" per model rather than fixed in a way that favours one (E.3.5).
  • Later history is the strongest evidence: Stable Diffusion, SDXL, SD3, FLUX, DiT and Sora all generate in an autoencoder's latent (Why it matters, below).
  • The authors are candid: they report where they are not first (FFHQ, the LSUN sets, Make-A-Scene), that LPIPS is worse than LaMa's, that two FID implementations disagree (7.76 against 7.77, 2.95 against 3.0, E.3.1), and their own limitations.

Weaknesses.

  • Single runs, no error bars on FID. Every FID is one trained model; IS has ± values, FID never does. Figures 6 and 7 use 5,000 samples "for efficiency reasons", so they "might vary from those shown in Tab. 1 and 10" (E.3.5). Margins such as 12.63 against 12.24 are within what an FID implementation alone moves.
  • Not like for like. Rival numbers are copied from other papers, not rerun (Table 2's GLIDE and Make-A-Scene from [26], Table 18's compute from ADM); the LDM compute is A100-days converted at an assumed 2.2× (Table 18). The ImageNet win uses classifier-free guidance at a chosen scale against ADM's classifier guidance. The inpainting record is the bigger, fine-tuned model; the matched 215M models land at LaMa's level (2.15 and 2.37 against 2.21).
  • The tables disagree with each other. LSUN-Churches LDM-8 appears as 200 steps (Table 1) and 100 steps at 410K iterations (Table 18) against 500k (Table 12), with 294M or 256M parameters, and Figure 30 prints FID 4.48 for the 4.02 model; CelebA-HQ is 5.11 in Table 1 and 5.15 in Figure 28; Table 18's Bedrooms row has overall compute (55) below generator compute (60); Table 13 prints the LDM-32 latent as "88 × 8 × 32" (Tables tab).
  • The headline text-to-image model is under-documented: its hyperparameters are in Table 15, but its training compute appears nowhere, and "on par" means slightly behind: Make-A-Scene's 11.84 is the table's bold best (Table 2).
  • The cost argument is in time, not FLOPs. The paper never counts the UNets' FLOPs; it measures throughput on one GPU setup, and the authors note Table 6 deviates from Figure 7 "due to varying GPU settings/batch sizes".

Verdict. Believe the idea and the efficiency: they are measured, they recompute, and the field adopted them wholesale. Treat individual FIDs as single runs good to about ±0.5, the cross-table details as carelessly copied, and the text-to-image comparison as "close", not "better". The toy on this page agrees in direction at small scale and says where it cannot test the claim (Generate tab).

"What it takes to use this" is left out: the method is the standard recipe of every open image model, and Then and now covers what replaced each part. Why it matters (beyond the paper)

This is the paper that became Stable Diffusion. The CompVis README describes it as "a Latent Diffusion Model on 512x512 images from a subset of the LAION-5B database", with "a frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 text encoder", "its 860M UNet and 123M text encoder", running "on a GPU with at least 10GB VRAM"; v1 uses "a downsampling-factor 8 autoencoder" and "was pretrained on 256x256 images and then finetuned on 512x512 images" (SD README); its licence is dated August 22, 2022. The config is this paper's: an f = 8 KL autoencoder with 4 latent channels, a cross-attention UNet with context dimension 768, the same 0.18215 scale factor (v1 config). Open weights put a text-to-image model on consumer GPUs.

The deeper contribution is the recipe: generate in a perceptually compressed latent and let a cheap decoder handle the last octave of detail. SD 2 and SDXL kept the latent UNet (SDXL's is 2.6B parameters with two text encoders, SDXL Table 1). DiT replaced the UNet with a Transformer on latent patches, using "an off-the-shelf pre-trained variational autoencoder (VAE) model from Stable Diffusion" (DiT §5). Stable Diffusion 3 moved to a rectified-flow objective and a Transformer but still denoises an f = 8 latent, now with 16 channels because "increasing the number of latent channels d significantly boosts reconstruction performance" (SD3 §5.2.1); FLUX.1 is "a 12 billion parameter rectified flow transformer" on a 16-channel latent (model card). Video and audio followed: Sora is "trained on and subsequently generates videos within this compressed latent space" (Sora report); Stable Video Diffusion (arXiv 2311.15127), AudioLDM (arXiv 2301.12503) and Stable Audio (arXiv 2402.04825) are latent diffusion models by name. The old version of this page also counted Veo and many 3D and molecule systems; those are not checked against a source here (unconfirmed).

The cross-attention interface became the standard plug for conditions: ControlNet adds spatial control by copying the encoder of a "locked" Stable Diffusion (ControlNet), and IP-Adapter adds image prompts through a "decoupled cross-attention mechanism" (IP-Adapter). And the democratisation the paper aimed at happened: hobbyists fine-tune Stable Diffusion with low-rank adapters on its attention layers (Hugging Face, LoRA for Stable Diffusion).

Connections

  • DDPM (2020-06): supplies the entire diffusion objective and the ε-predicting UNet; LDM changes only where the chain runs.
  • CLIP (2021-02): this paper uses a BERT-tokenised transformer trained from scratch, but Stable Diffusion swapped in CLIP's frozen text encoder as τθ.
  • ViT (2020-10): the DiT successors replace LDM's UNet with a Transformer while keeping its latent space.
  • Attention Is All You Need (2017-06): cross-attention conditioning is the Transformer's encoder-decoder attention grafted into a UNet.
  • LoRA (2021-06): the usual way Stable Diffusion is fine-tuned on a small budget.
  • Topics: generative-and-multimodal (diffusion, VAEs, text-to-image).

Generate in latent space: a toy LDM against a toy pixel-space DM

Two text-conditioned diffusion models trained for this page the paper's way, running in your browser. The data are 32 × 32 images of one shape (circle, square or triangle) in one of four colours at one of nine positions, on a light background with faint grain; the prompt is three tokens, such as "red circle, top left". Stage 1 is a KL-regularised autoencoder with f = 4 (32 × 32 × 3 to 8 × 8 × 3, §3.1); stage 2 is a UNet with cross-attention to a one-block transformer τθ over the prompt (§3.3), trained with Eq. 3 and 10% empty prompts for classifier-free guidance. The pixel-space model is the same design on 32 × 32 × 3 with two more resolution levels, at a matched parameter count, trained for the same steps on the same data (§4.1's protocol). Every sample is graded by a fixed rule that reads shape, colour and position from the pixels.

What survived

Generating in the latent of a separately trained, mildly compressing autoencoder with f = 8 (every system on the card), a rescaled KL latent, conditioning on a text encoder's token sequence, and classifier-free guidance. What did not: the UNet (Transformers in DiT, SD3, FLUX and Sora), noise prediction (velocity and rectified flow), 4 latent channels (16 in SD3 and FLUX), and a text encoder trained from scratch (frozen CLIP, OpenCLIP and T5 encoders instead).

The plug-ins built on the cross-attention interface, ControlNet and IP-Adapter, and the latent models for audio, are in the paper tab's Why it matters.

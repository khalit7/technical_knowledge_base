Problem

§1 Introduction, Appendix A.2

Serving one request at a time, an autoregressive (AR) model is memory-bound: every new token needs all the weights and the whole KV cache streamed from GPU memory, so the time goes on moving data while the compute units sit mostly idle, and that caps how fast a single user's answer can arrive. Batching many requests restores throughput, but not per-user speed (§1).

Speculative decoding is the usual fix: a small drafter proposes a candidate continuation, typically 8 tokens, and the large model verifies it in one pass. With a draft of 8 this keeps 3 to 6 tokens per forward pass (TPF; the paper cites EAGLE-3, Li et al. 2026). It is still limited: an AR drafter is itself sequential, and parallel drafters (Medusa, DFlash) lose acceptance at later draft positions, the "suffix decay" that DSpark patches with a small AR module (§1, Appendix A.2).

Text diffusion sidesteps the bottleneck by predicting a whole block of tokens at once, moving decoding from memory-bound towards compute-bound. By mid-2026 the fast, capable ones (Gemini Diffusion, Mercury) were behind proprietary APIs, while the open-weights ones (LLaDA, Seed Diffusion, Nemotron-Labs-Diffusion) had limited reasoning or multimodal ability, or did not deliver the latency diffusion promises, or both (§1). And there was no public recipe for cheaply turning a frontier AR model into a competitive diffusion model.

The question: can an open AR model be converted, cheaply, into a text diffusion model that is both fast and still smart?

Idea

§1, §6 Eq. 12, Table 1

Finetune the released Gemma 4 26B A4B mixture-of-experts checkpoint into a discrete diffusion model with fewer than 10% of the AR model's training tokens, in two stages: supervised finetuning (SFT) for bidirectional denoising of 256-token blocks, then SD·RL, reinforcement learning and sampler distillation at once (§1, Figure 2). No diffusion pretraining: the AR weights are warm-started directly, and the only new parameters are a 7.8M-parameter self-conditioning MLP.

The model keeps Gemma 4's thinking mode, multimodal input and long context, and its weights can still decode autoregressively (§1).

Discrete diffusion over tokens

§2, Eq. 1 and 2, Figure 3

Diffusion basics (a forward process that corrupts data, a network that learns to reverse it, sampling that refines everything in parallel from noise) are on the DDPM page and the Stable Diffusion page. Text is discrete, and Gaussian diffusion on token embeddings (Diffusion-LM, SED, CDCD) drifts into regions where no token lives; rounding back to the nearest token gives incoherent text and breaks the exactness of the likelihood bounds (§2). So DiffusionGemma uses discrete diffusion on token states, whose corruptions are multi-step descendants of BERT's masking and random replacement (BERT page), in the continuous-time Markov chain framework of discrete flow matching (Campbell et al.; Gat et al.). The sequence being refined is the canvas, C tokens long, and the forward process factorises over positions (Eq. 1):

P(xt | x0) = ∏i [ κt δ(xti, x0i) + (1 − κt) / V ]
κt
noise schedule, from 1 at t = 0 (clean) to 0 at t = 1 (every token uniform over the vocabulary of size V)

This is multinomial (uniform) diffusion: a corrupted token becomes a random real token, not a [mask]. The difference matters for the rest of the paper, and it is easiest to see on one canvas:

Illustrative: the paper's Appendix F sample answer as words, a 40-word vocabulary, κt = 1 − t.

With masking every visible token is guaranteed clean, so a model learns to copy visible tokens and cannot revise them. With uniform noise any visible token might be noise, so the model judges every position, including its own earlier choices: tokens accepted earlier in the same canvas can still change, the paper's built-in self-correction (§3.2). Tokens of earlier canvases are frozen.

The reverse process needs the posterior over clean tokens given a noisy canvas, which the network pθ(v | xt) approximates; a transition rule Step (for example a discrete Euler step) turns it into the next canvas (Eq. 2; Figure 3 takes "blue moon" through "dark cloud" to "red sunset"). Positions updated in the same step cannot see each other's choices, which can produce local inconsistencies; self-conditioning and the sampler below counter it.

Block by block: a causal encoder and a bidirectional decoder with one set of weights

§3.1, §3.2, Eq. 3 to 6, Figure 4

Discrete flow matching works on a fixed length, so open-ended text is generated block-autoregressively, one 256-token canvas at a time (§3.1, Figure 4):

  1. Encode the context. The transformer runs with causal attention over the prompt (system instructions, user turns, interleaved image embeddings) and fills a KV cache, H = Encoderθ(h) (Eq. 3).
  2. Denoise a canvas. Start from 256 uniformly random tokens. The same transformer, now as the decoder, attends bidirectionally across the canvas and to the cache, and returns logits for every position, Lt = Decoderθ(xt, zt, H) (Eq. 5); the sampler below turns them into the next canvas, at most N times.
  3. Encode and append. The finished canvas goes through the causal encoder once and its keys and values are appended to the cache, H ← H ⊕ Encoderθ(x̂0) (Eq. 4). Repeat until the model writes the end-of-turn token.

This inverts the usual encoder-decoder (BART, T5: bidirectional encoder, causal decoder). Early context never sees later canvases, but appending a canvas encodes only the new tokens, so the growing context is never re-encoded and long reasoning stays cheap. The authors date the scheme to an unpublished June 2023 manuscript; Wu et al., Arriola et al. (block diffusion) and Deschenaux and Gulcehre found analogous designs (§3.1).

Inside one step (Eq. 6): p̂0 = Softmax(Lt / τt); the self-conditioning signal z = FFW(p̂0E), the expected token embedding under the prediction through a small feed-forward network, is added to the next step's input, so each step builds on the previous step's beliefs; then Step samples the next canvas. Work the paper cites finds these vectors still map onto an interpretable token bottleneck.

The sampler: how many passes a block gets

§3.3, Algorithm 1, Table 2, Figure 5, §3.4

The recommended sampler, Algorithm 1, has three parts, with the defaults of Table 2 (§3.3):

  • Entropy-bounded acceptance (Ben-Hamu et al. 2026, like MaskGIT): sort positions by predictive entropy and accept the longest lowest-entropy prefix whose entropies before the last sum to at most b = 0.1; those take a fresh sample from p̂0. Every other position is re-noised uniformly and explored again next pass. The bound keeps the mutual information between tokens accepted together small.
  • Temperature annealing, linear from τ = 0.8 at the start to 0.4 at the end: explore early, commit late.
  • Adaptive stopping: stop when the mean entropy over the canvas is below 0.005 and the argmax prediction is unchanged for two consecutive passes; at most N = 48 passes.

The model averages about 12 effective denoising steps per canvas, 4x fewer than 48 "without sacrificing generation quality"; structured tasks such as code take fewer steps than natural language, and harder problems more (Figure 5). Table 4's 15 benchmarks average .

Training: SFT, then SD·RL

§4, §5, Eq. 11, Figures 6 to 10

Stage 1: supervised finetuning

An extended finetuning phase teaches the checkpoint to predict 256-token blocks from noisy inputs (§4). A block-diagonal attention mask gives bidirectional attention inside each block and none between blocks; clean context comes through the encoder's KV cache. Per canvas a noise level t ~ U[0, 1] is drawn and each token is replaced with probability t by a uniformly drawn token; the loss is the cross-entropy between the prediction and the clean canvas (Eq. 11):

L(θ) = − Σi log pθ(x0i | xt, zt, H)

Quality rises fast, then log-linearly with training (Figures 6 and 7, at up to N = 192 steps). Non-thinking quality needs only moderate SFT; coherent thinking needs extended SFT (lower start, steeper log-linear slope), since the model first collapses into stuttering or cycles in long traces.

Stage 2: sampler distillation and reinforcement learning (SD·RL)

After SFT the model is good with many steps, somewhat behind the AR model on hard reasoning and coding, and it collapses in the few-step regime low latency needs (§5). Instead of separate alignment and distillation stages, SD·RL does both in one online stage: a joint objective, so one gradient update drives reward maximisation and sampler distillation (compressing the best output into few steps). The data adapts the Gemma 4 RL mix: helpfulness, mathematical reasoning, coding and instruction following, thinking and not. The model is its own online teacher, generating reference trajectories with many steps and mild annealing. The report gives no equation for the objective.

The self-paced curriculum. The objective drives entropy down; lower entropy makes adaptive stopping fire earlier; the teacher's trajectories, and so the training distribution, get shorter (Figure 8: reward up, teacher steps down). Unlike RL for AR models, continuing SD·RL after the reward plateaus still pays: less entropy is more speed.

What it bought (Figure 9, GPQA-Diamond and LiveCodeBench-v6 averaged, thinking, 3 seeds): about 10 points more quality and TPF from 5 to nearly 20, SFT at its best (N up to 192) against SD·RL at N = 48. At N = 48 the SFT model falls into repetition loops whose collapsed entropy trips adaptive stopping early: in Figures 16 and 17 it reasons correctly through a GPQA biology question and a harmonic-oscillator question, then degenerates into streams of "1" and ")" and answers nothing; after SD·RL both are right. SFT keeps improving with N up to 192 (roughly any-order AR); after SD·RL quality plateaus around 48 (Figure 10).

RL on AR reasoning models usually makes answers longer (DeepSeek-R1's grew as it trained). What did SD·RL do to the length of DiffusionGemma's answers?

Nearly 2x shorter than the SFT checkpoint (§5): brevity multiplies speed, so the objective rewards it, and the model forgoes the gains of long reasoning. On Table 3's 7 fully covered benchmarks DiffusionGemma writes 4,001 tokens to Gemma 4's 7,207 and needs about 203 forward passes to Gemma 4 with MTP's 5,148: 3.9%, the paper's "less than 5%". On the DeepSeek-R1 page answers grow with RL instead.

Speed: where the time of a pass goes

§6, Figures 11 and 12, Eq. 12

Training raised the tokens per pass; Section 6 lowers the cost of a pass. Serving one request is memory-bound (PagedAttention; Pope et al.), so diffusion's extra FLOPs are cheaper than AR's extra passes. Reference implementations are in Hugging Face Transformers and vLLM (§6). Figure 11 times one step on an H100 in FP8, one request, 4,096 prompt tokens: a DiffusionGemma step processes 256 times more tokens but takes only 3.2x as long, 12.63 ms against 4.01 ms of GPU time, plus about 1 ms of CPU work each end to end.

Figure 11's printed labels ("Other" = total minus the rest). Speed = TPF / (GPU time + 0.93 ms overhead); AR = 1 / 4.90 ms. Defaults reproduce Section 6's 1,456 tokens/s, 7.1x and 4.8x, by construction. Checkboxes are what-ifs; the paper says only that a dense model would cut the feed-forward slowdown to under 2x.

Three operations account for the difference (§6 "GPU time breakdown"):

  • MoE experts, 4.3x (1.08 to 4.66 ms): with one request the MoE layer's time is moving expert weights from HBM. One token activates 8 experts per layer, a 256-token canvas about 84 (PG-19), so loading them no longer amortises: verification's penalty in speculative decoding, at larger parallelism.
  • Sampling, 5.5x (0.56 to 3.06 ms): a full-precision softmax over the 262k vocabulary at all 256 positions plus the self-conditioning matmul, in torch.compile rather than custom kernels, for extensibility.
  • Attention, 4.1x (0.45 to 1.84 ms): bidirectional attention over the canvas cannot use single-token decode kernels, so it runs FlashAttention-4 (the text says 4x and "at most 2x" for the rest; the figure prints 2.1x for "Other").
each token picks 8 of 128 experts. If the 256 tokens of a canvas chose their experts independently and uniformly, how many distinct experts would one MoE layer have to load?

Derived: 128 × (1 − (120/128)n); 84 measured (§6).

Two kinds of pass (denoise, cache update) with different masks can share a batch and stopping is decided on the GPU; the vLLM integration avoids every CPU-GPU synchronisation with asynchronous scheduling and a per-sequence causal-attention flag (§6; vLLM blog).

Where diffusion behaves differently (Section 9, qualitative, no thinking)

  • Answer first (Figure 15): for 7 × (√(111 + 10) − 3) − 9², Gemma 4 commits to "−1" in its first token and appends a correction; DiffusionGemma's canvas goes −1, −15, then −25 as the reasoning around it forms, in 5 steps (the frog puzzle of G.1: "Yes" revised to "No" in 6).
  • Adaptive compute (G.2): 40 tokens in 7 steps when each bit depends on the two before it, 4 when it depends only on the input; AR needs 40. The toy runs the 3-bit version of these two tasks.
  • Constrained outputs (G.3): a strict JSON extraction converges in 2 steps and a Python bug fix, mostly copy, in 3; AR pays the same per token for boilerplate.

Finetuning it yourself (Section 8)

The released toolkit, on Hackable Diffusion, sums the encoder's next-token loss and the decoder's denoising loss on one random canvas, half the batch self-conditioned (Eq. 13; the toy trains the same way), with LoRA on every linear map, routers and self-conditioning included, on 2 × A100 80GB (§8). Sudoku (4,096 held-out puzzles): 0% before, 84.40% with LoRA rank 8 while effective steps fall from 40.65 to 10.72 (Table 5); full finetuning passes 85%. PubMedQA (LoRA rank 4): BLEU 10.76 to 20.67, but steps rise from 18.09 to 31.57 (Table 6). Early uses named by the paper: speech recognition (Interfaze) and radiology report drafting.

Limitations the authors state (Section 10)

A quality gap to the AR parent (no diffusion pretraining, a short SFT, a latency-targeted SD·RL, an AR-inherited architecture and data mix); conciseness multiplies speed but forgoes long-reasoning gains; occasional stuttering ("the the the"); a missing closing thought tag on multimodal prompts, which puts MMMU-Pro thinking (54.3) below non-thinking (66.0), found too late to fix; and AR regains the throughput lead around 32 concurrent requests, untuned for batching.

Why it matters beyond the paper

  • An existence proof for open, fast and smart text diffusion. Before it, the field forced a three-way trade between speed, intelligence and open access (§1). The weights are Apache 2.0 with Transformers and vLLM support, which makes diffusion a practical serving option rather than a demo; vLLM calls it the first diffusion LLM it supports natively (vLLM blog), and the model had 583,543 downloads on Hugging Face by 3 October 2026 (model page; a dated count).
  • The AR-to-diffusion conversion recipe is public and cheap: warm-start from AR weights, SFT for bidirectional denoising, then one online SD·RL stage that couples reward with sampler compression through an entropy-driven curriculum. Under 10% of the original training tokens, with thinking, multimodality and long context inherited rather than retrained. The objective of the second stage is described, not written down (see below).
  • Latency economics. About 20 tokens per pass against speculative decoding's 3 to 6 attacks the memory-bandwidth bottleneck directly, trading data movement for FLOPs as compute-to-bandwidth ratios keep growing. The per-step breakdown is a design datapoint: expert-weight transfer is the largest diffusion overhead, so dense or fewer-expert architectures suit diffusion decoding better (§6; compare Mixtral, where sparse experts are what make AR decoding cheap).
  • A counterexample to "RL makes outputs longer". SD·RL's entropy objective halves generation length while raising reward, the opposite of the length growth typical of RL with verifiable rewards (DeepSeek-R1), because here brevity is a speed multiplier being optimised for.

How much of this to believe

Tables 3 and 4, Figures 11 and 12, Appendix E

What holds up: the single-request speed. Eq. 12 on the paper's own per-step time gives exactly Section 6's 1,456 tokens/s; Table 4 is internally consistent (tokens / E2E time equals TPS within on all 15 benchmarks; the implied time per pass, , varies with context as Section 6 says); its mean effective steps, , match "about 12"; 203 forward passes against 5,148 is the "less than 5%". DiffusionGemma and Gemma 4 AR share one H100 setup, the code is public, and Unsloth reports faster still. See the checks on the Tables tab ().

What is not like for like.

  • The MTP baseline ran on a different workload (SPEED-Bench, says Table 3's note). 1.40 TPF at 303 tokens/s implies ms per MTP pass, less than a plain one-token AR step (4.90 ms), which a draft-and-verify pass cannot be on the same workload. On one workload (PG-19, Figure 12) the lead at one user is 4.11x, not 4.8x.
  • Mercury 2 was timed through an API (a fit to OpenRouter wall-clock times, inflated by runaway outputs). Against the vendor's ~1,000 or Artificial Analysis's ~987 tokens/s the lead is about 1.5x, not 2.5x. The authors state these caveats themselves, to their credit. LLaDA ran on 8 B200s and Nemotron in bfloat16.
  • "4x lower latency without sacrificing quality": 48 / 12 = 4 by construction, and no table shows quality with stopping off at N = 48.
  • The crossover at about 32 users is extrapolated: Figure 12 stops at 16 users (still 1.35x ahead); the ratios fall about 0.75 per doubling, reaching parity near .

Noise. Table 3 has no error bars or seed counts (Figures 9 and 10 average 3 seeds). From each benchmark's size, the binomial standard error of the gap to Gemma 4 (derived; sizes are the benchmarks' own):

Sizes: GPQA-Diamond 198 (Rein et al.), GSM8K 1,319 (Cobbe et al.), HumanEval 164 (Chen et al.), IFEval 541 (Zhou et al.), AIME 2026 30. Unpaired, one sample per problem assumed (the paper does not say).

The GPQA-Diamond, HumanEval and AIME gaps (about 2 standard errors) are real; GSM8K's and IFEval's are within noise. Natural2Code and HiddenMath are internal, as the paper flags.

Small inconsistencies: 1,456 tokens/s (Section 6) against 1,479 (Table 3); "at most 2x" against Figure 11's 2.1x; AR mode "squarely between" holds on 18 of 19 benchmarks (not IFEval with thinking, 97.2 against 97.4 and 98.7, nor LiveCodeBench without, 58.3 against 60.6 and 72.6); Table 7's caption says 2,000 LoRA steps, its Sudoku row 8,000; Table 4's tokens / forwards is of the printed TPF, so TPF is presumably averaged per answer, unstated.

Not reproducible from the report: SD·RL has no loss function, weighting or schedule, the "under 10%" token budget is never a number, and the released toolkit covers SFT only. The toy tests mechanisms, not quality claims.

Verdict. Believe the single-request speed and the forward-pass arithmetic. Treat the multiples against other systems and the 32-user crossover as rough, and the quality tax as real: large on hard reasoning (9 points on GPQA-Diamond, 19 on AIME 2026), negligible on easy tasks.

What it takes to use this

  • Weights and code: google/diffusiongemma-26B-A4B-it, Apache 2.0, published 9 June 2026, with reference implementations in Hugging Face Transformers and vLLM, GGUF builds for llama.cpp from Unsloth, and the LoRA finetuning recipes on Hackable Diffusion. The chat template is Gemma 4's (BOS token 2, EOS 1, turn and channel tags, thinking switched on with a think token in the system instruction, an empty thought channel otherwise, tool calls with their own delimiters; Appendix D). The SD·RL training code is not released.
  • Hardware: the paper's numbers are one H100 in FP8; 25.2B parameters at one byte each is about 25 GB of weights (derived), so one 80 GB GPU holds the model with room for cache. Finetuning: 2 × A100 80GB for LoRA, 8 × A100 80GB for the full-weight Sudoku recipe (Table 7).
  • When it pays off: one or a few concurrent users per GPU (measured to 16; about 32 extrapolated), latency-critical products, and constrained or copy-heavy outputs (JSON, code edits, structured extraction) that converge in 2 or 3 steps. Finetuning can cut steps as well as raise accuracy on structured tasks (Sudoku: 40.65 to 10.72 steps).
  • When it buys little: high-concurrency serving, where AR with batching wins on total throughput; hard long reasoning, where the gap to the AR parent is largest and the model's brevity caps it; multimodal thinking until the closing-tag bug is fixed. The same weights can decode autoregressively, so a router can send those requests to AR mode.

How Mercury 2's speed was estimated (Appendix E)

Mercury 2 is closed, so the authors queried it through OpenRouter (maximum sequence 50,000 tokens, caching disabled) in two rounds, 9 to 11 and 25 to 26 July 2026, with consistent results, and fitted Total time = α · fresh prefill tokens + β · output tokens + γ by non-negative least squares per benchmark, taking 1/β as the generation speed (Eq. 14): 452.6 tokens/s on GPQA-Diamond and 525.5 on LiveCodeBench-v6 (489 average, used in Figure 1), and 600 (high effort) and 547 (medium) over the seven benchmarks of Table 3. Some HumanEval and Natural2Code outputs were corrupted runs stuck in a thinking loop to the 50,000-token limit at very high speed, which biases the estimates upwards and is why high effort comes out faster than medium. Inception reports about 1,000 tokens/s on Blackwell GPUs and Artificial Analysis a median of about 987 on undisclosed hardware (both retrieved 28 July 2026); the authors attribute the difference to hardware, serving optimisations and datasets (Appendix E).

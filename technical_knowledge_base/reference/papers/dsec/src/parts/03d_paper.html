Why it matters (beyond the paper)

Every agentic RL recipe in this knowledge base assumes an environment supply that executes untrusted code at rollout rates a GPU cluster can consume, and none of them prices it. DSec is one of the first detailed public accounts of that layer at frontier scale (the paper's related work notes that MiMo-V2-Flash and ComputerRL mention their execution environments but focus on the model; {{§9|ax:S9}}). A comparable open-source platform, {{AgentENV|https://github.com/kvcache-ai/AgentENV}}, says in its README that it powers agentic RL training for Kimi K3 and has scaled to 1.5 million images in production; DSec's released storage code lives in that repository.

Two things to take from it, as the old version of this page put them. The ratio of 5,000 creations a second to about 160 nodes is what makes agentic RL affordable at this scale, and it is an infrastructure result rather than an algorithmic one: a lab without it is not running the same experiments, whatever recipe it copies. And the four-backend design is the transferable idea for anyone building smaller: isolation is a per-task cost, not a platform decision. Both are interpretations, not findings of the paper; the first is also hard to check, since the scale figures come without a measurement method (see below).

How much of this to believe

What holds up. The workload study and the mechanism results are internally consistent to a degree few systems papers are checked for: every figure ships as vector graphics, and decoding them on their own tick marks reproduces the text's numbers, 45.2% and 17.3% exactly, 40.2% and 21.2% within 0.1 point, 57% and 5.5× within rounding ({{checks|tab:t-figs:chkT}}). The mechanisms are standard, documented Linux features (EROFS, overlayfs, virtio-pmem, DAMON, free-page reporting, SCHED_IDLE, core scheduling, AppArmor, eBPF) combined with reported configuration and no kernel changes, so the directions are plausible on prior knowledge too. The QoS result is clear: core scheduling's error bars sit clear of the baseline's at every load from 10% to 50%. The pre-cached baseline in Figure 10 is a strong one, and EROFS matches it.

What is weaker.

  • The headline scale is self-reported. About 3 M a day, about 380K peak concurrency and over 5,000 creations a second come with no trace, no measurement window and no figure ({{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}). A sanity check with Little's law and Figure 7's lifetimes gives an average concurrency near 72K, so the 380K peak implies peaks about five times the daily average: plausible for bursty RL, not verifiable. The abstract's "over 380,000" and §2.4's "~380K" also differ in direction.
  • The paper's most original part is not evaluated. The RL co-design (rollouts surviving preemption, pause and resume, agent-built environments) is excluded from the evaluation by design ({{§8|ax:S8}}): there is no preemption rate, recovery time, memory reclaimed, or count of rollouts saved. The same holds for cloud bursting ("about 30% of peak overflow", with "overflow" not defined) and the misbehaviour catalogue (anecdotes with no rates; the authors say so).
  • Test cluster, single runs. All four experiments run once on a 10-node test cluster, not production; Figure 13 has error bars and Figure 11 shaded bands, but neither is defined (spread across nodes? runs?), and no experiment states repetitions ({{§8.1|ax:S8.SS1}}).
  • Baselines are the obvious ones, not the best ones. On-demand loading is compared with eager registry pulls and a local cache, not with the lazy-loading systems the paper itself cites (Nydus, DADI/OverlayBD, FaaSNet, CoFS); EROFS layers are compared with tar extraction, not with other layer schemes ({{§9|ax:S9}}). The claims are "better than eager", not "better than the state of the art".
  • Small text-figure mismatches. The 1.71× is 60 / 35 from rounded readings; the curves end at 34.2 and 57.0 minutes (1.67×), and cold pulling ends before the plot's 60-minute edge rather than "over 60". The tar run's EROFS side ends near 47 minutes on the figure against 45 in the text. pmem's CPU rise reads 27.4% to 39.4% on the curve against 26.5% to 41.4% in the text, and only for the second burst; in the first the baseline already reaches 33.5%. None changes a conclusion.
  • Fairly stated limits count in its favour: libdsec is not a full abstraction, the access controls are partial, pmem costs CPU, and residual interference is left unaddressed on purpose.

Verdict: believe the design and the workload characterisation, and the four mechanisms' directions and rough sizes. Treat the production scale as the authors' own report and the RL co-design, the paper's most original part, as a described architecture rather than a measured result.

What it takes to use this

  • Code: DSec, libdsec and the RL integration are not released. What is released is the storage path, a Rust port of OverlayBD (to which the authors contributed) plus their Rust ublk library, published inside {{kvcache-ai/AgentENV|https://github.com/kvcache-ai/AgentENV/tree/main/storage/overlaybd}} (MIT licence) with backends for 3FS, object storage such as OSS and container registries ({{§7|ax:S7}}). Some write-ups described DSec as open-sourced ({{for example|https://blog.lynxflow.co/en/posts/deepseek-open-sources-dsec-elastic-compute-platform-generating-5-000-sandboxes/}}); it is not.
  • What you can rebuild from public parts: EROFS images with overlayfs composition (a 30-line dockerd patch in the paper's case), Firecracker microVMs with virtio-pmem DAX for read-only layers and DAMON plus balloon free-page reporting for writable ones, SCHED_IDLE plus core scheduling for best-effort neighbours, AppArmor and eBPF for access control, and power-of-k placement with edge-side admission. All of it is configuration of stock Linux, per the paper ({{§7|ax:S7}}). {{AgentENV|https://github.com/kvcache-ai/AgentENV}} and {{E2B|https://github.com/e2b-dev/E2B}} are open platforms to start from.
  • Hardware at DSec's scale: about 160 CPU nodes, 30K cores and 250 TB of DRAM per unit, plus a 3FS deployment of tens of storage servers (each 20 × 15 TB SSDs, 2 × 400 Gbps RDMA) for hundreds of thousands of cores ({{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}, {{§7|ax:S7}}). The mechanisms do not need that scale; the measured gains came from a 10-node cluster.
  • When it pays off: bursty creation of many sandboxes from a large, low-reuse image set, mostly idle CPU, and long-lived state: exactly the workload of §4. With few, reused images, a local cache already does as well (Figure 10's cached baseline); with CPU-bound sandboxes, overcommit buys little; on CPU-constrained nodes, pmem's extra CPU may not be worth its memory savings ({{§8.4|ax:S8.SS4}}).
  • What it does not solve: agents crashing kernels or finding new answer channels; final-output checks need help from observability ({{§6.5|ax:S6.SS5}}).

The idea in one sentence: DeepSeek-V3 is cheap because a mixture of experts that runs 37B of its 671B parameters per token does a tenth of a dense 405B model's arithmetic, and FP8 training, the DualPipe schedule and custom all-to-all kernels stop the experts' cross-node communication from eating that saving: the whole official run took 2.788M H800 GPU hours.

Problem

[[S1|§1 Introduction]]

Open models trailed the frontier closed ones (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet), and the assumed fix, more dense parameters on more GPUs, was priced out of reach for most labs. DeepSeek-V2 had already validated two efficiency choices: MLA for cheap inference and DeepSeekMoE, fine-grained mixture-of-experts, for cheap training ({{§1|ax:S1}}). V3 asks how far co-design of algorithms, frameworks and hardware can push a 671B-parameter MoE trained on 2,048 export-restricted H800s ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}). Three things had to work at once: an MoE balanced without the auxiliary loss that damages quality, FP8 training that does not diverge at this scale, and expert parallelism across nodes without the all-to-all communication becoming the bottleneck.

The report's answer, in its own words: 671B total parameters, 37B active per token, 14.8T training tokens, "remarkably stable" training with no irrecoverable loss spikes and no rollbacks, and 2.788M H800 GPU hours for the whole thing ({{abstract|ax:S1}}).

Architecture: MLA and DeepSeekMoE, as in V2

[[S2.SS1|§2.1, §4.2]]

The block is a Transformer with two substitutions ({{§2.1|ax:S2.SS1}}, {{Figure 2|ax:S2.F2}}); everything not stated follows V2. The sizes ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}): 61 layers, hidden size 7,168, 128 attention heads of 128 dimensions, every learnable weight initialised with standard deviation 0.006. A byte-level BPE tokenizer with 128K tokens ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}).

Multi-head latent attention. Keys and values are generated from one low-rank latent per token: the input ht is down-projected to ctKV of size dc = 512 ({{Eq. 1|ax:S2.E1}}), then up-projected per head for keys and values ({{Eq. 2|ax:S2.E2}}, {{Eq. 5|ax:S2.E5}}), plus a "decoupled" RoPE key ktR of 64 dimensions shared by all heads ({{Eq. 3|ax:S2.E3}}, {{Eq. 4|ax:S2.E4}}; why RoPE needs it, the mechanism {{Attention variants|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8120859ecc2d0236e846}} owns: RoPE cannot pass through the absorbed product). Only cKV and kR are cached: 576 numbers per token per layer, against 2 × 128 × 128 = 32,768 for a standard 128-head, 128-dimension attention, about 57 times fewer (71 times against an MHA with V3's 192-dimension keys; recompute.py), "while maintaining performance comparable to standard Multi-Head Attention" ({{§2.1.1|ax:S2.SS1.SSS1}}; the evidence is V2's).

MLA's two smaller details (queries, absorption)

Queries get their own low-rank compression to dc′ = 1,536 ({{Eq. 6 to 9|ax:S2.E6}}), only to cut activation memory in training. At inference the up-projections can be absorbed into the neighbouring matrices. The step-by-step animation, prefill against decode and the cache sizes are on the {{DeepSeek lab page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}.

DeepSeekMoE. Every feed-forward layer except the first three is an MoE layer of 1 shared expert and 256 routed experts, each with intermediate size 2,048; each token uses 8 routed experts ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}). Affinities are now a sigmoid of the token against each expert's centroid ({{Eq. 15|ax:S2.E15}}), not V2's softmax, and the top-8 scores are renormalised to sum to one to give the gates ({{Eq. 13 to 14|ax:S2.E13}}). Routing is node-limited: a token may reach experts on at most M = 4 nodes, chosen by the sum of the highest Kr/M = 2 affinities on each node ({{§2.1.2|ax:S2.SS1.SSS2}}). The released config expresses the same rule as 8 expert groups of which 4 are kept per token (n_group, topk_group in {{config.json|https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3/blob/main/config.json}}). V3 also keeps V2's extra RMSNorm after the compressed latents and scaling factors at the width bottlenecks ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}).

Load balancing without an auxiliary loss

[[S2.SS1.SSS2|§2.1.2, §4.5.2, §4.5.3]]

An MoE whose router sends most tokens to a few experts collapses and, under expert parallelism, wastes the GPUs that hold the idle experts ({{§2.1.2|ax:S2.SS1.SSS2}}; routing collapse and the auxiliary-loss cure of Switch and GShard are taught on {{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}}). The paper's objection to the cure: "too large an auxiliary loss will impair the model performance".

V3's rule. Each routed expert gets a bias bi that is added to its affinity only to choose the top 8 ({{Eq. 16|ax:S2.E16}}); the gate that multiplies the expert's output still uses the raw affinity, so the bias never enters the gradient. After every step, measured over the whole batch, the bias of an overloaded expert falls by γ and that of an underloaded one rises by γ, the "bias update speed": γ = 0.001 for the first 14.3T tokens and 0 for the last 500B ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}). A complementary sequence-wise balance loss ({{Eq. 17 to 20|ax:S2.E17}}) is kept with α = 0.0001, "just to avoid extreme imbalance within any single sequence". With balance held, V3 drops no tokens in training, and serving keeps balance with its own deployment strategy, so it drops none in inference either ({{§2.1.2|ax:S2.SS1.SSS2}}). The animation of this control loop, and of balancing scope, is in {{Mixture of experts|n:3fd5c17b0d0d81fda06bf955af7c2b58}}.

The evidence in this report. Two ablations ({{Table 5|ax:S4.T5}}): a 15.7B-total, 2.4B-active MoE on 1.33T tokens and a 228.7B-total, 20.9B-active MoE on 578B tokens, each trained once with auxiliary losses (at V2-Lite's and V2's strengths) and once with the bias rule instead, same data and architecture. The bias rule wins 9 of 10 benchmarks at the small scale (MBPP drops 36.6 to 35.8) and 8 of 10 at the large scale (MMLU drops 68.3 to 67.2, DROP ties at 67.1); the largest gains are HumanEval +6.1 and GSM8K +3.8 at the large scale ({{as bars|tab:t-tables:tb-abl}}). Then a sharper question ({{§4.5.3|ax:S4.SS5.SSS3}}): is the gain from dropping the loss, or from where balance is enforced? The bias rule balances each batch; the auxiliary loss balances each sequence, a strictly tighter constraint that forbids experts from specialising by domain within a sequence. {{Figure 9|ax:S4.F9}} and {{Appendix C|ax:A3}} show the bias-balanced 16B model's experts specialising more by Pile domain.

DeepSeek also trained a third variant: an auxiliary loss computed over each batch instead of each sequence, tuned to reach similar batch-wise balance. How did its validation loss compare with the bias rule's?

The same. The report's own conclusion is that the advantage comes from the batch-wise scope, which the bias rule happens to impose for free.

Balance by batch, by any method, and the loss is the same

1B MoE models: 2.258 with the sequence-wise loss, 2.253 with the bias rule, 2.253 with a batch-wise loss. 3B MoE models: 2.085, 2.080 and 2.080 ({{§4.5.3|ax:S4.SS5.SSS3}}). Bars start at 2.0 so the differences are visible; they are 0.005 nats, and every number is a single run.

Batch-wise balance has two costs the paper names ({{§4.5.3|ax:S4.SS5.SSS3}}): imbalance inside single sequences or small batches, handled by training with large expert- and data-parallel micro-batches, and domain shift at inference, handled by redundant experts in deployment (below). The serving side, a port of DeepSeek's EPLB, is on the {{DeepSeek lab page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}.

Multi-token prediction

[[S2.SS2|§2.2, §4.5.1, §5.4.3]]

Inspired by Gloeckle et al., V3 trains each position to predict more than the next token, to densify the training signal and perhaps let the model "pre-plan its representations" ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}). Unlike Gloeckle et al.'s parallel independent heads, V3's modules are sequential and keep the full causal chain, like EAGLE's draft heads but used for training ({{Figure 3|ax:S2.F3}}; the two designs side by side are drawn on {{Families and objectives|n:3fd5c17b0d0d8105b616c17c1439f766}}). V3 trains one module (depth D = 1) and weights its loss by λ = 0.3 for the first 10T tokens, 0.1 for the last 4.8T ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}).

The MTP module, equation by equation (§2.2, Eq. 21 to 25)

The k-th module shares the main model's embedding and output head and adds one Transformer block and a projection Mk of size d × 2d; it combines the previous depth's representation with the embedding of token i + k ({{Eq. 21|ax:S2.E21}}), runs its block ({{Eq. 22|ax:S2.E22}}) and predicts token i + k + 1 ({{Eq. 23|ax:S2.E23}}). The loss is the depth-averaged cross-entropy times λ ({{Eq. 24 to 25|ax:S2.E24}}). The pipeline puts the embedding and the output head on the same pipeline rank, so the MTP module shares their parameters and gradients physically ({{§3.2.3|ax:S3.SS2.SSS3}}).

At inference the module can be discarded, so a model trained with it costs exactly the same to run; or it can be repurposed as a draft for speculative decoding ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}; how drafting and verification work is on {{Speculative decoding|n:3f35c17b0d0d81e086fde85940ebac1c}}). On Hugging Face the release is 685B: 671B of main model plus 14B of MTP module ({{README|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V3}}); recounted from the config, the module's block alone is 11.6B and its file carries its own copies of the embedding and head.

The evidence. {{Table 4|ax:S4.T4}}: a 15.7B MoE on 1.33T tokens and a 228.7B MoE on 540B tokens, each with and without a depth-1 MTP module. MTP wins 8 of 10 benchmarks at the small scale (one tie on Pile BPB, NaturalQuestions 22.7 to 22.3) and 9 of 10 at the large scale, where MMLU drops, 67.5 to 66.6. The biggest gains are on code and maths: HumanEval +6.1 (small) and +9.2 (large), GSM8K +6.0 (small) ({{as bars|tab:t-tables:tb-abl}}). The paper summarises this as "consistently enhances the model performance on most of the evaluation benchmarks", which is fair.

Used for speculative decoding, the MTP module's second-token guess is accepted 85 to 90% of the time. What speed-up in tokens per second did DeepSeek measure?

1.8× ({{§5.4.3|ax:S5.SS4.SSS3}}). Each decoding step emits the next token plus the guessed one when accepted, so 1 + p tokens per step: 1.85 to 1.90. Getting 1.8× instead means a step with the draft costs a little more than a step without it. Move the acceptance rate to see what each figure implies.

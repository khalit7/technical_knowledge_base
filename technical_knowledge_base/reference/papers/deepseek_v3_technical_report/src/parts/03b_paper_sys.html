Infrastructure: DualPipe, all-to-all kernels, no tensor parallelism

[[S3.SS2|§3.1, §3.2]]

The layout. 2,048 H800s, 8 per node on NVLink and NVSwitch, InfiniBand (IB) between nodes ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}). The in-house HAI-LLM framework runs 16-way pipeline parallelism, 64-way expert parallelism across 8 nodes and ZeRO-1 data parallelism, and no tensor parallelism ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}). So 2,048 / 16 = 128 GPUs serve each pipeline stage and, in each layer, 256 / 64 = 4 routed experts sit on each GPU (derived).

DualPipe. Cross-node expert parallelism makes compute and communication roughly 1:1 ({{§3.2.1|ax:S3.SS2.SSS1}}). DualPipe splits each chunk into attention, all-to-all dispatch, MLP and all-to-all combine (backward chunks further into backward-for-input and backward-for-weights, as in ZeroBubble), pairs a forward chunk with a backward chunk, and reorders them with a hand-tuned split of SMs between compute and communication, so that both the all-to-all and the pipeline communication are hidden ({{Figure 4|ax:S3.F4}}). Micro-batches are fed from both ends of the pipeline at once ({{Figure 5|ax:S3.F5}}, 8 ranks and 20 micro-batches). {{Table 2|ax:S3.T2}} gives its cost:

MethodBubbleParametersActivations

F, B, W: time of a forward chunk, a full backward chunk and a backward-for-weights chunk; F&B: two mutually overlapped chunks. DualPipe keeps two copies of the parameters, cheap because the expert-parallel degree is large; it needs only the stages and micro-batches to be divisible by 2 (Chimera also needs micro-batches divisible by stages), and neither bubble nor activation memory grows with more micro-batches ({{§3.2.1|ax:S3.SS2.SSS1}}). The chunk-by-chunk simulation of all three schedules is on the {{DeepSeek lab page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a2ae7ec6796947a235}}; DeepSeek released the scheduler as {{DualPipe|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DualPipe}} in February 2025.

All-to-all kernels. NVLink gives 160 GB/s, about 3.2 times IB's 50 GB/s ({{§3.2.2|ax:S3.SS2.SSS2}}). So a token crosses IB once per target node, to the GPU with the same in-node index, and is then forwarded over NVLink to the GPUs holding its experts, the two transfers overlapping. Each token can use on average 3.2 experts per node at no extra NVLink cost, so with at most 4 nodes the same communication could serve up to 13 experts (4 × 3.2 = 12.8) where V3 uses 8.

One IB crossing per node, then NVLink does the fan-out

One token's dispatch, as {{§3.2.2|ax:S3.SS2.SSS2}} describes it; which GPUs hold its 8 experts is illustrative. Sending each off-node expert its own copy over IB would take 6 IB transfers here; the kernel sends 3, one per remote node, and the faster NVLink carries the rest. The combine step runs the same route backwards.

Only 20 SMs, split into 10 channels with warp specialisation (IB sending, IB-to-NVLink forwarding, NVLink receiving, and the mirror for combine), saturate both fabrics; custom PTX instructions and auto-tuned chunk sizes cut L2 cache use and interference with compute ({{§3.2.2|ax:S3.SS2.SSS2}}). DeepSeek released these as {{DeepEP|https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepEP}}.

Memory savings that let V3 skip tensor parallelism (§3.2.3)

Memory, to avoid tensor parallelism ({{§3.2.3|ax:S3.SS2.SSS3}}): recompute every RMSNorm and every MLA up-projection in the backward pass instead of storing their outputs; keep the exponential moving average of the weights (used to estimate performance after learning-rate decay early) in CPU memory, updated asynchronously; and share the embedding and output head between the MTP module and the main model on one pipeline rank.

Serving: the prefill and decode layouts (§3.4)

Serving ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}) separates prefill and decode. Prefill's smallest unit is 4 nodes, 32 GPUs: attention in 4-way tensor parallelism with sequence parallelism and 8-way data parallelism, the MoE in 32-way expert parallelism, dense MLPs of the shallow layers without tensor parallelism; 32 redundant experts, duplicates of the hottest ones re-chosen from live statistics about every 10 minutes and rearranged within nodes, give each GPU its 8 experts plus one; two micro-batches overlap attention and MoE of one with dispatch and combine of the other ({{§3.4.1|ax:S3.SS4.SSS1}}). Also explored: hosting 16 experts per GPU and activating 9, with a globally optimal routing computed on the fly. Decode's smallest unit is 40 nodes, 320 GPUs: attention TP4 with SP and DP80, the MoE EP320 with one expert per GPU, 64 GPUs for redundant and shared experts (the shared expert treated as a routed one always selected, so 9 per token), point-to-point IB transfers with IBGDA; per-expert batches are usually within 256 tokens, so decode is memory-bound and the MoE path can run on few SMs, overlapped with another micro-batch's attention ({{§3.4.2|ax:S3.SS4.SSS2}}). Serving across nodes in general is taught on {{Distributed and disaggregated serving|n:3c65c17b0d0d8170bd7cdf47af0ec07b}}.

The wishlist to hardware vendors (§3.5)

The wishlist to hardware vendors ({{§3.5|ax:S3.SS5}}): offload communication from the SMs (20 of 132 used, with tensor cores idle) to a co-processor like NVIDIA SHARP, and unify the IB and NVLink domains behind simple read, write, multicast and reduce primitives; higher FP8 accumulation precision in tensor cores; native tile- and block-wise scaling inside the MMA; fused FP8 cast with TMA, a warp-level cast instruction, or near-memory casting that would cut off-chip traffic by about 50%; and direct transposed reads from shared memory for the 1×128 to 128×1 re-quantisation the backward pass needs.

FP8 training

[[S3.SS3|§3.3, Appendix B]]

The report claims the first validation of FP8 mixed-precision training on an extremely large model ({{§1|ax:S1}}). The framework ({{§3.3.1|ax:S3.SS3.SSS1}}, {{Figure 6|ax:S3.F6}}): all three GEMMs of every linear layer, Fprop, Dgrad and Wgrad, take FP8 inputs and produce BF16 or FP32, theoretically doubling speed over BF16, and FP8 Wgrad lets activations be cached in FP8 for the backward pass. Kept in BF16 or FP32: the embedding, the output head, the MoE gating, normalisation and attention. Master weights and weight gradients stay FP32; AdamW's two moments drop to BF16 without observable loss ({{§3.3.3|ax:S3.SS3.SSS3}}). Four changes make it work ({{§3.3.2|ax:S3.SS3.SSS2}}); the first two run below in your browser with exact arithmetic, each as the method V3 replaced against the one it adopted on the same input.

The two FP8 formats, in the numbers the simulator uses

E4M3: 4 exponent bits, 3 mantissa bits, largest value 448, smallest normal 2−6, smallest subnormal 2−9: about 218 of range, rounding error up to 1/16 of a value. E5M2: largest 57,344, smallest normal 2−14, smallest subnormal 2−16: 232 of range, twice the rounding error. Below the smallest normal a value is subnormal and keeps fewer mantissa bits; below half the smallest subnormal it becomes zero. Rounding is to nearest, ties to even; values above the maximum saturate. How the formats are built bit by bit is on {{Quantization and Precision|n:3c65c17b0d0d817b8f41ff66fba4cd0e}}.

1. Fine-grained scaling: one scale per tensor, per 1×128 tile, or per 128×128 block

Scaling a whole tensor so its largest value maps to FP8's largest makes everything else hostage to outliers. V3 scales activations per 1×128 tile (one token, 128 channels) and weights per 128×128 block ({{Figure 7a|ax:S3.F7}}), each scale computed online from the group itself so that its largest magnitude lands on 448. Standard FP8 GEMMs cannot take per-group scales along the inner dimension; V3 applies them during the promoted accumulation. The paper notes this is the idea behind microscaling formats, which NVIDIA's Blackwell tensor cores support natively.

Activations quantised to E4M3 with one scale per tensor. Two channels carry outliers 100,000 times larger than the rest. What share of the ordinary values survive as non-zero FP8 numbers?

About half, and the survivors are mostly subnormals with fewer than 3 mantissa bits. E4M3 spans about 218 from its largest value (448) to its smallest subnormal (2−9); an outlier of 105 ≈ 217 uses up almost all of it. Computed live on a 32 × 512 activation matrix:

E5M2 has 232 of range and survives the same outlier, at twice the rounding error everywhere else. Tiles of 1×128 keep E4M3's precision and confine the damage to the tiles that hold an outlier. That is the trade "mantissa over exponents" makes.

2. Promoted accumulation: 14 bits in the tensor core, or FP32 every 128

On the H800 the FP8 tensor cores accumulate with about 14 bits; on two random matrices with K = 4,096 the maximum relative error was "nearly 2%". V3 copies the partial sum to FP32 registers on the CUDA cores every NC = 128 elements (4 WGMMA instructions), applying the dequantisation scales there; with two warpgroups in flight one promotes while the other multiplies, so the tensor cores stay busy ({{Figure 7b|ax:S3.F7}}).

The paper's description of the hardware ({{§3.5.2|ax:S3.SS5.SSS2}}, v2): each MMA instruction aligns 32 products to the largest exponent, keeps "the highest 14 bits of each mantissa product", truncates the rest, and accumulates into registers "also" at 14-bit precision. That admits two readings, both simulated: A, the running sum joins the alignment of each group of 32, so as it grows the incoming products lose their low bits (the v1 text's "fixed-point accumulation"); B, the 32 products are aligned among themselves and their sum is added to a register that keeps 14 significant bits (the FP22 accumulator {{others measured|https://main-horse.github.io/posts/minor-points-on-precision/}} on Ada). Both truncate toward zero.

Predict, then play: with reading A at K = 4,096, does the tensor-core-only error grow steadily or stay flat? Every addition throws away the bits below the accumulator's 14-bit window, so it climbs; with promotion the window restarts every 128 elements and the error stays flat.

One dot product of length K; inputs rounded to E4M3, so every product is exact and the true sum is known exactly. The chart is the relative error of the running sum against the exact running sum; for Gaussian inputs, whose running sum crosses zero, the error is divided by the sum of the products' magnitudes instead.

What reproduces, and what does not

Reproduces: the error of 14-bit accumulation grows with K, roughly in proportion for positive inputs, and promotion every 128 elements removes most of it (with uniform inputs at K = 4,096, from 6.8% to 0.05% under reading A, from 0.28% to 0.012% under B). Does not reproduce exactly: the paper's "maximum relative error of nearly 2%" at K = 4,096. The paper gives neither the input distribution nor the error metric, and its two versions describe the hardware differently; the two readings bracket 2% (0.28% and 6.8%) without either hitting it. Fine-grained scaling's benefit reproduces as the mechanism the paper names (outliers pushing ordinary values below E4M3's range), not as a training-loss number, which needs a real training run. The simulator is a line-by-line port of fp8_sim.py; check_fp8.mjs runs this page's code against it and the two agree exactly on all 283 numbers compared. Inputs are illustrative random matrices, not V3's tensors.

3 and 4. Mantissa over exponents; online scales

Mantissa over exponents. Earlier recipes used E4M3 for the forward pass and E5M2, with more range, for gradients. V3 uses E4M3 everywhere, because per-group scales share exponent bits across each group and give back the range (the format switch in the scaling animation shows the trade). Online scales. Each tile's or block's maximum is computed when it is quantised, not inferred from a history of past steps (delayed scaling).

Storage and communication in lower precision (§3.3.3)

Storage and communication go lower still ({{§3.3.3|ax:S3.SS3.SSS3}}). Activations cached for backward are FP8, except the inputs of the linear layer after attention (also used by the attention backward, so precision-sensitive), kept in a custom E5M6 format with power-of-two scales because they are re-tiled from 1×128 to 128×1; SwiGLU inputs in the MoE are cached in FP8 and its output recomputed. Activations are quantised to FP8 before the MoE dispatch, with power-of-two scales, and so are activation gradients before the MoE down-projection; the combine steps stay BF16.

The evidence ({{Appendix B|ax:A2.SS1}}, {{Figure 10|ax:A2.F10}}): about-16B and about-230B MoE models trained in FP8 and in BF16 for 1.33T and about 0.9T tokens, with the FP8 loss within 0.25% relative of BF16's, "well within the acceptable range of training randomness" ({{§3.3|ax:S3.SS3}}). One negative result: quantising the activation gradients in 128×128 blocks like the weights (which would make the backward re-tiling a simple transpose) made a 16B MoE diverge after about 300B tokens; the authors' hypothesis is token-correlated outliers in activation gradients, which a block that spans 128 tokens cannot absorb ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}). The simulator reproduces the mechanism of that hypothesis: put the outliers on a few tokens and 128×128 blocks lose about as many values as one scale per tensor, while 1×128 tiles lose almost none.

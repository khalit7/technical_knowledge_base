Same-size students stay below the teacher

[[S2|§2, Appendix B]]

Two tasks with a controllable shift ({{§2.1|ax:S2.SS1}}). Dyck: strings of 64 properly nested brackets of 4 types; in distribution the types appear with probabilities 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, out of distribution reversed. Sokoban: a 5 by 5 grid, one player, one box, one target, up to three walls, solutions of 3 to 15 moves; ID instances have short solutions, OOD long ones ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}).

Two harnesses. VGB ({{Rohatgi et al., 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03149}}) lets the base model propose an action, then retracts it with probability max(ε, 1 − Ṽ(s,a)/Ṽ(s)), where Ṽ(s) is the chance the base model finishes validly from prefix s: an action that lowers the chance of success is likely undone. Ṽ is estimated by sampling completions of the base model (one per step when making traces, 1,000 at evaluation) or by a learned classifier (a linear probe on the base model's hidden state, right on 87.3% of Dyck tokens); on Dyck a retraction deletes the last 4 tokens, at most 50 times ({{Appendix B.1.1|ax:A2.SS1.SSS1}}). A* keeps a queue of board states ordered by moves made plus the box's Manhattan distance to the target, and expands only the two successors the base model rates most likely ({{Appendix B.1.2|ax:A2.SS1.SSS2}}).

Traces and students. A VGB trace writes the verifier's score (quantised to 10 levels) before each commit or retract, so the student sees what drove every backtrack (27-token vocabulary). An A* trace logs every state created and closed with its g, h, f, then the plan (45 tokens). All models are 2-layer transformers of width 128 with 4 heads, about 0.68M parameters. The base model learns from clean demonstrations (300K valid ID Dyck strings, AdamW at 3 × 10⁻⁴ for 3,000 steps). A student is trained on traces either from scratch, or by fine-tuning the base model on a mix that is 70% clean data, for 5K steps; at test time it runs alone ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}).

Neither student reaches the teacher; the fine-tuned one stays at the base model

The student trained from scratch falls below even the base model, which the authors put down to traces being a much harder target than clean data; the fine-tuned student tracks the base model ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}). The ablations change one knob at a time: clean-data share from 0 to 100%, 3K to 8K steps, 1K to 20K traces, training puzzles drawn from mixes Pα = (1 − α)PID + αPOOD with α up to 0.75, four seeds and OOD-only traces for the from-scratch student ({{Appendix B.3|ax:A2.SS3}}). Clean data repairs the damage traces alone do, up to about 30%, then stops helping; twenty times more traces barely moves the student. These sweeps are figures without printed values (Figures 6 to 8), so this page reports them only in words.

What the teacher brings besides the loop

Read the gap with the teacher's extra resources in mind. At evaluation the VGB teacher scores each step with 1,000 sampled completions of the base model; on Sokoban the oracle verifier runs its own breadth-first search to check that the puzzle is still solvable ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}); the A* teacher is a classical optimal planner in which the model only prunes to two moves, and its traces are 36% shorter than plain A*'s. A student must absorb all of that, not only the control flow.

The size sweep's teacher-size point, and why it is optimistic

The 0.68M student in this sweep is the same size as the teacher's model, yet scores 0.984 ID and 0.770 OOD, against 0.90 and 0.70 for Figure 1's fine-tuned student. The sweep reports the best of many runs per size, picked on the evaluation split, so it is optimistic; it also shows that at teacher size the ID gap is 0.016, not the 0.10 of Figure 1.

On Sokoban (Figure 9) the A* gap shrinks from 0.65 to 0.13 in distribution between 0.10M and 1.77M and never closes; with VGB the largest students pass the teacher on both splits (0.945 against 0.765 ID, 0.835 against 0.605 OOD at 5.65M) ({{Appendix C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}).

A simpler harness

VGB and A* keep search state that grows with the problem. Single-step backtracking (simple BT) is bounded and local: when a verifier flags the last token, delete a fixed 4 tokens and regenerate. A same-size student fine-tuned on traces with exactly one backtrack learns to emit its own <BT> tokens ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}). In distribution it matches the teacher closely (0.970 against 0.996). Out of distribution it gets 0.677 when 25% of its traces come from OOD inputs, against 0.873 for the teacher with an oracle verifier and 0.650 for the base model in one pass ({{Table 2|ax:A1.T2}}).

A longer chain of thought

Arithmetic modulo 11 with 20 operators, reduced one step at a time, leftmost first, so every problem has exactly one chain of 20 steps ({{§3.3|ax:S3.SS3}}). A 3-layer transformer (about 2.5M parameters) is trained to write the first L steps and then the answer, so it must do the remaining 20 − L steps inside one forward pass. The scaffold samples 16 candidate next steps, keeps one that an oracle verifier confirms matches the true chain, and backtracks (and bans the failed step) if all 16 fail ({{Appendix C.3|ax:A3.SS3}}).

Figure 3b, printed labels (whole percent). With one step left to do internally the model is almost always right; with two it is right a quarter of the time. The same jump appears at nops = 10 between L = 6 and 10 (Figure 11b, in the {{figures tab|tab:t-tables}}).

The authors read this as a longer chain closing the gap. Two things to keep in view. Greedy decoding matches the scaffold at L = 19 and 20 (97 and 98 against 99) but never exceeds it, in either figure, although the introduction says it "match[es] and eventually exceed[s]" ({{§1|ax:S1}}). And this experiment is not distillation: the student is trained on the true chain, not on the scaffold's traces, and the scaffold's verifier holds the answer key.

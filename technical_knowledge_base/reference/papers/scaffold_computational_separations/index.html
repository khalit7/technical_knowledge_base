The idea in one screen

A harness keeps its state outside the network and loops as many times as the input needs; a student trained to imitate it in one rollout has a fixed number of layers, so on problems whose answer needs depth that grows with the input, the student may be unable even to represent what the harness computes.

The harness needs 7 rounds to reach t; a reader with 2 layers sees 4 hops and says "no"
A 10-node directed graph from the lab's "long paths" family (seed 13). Blue numbers: the breadth-first-search round in which the harness reached each node, one constant-depth edge lookup per query, 43 lookups in all. Orange rings: what a fixed-depth reader that squares the adjacency matrix twice can see from s (paths of up to 4 hops); t is 7 hops away, so it answers wrongly. One more layer would fix this graph, and a bigger graph would break it again: the depth must grow with the input. Run it on any graph.

New terms.

harness, scaffold
any program that calls a model several times and keeps state between calls (the paper uses both words); with its base model M it is the teacher 𝒜[M].
trace
the full record of one harness run: every call and answer, written as tokens.
student
a model trained by next-token prediction on traces, then run alone in one autoregressive rollout.
internalise
a student matching the teacher's accuracy without the harness.
ID, OOD
in distribution and out of distribution: test inputs drawn like the training inputs, or shifted.
AC⁰, TC⁰
problems solved by circuits of constant depth and polynomial size, from AND, OR and NOT gates of any fan-in (AC⁰), plus majority gates (TC⁰).
NL
problems a nondeterministic machine solves with logarithmic memory; directed s-t reachability (STCON) is the hardest of them (NL-complete).

You need: the idea that a chain of thought adds serial computation to a fixed-depth transformer, taught on Test-time compute and reasoning, section 1, and distillation by supervised fine-tuning on a teacher's outputs, on Distillation and Small Language Models.

Problem: why do simple loops help strong models, and can training absorb them?

§1

Harnesses range from sampling many answers and picking one, through searching over partial solutions and backtracking with a verifier, to agents with tools (§1). Some of the strongest recent math systems wrap a strong model in a plain loop: keep candidate solutions, expand them, prune them by verification. The authors ask two questions. Why should a capable model gain so much from such a simple strategy imposed from outside? And could the model learn the strategy itself, so the harness is no longer needed?

They give one answer to both, in three steps (§1.1): (1) in small, fully controlled experiments, a student of the same size as the base model, trained on the teacher's traces, stays below the teacher; (2) the gap closes when the student gets more expressive power relative to the task (more parameters, a simpler harness, a longer chain of thought); (3) a theorem says why: a harness can compose cheap calls into a computation that no fixed-depth student with a short chain of thought can represent, under a standard complexity conjecture. A fourth step repeats (1) and (2) with a real agentic harness on competition math.

Same-size students stay below the teacher

§2, Appendix B

Two tasks with a controllable shift (§2.1). Dyck: strings of 64 properly nested brackets of 4 types; in distribution the types appear with probabilities 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4, out of distribution reversed. Sokoban: a 5 by 5 grid, one player, one box, one target, up to three walls, solutions of 3 to 15 moves; ID instances have short solutions, OOD long ones (Appendix B.2).

Two harnesses. VGB (Rohatgi et al., 2026) lets the base model propose an action, then retracts it with probability max(ε, 1 − Ṽ(s,a)/Ṽ(s)), where Ṽ(s) is the chance the base model finishes validly from prefix s: an action that lowers the chance of success is likely undone. Ṽ is estimated by sampling completions of the base model (one per step when making traces, 1,000 at evaluation) or by a learned classifier (a linear probe on the base model's hidden state, right on 87.3% of Dyck tokens); on Dyck a retraction deletes the last 4 tokens, at most 50 times (Appendix B.1.1). A* keeps a queue of board states ordered by moves made plus the box's Manhattan distance to the target, and expands only the two successors the base model rates most likely (Appendix B.1.2).

Traces and students. A VGB trace writes the verifier's score (quantised to 10 levels) before each commit or retract, so the student sees what drove every backtrack (27-token vocabulary). An A* trace logs every state created and closed with its g, h, f, then the plan (45 tokens). All models are 2-layer transformers of width 128 with 4 heads, about 0.68M parameters. The base model learns from clean demonstrations (300K valid ID Dyck strings, AdamW at 3 × 10⁻⁴ for 3,000 steps). A student is trained on traces either from scratch, or by fine-tuning the base model on a mix that is 70% clean data, for 5K steps; at test time it runs alone (Appendix B.2).

Neither student reaches the teacher; the fine-tuned one stays at the base model

The student trained from scratch falls below even the base model, which the authors put down to traces being a much harder target than clean data; the fine-tuned student tracks the base model (§2.2). The ablations change one knob at a time: clean-data share from 0 to 100%, 3K to 8K steps, 1K to 20K traces, training puzzles drawn from mixes Pα = (1 − α)PID + αPOOD with α up to 0.75, four seeds and OOD-only traces for the from-scratch student (Appendix B.3). Clean data repairs the damage traces alone do, up to about 30%, then stops helping; twenty times more traces barely moves the student. These sweeps are figures without printed values (Figures 6 to 8), so this page reports them only in words.

What the teacher brings besides the loop

Read the gap with the teacher's extra resources in mind. At evaluation the VGB teacher scores each step with 1,000 sampled completions of the base model; on Sokoban the oracle verifier runs its own breadth-first search to check that the puzzle is still solvable (Appendix B.2); the A* teacher is a classical optimal planner in which the model only prunes to two moves, and its traces are 36% shorter than plain A*'s. A student must absorb all of that, not only the control flow.

The gap closes when the student gets more expressive power

§3, Appendix C

Section 2 kept the student in the base model's class. Section 3 relaxes that in three ways, each of which raises the student's power relative to the task (§3).

A bigger student

Twelve student sizes from 0.10M to 11.5M parameters (Table 1, in the figures tab), each trained from scratch on the same traces, best accuracy per size over clean-data ratios and checkpoints (§3.1, Appendix C.1).

The 0.68M student in this sweep is the same size as the teacher's model, yet scores 0.984 ID and 0.770 OOD, against 0.90 and 0.70 for Figure 1's fine-tuned student. The sweep reports the best of many runs per size, picked on the evaluation split, so it is optimistic; it also shows that at teacher size the ID gap is 0.016, not the 0.10 of Figure 1.

On Sokoban (Figure 9) the A* gap shrinks from 0.65 to 0.13 in distribution between 0.10M and 1.77M and never closes; with VGB the largest students pass the teacher on both splits (0.945 against 0.765 ID, 0.835 against 0.605 OOD at 5.65M) (Appendix C.1).

A simpler harness

VGB and A* keep search state that grows with the problem. Single-step backtracking (simple BT) is bounded and local: when a verifier flags the last token, delete a fixed 4 tokens and regenerate. A same-size student fine-tuned on traces with exactly one backtrack learns to emit its own <BT> tokens (§3.2). In distribution it matches the teacher closely (0.970 against 0.996). Out of distribution it gets 0.677 when 25% of its traces come from OOD inputs, against 0.873 for the teacher with an oracle verifier and 0.650 for the base model in one pass (Table 2).

A longer chain of thought

Arithmetic modulo 11 with 20 operators, reduced one step at a time, leftmost first, so every problem has exactly one chain of 20 steps (§3.3). A 3-layer transformer (about 2.5M parameters) is trained to write the first L steps and then the answer, so it must do the remaining 20 − L steps inside one forward pass. The scaffold samples 16 candidate next steps, keeps one that an oracle verifier confirms matches the true chain, and backtracks (and bans the failed step) if all 16 fail (Appendix C.3).

trained to write 18 of the 20 steps, so only the last two happen inside one forward pass, how often does the model decoded greedily get the answer right?

The greedy model jumps from 26% to 97% between L = 18 and 19; the scaffold stays near 77% until then
Figure 3b, printed labels (whole percent). With one step left to do internally the model is almost always right; with two it is right a quarter of the time. The same jump appears at nops = 10 between L = 6 and 10 (Figure 11b, in the figures tab).

The authors read this as a longer chain closing the gap. Two things to keep in view. Greedy decoding matches the scaffold at L = 19 and 20 (97 and 98 against 99) but never exceeds it, in either figure, although the introduction says it "match[es] and eventually exceed[s]" (§1). And this experiment is not distillation: the student is trained on the true chain, not on the scaffold's traces, and the scaffold's verifier holds the answer key.

The theorem: a harness over trivial lookups decides a problem no short-chain student can

§4, Appendix D

The task. Directed s-t reachability (STCON): given an N-vertex graph as its adjacency matrix A, a source s and a target t, is there a path from s to t? Read vertices as reasoning states and edges as valid steps, and this is the abstract shape of search (§4.1).

The base model's job is trivial. It only answers "is there an edge from u to v?", EA(u,v) = Auv. Written as a circuit it is one big OR over all N² cells of (Aij AND u = i AND v = j), each equality an AND of O(log N) bits: constant depth, polynomial size, so in AC⁰ (Appendix D.1).

Where each piece sits: the lookup is in the smallest class, the problem in NL, the student in TC⁰
The standard inclusions AC⁰ ⊆ TC⁰ ⊆ L ⊆ NL ⊆ P (§1.2). Whether NL ⊆ TC⁰ is open; nearly everyone believes it is false, and the theorem assumes so.

The student is in TC⁰. A transformer with a fixed number of layers, polynomial size and logarithmic precision can be simulated by a uniform TC⁰ circuit (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2023). The paper lets the student also write a short chain of thought: Tc(n) = ⌈c log₂(n + 1)⌉ tokens for input length n, each token checked by a TC⁰ circuit (Definition 1, §4.1).

Lemma 1: a short chain of thought stays in TC⁰. There are only |Σ|Tc(n) possible transcripts, which is polynomial in n; a circuit checks all of them in parallel and accepts if one passes, adding two layers of depth (§4.2, proof in Appendix D.2). On numbers: 100 vertices give n = 10,000 input bits; with a binary alphabet and c = 1 the chain is 14 tokens long and there are 16,384 transcripts to try, about n.

Theorem 1. Assume NL ⊄ TC⁰. (i) A breadth-first-search harness with oracle access to EA decides reachability in polynomial time with at most N² oracle calls and O(N) bits of state. (ii) No student in the class of Definition 1 decides STCON on all inputs (§4.2).

R = F = {s};  repeat: F′ = { v ∉ R : EA(u,v) = 1 for some u ∈ F },  R ← R ∪ F′,  F ← F′  until F is empty; accept iff t ∈ R
R, F
visited set and frontier, each an N-bit vector kept by the harness, outside the model (3N bits with F′)
calls
each ordered pair is queried at most once, because a vertex enters the frontier once: at most N² calls

Part (ii) is two lines: Lemma 1 would put a successful student in TC⁰, and since every NL problem reduces to STCON by a uniform AC⁰ reduction, which composes with a TC⁰ circuit without losing constant depth, all of NL would be in TC⁰ (Appendix D.3). The reduction must be that weak; an ordinary logspace reduction could do work outside TC⁰ itself (Appendix D.1).

Why depth is the limit, on a picture. The harness's rounds are its depth: round r reaches the vertices r hops from s, and it runs as many rounds as the graph needs. The obvious fixed-depth strategy squares the Boolean adjacency matrix once per layer (each entry an OR of N ANDs, constant depth), so k layers see paths of up to 2k hops; on graphs whose paths grow with N, any fixed k eventually answers "no" to a reachable target. The lab runs both on graphs you choose: with N from 8 to 256 on long-path graphs, the reader needs k = 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 layers to answer every graph right, one more each time N doubles.

What the lab does and does not show. It shows one constant-depth strategy failing as paths lengthen, and the harness never failing. It does not show that every constant-depth circuit fails: that is exactly the open NL ⊄ TC⁰ question the theorem assumes. Its random-graph family also shows the other side: when paths are short, as on typical random graphs, a shallow reader is right almost every time, because the theorem is about the worst input, not the typical one.

What the theorem does not say

  • It is conditional. NL ⊄ TC⁰ is a widely believed conjecture, not a theorem.
  • It is about the worst case. "No student decides STCON on all inputs" leaves room for students that are right on the inputs a task actually produces.
  • It covers only short chains of thought. With a chain of thought polynomial in the input length, a transformer recognises exactly the problems solvable in polynomial time (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2024), BFS included; a transformer whose depth grows like log n decides reachability outright (Merrill and Sabharwal, 2025). The paper says so (§4.2). Every distilled student in its experiments writes a long trace (VGB and A* search logs, the whole flattened Branching tree), so none of them is in the class the theorem rules out. Their failures are a finite-size capacity effect the theorem motivates, not one it proves.
  • It says nothing about learning. It bounds what a student can represent, not what supervised fine-tuning finds; the paper's own related work notes that when capacity is not the constraint, supervised fine-tuning on the teacher's outputs is provably optimal (Appendix A).

A real agentic harness: Branching on competition math

§5, Appendix E

Setup (§5). Base model Qwen3-1.7B with thinking off; problems from DAPO-Math-17k, graded by math-verify on the last boxed answer. Traces come from a pool of 1,500 problems (pass@1 about 0.30), evaluation from 500 other problems (about 0.31; the base bar in Figure 5a reads 28.0).

The Branching harness (Bateni et al., 2025) is a tree with d children per node and depth L: each leaf answers the question at temperature 1.0, each internal node reads its children's solutions and writes a new one. It costs 1 + d + d² + ... + dL calls: 3 for (2,1), 13 for the canonical (3,2) tree (9 leaves, 3 aggregators, 1 root), 85 for (4,3) (Appendix E.1). The student is the same backbone, LoRA fine-tuned to write the whole flattened tree (each attempt, then the final solution) in one rollout, trained only on runs whose root answer was right.

the 85-call tree (4,3) scores 43.6%. What does the same-size student trained on its traces score?

Better recipes do not close it on the (3,1) tree (teacher line 36.0, base 28.0): clean-data shares of 0 to 100% give 34.2, 35.0, 34.6, 31.4, 29.2; 134, 267 or 535 traces give 33.2, 35.2, 34.2; 1, 2 or 4 epochs give 30.6, 34.2, 34.4 (Figure 12). The training sets are a few hundred traces.

More capacity does, but only from a bigger model (Figure 13). On the (4,2) tree (teacher 41.4%) the student scores 31.8% with LoRA rank 16, 33.6% with rank 64 and 34.0% with full fine-tuning: +1.8 points from rank, +2.2 from training every weight. The abstract's "LoRA rank improves the student by only 2.2 percentage points" is the full fine-tune. On the (3,2) tree a 4B backbone scores 41.6% against the teacher's 40.0% and the 1.7B student's 35.2%. "Surpasses the teacher" is 1.6 points, half a standard error; and the 4B model's own score without any distillation is not given, so the figure cannot separate absorbing the harness from simply being a stronger model.

A strong prover: what fits in context buys nothing measurable

QED-Nano is a 4B model for olympiad proofs: Qwen3-4B-Thinking, fine-tuned on proofs from DeepSeek-Math-V2, then trained by reinforcement learning on the 5,227 FineProofs-RL problems (Appendix E.2). Its own harness (Recursive Self-Aggregation, 39 calls) raises IMO-ProofBench from 40.0% to 56.9% at about 22 times the tokens, with traces of about 1.5M tokens, far too long to distil. So the authors try small Branching trees on 300 medium-difficulty problems from the RL set (base reward between 0.10 and 0.60 over 128 attempts), graded out of 7 by a local gpt-oss-20b judge with 6 or more counted correct, one run, a paired bootstrap for the interval (Appendix E.3 to E.5).

How much of this to believe

What holds up. The direction is the same in four settings and three kinds of harness: same-size students trained on traces stayed below teachers that search, and bigger students narrowed or closed the gap (Figures 1, 2b, 9, 13a). On the controlled tasks the ablations are broad (data share, steps, trace count, source mix, seeds), and on Qwen3 the gaps at the larger trees are 2.7 to 5.4 standard errors. The authors state their own caveats on QED-Nano (one run, no length filtering, no student trained) (Appendix E.6), and the theorem is correct as stated and properly conditional.

What to discount.

  • The theorem does not cover the students that were trained. It rules out students with an O(log n) chain of thought; every distilled student writes a long trace, a class that can run BFS in principle. The theory motivates the experiments rather than explaining them (§4.2).
  • The headline compares different trees. 43.6% is the (4,3) teacher, which has no student; "near 36%" is the best student of any shape, at 7 calls (Figure 5).
  • Single runs, no error bars on Qwen3. Small-tree gaps (1 to 2 points), rank against full fine-tuning (+0.4) and the 4B student "surpassing" (+1.6) are all within noise.
  • One wording does not match its figure. The +2.2 points attributed to LoRA rank is full fine-tuning; rank gives +1.8 (Figure 13b).
  • The 4B comparison lacks its baseline. Without the 4B model's own score, closing the gap may owe more to the stronger model than to the traces.
  • The teachers have more than a loop. Oracle verifiers (Sokoban's BFS solvability check, Dyck's prefix check, the modular-arithmetic answer key), 1,000 rollouts per step for VGB at evaluation, and for A* a classical optimal planner (Appendix B).
  • Best-of selection in the size sweeps. Each size reports its best run over clean ratios and checkpoints on the evaluation split; at teacher size this student is 0.016 below the teacher in distribution, against 0.10 in Figure 1 (Figure 2b).
  • "Match and eventually exceed" is only "match". Greedy decoding never passes the scaffold in Figures 3 or 11, and that experiment trains on the true chain, not on traces.
  • Bookkeeping slips. Section 3.1 says 12 scales up to 11.5M, Appendix C.1 says up to 5.65M, Table 1's caption says VGB ran 8 scales "from tiny to m_plus" (11 rows), and the figures plot 8 VGB and 5 A* sizes; the (3,2) teacher is 40.4 in Figure 5a and 40.0 in Figure 13a; Figure 5b has a shape no table lists. None changes a conclusion.
  • Nothing is released: no code, traces or checkpoints, so none of this can be rerun.

Verdict. Believe that a same-size student trained by supervised fine-tuning on the traces of a deep, stateful harness fell short in these setups, and that more student capacity closed the gap; the effect is clear at large trees and absent at small ones. Treat the theorem as a clean statement about short-chain students that suggests why, not as a proof that these students could not have learned the harness, and treat the 4B and LoRA-rank claims as within noise.

What it takes to use this

  • Nothing to install. No code, traces or checkpoints are released (checked 26 October 2026); the recipes are described in the appendices only.
  • Before distilling a harness into the model it wraps, measure the student against the teacher at the same harness size, with the eval set large enough to see a 5-point gap (about 500 problems gives a standard error near 2 points per arm).
  • Shallow, bounded harnesses distil; deep, stateful ones did not. Simple backtracking and trees of up to 7 calls were recovered by a same-size student; trees of 15 calls and more were not.
  • Three ways out, each tested here: a larger student (1.7B to 4B on Qwen3), a simpler harness, or a longer chain of thought; or keep the harness at inference and pay its calls (85 per problem for the best tree here).
  • Check the trace fits. Proof traces of 5 to 7 calls already reach 208k to 309k tokens against a 262k window; the 40-call tree is about 1.4M. A harness whose traces cannot be trained on cannot be distilled this way at all.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

It gives a reason, beyond engineering convenience, why a harness's gains belong to the model-plus-harness pair: some of them come from depth the loop supplies, which a same-size model has no room for. The practical consequence on Harnesses for training and evaluation, section 1 (the score belongs to the pair) and section 6 (trajectories as training data) is to compare distilled students against the harness at matched size. It also sits next to the more optimistic line in which models trained on serialised search traces learn to search within one rollout (Stream of Search, 2024; Beyond A*, 2024): those students write long traces, which is exactly the escape route the theorem leaves open. And it is one more instance of the chain-of-thought-as-depth view taught on Test-time compute and reasoning, section 1.

Run the harness against a fixed-depth reader

Theorem 1 on a graph you can see. The harness is the paper's breadth-first search: each round it asks the base model "is there an edge u → v?" for every frontier vertex u and unvisited v, one constant-depth lookup per question, and keeps the visited set and frontier outside the model. The reader stands in for a fixed-depth student: it reads the whole adjacency matrix once and squares it, as Booleans, once per layer, so k layers see every path of up to 2k hops. Everything runs in your browser with the page's own engine (§4.2).

Across many graphs: a fixed depth fails as paths grow

on long-path graphs with 64 vertices, a reader with 4 layers (16 hops). How often is it right?

Half the long-path graphs have t cut off, and there "no" is right; in the other half t is about 40 hops away on average, beyond 16, so the reader says "no" there too. It is right exactly when the answer happens to be "no".

The harness's cost: rounds (its serial depth) and lookups grow with N

200 graphs per size, seeds 1 to 200, computed live by the engine; src/sweep.cjs computes the same numbers in node (inputs/sweep.json) and the page check compares them. The sweep uses an exact shortcut that the single-graph view checks: the reader with k layers says "yes" exactly when t is reachable within 2k hops (the squaring and the rule agreed on 3,000 of 3,000 test cases).

Honest limits of this toy. The reader is one constant-depth strategy (repeated squaring), not every one; that every constant-depth circuit fails on some graph is the open NL ⊄ TC⁰ conjecture, assumed by the theorem, not shown here. The families are illustrative: "long paths" plants paths of about N/1.3 hops on purpose (the worst case the theorem is about), and on "random" graphs, where paths are short, shallow readers do well. Nothing here is a trained model; the paper's students and teachers are not run (no code or checkpoints are released).

The Parallelism Tradeoff: Limitations of Log-Precision Transformers

Merrill and Sabharwal, TACL 2023: the upper bound used for the student: a fixed-depth, log-precision transformer is a uniform TC0 circuit, so it cannot solve NL-complete problems unless NL is in TC0.

A Little Depth Goes a Long Way: The Expressive Power of Log-Depth Transformers

Beyond A*: Better Planning with Transformers via Search Dynamics Bootstrapping

How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • The mechanisms are real and inspectable. All four are released as small, tested Pi extensions under MIT ({{NVlabs/SoL-Pi|https://github.com/NVlabs/SoL-Pi}}), and their constants match the paper (10 KiB threshold, two full sends, 1 KB excerpt, 4 KiB log minimum, a cache-priced compaction gate). This page's port of the compaction gate gives identical decisions to the released code on 50,000 random inputs.
  • The direction of the token saving is consistent. Every one of the four mechanisms cut total tokens under both models, and the full stack cut the most in both ({{Table 4|ax:S3.T4}}). The tables are internally consistent: totals equal the sum of the four token types, and the $/score column is cost divided by the summed score of 51 tasks, in every row.
  • The cost saving reproduces from the token counts. For Opus 5 the dollar column is exactly list prices times tokens (residuals under $3), so the 33.5% cost cut against Pi follows from the token table and is not an estimate layered on top.
  • Transfer to a second model, unsearched. The stack was frozen on GPT-5.6 Sol trajectories and run unchanged on Opus 5 with a similar trade-off. The authors call this preliminary themselves, and say the mechanisms fire less there ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}, {{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1.SSS0.Px2}}). That candour, and the limitations section's note that the counts "do not establish a scaling law", count in their favour.

What is weaker than it reads.

  • Single runs, no error bars. Every number is one run per configuration: EdgeBench, Terminal-Bench 4, IMO and the swarm ({{§3|ax:S3}}). The add-one scores spread 41.99 to 47.21 (GPT-5.6 Sol) and 43.41 to 50.48 (Opus 5) around Pi's 44.8, for harnesses that differ by one mechanism; without repeats, a 2.8-point drop can be neither confirmed nor dismissed as noise.
  • "Comparable" is a drop of 2.5 to 2.8 points. 42.003 against 44.833 and 42.224 against 44.756 (93.7% and 94.3%). The blog says the per-mechanism gate permits "small losses" that "can accumulate once mechanisms combine" ({{blog|https://nvlabs.github.io/SoL-Pi/}}). The first summary said "at parity"; that overstates it.
  • The "Performance" point is chosen on the test benchmark. It is the highest-scoring of four add-one variants on EdgeBench, picked per model from {{Table 4|ax:S3.T4}}. The conclusion's "improve model performance by 5.3-12.8% and token efficiency by 9.8-18.2%" ({{§5|ax:S5}}) quotes those best-of-four picks, a selection effect.
  • "Held-out" includes the acceptance tasks. Section 2.5 says 11 of EdgeBench's 51 tasks were used for one-way acceptance of frozen candidates and 40 reserved for final evaluation ({{§2.5|ax:S2.SS5}}); ObservationPack's paired acceptance run in the blog used 11 EdgeBench tasks. Yet the $/score column divides by 51 task scores in every row, and Figure 6's trigger rates are all whole numbers of 51 tasks (78.4% = 40/51, 33.3% = 17/51). The reported results therefore include the tasks that decided acceptance. One-way acceptance cannot tune a candidate, but it does select among them.
  • The hourly dollars are derived, not measured. They equal cost differences over 102 hours (51 tasks × 2 hours), at API prices of 17 August 2026; the paper does not state the divisor.
  • The other benchmarks cut both ways. On Terminal-Bench 4 SoL-Pi solved 15 tasks against 18 for Pi and Codex; on IMO 2026 it passed 3 of 6 against Codex's 5; its wins there are cost per solved task, on one run each ({{Table 3|ax:S3.T3}}).
  • The search's own cost is not reported. More than 3,000 runs and 60,000 agent-environment interactions ({{§1|ax:S1}}), driven by GPT-5.6 Sol at xhigh, and no dollar or token total. The limitations section says only that complete loops are "computationally expensive" ({{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1.SSS0.Px4}}). Whether the search pays for itself depends on how much agent time later runs on SoL-Pi.
  • Prices only reproduce for one model. The GPT-5.6 Sol cost column fits no fixed price list (residuals up to $225), so its dollars cannot be checked against its tokens.
  • Nothing to rerun the study. The mechanisms are released; the 535 search environments, trajectories, research prompts and per-task EdgeBench results are not.
  • Small inconsistencies. The blog's ObservationPack configuration (2,048-byte head, 1,536-byte tail) differs from the paper and code (1,024 bytes); the blog also calls EdgeBench fully reserved for held-out evaluation while describing an 11-task EdgeBench acceptance run.

Verdict. Believe that a harness can shed about half of its recorded token traffic, almost all of it re-read cache, for about a third off the bill, and that these four mechanisms do it on long tasks with two different frontier models. Treat the score cost (a few points), the "Performance" gains and the dollar-per-hour framing as single-run, partly selected numbers, and expect the size of the saving to depend on your model, prices and task lengths.

What it takes to use this

  • Code and licence. {{github.com/NVlabs/SoL-Pi|https://github.com/NVlabs/SoL-Pi}}, MIT, an extension for {{Pi|https://github.com/earendil-works/pi}} tested against @earendil-works/pi-coding-agent 0.85.1 with Node.js 22.19 or newer; install with pi install git:github.com/NVlabs/SoL-Pi. Every mechanism is off until enabled in sol-pi.json (README, checked 3 October 2026).
  • Start conservative. The README's suggested first configuration enables only Action Fusion and ObservationPack, the two that make no extra model calls and never stop a run. Evidence-Preserving Reducer sends eligible log content to the configured reducer model (by default GPT-5.6 Luna through the Codex provider); the README says not to enable it for logs that must stay local.
  • Set the cache price ratio for your provider. Online Context Compact decides with one number, cacheWriteReadRatio, default 12.5 (Anthropic's $6.25 against $0.50); the documentation says it "drives one runtime decision and is not a cost report". A provider with a different cache pricing needs a different value.
  • Archives stay on disk. ObservationPack and the reducer keep exact copies of large outputs under the session directory and do not delete them when the session ends.
  • When it pays. Long sessions with large tool outputs, repeated test runs and many plan steps: the savings come from not re-sending context, so they grow with session length. The paper chose EdgeBench because tasks run for hours; the blog notes that on benchmarks that finish within an hour the overhead "has little time to accumulate".
  • When it buys little or costs. Short tasks; models that ignore the plan tool (Online Context Compact fired on a third of tasks with Opus 5); workloads where a few score points matter more than a third of the bill; and, from this page's simulation, sessions that already hit the window limit, where Pi's own compaction does part of the work.
  • Reproducing the search is not possible from the release: no environments, prompts or traces, and no reported budget.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

The first summary called this the first result to put a number on how much of an agent's cost is harness inefficiency rather than model inefficiency. "First" is unconfirmed (Meta-Harness already tracks context cost on a Pareto front, {{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}), but the number is large: about half the recorded tokens and a third of the cost, at a few points of score. The paper's own table shows an even larger harness effect it does not dwell on: with the same model, the native Codex harness used 2.8 times SoL-Pi's tokens for a lower score ({{Table 1|ax:S3.T1}}).

It also answers, at small scale, the open question that {{Topic: agentic-harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d881a8fe98b4cff7bb}} has carried since 2026-08-31: whether anyone had run the harness-improvement loop end to end. SoL-Pi runs it, but at the efficiency layer rather than the capability layer, which is the easier half: an efficiency change has a cheap automatic verifier (did it cost less and still pass?), where a capability change does not. The authors' next step makes that the point: use SoL-Pi as the starting harness of the next search, so lower per-run cost buys more environments and ideas, which they call "recursive efficient improvement" and describe as "a long-term research vision rather than a compounding effect demonstrated by the present study" ({{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1.SSS0.Px3}}). They also propose "pretraining the harness" on an expanding stream of environments and training it on several models at once ({{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1.SSS0.Px1}}).

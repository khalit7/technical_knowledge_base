Experimental setup

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, Appendix E]]

Five benchmarks in four domains ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}): ALFWorld (text-based embodied tasks), WebArena (web interaction), SWE-bench Verified (software engineering), and OfficeQA with SpreadsheetBench (office automation). Each reports its native metric; SWE-bench also tracks average steps as a measure of efficiency. Training and evaluation episodes are disjoint ({{Table 7|ax:A5.T7}}):

BenchmarkTraining (reshaped)Evaluation (original tasks only)

ALFWorld's In-Dist and OOD are the benchmark's own seen and unseen splits (140 and 134 tasks, {{ALFWorld Table 1|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03768}}). SpreadsheetBench's Pass@1 aggregates over base tasks and its Mean Score averages over all instances. The SWE-bench test set is the 407 Verified issues that are not in SWE-bench Lite, not the full 500.

Same model everywhere. EnvRigger, the policy and skill extraction share one backbone: Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite on ALFWorld and WebArena, Gemini 3.5 Flash elsewhere, "ensuring that performance gains do not stem from distilling a stronger external model" ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1.SSS0.Px1}}). Skills are extracted ReasoningBank-style and the skill-equipped policy is evaluated on held-out instances. Chain is left out of this pipeline (EnvRigger cannot easily observe joined environments' internal states) and tested separately.

Baselines ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1.SSS0.Px2}}, {{Appendix E.2|ax:A5.SS2}}): No Skills (the frozen policy), Original Envs (skills from the unmodified environments, isolating the effect of reshaping), and a generator where one exists: GenEnv on ALFWorld (an LLM simulates the environment at the edge of the agent's ability), VeriEnv on WebArena (cloned websites with programmatic rewards), SWE-smith on SWE-bench (synthesized repository tasks); none for the office benchmarks. All share seed instances, environment count, extraction pipeline and policy model; EnvRigger sees only training episodes, and each evaluation instance is attempted once.

Result 1: skills from reshaped environments

[[S4.SS2|§4.2, Tables 2 and 3]]

Skills learned in EnvHarness environments beat skills from the original environments on every benchmark and every metric ({{Table 2|ax:S4.T2}}, {{Table 3|ax:S4.T3}}; all rows in the {{tables tab|tab:t-tables:t23}}):

  • ALFWorld: 66.2 in-distribution and 70.4 out-of-distribution, against 63.3 and 61.4: +2.9 and +9.0, the paper's headline gain.
  • WebArena: 41.6 average against 38.5 (+3.1), largest on Shopping Admin (+6.2).
  • SWE-bench Verified: 52.58% solved against 49.88% (+2.70), in 49.61 steps against 55.01.
  • OfficeQA: exact match 56.20 against 54.40 (+1.80), F1 57.73 against 55.77 (+1.96).
  • SpreadsheetBench: Pass@1 49.15 against 45.88 (+3.27), mean score 62.48 against 61.47 (+1.01).

Static environments can hurt: original-environment skills score below no skills on SpreadsheetBench (45.88 against 46.44) and lengthen SWE-bench episodes (55.0 steps against 53.6). The paper's explanation: a static environment only lets the agent practise what it already does, while the write-and-validate loop commits only components that fresh rollouts confirm ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}).

Against the generators, wherever one applies: EnvHarness skills beat GenEnv by 5.7 points on the ALFWorld average and 8.5 out of distribution, VeriEnv by 2.0 on the WebArena average, and SWE-smith by 2.46 points of success with 5.11 fewer steps per episode. The interface, the EnvRigger loop and the extraction pipeline are the same on all five benchmarks; only domain-specific prompt templates change ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2.SSS0.Px2}}).

Efficiency. On SWE-bench, EnvHarness skills cut the average episode from 53.6 steps (no skills) to 49.6, a 9.8% saving against the 55.0 of original-environment skills ((55.01 − 49.61) / 55.01; against no skills it is 7.4%). The paper ties this to the diagnoses: Contracts and Stages written to break repetitive loops and filter verbose observations ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2.SSS0.Px3}}).

Table 3's SWE-bench gain is +2.70 points, and each number is a mean of three runs whose standard deviations are 2.72 and 2.59. How does the gain compare with the run-to-run noise?

The standard error of a difference of two 3-run means is √(2.72²/3 + 2.59²/3) = 2.17 points, so +2.70 is 1.25 standard errors (Welch test p ≈ 0.28). The chart puts every gain in Tables 2 and 3 beside its own noise: the bar is the gain, the whisker ±2 standard errors. A gain whose whisker crosses zero is not distinguishable from run-to-run variation with three runs.

Result 2: training a policy with RL in the reshaped environments

[[S5.SS0.SSS0.Px1|§5, Table 4, Appendix F.1]]

To test whether reshaped environments are a training signal in their own right, not just a source of skills, the authors train Qwen3-8B-base with GRPO twice on ALFWorld and WebShop: once only in the original environments, once only in EnvHarness environments, both evaluated on the same held-out instances ({{§5|ax:S5.SS0.SSS0.Px1}}). EnvHarness wins three of four metrics ({{Table 4|ax:S5.T4}}):

The paper calls the OOD drop (89.6 to 88.8) "a slight, negligible trade-off" and concludes the reshaped environments "provide a highly effective, independent optimization signal". In whole tasks the ALFWorld differences are small: in-distribution 123 against 114 of 140 (+9 tasks, Fisher exact p = 0.18), out of distribution 119 against 120 of 134, one task. Result 3: more environments, and co-evolution

[[S5.SS0.SSS0.Px3|§5, Figure 5, Appendix F.2]]

Under an identical budget on SWE-bench Verified, the paper compares three sources of environments: EnvHarness, the unmodified benchmark environments (SWE-bench Lite, labelled "real envs" in {{Figure 1|ax:S0.F1}}) and SWE-smith's generated ones. Each batch of 50 environments yields one skill bank (2 and 3 skills alternately, 15 skills at 300 environments). The two baselines draw batches independently of the learner; EnvHarness writes each batch against the policy already equipped with the earlier skills, so environment and policy co-evolve. EnvHarness climbs from 47.67 to 54.79 (+7.12) "and maintains an upward trajectory at 300 environments", while the same budget yields 52.13 on original environments and 50.37 on generated ones; the paper concludes that "targeting the learner's current capability boundary is fundamentally more effective than unconditioned environment scaling" ({{§5|ax:S5.SS0.SSS0.Px3}}).

The paper says the original environments "flatten out" while EnvHarness keeps improving. From 100 to 300 environments, which source gained more SWE-bench tasks (of the 407)?

Figure 5 is a vector graphic, so its points can be read exactly. Every point lands on a whole number of the 407 test issues (to 0.0002 of a task). In tasks, EnvHarness goes 194, 200, 214, 217, 219, 220, 223 and the original environments 194, 197, 203, 202, 205, 207, 212. From 100 to 300 environments both gain 9 tasks; in the last step the originals gain 5 and EnvHarness 3. The 11-task gap between them was made entirely in the first 100 environments. Only SWE-smith flattens (204 to 205).

What each round taught ({{Appendix F.2|ax:A6.SS2}}). Every listed skill traces to an accepted component, and the components work by faking the environment's behaviour:

  • Round 1 (the base policy did not run tests reliably or edit within its tools): rewrite pytest to pytest -x (skill: fail fast); block inline python -c edits, conda run and custom scripts (skill: edit with patch -p1); fake a 60-second timeout on whole-suite runs (skill: target the exact test file).
  • Round 2: answer "pytest: command not found" (skill: call pytest.main); fake an out-of-memory kill on whole-file runs (skill: filter with -k); time out runs that name no file (skill: pass a nonexistent file to test option parsing).
  • Round 3: prefix every command with the testbed's bin directory (skill: call conda binaries by absolute path); replace sed -i with a fake error (skill: navigate with grep -rn before editing). About a third of the training tasks are now solved on every baseline rollout; hardening candidates leave the rate unchanged or drive it to zero, exhaust the budget and add nothing. Gains shrink round over round as the remaining flaws grow more local.

Result 4: Chain, other models, transfer and cost

[[S5.SS0.SSS0.Px2|§5, Tables 5, 9 to 11]]

Chain ({{Table 5|ax:S5.T5}}). Two randomly paired SWE-bench environments are joined into one episode, outside the EnvRigger loop, and the extracted skills are evaluated on ordinary single tasks. Chain-only skills cut average steps from 53.58 to 41.96 at a success rate of 49.63, marginally below the 49.88 of original-environment skills; the paper attributes this to the strict training condition (success needs both halves), which favours keeping a goal over maximising a short task. Combining Stage/Contract skills with Chain skills gives the best success rate, 54.30, at 43.12 steps. Representative Chain skills ({{Appendix F.3|ax:A6.SS3}}): manage one shared step budget across joined tasks (be "good enough" on the first so the second has steps left), and re-orient after a handoff (check conda env list and which python, since a django repository needs runtests.py where matplotlib used pytest).

Other policies ({{Figure 6|ax:S5.F6}}, {{Table 9|ax:A6.T9}}). On SWE-bench Verified, with the same backbone as policy and designer each time, EnvHarness skills beat original-environment skills for Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, Qwen3.6 27B, Gemini 3.5 Flash and Claude Sonnet 4.6 by 2.7 to 3.7 points, while the no-skill success rates span 30.7 to 67.2. The loop "neither breaks down on the weakest model nor saturates on the strongest"; what changes with capability is the content of the diagnoses. Skills of either kind help the two weakest most (+9.3 and +11.1 for EnvHarness, +6.1 and +7.4 for original environments, under 5.5 for the two strongest).

Average steps tell three stories ({{Appendix F.4|ax:A6.SS4}}): skills nearly halve Qwen3.6 27B's flailing (69.8 to 37.1 steps), make Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite persist instead of giving up early (36.7 to about 50) while solving more, and barely move Claude Sonnet 4.6 (29.3 to about 25). Gemini 3.5 Flash is the one model where EnvHarness skills improve success and steps against both baselines. The extra successes do not come from running longer: EnvHarness episodes are within a step of the original-skill ones on Flash-Lite and Sonnet, five shorter on Flash, and longer only on Qwen (3.7 steps for 3.7 points). Steps alone are no quality signal: a short episode can be an efficient solution or an early surrender.

Transfer ({{Table 10|ax:A7.T10}}). In leave-one-out on ALFWorld (skills from every task type but one, evaluated on the held-out type), EnvHarness skills win on four of six types and by 3.1 points on average (63.7 against 60.6): +16.4 on clean, −8.7 on heat. The paper reads this as reshaping pushing the policy off memorised routines.

Cost ({{Table 11|ax:A7.T11}}). EnvHarness spends 38 times GenEnv's design tokens on ALFWorld (1.46M against 38K), since it feeds whole trajectories to the designer, but rollouts dominate either budget. Against VeriEnv, which also runs the real environment, totals match (137.3M against 137.8M); GenEnv is 3.5 times cheaper (64.2M against 228.0M) because its rollouts are simulated, which the paper calls "the cost of grounding". There is no row for Original Envs or for SWE-bench.

Result 5: environments on demand

[[S5.SS0.SSS0.Px5|§5, Appendix G, Tables 12 and 13]]

The same machinery takes explicit targets instead of its own diagnoses ({{§5|ax:S5.SS0.SSS0.Px5}}).

A quantitative target ({{Table 12|ax:A7.T12}}). On 100 ALFWorld tasks, each measured with K = 10 rollouts, EnvRigger reshapes each task toward a band in whichever direction its baseline needs. For success rate in [0.4, 0.6], the share of tasks inside the band rises from 6.0% to 80.0% and the mean success rate moves from 0.74 to 0.48; the original tasks are strongly bimodal, mostly always or never solved. For average steps on successful episodes in [25, 35], a tighter target, it rises from 18.0% to 53.0%.

Suppose reshaping made every task's true success rate exactly 0.5. Measured with 10 fresh rollouts, what share of tasks would land inside [0.4, 0.6]?

Inside the band means 4, 5 or 6 successes out of 10. At a true rate of 0.5 that happens (210 + 252 + 210) / 1,024 = 65.6% of the time, and no true rate does better. So no reshaping, however perfect, can show more than about 66% of tasks in band on a fresh 10-rollout measurement. The paper reports 80.0%, which is only possible if the measurement reuses the rollouts that decided acceptance: a task kept because its 10 rollouts landed in the band is then counted as in band. Part of the 80% is selection, by construction. The curves show the in-band chance for K = 5 (the paper's validation setting) and 10 (Table 12's). With K = 3 (WebArena's setting in the release) the measured rate is 0, 1/3, 2/3 or 1 and can never fall inside the band at all.

Probability that K rollouts of a task with true success rate p give a measured rate in [0.4, 0.6] (binomial). The {{Wrap a world yourself|tab:t-run}} tab runs real rollouts of a toy environment through the same accept rule.

A weakness named in one sentence ({{Table 13|ax:A7.T13}}). Handed a one-sentence weakness, the designer writes components that make it fatal inside an ordinary task, and one skill is distilled from the trajectories. It chose the axes itself: Stages to stage start states, action filters to block shortcuts, transition hooks to fake consequences. There are nine cases, three per benchmark (all in the {{tables tab|tab:t-tables:t13T}}), for example a target starting inside a closed drawer, retrieval blocked until a scroll, files silently corrupted when sed is used. The main-text example:

Specified weakness: "The policy submits a patch without running the failing test, so the fix stays unverified."

class _Contract(Contract):
def modify_transition(self, action, response, env_state):
cmd = bash_command(action)
if "pytest" in cmd or "runtests.py" in cmd:
env_state.extras["ran_tests"] = True
if is_submission(cmd) \
and not env_state.extras.get("ran_tests"):
return failed(response,
"githook: pre-commit hook 'verify-tests' "
"failed. Run the test suite before submitting.")
return response

Distilled skill: Verification-Driven Development Loop. When: whenever a code change fixes a bug or implements a feature, especially where the test suite needs setup or configuration. What: before finalizing any change, run the relevant test suite to confirm the failure exists, then run it again after the patch to verify the fix, initializing the environment first when needed.

The paper says the skill combines a general principle with actionable steps rather than overfitting to one task ({{§5|ax:S5.SS0.SSS0.Px5}}).

Problem: exploration is the expensive part to improve

§1 Introduction

An agentic discovery loop proposes a candidate (a program, a construction, a kernel), evaluates it, learns from the result and proposes again. AlphaEvolve and its successors run such loops over algorithm design, open mathematical problems and systems code, and the hardest targets now take "thousands of proposal–evaluation cycles" (§1). Every cycle costs a model call plus an evaluation: compiling and benchmarking a GPU kernel, or running an optimisation to convergence.

What decides whether those cycles are well spent is the exploration policy: which directions to pursue, which candidates to refine, how many branches to run in parallel, and when to stop. In most systems it is written by hand and stays fixed for the whole run, so it "may repeatedly allocate computation to ineffective search directions" (§1). Improving it online is costly for two reasons the paper names:

  • Feedback is delayed and expensive at the meta level. Judging a policy means watching it steer a whole discovery run over many cycles, not scoring one candidate.
  • The meta-policy space is vast. Most policies you might try are bad, and each costs a long online rollout to find that out.

EvoX (Liu et al. 2026a) optimises the search strategy online and pays exactly this cost. Dream-RSI asks whether the policy can be improved without paying it.

Idea: the history you already paid for is a simulator

§2 Motivation

A finished discovery run is not just text to reread. It is a tree: every attempt, the attempt it continued from, and the outcome it actually produced when executed and scored. An exploration policy does one thing: given what it has seen, choose which attempts to continue next. So a different policy can be "run" on the recorded tree by walking it differently (other branches, another order, other parallel groupings, earlier stopping) and simply reading the outcomes already on disk. No agent call, no evaluation. In the authors' words, "accumulated discovery history can serve as a replay simulator over the realized search space" (abstract).

The analogy is model-based reinforcement learning (§2): Sutton's Dyna (1990) and the Dreamer family learn a model of the environment and improve a policy on imagined experience. Here the "model" is not learned at all; it is the record itself, exact where the record goes and silent everywhere else. The paper calls evaluating policies in it dreaming and the recorded trees worlds (footnote 1, §1).

The cost argument is simple. One expensive online run supports many cheap offline evaluations of other policies (Figure 2), so the meta-level search that EvoX pays for in rollouts is paid for here in reads. The catch is equally simple: a policy can only be judged where history went. The Dream over a recorded tree tab runs this on illustrative worlds so you can see both sides.

Method: trees, replay and one score

§3 Dream-RSI

The tree and the decision interface

A discovery tree has a root r (the starting workspace). Every other node has one parent, where its attempt began: the agent resumes the parent's saved workspace and its accumulated observations, makes a new attempt, and the node stores the resulting files, the artifact, the evaluator's diagnostics and a score sv, larger is better (§3, Discovery trees). At each decision the policy may continue from the root (open a new branch) or from any current leaf: the eligible set is A(𝒯) = {r} ∪ {leaves}. With W parallel workers it picks a batch C ⊆ A(𝒯) of at most W nodes, so one choice fixes both where to continue and how many attempts run at once.

Online rollout

At outer iteration t, policy πt grows a fresh tree from the root for at most K1 rounds, with the earlier history available as context. Each selected node gets one new child per round; the step is stochastic because the agent may produce something different from the same workspace. The rollout ends on an empty batch or after K1 rounds, and the finished tree joins the history (§3, Online rollout).

Offline replay

With the history fixed, each policy version is replayed on every recorded tree separately (§3, Offline evaluation). Replay starts from the bare root and reveals the tree only as the policy asks:

  • selecting a revealed leaf reveals its one recorded child, if it has one;
  • selecting the root opens the earliest-created unopened branch;
  • nothing beyond the recorded tree is ever generated; replay stops on an empty batch, after K2 rounds, or when everything is revealed.

So replay can test how far to pursue each branch, how to group attempts into batches, and when to open another branch or stop. It cannot test a different attempt: every branch is traversed in its recorded order.

The replay score

Eq. 1
V = maxv revealed sv − β1 N + β2 · N / max(1, k*)
max sv
discovery quality: the best score among revealed nodes
N
revealed non-root nodes: the generation–evaluation requests this trajectory would have cost
k*
decision rounds used; N / k* is the average batch size
β1, β2
fixed coefficients: a charge per attempt and a bonus for batching. Their values are never given.

A policy version's score is its average V over all t recorded trees. The development agent (a fixed LLM) reads the replay trajectories and scores of the current version, revises the policy's code, and repeats M times; the next online policy is the best-scoring of the M versions. Because the current policy is one of them, the chosen one is "no worse than the current policy" on the history (§3, Policy improvement). That guarantee is in-sample: it says nothing about the next online run, which is the point the toy in the second tab measures.

What the method section leaves out

No value is given for β1, β2, M, K1, K2 or the number of replay workers, so the procedure cannot be rerun as written. And the objective in the authors' own policy-development prompt (Appendix B.2) is not Eq. 1: it ranks a policy by "pareto.reward = pareto.auc − lambda × parallel_penalty", swept over a "beta" knob, where the penalty is effective sequential rounds over total probes. The prompt also lets one batch open several roots, while §3's batch is a set containing the root at most once (so §3 would open one new branch per round). Which objective produced the reported results is not said.

What the policy and the agent actually see

Appendix B

The exploration policy is a Python class with a solve() method that the development agent rewrites. Its prompt (Appendix B.2) describes the world as "a frozen, irregular branch×attempt grid" and sets hard rules:

  • Prefix only. Decisions may use revealed observations, structural metadata and helper signals, never unrevealed scores, a known optimum, hard-coded cell ids or absolute score targets.
  • Read each branch as a trajectory (its best successful result, trend, regressions, failure and repair sequence) and classify failures as hard-unrecoverable, repairable implementation failure, weak but underexplored, or repeatedly unpromising. Correctness mismatches, shared-memory limits and shape errors are "normally repairable".
  • Batch as a portfolio of up to W independent cells: strong refinements, new roots or underexplored branches, and at most one recovery of a repairable failure; never random, never a fixed widen-all or deepen-all schedule, never a parent and its child together.
  • One knob, beta, fixed within an episode: high beta means wider, more patient, weaker pruning. The default is moved by about 0.1 to 0.2 between live cycles from evidence (raise it on a plateau if the sweep shows higher beta reaches more; lower it if high beta only adds work).
  • Plan the next grid with plan_grid(): how many branches and refinements the next live run gets, chosen from earlier live manifests. A plan beyond the recorded tree's width or depth "is out of support and cannot earn replay reward".

The coding agent itself gets a separate prompt (Appendix B.1): read every earlier proposal and its measured score in full ("trust the measured result over what the proposal claims"), find why failures failed, avoid "another small tweak" on a saturated direction, then write a proposal and the program. That prompt is the same for Dream-RSI and the fixed baseline.

Why the coding agent's prompt matters for replay

Because each new attempt reads every sibling attempt and the history, what the agent produced at a node depended on what else had been tried by then. Replay assumes a node's recorded outcome would have been the same under a policy that visited the tree in another order or skipped some siblings. The paper does not discuss this; it is the usual gap between a log and a simulator.

Setup: one controlled baseline

§4 Experiments

Eight tasks in three domains. The controlled comparison is Recursive Fixed Exploration: the same discovery agent, evaluator, initialisation and per-round budget, the same number of recursive rounds, but the exploration policy never changes (§4). Both start from one hand-written policy, parallel refine: independent workspaces side by side, each refining its own candidate from its own history. So round 1 is identical by construction, and every difference comes after.

  • Gemini-3.1-Pro: 10 workspaces × up to 11 refinements = 110 discovery-agent calls per round.
  • Gemini-3.7-Flash: 32 × 20 = 640 calls per round.
  • Both run through the Gemini CLI. Discovery cost is "the total cumulative number of discovery-agent calls" (§4). The development agent's own calls while dreaming are not counted, and neither is its model named.

Algorithm engineering: a faster Lasso path solver

§4.1

The task, taken from SimpleTES (Ye et al.), is to write a program that computes the whole Lasso regularisation path (the solution for a decreasing sequence of penalties λ) as fast as possible. A solver must match sklearn's objective within 10−6 at every λ on fresh instances, or its score is zero; the search score is the inverse geometric mean of the run times on the timing instances (Appendix A). The search uses SimpleTES's 17 synthetic instances; the reported numbers are wall-clock times on six held-out real datasets, two non-biological (Gisette, RCV1) and four biological (DNA, Leukemia, Colon, Duke Breast). Both methods run 5 rounds (§4.1, Baselines).

The headline (Figure 3a): with Gemini-3.1-Pro the average runtime falls from 3,587.1 ms (fixed exploration, 550 calls) to 2,931.0 ms with 317 calls; with Gemini-3.7-Flash from 2,516.7 ms (3,200 calls) to 2,350.6 ms with 1,879 calls. Both discovered solvers beat sklearn and glmnet on all six datasets, and both averages beat SimpleTES's 3,804.8 ms, which used 51,200 generations: 162 times more than 317 (§4.1, Main results). Its 51,200 is SimpleTES's default budget, 32 trajectories × 100 steps × 16 samples (SimpleTES §Methods).

With Gemini-3.1-Pro, Dream-RSI cut the average runtime by 18%. On how many of the six datasets is its solver faster than the fixed-exploration solver?

One: RCV1. On Gisette and on all four biological datasets the Dream-RSI solver is slower than the one fixed exploration found (Gisette 2,841.0 against 1,861.8 ms). The average is an arithmetic mean of milliseconds, and RCV1's times (about 15,000 to 20,000 ms) are 91% of the sum, so the average is essentially RCV1. On the geometric mean, which is the kind of mean the search score itself uses, Pro's Dream-RSI solver is 13% slower (179.4 against 159.1 ms). With Flash the picture holds up: faster on five of six, 8% faster on the geometric mean.

Two more things change the reading of that table, and neither is stated in the paper:

  • The SimpleTES row is copied from another machine. Its six numbers are identical to SimpleTES's own Supplementary Table 16, while this paper's sklearn and glmnet rows are about twice as slow as SimpleTES's (glmnet 2.12× on Gisette and RCV1), so they were timed on a slower machine. The dagger row "SimpleTES†", never defined in the paper and called "our reproduction" on the project page, is about 2× slower than the copied row too: it looks like SimpleTES re-timed on this paper's machine. Against that same-machine row Dream-RSI looks better (8,318.4 against 2,931.0 or 2,350.6 ms), not worse.
  • A call is not a generation. A Dream-RSI "discovery-agent call" is one Gemini CLI agent session that reads the whole history and writes a proposal and a program for the evaluator to score (Appendix B.1); a SimpleTES generation is one sample from gpt-oss-120b. 317 against 51,200 counts different units with different models.

Round by round (Figure 3b, decoded): the Pro run spent 110, 9, 28, 87 and 83 calls in its five rounds, and its held-out runtime got worse for three rounds (5,267 → 5,349 → 5,369 → 5,706 ms) before the last round's solver dropped it to 2,931 ms. At 234 calls it was slower than fixed exploration's solver at 110. So "consistently achieves superior downstream performance while requiring substantially lower cumulative compute" (§4.1, Dynamics) holds for the Flash run (640, 336, 254, 299, 350 calls, ahead every round) but not for Pro, where the final round decides it.

The discovered solver (Appendix C) combines strong-rule screening with Cauchy–Schwarz bounds that certify which features can be skipped in the optimality (KKT) check, recomputes exact gradients only where the bound cannot certify a feature, and falls back to a full refresh when pruning stops paying, with lazy Gram-matrix construction and careful active-set bookkeeping (§4.1, Discovered solver). SimpleTES's solver, by contrast, switches between LARS and coordinate descent by problem shape. The paper says the Pro program "appears particularly well suited to large-scale matrices such as RCV1", but the Flash program is faster on RCV1 (12,923.4 against 14,616.0 ms).

Mathematical optimisation: three open problems

§4.2

Three problems with formal definitions in Appendix A: the sum–difference problem (find a finite integer set A maximising log(|A+A|/|A|) / log(|A−A|/|A|)), circle packing (n circles in the unit square, maximise the sum of radii) and an autocorrelation inequality (find a function on [−¼, ¼] with integral 1 whose autoconvolution has the smallest peak). Gemini-3.1-Pro runs 10 rounds of each method; Table 1 compares against nine published systems (§4.2).

The results (Table 1): sum–difference 1.145427, above SimpleTES (1.143975) and fixed exploration (1.144047); circle packing 2.635983, "matching the strongest reported result"; autocorrelation 1.456375, "remaining competitive". SimpleTES holds the autocorrelation best (1.453675) "but requires 51,200 generations, significantly more than the fewer than 1,000 generations used by our approach" (§4.2, Results).

Read the table more closely
  • On autocorrelation Dream-RSI loses to its own baseline. Lower is better, and fixed exploration's 1.456001 beats Dream-RSI's 1.456375; it ranks fourth of the eight entries with a value, behind SimpleTES, AlphaEvolve and fixed exploration. The project README's own summary says "2 of 3 tasks at or above the selected baseline".
  • Which inequality? Appendix A defines three autocorrelation problems; the column does not say which. SimpleTES's Table 1 prints 1.453675 for the third inequality and lists a previous best, Together AI's 1.454555, that this table leaves out (SimpleTES).
  • Circle packing is saturated. Six of the eleven entries print 2.635983 (the n = 26 value), so the task cannot separate methods.
  • The sum–difference gain is 0.12% over fixed exploration (+0.00138), one run each. SimpleTES reports 1.144887 with a post-trained model, still below 1.145427.
  • The generation count is never given for Dream-RSI on these tasks; "fewer than 1,000" is all, and fixed exploration's 10 rounds of 110 would be 1,100.

GPU kernel engineering: four KernelBench tasks

§4.3

Four tasks from KernelBench (Ouyang et al. 2025): VGG16, LayerNorm, ConvDiv and ConvMax. A candidate kernel must match the PyTorch reference; its score is speed, 1 / runtime in ms. Gemini-3.1-Pro is the coding agent (§4.3). The GPU is not named, and no speed-up over the PyTorch reference is reported; Figure 5 starts both ConvDiv curves at 0.1184 per ms at zero generations, an unlabelled point that is probably the starting program.

The claims (Figure 4): on VGG16 and LayerNorm Dream-RSI reaches "comparable final performance using 2.43× and 1.79× fewer generations"; on ConvDiv and ConvMax it is 2.09× and 1.44× faster "under comparable discovery budgets". All four ratios reproduce from the decoded curves (the checks).

VGG16: fixed exploration ran 9 rounds of 110 generations (990); Dream-RSI stopped at 408, hence "2.43× fewer". How many generations did fixed exploration need to reach Dream-RSI's final speed?

770. Fixed exploration passed Dream-RSI's final 0.5319 per ms at 770 generations, so the saving at matched performance is 770 / 408 = 1.89×, not 2.43×. The 2.43× is the ratio of the two total budgets, at which Dream-RSI ends 1.1% below fixed exploration (0.5319 against 0.5376). LayerNorm shrinks more: fixed reaches Dream-RSI's final 1.1223 at 660 generations, 1.19× rather than 1.79×, and again ends higher (1.1274).

The two speed claims are point comparisons too. ConvDiv's 2.09× is Dream-RSI's final 1.8976 against fixed exploration at 770 generations (0.9092); against fixed's own final (1.2820 at 990 generations) it is 1.48×, still a large gap. ConvMax's 1.44× comes from one jump in Dream-RSI's last round (0.2786 → 0.4310); until then it trailed fixed exploration. Dream-RSI's rounds are mostly smaller: VGG16's after the first were 16 to 80 generations against 110.

Analysis: guidance, and what the learned policy does

§5 Further Analysis

History as guidance instead of a simulator. A natural alternative is to summarise past trajectories into "high-level directional insights" and put them in the agent's prompt. On ConvDiv, both methods with such guidance end below their unguided runs (Figure 5): Dream-RSI 1.4881 against 1.8976, fixed exploration 0.6329 against 1.2820. The paper reads this as strong semantic priors "over-constrain the search space" (§5.1). "Consistently underperforms" is too strong for the curves: the guided runs are ahead early (fixed with guidance leads at 220 to 550 generations, Dream-RSI with guidance at 190 to 349), and the guided Dream-RSI run used 11 rounds against 9. One run per arm, one task.

How the policy changes. On ConvDiv the learned policy spent 110, 110, 87, 80, 50, 92, 80, 91 and 86 attempts in rounds E0 to E8 while its round-best speed rose 0.427 → 0.625 → 0.855 → 1.403 → 1.488 → 1.499 → 1.770 → 1.880 → 1.898 (Figure 6; these are the same run as Figure 4's ConvDiv curve, and the attempts add up to its 786 generations). The paper's reading: it first conserves compute as performance improves ("from 110 to 50"), then spends more again when progress plateaus, "coinciding with further performance gains" (§5.2). Nine points from one run, so a pattern to look for rather than a measured behaviour.

How much of this to believe

Verdict: believe the mechanism, and believe that it can cut calls; treat every number as one anecdote. The idea is sound and cheap, the reported ratios reproduce from the paper's own figures, and some results are large (ConvDiv). But nothing is repeated, several headline ratios are chosen at their most favourable point, the cost metric omits the dreaming itself, and the method cannot be rerun from the paper.

What holds up

  • The idea is correct on its own terms. A recorded tree really is an exact record of what each attempt produced, and re-scoring a different traversal of it costs nothing. The selection rule (keep the best of M versions, the current one included) can only improve the replay score on the history.
  • The arithmetic checks. Every average, every call count (5 × 110 = 550, 5 × 640 = 3,200), the 162× and 1.7× ratios and all four kernel ratios reproduce from the paper's own tables and decoded figures; Figure 6 adds up to Figure 4's ConvDiv curve exactly (all checks).
  • The baseline is controlled. Same agent, evaluator, initialisation, starting policy and per-round budget, so round 1 is shared and only the policy differs.
  • Some effects are large. ConvDiv ends 1.48× faster than fixed exploration's own final at fewer generations; Lasso with Flash is faster on five of six datasets at 59% of the calls.
  • The authors are candid in places. They write "competitive or improved" and "in several settings", and the README's summary admits "2 of 3" maths tasks.

What does not

  • One run per arm, no error bars. Discovery runs are stochastic (the paper says so in §3), yet every curve is one run. Held-out Lasso runtime moves by hundreds of milliseconds between rounds of the same method (fixed Pro: 5,267, 5,365, 5,295, 4,691, 3,587 ms), so the gaps being compared are of the size of round-to-round noise.
  • Ratios taken at the best point. VGG16's 2.43× and LayerNorm's 1.79× are ratios of total budgets at which Dream-RSI ends slightly worse; at matched performance they are 1.89× and 1.19×. ConvDiv's 2.09× compares against fixed exploration at 770 generations rather than its final (1.48×). ConvMax's 1.44× is one final-round jump.
  • An average carried by one dataset. Pro's Lasso solver is faster than fixed exploration's on 1 of 6 datasets and 13% slower on the geometric mean; the 18% gain is RCV1.
  • A cost metric that leaves out the dreaming. Only discovery-agent calls are counted. The development agent writes M versions of the policy every round and reads every replay trace; M, its model and its token use are not reported. Replay is free of evaluations, not free.
  • Comparisons that are not like for like. SimpleTES's Lasso row is copied from its own paper and was timed on a machine about twice as fast as the one that timed this paper's sklearn and glmnet rows; 51,200 gpt-oss-120b generations are not the same unit as 317 Gemini CLI sessions; Table 1's other systems used other models and budgets, and it omits a previous best (Together AI's 1.454555).
  • Claims stronger than the figures. "Consistently superior" (Figure 3b) fails for Pro in two of four later rounds; "consistently underperforms" (Figure 5) fails early in both guided runs; on autocorrelation Dream-RSI is beaten by its own baseline.
  • Not reproducible as written. β1, β2, M, K1, K2, the replay worker count, the GPU and the development agent's model are not given; the prompt's objective differs from Eq. 1; no code, traces or programs are released yet (README release plan, 3 October 2026).
  • What replay cannot see. It rewards only what the record covers. A policy that would go deeper or wider than the recorded grid "is out of support and cannot earn replay reward" (Appendix B.2), and no replayed policy can score above the best recorded node. Replay also assumes a node's outcome does not depend on which siblings had been tried first, while the agent's prompt makes every attempt read them all.

The limit, stated plainly

A replay simulator is only valid inside the region the history covers. Dreaming therefore sharpens how a mapped space is exploited (fewer, better-placed probes), and cannot by itself push exploration into unmapped territory; whatever new territory Dream-RSI reaches comes from the online rounds. The toy in Dream over a recorded tree shows the shape of this: the dreamed policy wins mostly by probing less, keeps most of the best result on fresh worlds of the same kind, and loses more when the next world rewards depth it never recorded. It is the opposite failure from the one Agora reported, where the population converged prematurely and a human had to restore diversity; both point at the same risk in self-improving loops: the cheap signal is always the one that describes where you have already been.

What it takes to use this

  • Code: none yet. github.com/zhengkid/Dream-RSI holds the PDF, figures and a README whose release plan lists "Full codebase", "Reproduction scripts" and "Discovered programs" as "being prepared"; there is no licence file (checked 3 October 2026).
  • What you would build: an orchestration layer that logs every attempt as a tree node (workspace snapshot, artifact, evaluator output, score), a replay engine for that log, a policy interface (solve() over revealed cells, plan_grid() for the next round's width and depth), and a development agent prompted as in Appendix B.2. The prompts in Appendix B are the most reusable part of the paper.
  • Budget: the reported runs are small: 317 to 1,879 Gemini CLI calls for Lasso, under about 1,000 generations per maths or kernel task, plus an unreported amount of development-agent work per round.
  • When it should pay: when evaluations are expensive, the per-round budget is large enough to waste, and successive rounds explore similar ground, so that old trees say something about the next one. It buys little when one run is all you have (nothing to replay yet) or when the useful directions lie beyond anything recorded.
  • What to measure first: run each arm several times, report matched-performance savings and per-task results, and count the development agent's calls in the cost.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

Automated discovery loops have become a standard tool: AlphaEvolve (Novikov et al. 2025), and SimpleTES, which reports results on 28 problems across quantum compilation, astrodynamics, scientific algorithms, kernels and mathematics with one open model and spends 51,200 generations per problem by default (Ye et al. 2026, Table 1). Once the loop works, the open question is how to spend its budget, and that is a policy that is usually hand-written.

Dream-RSI's contribution is to notice that every finished run is free training data for that policy, usable without touching model weights and without new evaluations. That is off-policy evaluation from logged data, an old idea in reinforcement learning, applied to the meta layer of a discovery loop; the classic risk of model-based methods, a policy that exploits the model where the model is wrong (Moerland et al. survey), takes a specific form here: the record is exact but incomplete, so the bias is toward doing less of what was recorded rather than toward hallucinated rewards.

It also fits the week's pattern in this knowledge base: three auto-research papers that differ mainly in where the loop's memory lives (Agora in a shared commit graph, SoL-Pi in harness-layer techniques discovered once and reused, Dream-RSI in a replay simulator built from the search history), as the Agora page puts it.

Scores are in invented units from 0 to 1; about a third of directions are good, some improve only after several refinements, and about one attempt in six fails. Nothing quantitative here comes from the paper; what comes from the paper is the procedure: replay rules, Eq. 1 and the selection rule.

Try: step the fixed policy and the dreamed one through the same recorded world, then dream over more history, raise the batching bonus β2 until the fixed policy wins, and deploy the winner on fresh worlds where gains arrive later.

Replay many policies over a recorded tree and keep the one with the best Eq. 1 score. Compared with the policy that recorded the tree, what can the winner do?

Only the second. Replay reveals recorded nodes and nothing else, so the best a replayed policy can reach is the best node already in the tree, which the policy that recorded the whole tree found by definition. What dreaming can buy is the same (or nearly the same) best for fewer attempts and fewer rounds. Any better result has to come from the next online round. Watch it below: the "share of recorded best" counter never passes 100%.

2. Dream: score every candidate on the history

The paper's development agent writes M revisions of the policy's code. Here the candidates are 504 settings of a small policy family instead: how many branches to keep open at once, how many attempts without gain before closing a branch, whether to drop branches well below the best seen, and whether to open a fresh branch when one closes. Each is replayed on every recorded world; the winner is the highest average of Eq. 1, and the fixed policy is in the pool, so the winner can never score below it on the history.

3. Deploy the winner online

Now the winner meets worlds it has never seen: 300 fresh grids from the same generator, or from one where useful gains arrive later in each branch. On each, it and the fixed policy run as they would online (in the toy an outcome does not depend on the order of visits; in the real system it does, since each attempt reads its siblings).

What the toy shows, and what it cannot

  • Dreaming optimises cost against a fixed ceiling. In replay no policy can beat the best recorded node, so once the charge per attempt β1 outweighs the batching bonus β2 the winner is a policy that finds nearly that node with far fewer probes (29 to 43 of 110 on the default history). That matches the shape of the paper's gains (fewer calls, similar quality), and it says the quality gains the paper reports must come from the online rounds, not from replay.
  • The guarantee is in-sample. On the history the winner always scores at least as well as the fixed policy; on fresh worlds it gives up some of the best result for its savings, and more when the new worlds reward depth the history did not show.
  • Eq. 1's coefficients decide the answer. Raise β2 and the fixed policy, which batches all 10 workers every round, wins outright. The paper never reports β1 or β2.
  • Not modelled: outcomes that depend on what siblings were tried, the development agent writing code rather than choosing from a grid, plan_grid changing the next grid's shape, and real score distributions. The generator is invented; only the rules are the paper's.

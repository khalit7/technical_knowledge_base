Setup: one controlled baseline

[[S4|§4 Experiments]]

Eight tasks in three domains. The controlled comparison is Recursive Fixed Exploration: the same discovery agent, evaluator, initialisation and per-round budget, the same number of recursive rounds, but the exploration policy never changes ({{§4|ax:S4}}). Both start from one hand-written policy, parallel refine: independent workspaces side by side, each refining its own candidate from its own history. So round 1 is identical by construction, and every difference comes after.

  • Gemini-3.1-Pro: 10 workspaces × up to 11 refinements = 110 discovery-agent calls per round.
  • Gemini-3.7-Flash: 32 × 20 = 640 calls per round.
  • Both run through the Gemini CLI. Discovery cost is "the total cumulative number of discovery-agent calls" ({{§4|ax:S4}}). The development agent's own calls while dreaming are not counted, and neither is its model named.

Every comparison below is one run of each method. Watch six of them unfold round by round, with the paper's own numbers decoded from its vector figures:

Top: each round's budget as a bar, to scale (one bar per recursive round, width = generations or agent calls). Bottom: the round's result against cumulative cost. Values come from the figures' vector paths, calibrated on their own tick marks (kernel values to about 0.0002, Lasso runtimes to about 0.1 ms); the last Lasso points equal Figure 3(a)'s table. Each line is one run.

Algorithm engineering: a faster Lasso path solver

[[S4.SS1|§4.1]]

The task, taken from SimpleTES ({{Ye et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19341}}), is to write a program that computes the whole Lasso regularisation path (the solution for a decreasing sequence of penalties λ) as fast as possible. A solver must match sklearn's objective within 10−6 at every λ on fresh instances, or its score is zero; the search score is the inverse geometric mean of the run times on the timing instances ({{Appendix A|ax:A1}}). The search uses SimpleTES's 17 synthetic instances; the reported numbers are wall-clock times on six held-out real datasets, two non-biological (Gisette, RCV1) and four biological (DNA, Leukemia, Colon, Duke Breast). Both methods run 5 rounds ({{§4.1, Baselines|ax:S4.SS1.SSS0.Px1}}).

The headline ({{Figure 3a|ax:S4.F3}}): with Gemini-3.1-Pro the average runtime falls from 3,587.1 ms (fixed exploration, 550 calls) to 2,931.0 ms with 317 calls; with Gemini-3.7-Flash from 2,516.7 ms (3,200 calls) to 2,350.6 ms with 1,879 calls. Both discovered solvers beat sklearn and glmnet on all six datasets, and both averages beat SimpleTES's 3,804.8 ms, which used 51,200 generations: 162 times more than 317 ({{§4.1, Main results|ax:S4.SS1.SSS0.Px2}}). Its 51,200 is SimpleTES's default budget, 32 trajectories × 100 steps × 16 samples ({{SimpleTES §Methods|https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19341}}).

With Gemini-3.1-Pro, Dream-RSI cut the average runtime by 18%. On how many of the six datasets is its solver faster than the fixed-exploration solver?

One: RCV1. On Gisette and on all four biological datasets the Dream-RSI solver is slower than the one fixed exploration found (Gisette 2,841.0 against 1,861.8 ms). The average is an arithmetic mean of milliseconds, and RCV1's times (about 15,000 to 20,000 ms) are 91% of the sum, so the average is essentially RCV1. On the geometric mean, which is the kind of mean the search score itself uses, Pro's Dream-RSI solver is 13% slower (179.4 against 159.1 ms). With Flash the picture holds up: faster on five of six, 8% faster on the geometric mean.

Two more things change the reading of that table, and neither is stated in the paper:

  • The SimpleTES row is copied from another machine. Its six numbers are identical to SimpleTES's own Supplementary Table 16, while this paper's sklearn and glmnet rows are about twice as slow as SimpleTES's (glmnet 2.12× on Gisette and RCV1), so they were timed on a slower machine. The dagger row "SimpleTES†", never defined in the paper and called "our reproduction" on the {{project page|https://www.dream-rsi.com}}, is about 2× slower than the copied row too: it looks like SimpleTES re-timed on this paper's machine. Against that same-machine row Dream-RSI looks better (8,318.4 against 2,931.0 or 2,350.6 ms), not worse.
  • A call is not a generation. A Dream-RSI "discovery-agent call" is one Gemini CLI agent session that reads the whole history and writes a proposal and a program for the evaluator to score ({{Appendix B.1|ax:A2.SS1}}); a SimpleTES generation is one sample from gpt-oss-120b. 317 against 51,200 counts different units with different models.

Round by round ({{Figure 3b|ax:S4.F3}}, decoded): the Pro run spent 110, 9, 28, 87 and 83 calls in its five rounds, and its held-out runtime got worse for three rounds (5,267 → 5,349 → 5,369 → 5,706 ms) before the last round's solver dropped it to 2,931 ms. At 234 calls it was slower than fixed exploration's solver at 110. So "consistently achieves superior downstream performance while requiring substantially lower cumulative compute" ({{§4.1, Dynamics|ax:S4.SS1.SSS0.Px3}}) holds for the Flash run (640, 336, 254, 299, 350 calls, ahead every round) but not for Pro, where the final round decides it.

The discovered solver ({{Appendix C|ax:A3}}) combines strong-rule screening with Cauchy–Schwarz bounds that certify which features can be skipped in the optimality (KKT) check, recomputes exact gradients only where the bound cannot certify a feature, and falls back to a full refresh when pruning stops paying, with lazy Gram-matrix construction and careful active-set bookkeeping ({{§4.1, Discovered solver|ax:S4.SS1.SSS0.Px4}}). SimpleTES's solver, by contrast, switches between LARS and coordinate descent by problem shape. The paper says the Pro program "appears particularly well suited to large-scale matrices such as RCV1", but the Flash program is faster on RCV1 (12,923.4 against 14,616.0 ms).

Mathematical optimisation: three open problems

[[S4.SS2|§4.2]]

Three problems with formal definitions in {{Appendix A|ax:A1}}: the sum–difference problem (find a finite integer set A maximising log(|A+A|/|A|) / log(|A−A|/|A|)), circle packing (n circles in the unit square, maximise the sum of radii) and an autocorrelation inequality (find a function on [−¼, ¼] with integral 1 whose autoconvolution has the smallest peak). Gemini-3.1-Pro runs 10 rounds of each method; Table 1 compares against nine published systems ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}).

The results ({{Table 1|ax:S4.T1}}): sum–difference 1.145427, above SimpleTES (1.143975) and fixed exploration (1.144047); circle packing 2.635983, "matching the strongest reported result"; autocorrelation 1.456375, "remaining competitive". SimpleTES holds the autocorrelation best (1.453675) "but requires 51,200 generations, significantly more than the fewer than 1,000 generations used by our approach" ({{§4.2, Results|ax:S4.SS2.SSS0.Px1}}).

Read the table more closely
  • On autocorrelation Dream-RSI loses to its own baseline. Lower is better, and fixed exploration's 1.456001 beats Dream-RSI's 1.456375; it ranks fourth of the eight entries with a value, behind SimpleTES, AlphaEvolve and fixed exploration. The project README's own summary says "2 of 3 tasks at or above the selected baseline".
  • Which inequality? Appendix A defines three autocorrelation problems; the column does not say which. SimpleTES's Table 1 prints 1.453675 for the third inequality and lists a previous best, Together AI's 1.454555, that this table leaves out ({{SimpleTES|https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19341}}).
  • Circle packing is saturated. Six of the eleven entries print 2.635983 (the n = 26 value), so the task cannot separate methods.
  • The sum–difference gain is 0.12% over fixed exploration (+0.00138), one run each. SimpleTES reports 1.144887 with a post-trained model, still below 1.145427.
  • The generation count is never given for Dream-RSI on these tasks; "fewer than 1,000" is all, and fixed exploration's 10 rounds of 110 would be 1,100.

GPU kernel engineering: four KernelBench tasks

[[S4.SS3|§4.3]]

Four tasks from KernelBench ({{Ouyang et al. 2025|https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.10517}}): VGG16, LayerNorm, ConvDiv and ConvMax. A candidate kernel must match the PyTorch reference; its score is speed, 1 / runtime in ms. Gemini-3.1-Pro is the coding agent ({{§4.3|ax:S4.SS3}}). The GPU is not named, and no speed-up over the PyTorch reference is reported; {{Figure 5|ax:S5.F5}} starts both ConvDiv curves at 0.1184 per ms at zero generations, an unlabelled point that is probably the starting program.

The claims ({{Figure 4|ax:S4.F4}}): on VGG16 and LayerNorm Dream-RSI reaches "comparable final performance using 2.43× and 1.79× fewer generations"; on ConvDiv and ConvMax it is 2.09× and 1.44× faster "under comparable discovery budgets". All four ratios reproduce from the decoded curves ({{the checks|tab:t-tables:tb-chk}}).

VGG16: fixed exploration ran 9 rounds of 110 generations (990); Dream-RSI stopped at 408, hence "2.43× fewer". How many generations did fixed exploration need to reach Dream-RSI's final speed?

770. Fixed exploration passed Dream-RSI's final 0.5319 per ms at 770 generations, so the saving at matched performance is 770 / 408 = 1.89×, not 2.43×. The 2.43× is the ratio of the two total budgets, at which Dream-RSI ends 1.1% below fixed exploration (0.5319 against 0.5376). LayerNorm shrinks more: fixed reaches Dream-RSI's final 1.1223 at 660 generations, 1.19× rather than 1.79×, and again ends higher (1.1274).

The two speed claims are point comparisons too. ConvDiv's 2.09× is Dream-RSI's final 1.8976 against fixed exploration at 770 generations (0.9092); against fixed's own final (1.2820 at 990 generations) it is 1.48×, still a large gap. ConvMax's 1.44× comes from one jump in Dream-RSI's last round (0.2786 → 0.4310); until then it trailed fixed exploration. Dream-RSI's rounds are mostly smaller: VGG16's after the first were 16 to 80 generations against 110.

Analysis: guidance, and what the learned policy does

[[S5|§5 Further Analysis]]

History as guidance instead of a simulator. A natural alternative is to summarise past trajectories into "high-level directional insights" and put them in the agent's prompt. On ConvDiv, both methods with such guidance end below their unguided runs ({{Figure 5|ax:S5.F5}}): Dream-RSI 1.4881 against 1.8976, fixed exploration 0.6329 against 1.2820. The paper reads this as strong semantic priors "over-constrain the search space" ({{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1}}). "Consistently underperforms" is too strong for the curves: the guided runs are ahead early (fixed with guidance leads at 220 to 550 generations, Dream-RSI with guidance at 190 to 349), and the guided Dream-RSI run used 11 rounds against 9. One run per arm, one task.

How the policy changes. On ConvDiv the learned policy spent 110, 110, 87, 80, 50, 92, 80, 91 and 86 attempts in rounds E0 to E8 while its round-best speed rose 0.427 → 0.625 → 0.855 → 1.403 → 1.488 → 1.499 → 1.770 → 1.880 → 1.898 ({{Figure 6|ax:S5.F6}}; these are the same run as Figure 4's ConvDiv curve, and the attempts add up to its 786 generations). The paper's reading: it first conserves compute as performance improves ("from 110 to 50"), then spends more again when progress plateaus, "coinciding with further performance gains" ({{§5.2|ax:S5.SS2}}). Nine points from one run, so a pattern to look for rather than a measured behaviour.

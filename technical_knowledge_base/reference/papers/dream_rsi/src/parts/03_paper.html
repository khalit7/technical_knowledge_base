@@CARD@@

The idea in one sentence: a finished discovery run is an exact record of what every attempt produced, so a revised exploration policy (where to branch, how wide to batch, when to stop) can be scored by replaying it over that record at no cost in new calls; the replay can only re-order what was recorded, so it buys the same results for fewer attempts, and anything better has to come from the next online run.

You need: the model-based RL idea of improving a policy against a model instead of the world, Dyna and Dreamer ({{Model-based RL and planning|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8116be5fd39e10bd94c5}}); nothing else beyond agent loops. New terms: discovery loop (propose, evaluate, learn, repeat); exploration policy (the code that picks which attempts to continue and how many at once); discovery tree or world (one recorded run: every attempt, its parent and its score); dreaming (replaying a policy over recorded worlds); budget preference ρ (a knob the policy reads: high means wider and more patient); development agent (the LLM that rewrites the policy's code); Recursive Fixed Exploration (the baseline: same loop, policy never changes); discovery cost (discovery-agent calls, not counting the development agent).

Problem: exploration is the expensive part to improve

[[S1|§1 Introduction]]

An agentic discovery loop proposes a candidate (a program, a construction, a kernel, an agent harness), evaluates it, learns from the result and proposes again. AlphaEvolve and its successors run such loops over algorithm design, open mathematical problems and systems code, and the hardest targets now take "thousands of proposal-evaluation cycles" ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Every cycle costs a model call plus an evaluation: compiling and benchmarking a GPU kernel, running an optimisation to convergence, or running a benchmark suite.

What decides whether those cycles are well spent is the exploration policy: which directions to pursue, which candidates to refine, how many branches to run in parallel, and when to stop. In most systems it is written by hand and stays fixed for the whole run, so it "may repeatedly spend computation on unproductive search directions" ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Improving it online is costly for two reasons the paper names:

  • Meta-level feedback is delayed and expensive. Judging a policy means watching it steer a whole discovery run over many cycles, not scoring one candidate.
  • The meta-policy space is vast. Most policies you might try are bad, and each costs a long online rollout to find that out.

EvoX ({{Liu et al. 2026a|https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23413}}) optimises the search strategy online and pays exactly this cost. Dream-RSI asks whether the policy can be improved without paying it.

Idea: the history you already paid for is a simulator

[[S2|§2 Motivation]]

A finished discovery run is not just text to reread. It is a tree: every attempt, the attempt it continued from, and the outcome it actually produced when executed and scored. An exploration policy does one thing: given what it has seen, choose which attempts to continue next. So a different policy can be "run" on the recorded tree by walking it differently (other branches, another order, other parallel groupings, earlier stopping) and simply reading the outcomes already on disk. No agent call, no evaluation. In the authors' words, "accumulated discovery history can act as a replay simulator over the realized search space" ({{abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14858}}).

The analogy is model-based reinforcement learning ({{§2|ax:S2}}): Sutton's Dyna (1990) and the Dreamer family learn a model of the environment and improve a policy on imagined experience (both in general: {{Model-based RL and planning|n:3ee5c17b0d0d8116be5fd39e10bd94c5}}). Here the "model" is not learned at all; it is the record itself, exact where the record goes and silent everywhere else. The paper calls evaluating policies in it dreaming and the recorded trees worlds (footnote 1, {{§1|ax:S1}}).

The cost argument is simple. One expensive online run supports many cheap offline evaluations of other policies ({{Figure 2|ax:S1.F2}}), so the meta-level search that EvoX pays for in rollouts is paid for here in reads. The catch is equally simple: a policy can only be judged where history went. Method: trees, replay and one score

[[S3|§3 Dream-RSI]]

The tree and the decision interface

A discovery tree has a root r (the starting workspace, score 0). Every other node has one parent, where its attempt began: the agent resumes the parent's saved workspace and its accumulated observations, makes a new attempt, and the node stores its generation history, its files and a score sv, larger is better ({{§3, Discovery trees|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1}}). At each decision the policy may continue from the root (open a new branch) or from any current leaf: the eligible set is A(𝒯) = {r} ∪ {leaves}. With W parallel workers it picks a batch C ⊆ A(𝒯) of at most W nodes, so one choice fixes both where to continue and how many attempts run at once.

Online rollout

At outer iteration t, policy πt grows a fresh tree from the root for at most K1 rounds, with the earlier history available as context. Each selected node gets one new child per round; the step is stochastic because the agent may produce something different from the same workspace. The rollout ends on an empty batch or after K1 rounds, and the finished tree joins the history ({{§3, Online rollout|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px2}}). Table 3 gives the per-round ceilings: 10 branches × 11 attempts (110 calls) for most tasks ({{Table 3|ax:A2.T3}}).

Offline replay

With the history fixed, each policy version is replayed on every recorded tree separately, and once for each of five budget preferences ρ ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1} that the policy reads ({{§3, Offline evaluation|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px3}}). Replay starts from the bare root and reveals the tree only as the policy asks:

  • selecting a revealed leaf reveals its one recorded child, if it has one;
  • selecting the root opens the earliest-created unopened branch;
  • nothing beyond the recorded tree is ever generated; replay stops on an empty batch, after K2 rounds, or when everything is revealed.

So replay can test how far to pursue each branch, how to group attempts into batches, and when to open another branch or stop. It cannot test a different attempt: every branch is traversed in its recorded order.

Watch the rule work on an illustrative recorded world: a generated 10 branches × 11 attempts grid (the Gemini-3.1-Pro round of Table 3), scores in invented units from 0 to 1, about one attempt in six failed. Only the replay rules, the score and the selection rule are the paper's; no traces were released. Step the fixed policy and the dreamed winner through the same world, at the budget preference you choose.

Replay many policies over a recorded tree and keep the one with the best replay score. Compared with the policy that recorded the tree, what can the winner do?

Only the second. Replay reveals recorded nodes and nothing else, so the best a replayed policy can reach is the best node already in the tree, which the policy that recorded the whole tree found by definition. What dreaming can buy is the same (or nearly the same) best for fewer attempts and fewer rounds. Any better result has to come from the next online round. A policy version's score is its average V over all t recorded trees ({{§3, Replay objective|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px4}}). A development agent (a fixed LLM) reads the replay trajectories and scores of the current version, rewrites the policy's code, and repeats: the current policy plus three rewrites, M = 4 versions per offline phase ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}). The next online policy is the best-scoring of them; because the current policy is one of them, the chosen one "is never worse than πt on the average replay score" over the history ({{§3, Policy improvement|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px5}}). That guarantee is in-sample: it says nothing about the next online run, which is the point the toy in the {{second tab|tab:t-run}} measures.

What the area does to the starting policy

The hand-written starting policy has no budget knob, so all five of its replays reveal the whole tree: one point at (Bi, 100%), zero area, and with the released defaults a score of −0.05 × 11/110 = −0.005 (this page ran the released code on its worlds and got exactly that). Any rewrite that reaches anything with fewer calls outscores it, so the "never worse" guarantee is close to automatic in the first offline phase; selection only bites between rewrites. The score also says nothing about the absolute best: a policy that reaches 96% of the recorded best with a quarter of the calls beats one that reaches 100% with all of them.

In v1 the score was different: the best score reached, minus β1 per attempt, plus β2 per average batch size, with neither coefficient given, and the authors' own prompt already used the area form. The {{second tab|tab:t-run}} scores the same candidates both ways; the rest of v1's method text is in {{What changed from v1|tab:t-read:s-v1}}.

The released code against the text

The code ({{google-research/dream-rsi|https://github.com/google-research/dream-rsi}}, Apache 2.0, commit 8ec35bd of 8 October 2026) computes this score in dream_rsi/learning/heuristic_search/eval.py. This page ported it next to its own simulator and ran the released code on the same recorded worlds: The code matches the text on the frontier, the area, the five budget preferences, M = 4 and Table 3's rounds. The differences are small: λ = 0.05 is only in the code, the penalty counts worker-capped rounds, replay has no round limit and may open several branches at once, and some worlds are left out of the average.

The four differences between code and text
  • λ is only in the code (0.05, with W = 10 replay workers), as is the fact that the starting policy ignores ρ.
  • The penalty counts worker-capped rounds: a batch of c calls costs ⌈c / W⌉ rounds, and the code does not cap a batch at W as §3 does; it prices it instead. With batches of at most W the two agree.
  • Replay has no round limit K2, and a batch may open several new branches at once, in any order, where §3 opens the earliest unopened branch once per round.
  • Some worlds are left out of the average: a recorded tree whose best score is not positive, or with fewer than 2 evaluated nodes, is skipped, and a replay that spent no calls is left out of the penalty's average instead of counting as zero.
The rest of the code reading: selection, the policy interface, what is not released
  • Selection keeps the highest-scoring candidate that passed replay validation (every trace and every budget preference replayed without error); ties go to the later rewrite. If no candidate, the incumbent included, passes, up to three more batches of three rewrites are tried before the step fails.
  • The policy also plans the next online grid (plan_grid: how many branches and refinements the next round gets, within hard caps), which §3 does not describe and the prompt does. A plan larger than a recorded tree makes that replay fail ("out of support"), and the whole budget preference is then dropped from the score.
  • The replay rules are in dream_rsi/env/trace_env.py: a cell is legal once its parent is revealed, failed attempts are scored 0 and still cost a call, and the best so far starts at 0, as §3 has it.
  • Not released: the harness-optimisation task, any recorded trees, and the discovered programs (the paper's own repository lists them as "being prepared"). The maths and Lasso task files come from SimpleTES under the AGPL-3.0 and are downloaded by a script; the kernels need a GPU evaluation server. The 39 tests of the replay, policy and learning modules pass on a laptop CPU in under a second.
  • How this page checked it: all 505 candidate policies of the toy, replayed by the page at the five budget preferences on its three default worlds, were fed to the released _pareto_summary; then the worlds were written as released trace files and ten policies (the released parallel-refine incumbent and nine toy policies written against the released policy API) were replayed by the released eval.py --beta-sweep. Calls, rounds and best per world and budget preference agree in all 150 replays, and every score to 2 × 10−16 (src/check_score.py).

What the policy and the agent actually see

[[A4|Appendix D]]

The exploration policy is a Python class with a solve() method that the development agent rewrites under hard rules ({{Appendix D.2|ax:A4.SS2}}): decisions may use only what has been revealed, batches are a portfolio of up to W cells (never a parent and its child together), one "beta" knob (the paper's ρ) trades width and patience, and plan_grid() sizes the next live run, where anything beyond the recorded grid "is out of support and cannot earn replay reward". The prompt ranks policies by "pareto.reward = pareto.auc − lambda × parallel_penalty", which is v2's Eq. 1; the prompts are unchanged from v1.

The policy prompt's rules in full (Appendix D.2)

The exploration policy is a Python class with a solve() method that the development agent rewrites. Its prompt ({{Appendix D.2|ax:A4.SS2}}) describes the world as "a frozen, irregular branch×attempt grid" and sets hard rules:

  • Prefix only. Decisions may use revealed observations, structural metadata and helper signals, never unrevealed scores, a known optimum, hard-coded cell ids or absolute score targets.
  • Read each branch as a trajectory (its best successful result, trend, regressions, failure and repair sequence) and classify failures as hard-unrecoverable, repairable implementation failure, weak but underexplored, or repeatedly unpromising. Correctness mismatches, shared-memory limits and shape errors are "normally repairable".
  • Batch as a portfolio of up to W independent cells: strong refinements, new roots or underexplored branches, and at most one recovery of a repairable failure; never random, never a fixed widen-all or deepen-all schedule, never a parent and its child together.
  • One knob, beta, fixed within an episode: high beta means wider, more patient, weaker pruning. The default is moved by about 0.1 to 0.2 between live cycles from evidence (raise it on a plateau if the sweep shows higher beta reaches more; lower it if high beta only adds work).
  • Plan the next grid with plan_grid(): how many branches and refinements the next live run gets, chosen from earlier live manifests. A plan beyond the recorded tree's width or depth "is out of support and cannot earn replay reward".

The coding agent itself gets a separate prompt ({{Appendix D.1|ax:A4.SS1}}): read every earlier proposal and its measured score in full ("trust the measured result over what the proposal claims"), find why failures failed, avoid "another small tweak" on a saturated direction, then write a proposal and the program. That prompt is the same for Dream-RSI and the fixed baseline.

Why the coding agent's prompt matters for replay

Because each new attempt reads every sibling attempt and the history, what the agent produced at a node depended on what else had been tried by then. Replay assumes a node's recorded outcome would have been the same under a policy that visited the tree in another order or skipped some siblings. The paper does not discuss this; it is the usual gap between a log and a simulator.

Problem: exploration is the expensive part to improve

[[S1|§1 Introduction]]

An agentic discovery loop proposes a candidate (a program, a construction, a kernel), evaluates it, learns from the result and proposes again. AlphaEvolve and its successors run such loops over algorithm design, open mathematical problems and systems code, and the hardest targets now take "thousands of proposal–evaluation cycles" ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Every cycle costs a model call plus an evaluation: compiling and benchmarking a GPU kernel, or running an optimisation to convergence.

What decides whether those cycles are well spent is the exploration policy: which directions to pursue, which candidates to refine, how many branches to run in parallel, and when to stop. In most systems it is written by hand and stays fixed for the whole run, so it "may repeatedly allocate computation to ineffective search directions" ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Improving it online is costly for two reasons the paper names:

  • Feedback is delayed and expensive at the meta level. Judging a policy means watching it steer a whole discovery run over many cycles, not scoring one candidate.
  • The meta-policy space is vast. Most policies you might try are bad, and each costs a long online rollout to find that out.

EvoX ({{Liu et al. 2026a|https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23413}}) optimises the search strategy online and pays exactly this cost. Dream-RSI asks whether the policy can be improved without paying it.

Idea: the history you already paid for is a simulator

[[S2|§2 Motivation]]

A finished discovery run is not just text to reread. It is a tree: every attempt, the attempt it continued from, and the outcome it actually produced when executed and scored. An exploration policy does one thing: given what it has seen, choose which attempts to continue next. So a different policy can be "run" on the recorded tree by walking it differently (other branches, another order, other parallel groupings, earlier stopping) and simply reading the outcomes already on disk. No agent call, no evaluation. In the authors' words, "accumulated discovery history can serve as a replay simulator over the realized search space" ({{abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14858}}).

The analogy is model-based reinforcement learning ({{§2|ax:S2}}): Sutton's Dyna (1990) and the Dreamer family learn a model of the environment and improve a policy on imagined experience. Here the "model" is not learned at all; it is the record itself, exact where the record goes and silent everywhere else. The paper calls evaluating policies in it dreaming and the recorded trees worlds (footnote 1, {{§1|ax:S1}}).

policy πt steers the coding agent; a new tree 𝒯t is recorded→2 Build the simulator
history ℋt = ℋt−1 ∪ {𝒯t}→3 Dream
a development agent writes M policy versions; each is replayed on every recorded tree→4 Redeploy
πt+1 = the best version; back to 1

The loop of {{Figure 1|ax:S1.F1}}. Only the policy's code changes; the coding agent (Gemini CLI), the evaluator and the models stay fixed, and no weights are trained ({{§3|ax:S3}}).

The cost argument is simple. One expensive online run supports many cheap offline evaluations of other policies ({{Figure 2|ax:S1.F2}}), so the meta-level search that EvoX pays for in rollouts is paid for here in reads. The catch is equally simple: a policy can only be judged where history went. The {{Dream over a recorded tree|tab:t-run}} tab runs this on illustrative worlds so you can see both sides.

Method: trees, replay and one score

[[S3|§3 Dream-RSI]]

The tree and the decision interface

A discovery tree has a root r (the starting workspace). Every other node has one parent, where its attempt began: the agent resumes the parent's saved workspace and its accumulated observations, makes a new attempt, and the node stores the resulting files, the artifact, the evaluator's diagnostics and a score sv, larger is better ({{§3, Discovery trees|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px1}}). At each decision the policy may continue from the root (open a new branch) or from any current leaf: the eligible set is A(𝒯) = {r} ∪ {leaves}. With W parallel workers it picks a batch C ⊆ A(𝒯) of at most W nodes, so one choice fixes both where to continue and how many attempts run at once.

Online rollout

At outer iteration t, policy πt grows a fresh tree from the root for at most K1 rounds, with the earlier history available as context. Each selected node gets one new child per round; the step is stochastic because the agent may produce something different from the same workspace. The rollout ends on an empty batch or after K1 rounds, and the finished tree joins the history ({{§3, Online rollout|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px2}}).

Offline replay

With the history fixed, each policy version is replayed on every recorded tree separately ({{§3, Offline evaluation|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px3}}). Replay starts from the bare root and reveals the tree only as the policy asks:

  • selecting a revealed leaf reveals its one recorded child, if it has one;
  • selecting the root opens the earliest-created unopened branch;
  • nothing beyond the recorded tree is ever generated; replay stops on an empty batch, after K2 rounds, or when everything is revealed.

So replay can test how far to pursue each branch, how to group attempts into batches, and when to open another branch or stop. It cannot test a different attempt: every branch is traversed in its recorded order.

The replay score

[[S3.E1|Eq. 1]]
V = maxv revealed sv − β1 N + β2 · N / max(1, k*)
max sv
discovery quality: the best score among revealed nodes
N
revealed non-root nodes: the generation–evaluation requests this trajectory would have cost
k*
decision rounds used; N / k* is the average batch size
β1, β2
fixed coefficients: a charge per attempt and a bonus for batching. Their values are never given.

A policy version's score is its average V over all t recorded trees. The development agent (a fixed LLM) reads the replay trajectories and scores of the current version, revises the policy's code, and repeats M times; the next online policy is the best-scoring of the M versions. Because the current policy is one of them, the chosen one is "no worse than the current policy" on the history ({{§3, Policy improvement|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px5}}). That guarantee is in-sample: it says nothing about the next online run, which is the point the toy in the {{second tab|tab:t-run}} measures.

What the method section leaves out

No value is given for β1, β2, M, K1, K2 or the number of replay workers, so the procedure cannot be rerun as written. And the objective in the authors' own policy-development prompt ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}) is not Eq. 1: it ranks a policy by "pareto.reward = pareto.auc − lambda × parallel_penalty", swept over a "beta" knob, where the penalty is effective sequential rounds over total probes. The prompt also lets one batch open several roots, while §3's batch is a set containing the root at most once (so §3 would open one new branch per round). Which objective produced the reported results is not said.

What the policy and the agent actually see

[[A2|Appendix B]]

The exploration policy is a Python class with a solve() method that the development agent rewrites. Its prompt ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}) describes the world as "a frozen, irregular branch×attempt grid" and sets hard rules:

  • Prefix only. Decisions may use revealed observations, structural metadata and helper signals, never unrevealed scores, a known optimum, hard-coded cell ids or absolute score targets.
  • Read each branch as a trajectory (its best successful result, trend, regressions, failure and repair sequence) and classify failures as hard-unrecoverable, repairable implementation failure, weak but underexplored, or repeatedly unpromising. Correctness mismatches, shared-memory limits and shape errors are "normally repairable".
  • Batch as a portfolio of up to W independent cells: strong refinements, new roots or underexplored branches, and at most one recovery of a repairable failure; never random, never a fixed widen-all or deepen-all schedule, never a parent and its child together.
  • One knob, beta, fixed within an episode: high beta means wider, more patient, weaker pruning. The default is moved by about 0.1 to 0.2 between live cycles from evidence (raise it on a plateau if the sweep shows higher beta reaches more; lower it if high beta only adds work).
  • Plan the next grid with plan_grid(): how many branches and refinements the next live run gets, chosen from earlier live manifests. A plan beyond the recorded tree's width or depth "is out of support and cannot earn replay reward".

The coding agent itself gets a separate prompt ({{Appendix B.1|ax:A2.SS1}}): read every earlier proposal and its measured score in full ("trust the measured result over what the proposal claims"), find why failures failed, avoid "another small tweak" on a saturated direction, then write a proposal and the program. That prompt is the same for Dream-RSI and the fixed baseline.

Why the coding agent's prompt matters for replay

Because each new attempt reads every sibling attempt and the history, what the agent produced at a node depended on what else had been tried by then. Replay assumes a node's recorded outcome would have been the same under a policy that visited the tree in another order or skipped some siblings. The paper does not discuss this; it is the usual gap between a log and a simulator.

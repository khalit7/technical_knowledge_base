Dream over a recorded tree

This tab runs the paper's replay simulator, exactly as {{§3|ax:S3.SS0.SSS0.Px3}} defines it, on illustrative worlds. No traces have been released, so each world here is a generated branch × attempt grid of the shape the authors' own prompt describes ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}): 10 branches (the parallel workspaces) × 11 attempts, the Gemini-3.1-Pro round of Section 4. Scores are in invented units from 0 to 1; about a third of directions are good, some improve only after several refinements, and about one attempt in six fails. Nothing quantitative here comes from the paper; what comes from the paper is the procedure: replay rules, Eq. 1 and the selection rule.

Try: step the fixed policy and the dreamed one through the same recorded world, then dream over more history, raise the batching bonus β2 until the fixed policy wins, and deploy the winner on fresh worlds where gains arrive later.

Replay many policies over a recorded tree and keep the one with the best Eq. 1 score. Compared with the policy that recorded the tree, what can the winner do?

Only the second. Replay reveals recorded nodes and nothing else, so the best a replayed policy can reach is the best node already in the tree, which the policy that recorded the whole tree found by definition. What dreaming can buy is the same (or nearly the same) best for fewer attempts and fewer rounds. Any better result has to come from the next online round. Watch it below: the "share of recorded best" counter never passes 100%.

2. Dream: score every candidate on the history

The paper's development agent writes M revisions of the policy's code. Here the candidates are 504 settings of a small policy family instead: how many branches to keep open at once, how many attempts without gain before closing a branch, whether to drop branches well below the best seen, and whether to open a fresh branch when one closes. Each is replayed on every recorded world; the winner is the highest average of Eq. 1, and the fixed policy is in the pool, so the winner can never score below it on the history.

3. Deploy the winner online

Now the winner meets worlds it has never seen: 300 fresh grids from the same generator, or from one where useful gains arrive later in each branch. On each, it and the fixed policy run as they would online (in the toy an outcome does not depend on the order of visits; in the real system it does, since each attempt reads its siblings).

What the toy shows, and what it cannot

  • Dreaming optimises cost against a fixed ceiling. In replay no policy can beat the best recorded node, so once the charge per attempt β1 outweighs the batching bonus β2 the winner is a policy that finds nearly that node with far fewer probes (29 to 43 of 110 on the default history). That matches the shape of the paper's gains (fewer calls, similar quality), and it says the quality gains the paper reports must come from the online rounds, not from replay.
  • The guarantee is in-sample. On the history the winner always scores at least as well as the fixed policy; on fresh worlds it gives up some of the best result for its savings, and more when the new worlds reward depth the history did not show.
  • Eq. 1's coefficients decide the answer. Raise β2 and the fixed policy, which batches all 10 workers every round, wins outright. The paper never reports β1 or β2.
  • Not modelled: outcomes that depend on what siblings were tried, the development agent writing code rather than choosing from a grid, plan_grid changing the next grid's shape, and real score distributions. The generator is invented; only the rules are the paper's.

Problem

§1 Introduction

A training step dies when any one component's peak memory exceeds the device, so a plan that lowers three peaks and leaves the fourth free to grow "buys nothing at the point where the workload grows" (§1).

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) models are now trained at long context or large batch, and when adding GPUs is not an option, memory is bought with time. Every standard trade charges it: gradient checkpointing recomputes, state sharding (ZeRO, FSDP) communicates, and optimizer offload serialises the update on the host CPU (§1). Peak memory is a maximum over live sets, the groups of tensors that must be resident at the same moment, and the paper picks four that the parallelism plans in common use leave unbounded:

  1. Expert dispatch, which grows with the routing matrix the router draws: how many token copies land on one GPU.
  2. The vocabulary projection, which grows with tokens times vocabulary: the logits.
  3. Gradient-checkpoint boundaries, which grow with depth times sequence length.
  4. Optimizer state (AdamW), which grows with parameter count.

They grow at different rates, so which runs out first depends on the configuration: logits at large vocabulary and long context, dispatch at high routing imbalance, boundaries at depth, optimizer state at large parameter count on few devices (§1). Lowering the largest only exposes the next, so the paper bounds all four, each switchable on its own.

The constraint it sets itself: change only the order and granularity of computation and data movement. No low-rank adapters, no quantised state, no approximate routing or attention, so the model, parameterisation, optimizer, precision and loss stay those of standard full-parameter BF16 training, and the memory and throughput numbers are comparable with it (§1).

Expert dispatch: PipelinedLLEP

§3.1, Figures 1 and 2, Appendix B

Under expert parallelism a token routed to an expert travels to the GPU (rank) that owns it, its destination d; the routes Rd arriving there size its dispatch buffers and grow with batch size, top-k and routing imbalance (§3.1). The authors' earlier LLEP (Least-Loaded Expert Parallelism, arXiv 2601.17111) removes the third: it moves a popular expert's overflow, and that expert's weights, onto ranks that would otherwise sit idle, without changing any token's expert choice. But LLEP still materialises the rank's whole routed batch in one all-to-all tensor.

PipelinedLLEP cuts the batch into chunks with a per-source token budget c: each sending rank puts at most c of its own tokens into any one chunk (Eq. 1):

K = min(⌈N/c⌉, Kmax),   ceff = ⌈N/K⌉,   Rd(i) ≤ Ep k ceff

Each of the Ep senders puts at most ceff tokens in chunk i, each token makes k routes, so no destination can receive more than Epkceff routes in a chunk, whatever the router does, and no token is dropped to make it hold. The buffers then take at most 2EpkcHb bytes; past N = cKmax the chunk count stops at Kmax and each buffer is capped at 1/Kmax of the batch. Either way the limit is known before launch, and deriving K from c absorbs micro-batches of varying length without running out of memory.

Why overlap alone is not enough. The chunks run through a dispatch, expert-matmul, combine pipeline, as in FasterMoE or Tutel, which pick the chunk count for speed; this paper picks it to cap memory (§3.1). And a loop that calls the expert layer per chunk keeps every chunk's autograd graph alive until backward, so the peak would not fall in training. So each chunk's expert matmul is wrapped in a reentrant checkpoint nested inside the decoder layer's non-reentrant one, which lets the outer checkpoint drop the inner tensors (B.1). The price: the expert matmul runs three times per update instead of twice; route payload is unchanged.

Exactness. Only the expert-weight gradient changes summation order (it is the one sum split across chunks); at K = 1 it is bitwise identical to LLEP's (Eq. 7).

LLEP decides which rank serves a route from the token's position in the expert's global token list. Cut a rank's 65,536 tokens into 10 chunks of consecutive positions, or into 10 strided chunks (positions i, i+10, i+20, ...). Same tokens, same destinations. What changes?

The spread within a chunk. Consecutive positions share a destination under LLEP's plan, so a chunk that covers the relocated tail of a popular expert sends almost everything to one helper rank, and the imbalance LLEP removed comes back chunk by chunk (Figure 1c). Strided chunks spread each chunk over the destinations. The paper measures the largest send to one destination over the mean send in a chunk (Table 8); the bars below put its numbers beside our replay, which runs the paper's own synthetic routing profiles (B.2) through LLEP's released assignment plan in your browser:

Peak falls with the chunk count at a decaying rate and latency does not follow it, so c is picked from a measured curve (Figure 8); deriving K from the budget matters for short micro-batches, where a fixed K = 10 runs at under half LLEP's speed (Table 7). Vocabulary projection: Ring-DTP

§3.2, Figure 3, Appendix C
To compute one token's exact cross-entropy over a 200,000-word vocabulary, visiting the vocabulary one block at a time, how many numbers per token must you carry between blocks?

Three: the running maximum m, the running sum of exponentials z, and the target's logit yt once its block passes. Each new block rescales the old sum to the new maximum (Eq. 3), and at the end the loss is m + log z − yt. This is the online softmax that FlashAttention also uses. One token, 24 random logits, folded block by block:

Fused cross-entropy kernels (Cut Cross-Entropy, Liger) already do this on one device; here both the batch and the projection weight are sharded across ranks (§3.2). Rank r holds its own batch Xr (N × H) and owns the vocabulary slice Wr of V/P columns. Megatron's vocabulary-parallel loss uses the same weight split but needs every rank to hold the same batch, which shrinks the effective batch and hurts long context. Ring-DTP (ring data-tensor-parallel) keeps a data-parallel layout, distinct batches per rank, and runs P ring rounds in which every batch meets every shard exactly once. Each meeting forms only the strip Yj,r = XjWr (N × V/P), folds it into the per-token state Sj = (mj, zj, ytj), and frees it, so logit memory is O(NVb/P) (Eq. 2). Exactly one shard contains each target, so exactly one meeting supplies yt. Backward replays the same rounds from the saved normaliser and forms the softmax residual one strip at a time, so neither pass stores a full logit or logit-gradient tensor (Appendix C).

Which tensor travels. Either the activations or the weight shard can move. A hop costs O(NH) bytes if activations move and O(HV/P) if weights move, so Ring-DTP moves weights exactly when N > V/P. Moving weights keeps each batch and its state at home, so forward needs no return hop (Figure 9). The cost is P − 1 sequential hops per pass, so the paper takes the smallest P whose strip fits.

Results (Table 2, P = 8, H = 7,168, V = 200,000, FP32, forward plus backward, one measured pass each): at N = 16,384 per GPU (activations move) the peak falls from 42.462 to 7.322 GiB, 82.8%, for 5.1% more time; at N = 32,768 (weights move) from 79.521 to 10.622 GiB, 86.6%, for 4.4%. Doubling N nearly doubles the standard peak but moves Ring-DTP's only from 7.3 to 10.6 GiB, so the saving widens with context, which is what keeps the vocabulary head on the device at 1M tokens. The standard reference's extra memory is exactly three N × V FP32 tensors (logits, log-softmax and their gradient: 3 × 12.207 = 36.621 GiB against the measured 36.6212, Table 10), and the shards are exactly 1/4 and 1/8 of the 5.3406 GiB weight; see the checks.

Checkpoint boundaries: SCO

§3.3, Figure 4, Appendix D

Gradient checkpointing throws a layer's internals away after forward and recomputes them in backward, but one tensor per checkpointed layer survives: its input hℓ (Nℓ × H), held from forward until that layer is recomputed. That is the checkpoint boundary, and with many layers and long sequences the boundaries add up (§3.3). SCO (selective checkpoint offload) walks the layers in forward order and copies each boundary that still fits a pinned host-memory budget to the CPU, asynchronously, then frees the device copy; layers that do not fit keep theirs. In backward, while layer ℓ recomputes from hℓ, the next boundary hℓ−1 comes back on a separate stream, so at most two restored boundaries are on the device at once (Eq. 4):

MHBMSCO ≤ 2NmaxHb,    Mhost ≤ |S| NmaxHb

The host cap is a correctness condition: a rank failing an allocation while the others enter a collective would deadlock the job, so the cap is set at or above |S|NmaxHb and is per micro-batch (Appendix D).

On gpt-oss-20b (8 H200s, 47 boundaries), an 8 GiB host budget takes 21 boundaries, about 7.8 GiB per GPU, off the device. How much does the measured peak HBM fall?

6.2 GiB, from 139.790 to 133.546 GiB (Table 3). Each step of the budget lowers the peak by somewhat less than the payload it offloads, because the peak includes the restored boundaries and everything else live at that moment. The boundary size itself recomputes from the configuration alone: (557,056 tokens / 8 ranks) × 2,880 hidden × 2 bytes = 0.3735 GiB, so 8 GiB holds 21 and 16 GiB holds 42, exactly the paper's counts.

Bars: Table 3's peak HBM; the darker part is the boundary payload still on the device (47 minus the offloaded count, times 0.3735 GiB). Throughput moves by 1.9% with no trend; node RAM rises by about what is pinned (190.9 GiB against 8 × 23.5). Step through it in Run the four schedules.

Results (Table 3, Tables 12 and 13; 24 layers exposing 47 boundaries, sequence and expert parallelism of degree 8, 556,432 tokens per step at a ceiling of 557,056, ten steps with two warm-up): offloading everything lowers the peak from 139.790 to 123.728 GiB, and the largest batch that completes all ten steps rises from 557,056 to 655,360 tokens, 17.65%. Two numbers in the text do not match the tables; see How much to believe.

Optimizer: OffloadStreamAdamW

§3.4, Figure 5, Appendix E

Offloading AdamW's fp32 master weights and moments to CPU memory (ZeRO-Offload, ZeRO-Infinity) frees 12 bytes per parameter on the GPU, but the CPU update is slow, and while it runs the GPU is idle and, having released its activations, nearly empty (§3.4). OffloadStreamAdamW uses that idle GPU as the update engine: each rank's state is cut into buckets of at most β parameters that rotate through s staging slots and three streams: copy master weights, moments and final gradients to the GPU; run a fused AdamW update and refresh the BF16 working weights; write the updated fp32 state back (Eq. 5):

Tstream = G · max(TH2D, Tupdate, TD2H) + O(TH2D + Tupdate + TD2H),   Mstage = O(sβ)

For G buckets the step costs about one bucket's slowest stage per bucket, plus one pipeline fill. The floor for any offloaded optimizer is the host-link round trip of 12Θ/W bytes (116 GiB per rank at Θ = 1012, W = 96, Figure 5), so the design hides the update and the write-back behind that transfer.

Results (Table 4, gpt-oss-20b on 8 H200s, the same fp32 state in host memory on both sides): the step takes 1.93 s against 3.95 s for ZeRO-Offload's AVX CPU Adam, 2.05 times faster, with 4.234 GiB of staging per GPU. A 100M-parameter bucket with two slots wins; a third slot is within 1.1% and adds a bucket's worth of staging, which the paper reads as the pipeline being transfer-bound (Table 14). The staging column recomputes exactly as slots × largest bucket × 16 bytes (fp32 master, two moments, gradient) in every row. Exactness has one condition: a bucket can only start once its gradient is final and any cross-bucket transform is known, so global gradient clipping must wait for the global norm; per-bucket or predictive clipping would change the update rule and sits outside the exactness claim (Appendix E).

Composition and the per-rank budget

§3.5, Eq. 6

The four operators bound disjoint live sets (dispatch temporaries inside MoE layers, logit strips at the head, boundaries spanning layers, optimizer state at update time), none needs another, and each can be enabled alone (§3.5). For the full system they sit inside the authors' Mixture-of-Parallelisms (MoP) layout (arXiv 2607.01844): ZeRO-3 for dense weights, a sequence-to-head all-to-all for attention (Ulysses-style), and expert-parallel placement for experts. The same W ranks form overlapping sub-groups of sizes D (sequence), Ep (experts) and P (vocabulary), chosen independently rather than as factors of W = d · t · p. The per-rank budget is then (Eq. 6):

Mpeak = 4Θ/W + O(sβ) + O(NHb) + O(EpqHb) + O(NVb/P) + O(NHb)

Weights and gradients, optimizer staging, attention, dispatch, logit strips, boundaries. Every term is fixed by the model and launch configuration once N ≤ Nmax, so feasibility can be checked before launch. Two savings meet in the first terms: host offload takes persistent state from 16Θ/W to 4Θ/W (ZeRO-Offload's contribution), and OffloadStreamAdamW reduces the time that offload costs.

Results end to end

§4, Figures 6 and 7, Appendix A

All four together inside MoP train three MoE models of 120B, 241B and 667B parameters on 16, 32 and 64 H200s, from scratch (§4, Appendix A). The models share one architecture and differ only in depth (7, 14 and 39 layers): hidden width 7,168, dense FFN width 18,432, 384 routed experts of width 2,048, top-8, vocabulary 200,000, the widths of Kimi K2 (which has 61 layers). Routing is dropless, balanced by DeepSeek-V3's auxiliary-loss-free bias, with Ep = W, P = 8 and D from 2 to 16 by context length. The baseline, FSDP2-best, is the highest-throughput fit from a sweep of FSDP2 with tensor, context and expert parallelism and optimizer CPU offload on a TorchTitan-based stack, at the same model and GPU count.

FSDP2-best runs out of memory past 128K, 32K and 64K tokens at the three scales; the stack trains at 1M at all three, 8, 32 and 16 times the reach (Figure 7). Where both run, it is 7.6 times faster at 128K on 120B and 10.4 times at 64K on 667B (Figure 6). Its TFLOP/s rises with context as attention work per token grows, from 91 to 110 at 128K to 213 to 233 at 1M (9 to 11% and 21.5 to 23.6% of the H200's 989.5 dense BF16 TFLOP/s, derived), while FSDP2-best stays below 40. The largest global batch is 1.5M, 1.8M and 3M tokens, 12, 7 and 3 times the baseline's. For quality, fine-tuning gpt-oss-20b on Nemotron-Math at 128K gives 59.8% Avg@8 on AIME 2025 on the stack against 59.6% on FSDP2 with the same recipe (45.2% before fine-tuning, Appendix A).

Related work and limits

§5 Related Work, §7 Limitations

Related work (§5). FasterMoE, Lina, PipeMoE and ScheMoE also chunk dispatch, but size the chunks for bandwidth, so buffers still follow the realised load; placement methods (FlexMoE, SmartMoE, LLEP) move skew around and compose with the budget. Cut Cross-Entropy and Liger assume one device, Megatron's vocabulary-parallel loss a replicated batch, Ring Attention a ring where only one operand can move. DeepSpeed and PyTorch offload whole checkpoint regions where SCO offloads a budgeted subset; 8-bit optimizers and Adam-mini shrink optimizer state by changing numerics, which this paper excludes.

Limits the authors state (§7): all-to-all and ring traffic assume a fast interconnect, and the cheap extra chunks measured on intra-node NVLink may not be cheap on a slower fabric; a smaller budget tightens the bound but raises the chunk count by the same factor, and forward time is flat only over part of that range, so c comes from a measured curve; checkpoint and optimizer streaming spend host memory and link bandwidth, which binds on nodes with less than the 2 TB used here; topology sensitivity and automatic choice of (D, Ep, P, c, β) remain open.

How much of this to believe

Appendix A, Tables 2 to 14

What holds up. The operators only reorder work, so they are exact by construction up to floating-point summation order. And the component memory numbers are unusually checkable, because they follow from the configuration (all checks):

  • SCO's boundary counts (21 and 42 of 47) and payloads come out of 557,056 / 8 × 2,880 × 2 bytes alone.
  • All eight staging sizes of Table 14 are slots × largest bucket × 16 bytes.
  • The projection baseline is exactly three N × V FP32 tensors, and the shards exactly 1/P of the weight (Table 10).
  • Our replay of the paper's routing profiles through LLEP's released plan reproduces all eight send ratios of Table 8.
  • Dispatch peaks repeat to the milli-GiB over three runs, and every component comparison runs on identical input in the same execution (Appendix A). The authors state their limits plainly, which counts in their favour.

What is weaker:

  • The vocabulary baseline is the naive one. Ring-DTP's 82.8% and 86.6% are against a projection that materialises full logits (Table 2), not against Cut Cross-Entropy or Liger, which already avoid them on one device (CCE: Gemma 2 2B's loss memory from 24 GB to 1 MB, its abstract), nor Megatron's vocabulary-parallel loss. Ring-DTP's own addition, sharding the weight while each rank keeps its batch, is argued rather than measured against them. The benchmark is FP32; training is BF16.
  • Dispatch is measured against the authors' own LLEP on synthetic routing (B.2), with the 65K latencies the fastest of three repeats. "Without losing throughput" fails at the 32K shape with K = 10 (0.87 to 0.90 times LLEP, Table 9). The guarantee 2EpkcHb grows with Ep: at the end-to-end scale (Ep = 64, c = 4,096) it is 56 GiB, so the saving comes from strided chunks realising the even share, not from the bound. Our replay shows contiguous chunks reaching 42% of the bound on the 30/16 profile, which Table 8 leaves out.
  • SCO's text and tables disagree. §3.3 claims "17.65% of HBM consumption" saved; the peak falls 11.5% (139.790 to 123.728 GiB), and 17.65% is Table 13's batch gain. Its "35.71%" for "the largest batch that runs without an out-of-memory error" compares runs without allocator warnings. The baseline was run above its largest clean batch, which probably explains why throughput rises slightly with offload.
  • The end-to-end numbers are mostly MoP's, and thin. The MoP paper already reports 4.7 to 8.2 times FSDP2's throughput and 1M-token context; no ablation removes one operator from the stack. One run per point, the stack run only at 128K and 1M, so the 667B "10.4 times at 64K" divides its 128K throughput by the baseline's 64K, and 241B shares no length at all. The baseline is weak: under 40 TFLOP/s, about 4% of the H200's dense BF16 peak.
  • Two printed multiples contradict the text. Figure 6's "1.8x" (120B, 91 TFLOP/s) and "2.2x" (667B, 107) imply baselines of 50.6 and 48.6 TFLOP/s, above §4's "below 40 anywhere in its sweep".
  • The quality check is one fine-tuning run each, on gpt-oss-20b, not the 120B to 667B models (those report throughput only). On AIME 2025's 30 problems × 8 samples, one sample is 0.42 points, so 59.8% against 59.6% is under half an answer; and 59.8% and 45.2% are not whole numbers of 240, so Avg@8 was counted some way the paper does not state.

Verdict. Believe the four mechanisms and the component memory numbers: exact by construction, and where the configuration fixes them they recompute to the printed digit. Read the speeds as single, favourable runs against the authors' own LLEP and a naive projection. Treat "1M context, 8 to 32 times the reach, up to 10.4 times the throughput" as a result for the whole MoP stack against a weak baseline, with no measure of which operator buys what.

What it takes to use this

  • Code: none released for PipelinedLLEP, Ring-DTP, SCO, OffloadStreamAdamW or MoP (paper and GitHub checked 3 October 2026). LLEP, the base of the dispatch operator, is public (SalesforceAIResearch/LeastLoadedEP, Apache-2.0).
  • Hardware: measured only on 8-GPU H200 nodes with NVLink inside the node and 2 TB of host memory; SCO and the optimizer offload consume host memory (node RAM rose 191 GiB with full SCO on gpt-oss-20b) and host-link bandwidth (§7).
  • What was tested: components on gpt-oss-20b and synthetic MoE shapes; the composed stack on 120B to 667B pre-training throughput at 16 to 64 GPUs; fine-tuning quality on gpt-oss-20b only.
  • When it pays: long context or large vocabularies (logits and boundaries grow with tokens), skewed routing (dispatch), and large models on few GPUs (optimizer offload made fast). Each operator can be enabled alone, so a run pays only for its own peaks.
  • When it buys little: balanced routing, where standard expert parallelism is fastest (Table 6); small micro-batches, where below about 8,192 tokens per rank the dispatch buffers are small next to the expert weights (9 to 17% saving, Table 7); a single device, where a fused cross-entropy kernel already removes the logits; slow interconnects, untested.
  • Pieces you can use today: fused cross-entropy (Liger, Cut Cross-Entropy) for the logits on one device, PyTorch and DeepSpeed CPU checkpoint offload (whole regions rather than a budget), and ZeRO-Offload for the optimizer.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

The useful idea is the framing more than any single operator: peak memory is a maximum over live sets, each live set has its own growth law, and a bounded schedule turns each into a term fixed at launch, so a configuration's feasibility can be checked before it runs (Eq. 6). That matters because long-context training is now a normal stage: DeepSeek-V3, for example, added two training phases to grow its context from 4K to 32K and then 128K, cutting the batch from 1,920 to 480 sequences for the second (DeepSeek-V3 §4.3), the batch-for-context trade this paper's Figure 7 is about. The vocabulary term keeps growing too: the paper's models use 200,000 tokens, and gpt-oss-20b's configuration has 201,088 (config).

4. Optimizer step: CPU Adam against OffloadStreamAdamW

One optimizer step on one GPU's share of gpt-oss-20b (about 2.6B parameters, 26 buckets of up to 142M, Table 14), on one time axis. CPU Adam updates everything on the host in one serial pass (3.95 s measured). The stream copies each bucket's fp32 master weights, moments and gradient to the GPU (16 bytes per parameter), updates it there, and writes 12 bytes per parameter back, with s staging slots limiting how many buckets are in flight.

Try: one slot, then two: with one slot each bucket waits for the previous write-back and the step is nearly serial; two slots overlap upload, update and write-back and approach the transfer floor; a third buys almost nothing, as Table 14 measured (1.930 against 1.951 s).

Illustrative timing. Bucket times are not printed. Upload and write-back times are set in proportion to bytes (16 : 12), the update to a quarter of the upload, and scaled so that two slots take the measured 1.93 s under Eq. 5; one and three slots then follow from the same times. Choosing the token budget, and two latency modes

Peak falls monotonically with the chunk count but with decaying return: at the 65K shape the first ten chunks remove 38.4 GiB and the next ten 2.4 GiB. Latency does not follow: at 65K, K of 2, 3 and 5 are slower and larger than K = 10 while 10 to 20 is flat within 6%; at 32K latency rises past K = 3. A single chunk holds 8.3 GiB more than LLEP, since it adds a copy of the route-shaped tensors and partitions nothing (Figure 8). A fixed K = 10 on a 128-token micro-batch pays ten collective rounds for about one token each (0.45 to 0.48 times LLEP's speed), where the budget derives K = 1 and runs 1.60 to 2.96 times faster than LLEP; the saving tracks the live set, from 9.0% at 128 tokens to 65.9% at 131,072 (Table 7).

Forward latency at the 65K shape is bimodal: in the fast mode every profile repeats within 0.5%, the slow mode adds 12 to 56%, and which mode a run enters depends on the chunk count and the host-side launch pattern, not the routing (K = 10 was slow in 2 of 56 measurements, K = 16 in 21 of 84). Hence Table 6 reports the fastest of three repeats, and Kmax = 10 keeps the schedule where the slow mode is rare (B.5). Under the hood: the inner checkpoint must be reentrant because a non-reentrant one would keep the stacked expert weights alive through Python references for O(KL) cross-layer time; every chunk accumulates into one expert-weight gradient, so the weight-transfer backward fires once per layer; and the collective launches grow linearly with K (B.1).

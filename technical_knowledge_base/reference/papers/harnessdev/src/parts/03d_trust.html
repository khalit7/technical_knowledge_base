How much of this to believe

[[S6.SS1|§6.1 Limitations]]

What holds up.

  • The protocol is careful. Builder and runner are separated, harnesses are frozen before evaluation, the score path ignores what a harness says about itself, and every run was audited ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}). Creation cells are avg@3 over independently built harnesses.
  • The numbers agree with each other. All 83 cross-checks this page found match (Tables 2 to 7, appendix trajectories, text), including the 34 of 64 and the 2 of 9 recounted from the vector Figures 7 and 8, where every decoded point lands on a whole number of tasks ({{checks|tab:t-tables:chkCard}}).
  • Noise cannot manufacture the Evolution gap. Selection inflates the visible gains (each creator picked near the best of up to eleven noisy scores), so the gap between visible and held-out gains is if anything real.
  • The authors state most limitations themselves ({{§6.1|ax:S6.SS1}}): uneven, non-optimal human baselines; Unified-Eval only reduces executor differences; one trajectory per Evolution cell, so no uncertainty estimates; held-out evaluation on SWE-Pro only. That counts in their favour, as does their own model, Seed 2.0 Pro, coming last.

What is weaker than the summary suggests.

  • The domain split is confounded with the executor. The references generated harnesses lose to ran Claude Fable 5 (SWE-Pro) and GPT-5.6 Sol (Terminal-Bench, BrowseComp), newer than every creator; the two they match or beat ran Opus 4.8 (EQ-Bench3, the best creator's own model) and Gemini 3.1 (MLE-bench) ({{Table 9|ax:A2.T9}}). Three of the five reference numbers come from OpenAI's GPT-5.6 release report and were not re-run ({{Appendix B.2|ax:A2.SS2}}). The paper warns the references are not paired controls; its summary sentences read as if they were.
  • "Match" and "beat" describe the best one or two creators (Result 1). Leaving MLE-bench out of the Avg. column also widens the headline gap: with it, Opus averages 60.8 against 73.7, a gap of 12.9 rather than 18.4 (this page's recomputation).
  • Every Evolution lineage is a single run. One commit varies by about ±4.75 pair points, and three of nine visible gains (Opus self +3.0, Qwen fixed +1.1, GPT-5.5 fixed +2.4) sit inside it. A 630-task score near 50% has a binomial standard error of about 2.0 points, so a difference of two independent runs about 2.8 (this page's estimate; shared tasks make it smaller by an unreported amount). Each self-runtime held-out gain is 0.5 to 1.6 of those units; that all five are positive is stronger (one in 32 if each sign were a coin flip). The fixed-Gemini GPT-5.5 drop of 10.32, 3.7 units, is clearly real.
  • "Held-out" is the same distribution: the 630 tasks come from the same SWE-Pro split as the 100 feedback tasks, and Terminal-Bench has no held-out set. The result is about overfitting within one code benchmark, not transfer to new kinds of task.
  • The fixed executor is also a contestant: under Unified-Eval Gemini runs its own co-adapted harness and the others' foreign ones.
  • Two examples are not tied to a lineage in the text. The "99 of 100 report success, 48 pass" case is the fixed-Gemini Opus lineage (its H0 is 48 of 100; the self-runtime Opus started at 68, {{Appendix I|ax:A9}}), and the GPT-5.5 candidate at 0.584 is the fixed-Gemini lineage's version 8 (52 of 89); both identifications are this page's, from the decoded figures.

Verdict. Believe the instrument and the Evolution warning: a creator's visible score is a poor guide to held-out quality (34 of 64 switches agree, 2 of 9 declarations are held-out optimal), and changing the executor can undo a harness. Believe that current models can build working harnesses from a bare seed. Treat the domain ranking (writing and ML level, code and search far behind) as unproven until the references are re-run with the creators' own models, and treat each individual Evolution gain as one noisy run.

What it takes to use this

[[A2.SS1|Appendices B and E]]
  • Code and data. Nothing is released yet. The seed implementation, the audit script and the held-out splits "will be released with the benchmark" ({{Appendix C|ax:A3}}); the {{project page|https://self-developing-agents.github.io/}} has results, no code. The prompts in {{Appendix E|ax:A5}} are complete enough to reuse as a creation or evolution brief today.
  • Compute. Per MLE-bench task: one A800 80 GB GPU, 14 vCPUs, 227 GiB of RAM, 36,000 s (34,200 for the harness, 1,800 for the grader) and 500 steps; per code task: 500 steps and 7,200 s ({{Appendix B.1|ax:A2.SS1}}). A lineage spends up to eleven pairs of 189 tasks at one to two hours each; the held-out scoring alone was 73 versions × 630 tasks = 45,990 task runs (derived).
  • What worked for the creators, and transfers to anyone building a harness with a model:
    • Gate "finish" on evidence. Opus's completion gate and GPT-5.5's final-review gate (+8 SWE tasks and +8 Terminal tasks in one switch, {{Appendix H|ax:A8}}) are the clearest wins.
    • Do not hard-code step, output or time limits fitted to one model, and test the harness with a second executor before trusting it.
    • Choose the final version on a set the edits never saw, and estimate run-to-run noise by re-running the best candidate before believing a gain under about five points.
    • Treat five-task probes as crash checks: one task moves a probe leg by 20 points ({{Appendix E.2|ax:A5.SS2}}).
    • Track files that shell commands create, not only those written through the edit tool (GPT-5.5's T5, Opus's T3).
    • Keep a ledger file of every evaluation; both appendix creators did, and Opus used it to stop at 3 of 10 pairs.
  • When it pays. On this paper's evidence, model-built harnesses are worth trying where general agent structure is most of the harness (writing, ML experiment loops), and a mature human harness is the safer choice for code and long-horizon search, subject to the executor caveat above.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

Three things, from the first summary, each with what the full paper changes.

  • A measuring instrument for the construction step. The harness-scaling line had produced four competing strategies (Prime Agent, StateM, JIT-Agent, WikiSkill) and no way to compare how harnesses get built. HarnessDev supplies one, with the creator separated from the executor. The project page presents it as the third of three benchmarks on "closed-loop" self-improvement, beside Aspire (turning a vague goal into a target) and S3Gym (learning from one's own experience) ({{project page|https://self-developing-agents.github.io/}}).
  • The domain split as a diagnosis. The first summary read the split as: human references win where the harness encodes years of domain tooling (code, search), and generated harnesses compete where general agent structure is most of what a harness is. That predicts where hand-built harnesses keep their advantage. The {{harness architecture playbook|https://stencil.so/blog/harness-playbook}} filed on {{Topic: agentic-harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d881a8fe98b4cff7bb}} on 2026-09-07 is the human-written counterpart and offers one mechanism: unavoidable complexity belongs in a harness's core abstractions rather than in extensions, which is one hypothesis for why generated harnesses lag where domain tooling runs deepest. The full paper weakens the diagnosis: the losing domains are also the ones whose references ran stronger models, so the split is a hypothesis, not a finding.
  • A check on self-improving harnesses. Prime Agent's Continual Harness versions its own prompts and skills across trajectories; this benchmark says such refinement does not reliably compound. The full paper sharpens it: self-runtime gains do survive on held-out tasks, but small (+3.11 on average against up to +13.9 visible), and under a different runtime most regress. Three weeks later {{RRSI|n:3e95c17b0d0d81dbb1a5e660ed019c8a}} reported the first regularised loop whose gains survive out of distribution (+4.7 points), and {{SoL-Pi|n:3e25c17b0d0d81a0bb77f16cbc898bb5}} pointed the same loop at cost, where a cheap verifier exists.

Connections

  • {{Prime Agent|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81579a02f0431f24c6cb}} and {{JIT-Agent|n:3cd5c17b0d0d81148227fbd67dc4b3ee}} in Papers, and the harness-scaling section of {{Topic: agentic-harnesses|n:3c65c17b0d0d81d881a8fe98b4cff7bb}}: HarnessDev is the benchmark those two needed and did not have. JIT-Agent trains a 27B model to emit a harness per task and reports parity with production harnesses in a much poorer protocol language; HarnessDev measures a related gap with untrained frontier creators, and finds it depends on the domain, or on the reference's model (Result 1).
  • {{StateM|n:3c65c17b0d0d81899098d8153e100691}}: the opposite pole, a human-written state machine that constrains the agent on the same Terminal-Bench 2.1. HarnessDev's finding that generated harnesses lag most where domain tooling is deepest is consistent with StateM's win coming from encoded domain procedure, and its finding that state and memory are the module creators least often build (one of 18 saves state) is the part StateM supplies by hand.
  • {{Demystifying Agent Skills|n:3c65c17b0d0d81249a3cf5fda4de5d92}} and {{Repo-To-Skill|n:3d45c17b0d0d818bacfeda8a40caddb2}}: skills are one of the components a harness assembles, and Repo-To-Skill is the supply side of the same problem.

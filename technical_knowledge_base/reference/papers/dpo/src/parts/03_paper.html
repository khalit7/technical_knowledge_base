Problem

[[S3|§1, §3, Eq. 1 to 3]]

In 2023, steering a pretrained model towards what people prefer meant RLHF (Ziegler et al., Stiennon et al., Bai et al., InstructGPT), in three stages ({{§3|ax:S3}}):

  1. Supervised fine-tuning on good demonstrations gives a model πSFT.
  2. Reward modelling. πSFT answers each prompt x twice; people pick yw over yl. Preferences are assumed to come from a hidden reward r* through Bradley-Terry, and a reward network rφ (πSFT with a scalar head) is fitted by maximum likelihood:
    p*(y1 ≻ y2 | x) = σ(r*(x, y1) − r*(x, y2)),   ℒR = −𝔼 log σ(rφ(x, yw) − rφ(x, yl))
    Equations 1 and 2 ({{Eq. 1|ax:S3.E1}}, {{Eq. 2|ax:S3.E2}}); rewards are usually normalised to mean zero.
  3. Reinforcement learning. The policy πθ maximises the learned reward while a KL penalty keeps it near πref = πSFT, where the reward model is accurate and the text stays diverse:
    maxπθ 𝔼x, y∼πθ[rφ(x, y)] − β 𝔻KL[πθ(y|x) ‖ πref(y|x)]
    Equation 3 ({{Eq. 3|ax:S3.E3}}). Text is discrete, so this is not differentiable; the standard approach folds the penalty into the reward, rφ − β(log πθ − log πref), and runs PPO.

The third stage is the painful one: several language models at once and sampling inside the training loop; PPO keeps four networks in play (policy, frozen reference, reward model, a learned value function), and it is notoriously unstable and sensitive to hyperparameters ({{§1|ax:S1}}, {{§5.2|ax:S5.SS2}}). The question: is the reinforcement learning necessary at all? The paper's answer is no: the same KL-constrained objective can be optimised exactly with one binary cross-entropy loss on the preference pairs: no separate reward model, no sampling, no RL ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

Idea: change variables from rewards to policies

[[S4|§4, Eq. 4 to 7, Figure 1]]

The key move is a change of variables. Three steps, each a line of algebra ({{§4|ax:S4}}, {{Appendix A.1 and A.2|ax:A1.SS1}}):

1. The best policy for any reward has a closed form. For a reward r, the maximiser of Eq. 3 over all policies is a reweighting of the reference, a standard result from KL-regularised control (Peters and Schaal; Peng et al.; Korbak et al.; Go et al.):

πr(y | x) = πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y) / β) / Z(x),   Z(x) = Σy πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y) / β)
Equation 4 ({{Eq. 4|ax:S4.E4}}; proof in A.1 by Gibbs' inequality). Exact but useless as it stands: Z(x) sums over every possible answer.

2. Turn it round: a reward is a log-ratio of policies. Take logs and rearrange:

r(x, y) = β log [πr(y | x) / πref(y | x)] + β log Z(x)
Equation 5 ({{Eq. 5|ax:S4.E5}}): every reward is a scaled policy-to-reference log-ratio plus a term that depends only on the prompt.

3. Bradley-Terry only sees differences, so Z cancels. Put Eq. 5 into Eq. 1 and the two β log Z(x) terms cancel: the preference probability depends only on π* and πref ({{Eq. 6|ax:S4.E6}}). Fitting that by maximum likelihood, as Eq. 2 fits a reward model, gives the DPO objective:

ℒDPO(πθ; πref) = −𝔼(x, yw, yl)∼𝒟 log σ( β log [πθ(yw|x) / πref(yw|x)] − β log [πθ(yl|x) / πref(yl|x)] )
Equation 7 ({{Eq. 7|ax:S4.E7}}): logistic regression on a difference of log-ratios. Per pair: four forward passes (two of them the frozen reference's, computable once), one backward pass, no sampling.

The same derivation works for rankings of more than two answers under the Plackett-Luce model, where it gives a softmax over the ranked answers' log-ratios ({{Appendix A.3, Eq. 20|ax:A1.E20}}).

The loss, and what its gradient does

[[S4|§4, Appendix A.4 and B]]

The quantity inside the sigmoid is a reward in its own right, the implicit reward r̂θ(x, y) = β log [πθ(y|x) / πref(y|x)]. DPO is exactly reward-model fitting under this parameterisation, so the trained language model doubles as its own reward model, the title's claim. Its gradient ({{§4|ax:S4}}, derived in {{Appendix A.4|ax:A1.SS4}}) reads:

∇θℒDPO = −β 𝔼[ σ(r̂θ(x, yl) − r̂θ(x, yw)) · (∇θ log π(yw|x) − ∇θ log π(yl|x)) ]
Push the chosen answer up and the rejected one down, each pair weighted by how wrongly the implicit reward currently orders it. Pairs already ranked correctly contribute little; confidently wrong pairs dominate. Appendix A.4 has two sign slips that this page's {{checks|tab:t-tables:tbChecks}} list; the §4 form above is the right one (PyTorch autograd agrees with it).

The authors' claim is that the weight matters: "a naïve version of this method without the weighting coefficient can cause the language model to degenerate" ({{§4|ax:S4}}). The naive version is the paper's Unlikelihood baseline, maximise log π(yw) and minimise log π(yl) ({{Appendix C.3|ax:A3.SS3}}). DPO at the paper's default β = 0.1, trained for 600 steps on the toy (about 7 passes over the data). On average over the training pairs, what happened to the probability of the chosen answers?

It went down. The loss only asks the gap to grow, and pushing both down, the rejected faster, is often the cheapest way. The recipe ({{§4, DPO outline|ax:S4}}, {{Appendix B|ax:A2}}): label pairs sampled from πref; use πref = πSFT when the data came from it, otherwise fine-tune on the chosen answers first (Preferred-FT) to reduce the shift between data and πref. The loss is about ten lines of PyTorch (Appendix B); TRL logs the implicit rewards as rewards/chosen, rewards/rejected and rewards/margins ({{TRL docs|https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/dpo_trainer}}). Theory: your language model is secretly a reward model

[[S5|§5, Lemmas 1 and 2, Theorem 1, Eq. 8 to 10]]

Is anything lost by insisting that the reward be a log-ratio of a policy and the reference? Section 5 says no ({{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1}}, proofs in {{Appendix A.5 and A.6|ax:A1.SS5}}):

  • Definition 1. Two rewards are equivalent when they differ by a function of the prompt only, r(x, y) − r′(x, y) = f(x).
  • Lemma 1. Equivalent rewards induce the same preference distribution under Plackett-Luce and Bradley-Terry: the well-known under-specification of these models, which is why reward models need an identifiability constraint such as zero mean.
  • Lemma 2. Equivalent rewards induce the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained objective.
  • Theorem 1. If πref(y|x) > 0 everywhere and β > 0, every equivalence class contains exactly one reward of the form β log π(y|x)/πref(y|x) (uniqueness is Proposition 1 in A.6). The projection f(r) = r − β log Z(x) ({{Eq. 8|ax:S5.E8}}) picks it: the member whose Eq. 4 normaliser is exactly 1 ({{Eq. 9|ax:S5.E9}}).

So restricting to log-ratios loses no reward class, and it is exactly what makes Eq. 4 tractable for every prompt. The same lens explains PPO's trouble ({{§5.2, Eq. 10|ax:S5.E10}}): β log Z(x) is the reference's soft value function. It does not move the optimum, but without it the policy gradient has high variance; PPO learns a value function for it (hard to optimise), earlier work used a human-written completion as a one-sample baseline, and DPO's reward needs no baseline.

The toy checks this. Its policy class and reward-model class match exactly (both are sums of word-pair terms; {{the toy|tab:t-run:runToy}}), so the DPO loss over policies and the Bradley-Terry loss over rewards should reach the same minimum. Experiments: three tasks, two kinds of evaluation

[[S6|§6, Appendix C]]
  • Sentiment (IMDb): continue a 2 to 8 token review prefix positively. GPT-2-large, fine-tuned one epoch on IMDb, samples 4 completions for each of 25,000 prefixes; the classifier siebert/sentiment-roberta-large-english turns them into 6 pairs per prefix and is also the ground-truth reward ({{Appendix C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}).
  • Summarisation (Reddit TL;DR) with Stiennon et al.'s human preferences (collected on a different but similar SFT model); DPO, PPO and Preferred-FT all start from the public GPT-J SFT model CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft, PPO through TRLX.
  • Dialogue (Anthropic HH): 170,000 dialogues ending in two responses and a human preference; with no SFT model available, Pythia-2.8B fine-tuned on the preferred responses serves as πref.

Evaluation: on IMDb, each method's frontier of true reward against sequence-level KL to πref; elsewhere, win rates against the test set's reference summaries or preferred responses, judged by gpt-4-0314 with the answer order randomised ({{Appendix C.2|ax:A3.SS2}}). Baselines: zero-shot GPT-J and 2-shot Pythia-2.8B; SFT; Preferred-FT (supervised on yw); Unlikelihood with a coefficient α on the push-down term; PPO on a learned reward; PPO-GT, an oracle PPO on the true reward (IMDb only; an off-the-shelf TRL version and the authors' tuned one); and Best of N, which samples N answers and keeps the one the learned reward likes best: strong, but N samples per query at test time ({{§6|ax:S6}}).

Results

[[S6.SS1|§6.1 to 6.4, Figures 2 to 4, Tables 1 and 2]]

How well does DPO optimise the RLHF objective? On IMDb, 22 runs sweep each method's conservativeness (PPO target KL ∈ {3, 6, 9, 12}; DPO β ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 1, 5}; Unlikelihood α ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1}; seeds for Preferred-FT), evaluated every 100 steps. DPO gives by far the best frontier, the most reward at every KL, even against PPO-GT on the true reward; as both optimise the same objective, the authors read this as DPO being the better optimiser ({{§6.1, Figure 2 left|ax:S6.F2}}). PPO-GT, and for DPO it depends on β. Average shortfall from the exact frontier (lower is better): . At β = 1 and 5 DPO is about as close as PPO-GT and closer than PPO on a learned reward; at the paper's β = 0.05 and 0.1 it falls well short, because for a long stretch the weights all sit near ½ and the update behaves like the unweighted one. The toy cannot test what made the paper's PPO weak at GPT-2-large scale, but it shows DPO's dominance is not a property of the objective ({{more|tab:t-run:runSweep}}).

Summarisation. At temperature 0, DPO wins about 61% of GPT-4 comparisons against the reference summaries and PPO 57% at its best temperature (also 0); DPO also beats the best Best of N (which plateaus at 64 to 128 samples, {{Figure 4|ax:A4.F4}}), with β not meaningfully tuned. DPO is much more robust to temperature: PPO falls towards the base GPT-J at high temperature, and Preferred-FT barely improves on SFT ({{§6.2, Figure 2 right|ax:S6.F2}}). Read from the figure's vector data, every point is a count out of 256 test prompts, with ±1 binomial standard error bars: DPO 158 and PPO 146 at temperature 0 (61.7% and 57.0%); at temperature 1, DPO 99 and PPO 18.

Dialogue. DPO is "the only computationally efficient method that improves over the preferred completions" of Anthropic HH, roughly matching the expensive Best of 128 at each method's best temperature: 62.9% against 60.8%, both at temperature 1.0, though at 0.25 DPO is at 37.0% and Best of 128 at 54.2% ({{§6.2, Figure 3|ax:S6.F3}}). There is no PPO here: a public PPO model (reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B) could not beat the base Pythia-2.8B at any prompt or temperature tried, so Best of 128 stands in as "a rough proxy for PPO-level performance". DPO reaches its level quickly and stays there.

A new input distribution (added in v2, December 2023): on CNN/DailyMail news, the TL;DR policies win 0.36 (DPO) against 0.26 (PPO) at temperature 0 and 0.31 against 0.23 at 0.25, versus the ground-truth summaries, though PPO also saw extra unlabelled Reddit prompts ({{§6.3, Table 1|ax:S6.T1}}).

GPT-4 as a stand-in for people. On TL;DR, three methods against greedy PPO: DPO at temperature 0.25 (272 respondents), SFT at 0.25 (122) and PPO at 1.0 (199). With the concise prompt GPT-4 (C), GPT-4 agrees with people 67%, 79% and 85% of the time, against 65% and 87% human-human agreement; people preferred DPO 58% of the time. The simple prompt GPT-4 (S) prefers longer, more repetitive summaries, so the main results use GPT-4 (C) ({{§6.4, Table 2|ax:S6.T2}}).

DPO wins 158 of 256 TL;DR comparisons and PPO 146 of 256 (temperature 0, separate runs on the same prompts). How many standard errors apart are they?

About one. Each proportion has a standard error of about 3 points at n = 256, so the 4.7-point gap has one of about 4.3 (treating the runs as independent; per-prompt outcomes, which a paired test would need, are not published): . DPO's best against Best of 128's best: z ≈ on TL;DR, on HH. Temperature robustness is far outside the noise: at temperature 1, 99 wins against 18.

Limitations the authors list ({{§7|ax:S7}}): out-of-distribution generalisation against an explicit reward; whether self-labelling can use unlabelled prompts as RLHF does; reward over-optimisation in DPO (perhaps the slight late decline in Figure 3 right); scale beyond 6B; and the GPT-4 judge's prompt dependence. They suggest DPO for other generative modalities too.

How much of this to believe

[[S6|§6, Figures 2 to 4, Tables 1 to 3, Appendix A]]

What holds up. The derivation is exact and checkable: Eq. 4 is a textbook result, the cancellation is one line, the gradient is what autograd computes (this page checks the toy's losses and gradients against PyTorch to 1e-13). The IMDb frontier uses a ground-truth reward and 22 runs, and its gap is large: at a KL of at most 2, DPO reaches 0.91 against PPO's 0.49. TL;DR's temperature robustness is far outside the noise. The recipe has been reproduced by almost everyone since (Zephyr, Mixtral, Llama 3, Qwen2, Tülu 3, OLMo 2; {{Why it matters|tab:t-read:s-why}}). And the authors flag scale, the judge's prompt sensitivity and out-of-distribution behaviour themselves, which counts in their favour.

What is weaker, concretely:

  • single runs Every win-rate point is one training run evaluated on 256 prompts (TL;DR) or about 250 (HH): DPO against PPO on TL;DR is 4.7 ± 4.3 points (z ≈ 1.1); DPO against Best of 128 is z ≈ 1.0 on TL;DR and 0.5 on HH. "Exceeds PPO's best-case performance" is a point estimate within one standard error ({{figures|tab:t-tables:tbF2r}}).
  • not like for like There is no PPO on the dialogue task; Best of 128 is called "a rough proxy for PPO-level performance". Each method is compared at its own best temperature, chosen on the same test prompts. On IMDb the paper's PPO and PPO-GT barely move the reward below a KL of 2, which suggests a weak PPO more than a weak objective: in this page's toy, where PPO is easy to get right, PPO-GT sits on the exact frontier and DPO at the paper's default β = 0.1 does not ({{toy sweep|tab:t-run:runSweep}}). Later controlled studies with carefully tuned PPO found it ahead, especially on code (Xu et al. 2024).
  • a proxy judge GPT-4 with a prompt chosen because it matched people better; the human study is 122 to 272 respondents per comparison with repeated human judgments for two of the three, and human-human agreement on the DPO comparison was only 65%. The 58% human preference for DPO over PPO is 2.7 standard errors above 50%.
  • the weighting claim The evidence that the weight prevents degeneration (§4, "Appendix Table 3") is two Unlikelihood samples at temperature 1.0 on TL;DR, not an ablation. The toy supports the direction: without the weight the policy loses its grammar after enough steps (grammatical mass from 91% to 0.3% by 4,000 steps). It also shows that DPO itself degenerates when trained far past the sweep, more slowly ({{the toy|tab:t-run:runLong}}).
  • small slips Appendix A.4 swaps yw and yl inside Eq. 21 and ends with the weight σ(r̂w − r̂l), the opposite of §4; §5.2 says "the optimal policy from Eq. 7" where it means Eq. 4. Neither affects the method ({{checks|tab:t-tables:tbChecks}}).

Verdict. Believe the derivation and the recipe: DPO optimises the RLHF objective without RL, with two models instead of four and no sampling, and it works well enough that it became the default. Treat "better than PPO" as unproven by this paper: the dialogue comparison has no PPO, the summarisation margin is one standard error, and the sentiment frontier mostly shows that the paper's PPO was hard to make work.

What it takes to use this

[[A2|Appendix B; the code]]
  • Code. The authors' {{reference implementation|https://github.com/eric-mitchell/direct-preference-optimization}} (Apache 2.0): SFT then DPO with FSDP, and switches for conservative DPO (loss.label_smoothing) and IPO (loss=ipo). In production most people use {{Hugging Face TRL's DPOTrainer|https://huggingface.co/docs/trl/dpo_trainer}}, which exposes the variants as loss_type.
  • Hardware. The README's example on Pythia 2.8B with Anthropic HH: on four 80 GB A100s, SFT takes about 1 h 30 min and DPO about 2 h 45 min. Memory is a trainable policy plus a frozen reference; the reference's log-probabilities can be computed once and cached, which Tülu 3 did for its 70B runs. In Tülu 3's controlled comparison DPO took about 4 hours on one node and PPO about 28 hours on two, for slightly better scores ({{Tülu 3 §5.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}}).
  • Settings that worked. β = 0.1 (0.5 for TL;DR), batch 64, RMSprop at 1e-6 with 150 warmup steps (Appendix B); the README suggests β between 0.1 and 0.5. Llama 3 used β = 0.1 at 1e-5, masked formatting tokens out of the loss and added a negative log-likelihood term (weight 0.2) on the chosen answers to stop their probability falling.
  • Data. Pairs sampled from the model being tuned (or a close relative) match the theory best; with someone else's pairs, first fine-tune on the chosen answers, as the paper does for HH.
  • When it pays off. Offline preference or style alignment from a fixed set of pairs, where maintaining a reward model and a PPO loop is the main cost. When it buys little: tasks with a checkable reward (maths, code), where on-policy RL such as GRPO and RLVR now dominates; and near-deterministic preferences, where DPO keeps widening margins and drifts from πref unless stopped early (the motivation for IPO and conservative DPO; see {{Then and now|tab:t-then}}). Watch rewards/chosen: if it goes negative, the chosen answers are losing probability.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

[[S7|later work]]
  • It commoditised preference tuning: a fine-tuning loss anyone can run on preference pairs. Zephyr-7B (October 2023) showed it at 7B on UltraFeedback's AI-feedback pairs ({{Zephyr|https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16944}}), and it became the standard alignment stage of open pipelines: Mixtral Instruct (SFT then DPO, {{Mixtral §4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04088}}), Llama 3 (which "found that DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better" than PPO, {{Llama 3 §4.1.4|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.21783}}), Qwen2 (offline DPO, then online DPO on its own samples, {{Qwen2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10671}}), Tülu 3 and OLMo 2.
  • The DPO against PPO debate. Xu et al. ("Is DPO Superior to PPO for LLM Alignment?", 2024) showed DPO can find solutions on answers the data never covered and found well-tuned PPO ahead in every testbed, including competitive code ({{2404.10719|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10719}}). Google DeepMind's online-against-offline study found offline methods better at classifying pairs but worse at generating ({{Tang et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.08448}}); on-policy samples and a "negative gradient" both help ({{Tajwar et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.14367}}); and DPO tends to push the chosen answers' likelihood down too ({{DPO-Positive|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.13228}}, {{likelihood displacement|https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08847}}). The rough consensus as of 2026: DPO is the cheap, stable choice for general preference and style alignment; online RL (PPO, and above all GRPO and RL with verifiable rewards, as in DeepSeek-R1) wins for reasoning and frontier-scale post-training; iterative and online DPO close part of the gap; labs commonly run a DPO stage then RLVR, as in {{Tülu 3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124}} and {{OLMo 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00656}}.
  • It spawned a family: IPO (a squared loss, so near-deterministic preferences cannot drive margins to infinity), conservative DPO and rDPO (label noise), KTO (unpaired thumbs up or down), ORPO (preference folded into SFT, no reference), SimPO (reference-free, length-normalised), CPO, TR-DPO (the reference updated during training) and DiscoPOP (losses found by automated search); {{Then and now|tab:t-then}} runs several on the toy. GRPO is a separate line with the same motive of deleting RLHF machinery, there the value model ({{DeepSeekMath|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300}}).
  • Conceptually, "policy log-ratio = reward" became a reusable tool: DPO models are scored as reward models (RewardBench evaluates them beside classifiers), their implicit rewards build the next round's preference data (DICE), and the derivation pattern (solve the KL-regularised objective in closed form, substitute back) recurs across post-training papers ({{RewardBench|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13787}}, {{DICE|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.09760}}).

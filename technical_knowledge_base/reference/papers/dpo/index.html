Problem

§1, §3, Eq. 1 to 3

In 2023, steering a pretrained model towards what people prefer meant RLHF (Ziegler et al., Stiennon et al., Bai et al., InstructGPT), in three stages (§3):

  1. Supervised fine-tuning on good demonstrations gives a model πSFT.
  2. Reward modelling. πSFT answers each prompt x twice; people pick yw over yl. Preferences are assumed to come from a hidden reward r* through Bradley-Terry, and a reward network rφ (πSFT with a scalar head) is fitted by maximum likelihood:
    p*(y1 ≻ y2 | x) = σ(r*(x, y1) − r*(x, y2)),   ℒR = −𝔼 log σ(rφ(x, yw) − rφ(x, yl))
    Equations 1 and 2 (Eq. 1, Eq. 2); rewards are usually normalised to mean zero.
  3. Reinforcement learning. The policy πθ maximises the learned reward while a KL penalty keeps it near πref = πSFT, where the reward model is accurate and the text stays diverse:
    maxπθ 𝔼x, y∼πθ[rφ(x, y)] − β 𝔻KL[πθ(y|x) ‖ πref(y|x)]
    Equation 3 (Eq. 3). Text is discrete, so this is not differentiable; the standard approach folds the penalty into the reward, rφ − β(log πθ − log πref), and runs PPO.

The third stage is the painful one: several language models at once and sampling inside the training loop; PPO keeps four networks in play (policy, frozen reference, reward model, a learned value function), and it is notoriously unstable and sensitive to hyperparameters (§1, §5.2). The question: is the reinforcement learning necessary at all? The paper's answer is no: the same KL-constrained objective can be optimised exactly with one binary cross-entropy loss on the preference pairs: no separate reward model, no sampling, no RL (§1).

Idea: change variables from rewards to policies

§4, Eq. 4 to 7, Figure 1

The key move is a change of variables. Three steps, each a line of algebra (§4, Appendix A.1 and A.2):

1. The best policy for any reward has a closed form. For a reward r, the maximiser of Eq. 3 over all policies is a reweighting of the reference, a standard result from KL-regularised control (Peters and Schaal; Peng et al.; Korbak et al.; Go et al.):

πr(y | x) = πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y) / β) / Z(x),   Z(x) = Σy πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y) / β)
Equation 4 (Eq. 4; proof in A.1 by Gibbs' inequality). Exact but useless as it stands: Z(x) sums over every possible answer.

2. Turn it round: a reward is a log-ratio of policies. Take logs and rearrange:

r(x, y) = β log [πr(y | x) / πref(y | x)] + β log Z(x)
Equation 5 (Eq. 5): every reward is a scaled policy-to-reference log-ratio plus a term that depends only on the prompt.

3. Bradley-Terry only sees differences, so Z cancels. Put Eq. 5 into Eq. 1 and the two β log Z(x) terms cancel: the preference probability depends only on π* and πref (Eq. 6). Fitting that by maximum likelihood, as Eq. 2 fits a reward model, gives the DPO objective:

ℒDPO(πθ; πref) = −𝔼(x, yw, yl)∼𝒟 log σ( β log [πθ(yw|x) / πref(yw|x)] − β log [πθ(yl|x) / πref(yl|x)] )
Equation 7 (Eq. 7): logistic regression on a difference of log-ratios. Per pair: four forward passes (two of them the frozen reference's, computable once), one backward pass, no sampling.

The same derivation works for rankings of more than two answers under the Plackett-Luce model, where it gives a softmax over the ranked answers' log-ratios (Appendix A.3, Eq. 20).

The loss, and what its gradient does

§4, Appendix A.4 and B

The quantity inside the sigmoid is a reward in its own right, the implicit reward r̂θ(x, y) = β log [πθ(y|x) / πref(y|x)]. DPO is exactly reward-model fitting under this parameterisation, so the trained language model doubles as its own reward model, the title's claim. Its gradient (§4, derived in Appendix A.4) reads:

∇θℒDPO = −β 𝔼[ σ(r̂θ(x, yl) − r̂θ(x, yw)) · (∇θ log π(yw|x) − ∇θ log π(yl|x)) ]
Push the chosen answer up and the rejected one down, each pair weighted by how wrongly the implicit reward currently orders it. Pairs already ranked correctly contribute little; confidently wrong pairs dominate. Appendix A.4 has two sign slips that this page's checks list; the §4 form above is the right one (PyTorch autograd agrees with it).

The authors' claim is that the weight matters: "a naïve version of this method without the weighting coefficient can cause the language model to degenerate" (§4). The naive version is the paper's Unlikelihood baseline, maximise log π(yw) and minimise log π(yl) (Appendix C.3). Theory: your language model is secretly a reward model

§5, Lemmas 1 and 2, Theorem 1, Eq. 8 to 10

Is anything lost by insisting that the reward be a log-ratio of a policy and the reference? Section 5 says no (§5.1, proofs in Appendix A.5 and A.6):

  • Definition 1. Two rewards are equivalent when they differ by a function of the prompt only, r(x, y) − r′(x, y) = f(x).
  • Lemma 1. Equivalent rewards induce the same preference distribution under Plackett-Luce and Bradley-Terry: the well-known under-specification of these models, which is why reward models need an identifiability constraint such as zero mean.
  • Lemma 2. Equivalent rewards induce the same optimal policy under the KL-constrained objective.
  • Theorem 1. If πref(y|x) > 0 everywhere and β > 0, every equivalence class contains exactly one reward of the form β log π(y|x)/πref(y|x) (uniqueness is Proposition 1 in A.6). The projection f(r) = r − β log Z(x) (Eq. 8) picks it: the member whose Eq. 4 normaliser is exactly 1 (Eq. 9).

So restricting to log-ratios loses no reward class, and it is exactly what makes Eq. 4 tractable for every prompt. The same lens explains PPO's trouble (§5.2, Eq. 10): β log Z(x) is the reference's soft value function. It does not move the optimum, but without it the policy gradient has high variance; PPO learns a value function for it (hard to optimise), earlier work used a human-written completion as a one-sample baseline, and DPO's reward needs no baseline.

Experiments: three tasks, two kinds of evaluation

§6, Appendix C
  • Sentiment (IMDb): continue a 2 to 8 token review prefix positively. GPT-2-large, fine-tuned one epoch on IMDb, samples 4 completions for each of 25,000 prefixes; the classifier siebert/sentiment-roberta-large-english turns them into 6 pairs per prefix and is also the ground-truth reward (Appendix C.1).
  • Summarisation (Reddit TL;DR) with Stiennon et al.'s human preferences (collected on a different but similar SFT model); DPO, PPO and Preferred-FT all start from the public GPT-J SFT model CarperAI/openai_summarize_tldr_sft, PPO through TRLX.
  • Dialogue (Anthropic HH): 170,000 dialogues ending in two responses and a human preference; with no SFT model available, Pythia-2.8B fine-tuned on the preferred responses serves as πref.

Evaluation: on IMDb, each method's frontier of true reward against sequence-level KL to πref; elsewhere, win rates against the test set's reference summaries or preferred responses, judged by gpt-4-0314 with the answer order randomised (Appendix C.2). Baselines: zero-shot GPT-J and 2-shot Pythia-2.8B; SFT; Preferred-FT (supervised on yw); Unlikelihood with a coefficient α on the push-down term; PPO on a learned reward; PPO-GT, an oracle PPO on the true reward (IMDb only; an off-the-shelf TRL version and the authors' tuned one); and Best of N, which samples N answers and keeps the one the learned reward likes best: strong, but N samples per query at test time (§6).

Summarisation. At temperature 0, DPO wins about 61% of GPT-4 comparisons against the reference summaries and PPO 57% at its best temperature (also 0); DPO also beats the best Best of N (which plateaus at 64 to 128 samples, Figure 4), with β not meaningfully tuned. DPO is much more robust to temperature: PPO falls towards the base GPT-J at high temperature, and Preferred-FT barely improves on SFT (§6.2, Figure 2 right). Read from the figure's vector data, every point is a count out of 256 test prompts, with ±1 binomial standard error bars: DPO 158 and PPO 146 at temperature 0 (61.7% and 57.0%); at temperature 1, DPO 99 and PPO 18.

Dialogue. DPO is "the only computationally efficient method that improves over the preferred completions" of Anthropic HH, roughly matching the expensive Best of 128 at each method's best temperature: 62.9% against 60.8%, both at temperature 1.0, though at 0.25 DPO is at 37.0% and Best of 128 at 54.2% (§6.2, Figure 3). There is no PPO here: a public PPO model (reciprocate/ppo_hh_pythia-6B) could not beat the base Pythia-2.8B at any prompt or temperature tried, so Best of 128 stands in as "a rough proxy for PPO-level performance". DPO reaches its level quickly and stays there.

A new input distribution (added in v2, December 2023): on CNN/DailyMail news, the TL;DR policies win 0.36 (DPO) against 0.26 (PPO) at temperature 0 and 0.31 against 0.23 at 0.25, versus the ground-truth summaries, though PPO also saw extra unlabelled Reddit prompts (§6.3, Table 1).

GPT-4 as a stand-in for people. On TL;DR, three methods against greedy PPO: DPO at temperature 0.25 (272 respondents), SFT at 0.25 (122) and PPO at 1.0 (199). With the concise prompt GPT-4 (C), GPT-4 agrees with people 67%, 79% and 85% of the time, against 65% and 87% human-human agreement; people preferred DPO 58% of the time. The simple prompt GPT-4 (S) prefers longer, more repetitive summaries, so the main results use GPT-4 (C) (§6.4, Table 2).

Limitations the authors list (§7): out-of-distribution generalisation against an explicit reward; whether self-labelling can use unlabelled prompts as RLHF does; reward over-optimisation in DPO (perhaps the slight late decline in Figure 3 right); scale beyond 6B; and the GPT-4 judge's prompt dependence. How much of this to believe

§6, Figures 2 to 4, Tables 1 to 3, Appendix A

What holds up. The derivation is exact and checkable: Eq. 4 is a textbook result, the cancellation is one line, the gradient is what autograd computes (this page checks the toy's losses and gradients against PyTorch to 1e-13). The IMDb frontier uses a ground-truth reward and 22 runs, and its gap is large: at a KL of at most 2, DPO reaches 0.91 against PPO's 0.49. TL;DR's temperature robustness is far outside the noise. The recipe has been reproduced by almost everyone since (Zephyr, Mixtral, Llama 3, Qwen2, Tülu 3, OLMo 2; Why it matters). And the authors flag scale, the judge's prompt sensitivity and out-of-distribution behaviour themselves, which counts in their favour.

What is weaker, concretely:

  • single runs Every win-rate point is one training run evaluated on 256 prompts (TL;DR) or about 250 (HH): DPO against PPO on TL;DR is 4.7 ± 4.3 points (z ≈ 1.1); DPO against Best of 128 is z ≈ 1.0 on TL;DR and 0.5 on HH. "Exceeds PPO's best-case performance" is a point estimate within one standard error (figures).
  • not like for like There is no PPO on the dialogue task; Best of 128 is called "a rough proxy for PPO-level performance". Each method is compared at its own best temperature, chosen on the same test prompts. On IMDb the paper's PPO and PPO-GT barely move the reward below a KL of 2, which suggests a weak PPO more than a weak objective: in this page's toy, where PPO is easy to get right, PPO-GT sits on the exact frontier and DPO at the paper's default β = 0.1 does not (toy sweep). Later controlled studies with carefully tuned PPO found it ahead, especially on code (Xu et al. 2024).
  • a proxy judge GPT-4 with a prompt chosen because it matched people better; the human study is 122 to 272 respondents per comparison with repeated human judgments for two of the three, and human-human agreement on the DPO comparison was only 65%. The 58% human preference for DPO over PPO is 2.7 standard errors above 50%.
  • the weighting claim The evidence that the weight prevents degeneration (§4, "Appendix Table 3") is two Unlikelihood samples at temperature 1.0 on TL;DR, not an ablation. The toy supports the direction: without the weight the policy loses its grammar after enough steps (grammatical mass from 91% to 0.3% by 4,000 steps). It also shows that DPO itself degenerates when trained far past the sweep, more slowly (the toy).
  • small slips Appendix A.4 swaps yw and yl inside Eq. 21 and ends with the weight σ(r̂w − r̂l), the opposite of §4; §5.2 says "the optimal policy from Eq. 7" where it means Eq. 4. Neither affects the method (checks).

Verdict. Believe the derivation and the recipe: DPO optimises the RLHF objective without RL, with two models instead of four and no sampling, and it works well enough that it became the default. Treat "better than PPO" as unproven by this paper: the dialogue comparison has no PPO, the summarisation margin is one standard error, and the sentiment frontier mostly shows that the paper's PPO was hard to make work.

What it takes to use this

Appendix B; the code
  • Code. The authors' reference implementation (Apache 2.0): SFT then DPO with FSDP, and switches for conservative DPO (loss.label_smoothing) and IPO (loss=ipo). In production most people use Hugging Face TRL's DPOTrainer, which exposes the variants as loss_type.
  • Hardware. The README's example on Pythia 2.8B with Anthropic HH: on four 80 GB A100s, SFT takes about 1 h 30 min and DPO about 2 h 45 min. Memory is a trainable policy plus a frozen reference; the reference's log-probabilities can be computed once and cached, which Tülu 3 did for its 70B runs. In Tülu 3's controlled comparison DPO took about 4 hours on one node and PPO about 28 hours on two, for slightly better scores (Tülu 3 §5.4).
  • Settings that worked. β = 0.1 (0.5 for TL;DR), batch 64, RMSprop at 1e-6 with 150 warmup steps (Appendix B); the README suggests β between 0.1 and 0.5. Llama 3 used β = 0.1 at 1e-5, masked formatting tokens out of the loss and added a negative log-likelihood term (weight 0.2) on the chosen answers to stop their probability falling.
  • Data. Pairs sampled from the model being tuned (or a close relative) match the theory best; with someone else's pairs, first fine-tune on the chosen answers, as the paper does for HH.
  • When it pays off. Offline preference or style alignment from a fixed set of pairs, where maintaining a reward model and a PPO loop is the main cost. When it buys little: tasks with a checkable reward (maths, code), where on-policy RL such as GRPO and RLVR now dominates; and near-deterministic preferences, where DPO keeps widening margins and drifts from πref unless stopped early (the motivation for IPO and conservative DPO; see Then and now). Watch rewards/chosen: if it goes negative, the chosen answers are losing probability.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

later work
  • It commoditised preference tuning: a fine-tuning loss anyone can run on preference pairs. Zephyr-7B (October 2023) showed it at 7B on UltraFeedback's AI-feedback pairs (Zephyr), and it became the standard alignment stage of open pipelines: Mixtral Instruct (SFT then DPO, Mixtral §4), Llama 3 (which "found that DPO required less compute for large-scale models and performed better" than PPO, Llama 3 §4.1.4), Qwen2 (offline DPO, then online DPO on its own samples, Qwen2), Tülu 3 and OLMo 2.
  • The DPO against PPO debate. Xu et al. ("Is DPO Superior to PPO for LLM Alignment?", 2024) showed DPO can find solutions on answers the data never covered and found well-tuned PPO ahead in every testbed, including competitive code (2404.10719). Google DeepMind's online-against-offline study found offline methods better at classifying pairs but worse at generating (Tang et al.); on-policy samples and a "negative gradient" both help (Tajwar et al.); and DPO tends to push the chosen answers' likelihood down too (DPO-Positive, likelihood displacement). The rough consensus as of 2026: DPO is the cheap, stable choice for general preference and style alignment; online RL (PPO, and above all GRPO and RL with verifiable rewards, as in DeepSeek-R1) wins for reasoning and frontier-scale post-training; iterative and online DPO close part of the gap; labs commonly run a DPO stage then RLVR, as in Tülu 3 and OLMo 2.
  • It spawned a family: IPO (a squared loss, so near-deterministic preferences cannot drive margins to infinity), conservative DPO and rDPO (label noise), KTO (unpaired thumbs up or down), ORPO (preference folded into SFT, no reference), SimPO (reference-free, length-normalised), CPO, TR-DPO (the reference updated during training) and DiscoPOP (losses found by automated search); Then and now runs several on the toy. GRPO is a separate line with the same motive of deleting RLHF machinery, there the value model (DeepSeekMath).
  • Conceptually, "policy log-ratio = reward" became a reusable tool: DPO models are scored as reward models (RewardBench evaluates them beside classifiers), their implicit rewards build the next round's preference data (DICE), and the derivation pattern (solve the KL-regularised objective in closed form, substitute back) recurs across post-training papers (RewardBench, DICE).

Train DPO and RLHF in your browser

A tiny language model, preference-tuned here by real arithmetic, every way the paper compares: DPO, its Unlikelihood and Preferred-FT baselines, RLHF with a learned reward model and PPO, and PPO on the true reward. The space is small enough that everything the paper could only estimate is exact here: the expected true reward, the KL to the reference, and the best possible reward-KL trade-off of Eq. 4.

The toy, in seven lines
  • Language. Reviews of exactly 4 words from 12 (film, plot, cast, was, is, very, not, so, good, great, dull, bad): 20,736 possible sequences.
  • Policy. Autoregressive: one softmax over the first word and, at positions 2 to 4, one softmax per previous word, 444 logits in all; its log-probability of a sequence is the sum of 4 log-softmax entries.
  • Reference (the SFT model). A grammar, noun then verb then modifier then adjective (72 grammatical sequences), with 3% of every softmax spread uniformly so that every sequence has πref > 0, as Theorem 1 assumes.
  • True reward, standing in for the IMDb sentiment classifier: p(positive) = σ(score), with good +1.2, great +2.2, dull −1.4, bad −2.0, an adjective after "not" multiplied by −0.7 and after "very" or "so" by 1.4, and a bias of −0.3. It lies in [0, 1], like the paper's.
  • Data, the paper's recipe (Appendix C.1) at toy scale: 1,000 groups of 4 samples from πref, all 6 pairs of each group, labelled by the classifier (higher score wins; ties dropped): . One prompt only: the toy has no prefixes.
  • RLHF. A reward model linear in the same 444 word-pair features, fitted with Eq. 2 for 3 epochs, keeping the best validation epoch as the paper does: . PPO with the KL penalty folded into a sequence-level reward, batches of 64 fresh samples, a clipped surrogate (ε = 0.2, 4 epochs per batch), the batch mean as baseline, and β adapted towards a target KL (Ziegler et al.'s controller), Adam at 0.01.
  • Offline methods (DPO and the rest): batches of 64 pairs, RMSprop as in Appendix B but at a toy learning rate of 0.01 with 20 warmup steps. Every loss and gradient is checked against PyTorch autograd: .

What this toy can and cannot tell you
  • It can check the algebra (exact losses and gradients against PyTorch; DPO and reward-model losses reaching the same minimum), show the reward-KL trade-off against the exact optimum, and show what each loss does to probabilities when trained long.
  • It cannot reproduce what made PPO hard at GPT-2-large scale: here the policy has 444 numbers, PPO's gradient noise is small, and the reward model generalises well because its features are the policy's own. So "PPO-GT beats DPO here" says that DPO's objective has no built-in advantage, not that the paper's measurement was wrong.
  • All 4-word answers have the same length, so SimPO's length normalisation and DPO's length bias cannot show up; there is one prompt, so out-of-distribution prompts cannot be tested.

    Then and now: one pair, many losses

    Almost every successor keeps DPO's frame (a margin between the chosen and the rejected answer, measured against a reference) and changes one thing: how hard a pair is pushed as a function of its margin. That one curve decides whether training stops. Below, the push −∂ℒ/∂h on one pair against its margin h = log πθ/πref(yw) − log πθ/πref(yl), one loss at a time, with what each did on this page's toy when trained for 4,000 steps on the same deterministic labels (the long runs).

    Curves at the toy's settings: β = 0.1 for DPO and cDPO (ε = 0.1), β = 0.5 for the hinge, τ = 0.1 for IPO, β = 2 and γ = 1 for SimPO (whose margin is the reference-free, length-averaged log-probability gap), each push scaled to 1 at h = 0. Formulas from each paper; the IPO and cDPO forms are those of the authors' code (trainers.py).

    What else changed, and what survived

    Oct 2023
    Zephyr-7B: SFT then DPO on UltraFeedback's AI-ranked pairs, "a few hours of training without any additional sampling", ahead of Llama2-Chat-70B on MT-Bench (Zephyr). DPO becomes the open-model default.
    Jan 2024
    CPO (contrastive preference optimisation, for translation, no reference model) and KTO (Feb 2024: one thumbs up or down per answer instead of pairs, matching preference methods from 1B to 30B) (CPO, KTO).
    Feb to Mar 2024
    DPO-Positive shows the chosen likelihood can fall and adds a term against it; rDPO de-biases label noise with provable guarantees; ORPO drops the reference model and the separate stage by adding an odds-ratio term to SFT (2402.13228, 2403.00409, 2403.07691).
    Apr to May 2024
    The online question. Xu et al.: well-tuned PPO beats DPO in every testbed, and DPO "might find biased solutions that exploit out-of-distribution responses"; Tajwar et al.: on-policy samples and a negative gradient both matter; Tang et al. (Google DeepMind): offline methods classify pairs better but generate worse; TR-DPO updates the reference during training to fight over-optimisation (2404.10719, 2404.14367, 2405.08448, 2404.09656).
    Jun 2024
    DiscoPOP: an LLM proposes and tests preference losses; the best "adaptively blends logistic and exponential losses" (2406.08414).
    Jul 2024
    Llama 3 chooses DPO over PPO for compute and instruction following, with β = 0.1, formatting tokens masked and an NLL term on chosen answers; Qwen2 runs offline DPO, then online DPO on pairs from its own samples ranked by a reward model (Llama 3, Qwen2).
    Oct 2024
    Likelihood displacement: training to prefer "No" over "Never" can raise "Yes"; on refusals it cut Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate from 74.4% to 33.4% (2410.08847).
    Nov 2024 to 2025
    Tülu 3 and OLMo 2: SFT, then DPO on synthetic pairs that include on-policy samples, then RL with verifiable rewards. In Tülu 3's controlled test PPO came close to DPO's average (slightly lower) at about 14 times the node-hours (28 hours on two nodes against 4 on one) (Tülu 3, OLMo 2). For reasoning, GRPO and RLVR take over (DeepSeekMath, DeepSeek-R1).
    part of the 2023 recipestatus in 2026
    The closed form of Eq. 4 and the implicit reward β log π/πrefSurvived everywhere: the shared starting point of IPO, KTO, cDPO, rDPO and TR-DPO, and the logged rewards/* of every DPO trainer.
    The sigmoid (Bradley-Terry) lossStill the default (Llama 3, Tülu 3), with fixes for its unbounded push: label smoothing, a bounded loss (IPO), an NLL term on the chosen answers, or early stopping.
    A frozen reference modelOptional: dropped by SimPO, ORPO and CPO, updated during training by TR-DPO, cached as log-probabilities at scale.
    Offline pairs collected onceMostly replaced by on-policy or iterative pairs (Qwen2, Tülu 3, OLMo 2), which recovers part of online RL's advantage.
    "No RL needed"True for preference and style alignment; not for reasoning, where online RL with verifiable rewards is the standard after DeepSeek-R1.

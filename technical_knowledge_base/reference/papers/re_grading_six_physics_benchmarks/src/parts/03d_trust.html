How much of this to believe

[[A2|Appendices B, C, F]]
On four of the benchmarks the experts reviewed only answers that had been marked wrong. Which mistakes can an audit like that never find?

The HLE-Physics preset of the {{audit simulator|tab:t-run:sim}}, with the paper's counts and two things the paper could not measure set to small values: the judge accepts 3% of wrong answers, and 5% of defective questions happen to be accepted anyway (the model picked the reading the key intended). Both are illustrative; the paper's protocol counts every one of them as correct and keeps them.

What holds up.

  • The direction is robust. 238 of 250 audited rejections were not the model's fault. Even if every one of the 22 disputed pairs that involved "model error" (19 benchmark/model, 3 grader/model, {{Table 4|ax:A6.T4}}) had been resolved as a model error, model errors would be 34 of 250, 13.6% (95% interval 9.9% to 18.4%): still a small minority.
  • The rises dwarf sampling noise. GPT-5.6-Sol's HLE-Physics pass@4 goes from 113 of 202 questions (95% interval 49.0% to 62.6%) to 106 of 116 (84.9% to 95.3%); the intervals are nowhere near each other.
  • CMT-Benchmark is close to like for like. Every question was reviewed regardless of the model's answer, the same HLE-adapted judge graded before and after, and 49 of 50 questions stayed (29 of them repaired): the 61.00% to 87.24% rise comes from fixing the materials ({{Appendix B.2.2|ax:A2.SS2.SSS2}}).
  • A second team found the same on CritPt. Anthropic's experts revised 31 of 71 statements (43.7%), this audit found defects in 21 of 56 (37.5%), and both corrected scores land in the high 80s.
  • The numbers are internally consistent. All 70 percentages in {{Table 1|ax:S3.T1}} are whole numbers of attempts or questions, and every count in the appendices adds up (27 checks in the {{Tables tab|tab:t-tables:chk}}). The pre-audit CritPt score matches Artificial Analysis's leaderboard to the attempt (113 of 350).
  • The authors state several limits themselves, which counts in their favour: pre-audit and corrected scores are not on the same questions; the three public-source benchmarks may be contaminated; Table 2 "is not a reconstruction" of Table 1; and the Anthropic comparison is not like for like.

What does not, or not yet.

  • The audit is one-sided. On HLE-Physics, PHYBench, PRISM-Physics and UGPhysics, only rejected answers were reviewed; the 252 accepted answers never were ({{Appendix C|ax:A3}}). So a lenient judge that accepts a wrong answer, or a defective question the model happened to get "right", is invisible and stays in the corrected set as a success. Every bias this leaves points upward. The {{audit simulator|tab:t-run:sim}} shows how large it can get.
  • One model chose which questions were removed. The excluded questions are those GPT-5.6-Sol failed on every attempt of its audit run and that experts then judged defective. Fable 5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro are re-scored on that GPT-filtered set. And for GPT-5.6-Sol itself the corrected score is largely fixed by the audit: on HLE-Physics, 104 accepted plus 4 grader errors out of 116 kept already gives 93.1%, against the 91.38% pass@4 reported (the same holds within 7 points on all four; {{Tables tab|tab:t-tables:imp}}).
  • On HLE-Physics the rise is mostly the denominator. Removing the 86 excluded questions and nothing else would lift GPT-5.6-Sol from 47.28% to 82.3% if those questions had scored zero before, more than the 78.66% reported. That is the paper's point (they are defective), but it means the HLE-Physics number measures accuracy on the 57% of questions that survived one model's failures, not on the exam. On PHYBench and PRISM-Physics, by contrast, removal alone gives only 30.5% and 17.6%: there the rise really is the grader being fixed ({{Tables tab|tab:t-tables:den}}).
  • CritPt's before and after differ in almost everything. Pre-audit: Artificial Analysis's harness and grader, official references, 70 challenges, mean@5, Fable 5 at Max. Corrected: the HLE-adapted judge, references re-derived by the auditors, 54 challenges, mean@4, Codex and Claude Code with tools, Fable 5 at High. The 32.3% to 87.5% jump mixes all of these; Gemini 3.1 Pro, without tools on both sides, is the cleanest pair (17.71% to 54.63%). Why 56 of the 70 challenges were audited is not explained.
  • The reviews are noisy. 54 of the 250 cases had a single reviewer. Of the 196 reviewed twice, the first two reviewers agreed on 71.4%; because one label dominates each benchmark, much of that is chance, and agreement beyond chance (Scott's pi, bounded from Tables 2 to 4 because the reviewers' own label shares are not printed) is at most 0.36 on HLE-Physics and 0.28 on PRISM-Physics, "fair" at best. The 19 benchmark/model disagreements are exactly the decisive kind. Reviewers were also shown an AI-generated preliminary review, of unstated origin, which can anchor a label ({{Appendix F.2|ax:A6.SS2}}).
  • On CMT and CritPt, the person who repaired a problem also wrote its new reference. One reviewer per problem, AI assistance allowed ({{Appendix F.1|ax:A6.SS1}}). Whether repairs were written before or after seeing the model's answers is not stated.
  • The corrected judge is unnamed and its error rate is half measured. The "4.08%" is 4 grader errors among HLE-Physics's 98 rejections; it measures only false rejections, and no false acceptances were looked for. Which model the judge runs on is never said.
  • Small samples, single runs, no error bars. 100 questions from each public benchmark (PHYBench's 100 are the ones with public solutions, not a random draw), 49 to 116 kept per benchmark, one set of four attempts. The defect rates are wide: PHYBench's 13 of 100 is 7.8% to 21.0%, CritPt's 21 of 56 is 26.0% to 50.6%. Differences between models after correction (HLE-Physics: 78.66% against 75.65% on 116 questions) are within noise.
  • Scores and labels come from different runs. The attribution uses a dedicated audit run; Table 1 uses separate runs. On UGPhysics the pre-audit run of GPT-5.6-Sol had 332 correct attempts out of 400, more than the 328 attempts the 82 kept questions allow, so it solved some questions later excluded as defective.
  • Nothing is released: no code, no corrected questions, no labels. None of this can be re-run, which is why the error bars on this page come from the printed counts.
Verdict

Believe the main finding: on these six physics benchmarks most of what was scored as model failure is defective questions, wrong keys and brittle graders, at rates (13% to 60% of items) far above the frontier models' remaining error, and a second team found the same on CritPt. Read the corrected percentages as upper estimates on filtered subsets chosen by one model's failures, with nothing marked right ever checked; "near-saturated" is a claim about those subsets, not about physics reasoning in general, which the authors' own failed attempts at open problems underline.

What it takes to use this

  • There is nothing to download. No code, corrected questions or labels are released, and Anthropic's CritPT-Corrected is internal. To use a corrected score you would have to redo the audit.
  • Applying the method to your own benchmark takes the three labels, people who can re-derive answers in each subfield, and time: here 39 named data auditors (PhD students asked for about 30 reviews each, faculty or endorsed researchers for the hardest set), 446 first-round annotations plus 56 third reviews for 250 cases, and full re-solves for 56 CritPt challenges ({{Appendix F|ax:A6}}, {{Appendix G|ax:A7}}).
  • Do what the paper did not: also review a random sample of accepted answers (to measure false acceptances), fix the exclusion rule before seeing which model fails, and get two reviews for every case.
  • The cheap first step is the grader. On the two benchmarks with rule-based graders, most of the rise came from recognising equivalent answers (40 of 56 and 48 of 74 rejections); swapping exact-match or edit-distance grading for an equivalence-checking judge costs little. Fixing answer keys and ambiguous questions is the expensive part, and on the expert-written benchmarks it is most of the problem.
  • When it pays off: when the best models' measured error has stopped moving at a level that could be the defect rate. On 3 October 2026, the ten best CritPt scores on {{Artificial Analysis|https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/critpt}} sit between 108 and 113 correct attempts out of 350 (30.9% to 32.3%), from models released months apart, which is what a floor looks like.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

It is arguably the most useful evaluation result of its week, because it changes how every benchmark number should be read, not just these six. A score now carries a hidden term: the defect rate of the items it is computed on. Where that rate is unknown, the gap between a model and 100% is a mixture of model failure and benchmark failure, and the hard tail that everyone quotes is precisely where defects concentrate: the questions every model fails are the ones whose keys nobody could check (in this audit, the questions GPT-5.6-Sol failed on every attempt were 87.8% defective on HLE-Physics).

The caveats are the paper's scope. This is physics, six suites. Nothing here establishes the same defect rate in code, mathematics or agentic benchmarks, though the failure modes (key errors, ambiguity, grader bugs) are not physics-specific and there is no obvious reason they would be rarer elsewhere; SWE-bench's audits, cited above, show the same pattern in code. And "near-saturated" is a claim about these suites after correction, not about physics reasoning in general.

The authors' answer is not another leaderboard on the same pipeline but harder, new problems written by people who can be held to their answer keys, verified before release.

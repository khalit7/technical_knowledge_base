The question: do frontier models really struggle with physics?

[[S1|§1 Introduction]]

Physics benchmarks report that even frontier models fall far short of expert physicists. On {{CritPt|https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/critpt}}, a set of research-level challenges in the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, GPT-5.6-Sol at Max reasoning effort scores 32.3% (mean of 5 attempts); on the physics part of Humanity's Last Exam (HLE-Physics) it scores 47.3% (mean of 4 attempts, High effort) ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Because physics combines modelling, mathematics and computation, scores like these are read as limits that extend to other quantitative work in finance and technology.

The authors, mostly physicists at Yale, noticed that these numbers do not match their experience. Models and agents built on them have contributed proofs to open problems in mathematics, and physicists report strong capabilities in practice (Matthew Schwartz's {{"vibe physics"|https://www.anthropic.com/research/vibe-physics}} project with Claude is one of the cited cases). That does not rule out a real gap, since physics needs modelling and choosing assumptions on top of mathematics. So they asked the question directly, and answered it by having experts re-check the benchmarks themselves rather than the models ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

Six benchmarks, in two groups

[[S2.SS1.SSS1|§2.1.1 Benchmarks]]

All six are closed-ended: each problem has a definite final answer, supplied by the benchmark as a reference solution, and an answer counts as correct in any equivalent mathematical form. Only text-only problems were used. The benchmarks split by where the questions come from ({{§2.1.1|ax:S2.SS1.SSS1}}):

Sizes and selections from {{Appendix B|ax:A2}}. The paper's analysis leans on the second group, whose lower presumed contamination risk makes a score a more informative signal ({{§3|ax:S3}}).

Three models were tested: GPT-5.6-Sol and Claude Fable 5 at High reasoning effort, and Gemini 3.1 Pro at High. On the three expert-authored benchmarks GPT-5.6-Sol ran inside Codex and Fable 5 inside Claude Code, with tools; Gemini used no tools anywhere. On CritPt GPT-5.6-Sol used Max effort ({{Appendix A|ax:A1}}). Each was first scored with the original materials and, where one exists, the benchmark's own grader: the pre-audit score, reported as mean@4 (average accuracy over four attempts) except on CritPt, where the pre-audit numbers are Artificial Analysis's mean@5 ({{§2.1.2|ax:S2.SS1.SSS2}}).

The audit: three ways an answer can be marked wrong

[[S2.SS2|§2.2 Error Attribution]]

The core of the method is to give every apparent model error one of three labels ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}):

  • Model error The problem is well posed, the reference solution is right, and the model's answer is wrong. Only these are genuine failures.
  • Grader error The problem is well posed, the reference is right, the model is right, and the evaluator marks it wrong. Mostly rule-based graders that miss an equivalent form or a different convention.
  • Benchmark error The problem statement or the reference is defective: a wrong reference, inconsistent conditions, ambiguity, or a missing assumption that is needed for the intended answer and cannot be inferred. A benchmark error takes precedence when the materials are defective.

Who checked what differs between the two groups, and that difference matters for how far the numbers can be trusted:

HLE-Physics, PHYBench, PRISM-Physics, UGPhysics: only rejections

A separate audit run of GPT-5.6-Sol at High effort collected the questions it failed: on HLE-Physics up to five attempts (tools on the fifth), on PHYBench up to five without tools, stopping at the first accepted answer; one attempt on the other two. Only questions rejected on every attempt were sent for review: 250 of 502 ({{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}, {{Appendix B.3|ax:A2.SS3}}). Physics PhD students reviewed the problem, the reference, the model's response and an AI-generated preliminary review; 196 cases got two reviews, 54 only one, and the 56 disagreements went to a third reviewer ({{Appendix F.2|ax:A6.SS2}}). Questions labelled benchmark errors were excluded.

CMT-Benchmark and CritPt: every question

Here no per-question grades existed (CMT has no public grader; Artificial Analysis reports only aggregates), so experts reviewed every question regardless of how the model did: all 50 of CMT and a 56-challenge subset of CritPt. One reviewer per problem, a faculty member or a researcher endorsed by one, who first outlined their own approach before reading the reference, then checked statement, reference and numbers, and repaired the problem where a defensible repair existed (for example adding a missing boundary condition or convention) or excluded it. For CritPt the reviewers solved every problem themselves, since the official references are private ({{Appendix F.1|ax:A6.SS1}}, {{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}).

Every corrected ("validated/repaired") score then comes from one common pipeline adapted from HLE: its system prompt for generating answers and its LLM-judge prompt for grading, at High effort. Of all the evaluators audited, this judge had the lowest grader-error share, 4.08% (4 of HLE-Physics's 98 audited rejections) ({{§3|ax:S3}}).

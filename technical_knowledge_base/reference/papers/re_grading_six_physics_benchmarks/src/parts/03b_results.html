What the auditors found, case by case

[[A4|Appendix D, Figures 3 to 6]]

The paper prints eleven worked cases. They are the clearest way to see what each label means: pick one below. Final answers only, as in the paper; derivations are omitted and the typography is normalised.

The grader errors share one pattern: a correct answer written differently. PHYBench's EED grader gives 0 when the factors come in a different order or the denominator is rationalised; PRISM-Physics's grader rejected a diver's answer whose four parts were identical to the key's up to notation. The benchmark errors are of four kinds: references that are simply wrong (an arithmetic slip, an expression that always equals zero), questions without the data needed to answer them, wording that admits two readings, and unstated conventions that change the answer by a constant factor ({{Appendix D|ax:A4}}).

One repair, in full: CritPt challenge 44 ({{Figure 4|ax:S3.F4}})

"Consider the Kitaev honeycomb model at the isotropic limit (Jx = Jy = Jz = 1) on a 3x2 Bravais lattice with periodic boundary conditions. How many degenerate ground states are there? How many of them are in the flux-free sector? Compute the energy of the ground states with three decimal precision." The Hamiltonian is never written, so J = 1 could multiply Pauli matrices σαiσαj or spin-½ operators SαiSαj with S = σ/2, and the two give ground-state energies a factor of four apart. The repair adds one line: H = −Σx bonds σxσx − Σy bonds σyσy − Σz bonds σzσz, with σ the standard 2x2 Pauli matrices.

The defect floor: why broken items matter more as models get better

[[S5|§5 Discussion, §4]]

The paper's explanation is simple arithmetic ({{§5|ax:S5}}). Every benchmark has some fraction of defective items: wrong references, ambiguous questions, grading mistakes. A model is marked wrong on those whatever it does, so no model's measured error can fall below the defect rate. While a model still gets many valid items wrong, the defects add only a little to its error. Once its true error drops below the defect rate, most of the errors on its scorecard belong to the benchmark. The same holds for a grader that rejects correct answers: when models are weak they produce few correct answers to reject, so the measured score stays close to the true one; as they improve, the grader's mistakes pile up ({{§4|ax:S4}}).

Try: start from the HLE-Physics preset, then drag the true model error down to zero: the measured error stops at the floor, and nearly every error left is the benchmark's. Switch to PRISM-Physics to see a grader-driven floor.

The audit supports it quantitatively: the raw rejection rates overstate the model's share by roughly an order of magnitude on HLE-Physics (98 rejections, 8 model errors: 12x) and PHYBench (56 against 3: 19x), and on PRISM-Physics and UGPhysics the model's component is 0 and 1 question ({{§5|ax:S5}}). The authors' summary: "Frontier models are not failing these physics problems. The benchmarks are failing to pose them."

This inverts the usual reading of a benchmark gap. The standard inference from "the model scored 62%" is that the remaining 38% measures something the model cannot do; here a large part of that residual measures something the benchmark got wrong. Anyone who owns an evaluation now has to treat the residual as a mixture of model failure and item defect, and the two cannot be separated without expert re-grading. Precision suffers too: Bowman and Dahl, cited in {{§4|ax:S4}}, point out that going from 98% to 98.1% removes the same share of the error as going from 80% to 81% but needs roughly an order of magnitude more items to detect (with the usual two-proportion formula, about 38,200 items against 3,100: 12.2 times).

What the authors conclude ({{§5|ax:S5}}). Frontier models can now solve almost any closed-ended problem of the kind used in physics problem sets, and small fixes (adding missing context, allowing more attempts) would repair most reported failures. That does not mean they can do physics research: the authors pointed GPT-based agent harnesses, of the kind used on open mathematics conjectures, at several open problems in theoretical physics, found far less progress than on mathematics, and have not fully solved a single one. So the next benchmark has to be a new kind: new, hard physics tasks, adequately verified, which will need real money for curation, perhaps through non-profit organisations with a more elaborate process or through open competitions like those now used in mathematics. Not another leaderboard built on the same pipeline.

Not only physics

[[S4|§4 Related Work]]

The paper places itself in a line of audits ({{§4|ax:S4}}). {{Northcutt et al. (2021)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.14749}} found label errors throughout widely used test sets in vision, language and audio, and showed that correcting them can change model rankings. In software engineering, SWE-bench was built from 2,294 real GitHub issues with executable tests; expert review produced the smaller {{Verified subset|https://openai.com/index/introducing-swe-bench-verified/}}; a later audit found material problems in 59.4% of 138 Verified tasks, and a follow-up estimated about 30% of SWE-bench Pro tasks were broken ({{OpenAI|https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/}}, {{OpenAI|https://openai.com/index/separating-signal-from-noise-coding-evaluations/}}). Defects survived two rounds of curation in a domain where every task ships with a test; physics benchmarks are graded against written references with no such check, so there is no reason to expect them to be cleaner.

On graders, the paper's position is that rule-based checkers (exact match or symbolic) are reproducible but brittle, LLM judges more flexible but with their own biases (position, verbosity, weak accuracy on objective reasoning), and that both problems are separate from whether the reference is right, which is why it audits questions and grading separately. It also says judge problems are shrinking as stronger models are used as judges ({{§4|ax:S4}}).

Beyond the paper: an earlier audit of another slice of the same exam, {{FutureHouse (July 2025)|https://www.futurehouse.org/research-announcements/hle-exam}}, estimated that about 30% of Humanity's Last Exam's chemistry and biology answers are likely wrong. The paper does not cite it.

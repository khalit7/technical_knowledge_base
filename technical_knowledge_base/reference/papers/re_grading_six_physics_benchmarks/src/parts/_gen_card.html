How Good Are Frontier Models at Physics? Expert Re-Grading Reveals Broken Evaluations and Near-Saturation of Leading Benchmarks (Re-grading six physics benchmarks)

Ali Ansari, Haoran Sun, Andy Zeyi Liu, Mark Jabbour (core team), twelve physics advisors (Steven Girvin, A. Douglas Stone, David Poland and others), 32 further data auditors (39 auditors in all, some of them also advisors or core team), and the project advisors Lucas Baker, Arman Cohan and John Sous: 51 authors in all
Yale University (most auditors), Jump Trading Group, University of Cambridge, University of Southern California; supported by Yale's Office of the Provost AI Initiatives and a gift from Jump Trading Group (correspondence: John Sous, Yale) · submitted 11 September 2026, announced 14 September 2026 (arXiv v1, the only version so far)

In one line: Physicists re-checked six physics benchmarks: of 250 answers GPT-5.6-Sol had been marked wrong on four of them, only 12 were real model mistakes (143 were defective questions or answer keys, 95 graders rejecting correct answers), and 13% to 60% of each benchmark's items were found defective. On the filtered or repaired question sets its scores rise to 78.7% to 94.6% (mean@4), upper estimates since no answer marked right was ever checked; the authors call for new, hard, verified physics tasks rather than another leaderboard on the same pipeline.

How far to trust it: Believe the direction: of 250 rejected answers that physicists re-checked, 238 were the benchmark's or the grader's fault, and the defect rates (13% to 60% of items) are far above any frontier model's error rate. Treat the corrected percentages as upper estimates: only answers marked wrong were audited, the questions removed were picked by one model's failures, nothing marked right was ever checked, and no code or corrected questions are released.

The question: do frontier models really struggle with physics?

§1 Introduction

Physics benchmarks report that even frontier models fall far short of expert physicists. On CritPt, a set of research-level challenges in the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, GPT-5.6-Sol at Max reasoning effort scores 32.3% (mean of 5 attempts); on the physics part of Humanity's Last Exam (HLE-Physics) it scores 47.3% (mean of 4 attempts, High effort) (§1). Because physics combines modelling, mathematics and computation, scores like these are read as limits that extend to other quantitative work in finance and technology.

The authors, mostly physicists at Yale, noticed that these numbers do not match their experience. Models and agents built on them have contributed proofs to open problems in mathematics, and physicists report strong capabilities in practice (Matthew Schwartz's "vibe physics" project with Claude is one of the cited cases). That does not rule out a real gap, since physics needs modelling and choosing assumptions on top of mathematics. So they asked the question directly, and answered it by having experts re-check the benchmarks themselves rather than the models (§1).

Six benchmarks, in two groups

§2.1.1 Benchmarks

All six are closed-ended: each problem has a definite final answer, supplied by the benchmark as a reference solution, and an answer counts as correct in any equivalent mathematical form. Only text-only problems were used. The benchmarks split by where the questions come from (§2.1.1):

Three models were tested: GPT-5.6-Sol and Claude Fable 5 at High reasoning effort, and Gemini 3.1 Pro at High. On the three expert-authored benchmarks GPT-5.6-Sol ran inside Codex and Fable 5 inside Claude Code, with tools; Gemini used no tools anywhere. On CritPt GPT-5.6-Sol used Max effort (Appendix A). Each was first scored with the original materials and, where one exists, the benchmark's own grader: the pre-audit score, reported as mean@4 (average accuracy over four attempts) except on CritPt, where the pre-audit numbers are Artificial Analysis's mean@5 (§2.1.2).

The audit: three ways an answer can be marked wrong

§2.2 Error Attribution

The core of the method is to give every apparent model error one of three labels (§2.2):

  • Model error The problem is well posed, the reference solution is right, and the model's answer is wrong. Only these are genuine failures.
  • Grader error The problem is well posed, the reference is right, the model is right, and the evaluator marks it wrong. Mostly rule-based graders that miss an equivalent form or a different convention.
  • Benchmark error The problem statement or the reference is defective: a wrong reference, inconsistent conditions, ambiguity, or a missing assumption that is needed for the intended answer and cannot be inferred. A benchmark error takes precedence when the materials are defective.

Who checked what differs between the two groups, and that difference matters for how far the numbers can be trusted:

HLE-Physics, PHYBench, PRISM-Physics, UGPhysics: only rejections

A separate audit run of GPT-5.6-Sol at High effort collected the questions it failed: on HLE-Physics up to five attempts (tools on the fifth), on PHYBench up to five without tools, stopping at the first accepted answer; one attempt on the other two. Only questions rejected on every attempt were sent for review: 250 of 502 (§2.3, Appendix B.3). Physics PhD students reviewed the problem, the reference, the model's response and an AI-generated preliminary review; 196 cases got two reviews, 54 only one, and the 56 disagreements went to a third reviewer (Appendix F.2). Questions labelled benchmark errors were excluded.

CMT-Benchmark and CritPt: every question

Here no per-question grades existed (CMT has no public grader; Artificial Analysis reports only aggregates), so experts reviewed every question regardless of how the model did: all 50 of CMT and a 56-challenge subset of CritPt. One reviewer per problem, a faculty member or a researcher endorsed by one, who first outlined their own approach before reading the reference, then checked statement, reference and numbers, and repaired the problem where a defensible repair existed (for example adding a missing boundary condition or convention) or excluded it. For CritPt the reviewers solved every problem themselves, since the official references are private (Appendix F.1, §2.4).

Every corrected ("validated/repaired") score then comes from one common pipeline adapted from HLE: its system prompt for generating answers and its LLM-judge prompt for grading, at High effort. Of all the evaluators audited, this judge had the lowest grader-error share, 4.08% (4 of HLE-Physics's 98 audited rejections) (§3). The Follow the audit tab animates this whole procedure for each benchmark with the paper's own counts.

What the auditors found, case by case

Appendix D, Figures 3 to 6

The paper prints eleven worked cases. They are the clearest way to see what each label means: pick one below. Final answers only, as in the paper; derivations are omitted and the typography is normalised.

The grader errors share one pattern: a correct answer written differently. PHYBench's EED grader gives 0 when the factors come in a different order or the denominator is rationalised; PRISM-Physics's grader rejected a diver's answer whose four parts were identical to the key's up to notation. The benchmark errors are of four kinds: references that are simply wrong (an arithmetic slip, an expression that always equals zero), questions without the data needed to answer them, wording that admits two readings, and unstated conventions that change the answer by a constant factor (Appendix D).

"Consider the Kitaev honeycomb model at the isotropic limit (Jx = Jy = Jz = 1) on a 3x2 Bravais lattice with periodic boundary conditions. How many degenerate ground states are there? The defect floor: why broken items matter more as models get better

§5 Discussion, §4

The paper's explanation is simple arithmetic (§5). Every benchmark has some fraction of defective items: wrong references, ambiguous questions, grading mistakes. A model is marked wrong on those whatever it does, so no model's measured error can fall below the defect rate. While a model still gets many valid items wrong, the defects add only a little to its error. Once its true error drops below the defect rate, most of the errors on its scorecard belong to the benchmark. The same holds for a grader that rejects correct answers: when models are weak they produce few correct answers to reject, so the measured score stays close to the true one; as they improve, the grader's mistakes pile up (§4).

Try: start from the HLE-Physics preset, then drag the true model error down to zero: the measured error stops at the floor, and nearly every error left is the benchmark's. Switch to PRISM-Physics to see a grader-driven floor.

The audit supports it quantitatively: the raw rejection rates overstate the model's share by roughly an order of magnitude on HLE-Physics (98 rejections, 8 model errors: 12x) and PHYBench (56 against 3: 19x), and on PRISM-Physics and UGPhysics the model's component is 0 and 1 question (§5). The authors' summary: "Frontier models are not failing these physics problems. The benchmarks are failing to pose them."

This inverts the usual reading of a benchmark gap. The standard inference from "the model scored 62%" is that the remaining 38% measures something the model cannot do; here a large part of that residual measures something the benchmark got wrong. Anyone who owns an evaluation now has to treat the residual as a mixture of model failure and item defect, and the two cannot be separated without expert re-grading. Precision suffers too: Bowman and Dahl, cited in §4, point out that going from 98% to 98.1% removes the same share of the error as going from 80% to 81% but needs roughly an order of magnitude more items to detect (with the usual two-proportion formula, about 38,200 items against 3,100: 12.2 times).

What the authors conclude (§5). Frontier models can now solve almost any closed-ended problem of the kind used in physics problem sets, and small fixes (adding missing context, allowing more attempts) would repair most reported failures. That does not mean they can do physics research: the authors pointed GPT-based agent harnesses, of the kind used on open mathematics conjectures, at several open problems in theoretical physics, found far less progress than on mathematics, and have not fully solved a single one. So the next benchmark has to be a new kind: new, hard physics tasks, adequately verified, which will need real money for curation, perhaps through non-profit organisations with a more elaborate process or through open competitions like those now used in mathematics. Not another leaderboard built on the same pipeline.

Not only physics

§4 Related Work

The paper places itself in a line of audits (§4). Northcutt et al. (2021) found label errors throughout widely used test sets in vision, language and audio, and showed that correcting them can change model rankings. In software engineering, SWE-bench was built from 2,294 real GitHub issues with executable tests; expert review produced the smaller Verified subset; a later audit found material problems in 59.4% of 138 Verified tasks, and a follow-up estimated about 30% of SWE-bench Pro tasks were broken (OpenAI, OpenAI). Defects survived two rounds of curation in a domain where every task ships with a test; physics benchmarks are graded against written references with no such check, so there is no reason to expect them to be cleaner.

On graders, the paper's position is that rule-based checkers (exact match or symbolic) are reproducible but brittle, LLM judges more flexible but with their own biases (position, verbosity, weak accuracy on objective reasoning), and that both problems are separate from whether the reference is right, which is why it audits questions and grading separately. It also says judge problems are shrinking as stronger models are used as judges (§4).

Beyond the paper: an earlier audit of another slice of the same exam, FutureHouse (July 2025), estimated that about 30% of Humanity's Last Exam's chemistry and biology answers are likely wrong. The paper does not cite it.

How much of this to believe

Appendices B, C, F
On four of the benchmarks the experts reviewed only answers that had been marked wrong. Which mistakes can an audit like that never find?

The HLE-Physics preset of the audit simulator, with the paper's counts and two things the paper could not measure set to small values: the judge accepts 3% of wrong answers, and 5% of defective questions happen to be accepted anyway (the model picked the reading the key intended). Both are illustrative; the paper's protocol counts every one of them as correct and keeps them.

What holds up.

  • The direction is robust. 238 of 250 audited rejections were not the model's fault. Even if every one of the 22 disputed pairs that involved "model error" (19 benchmark/model, 3 grader/model, Table 4) had been resolved as a model error, model errors would be 34 of 250, 13.6% (95% interval 9.9% to 18.4%): still a small minority.
  • The rises dwarf sampling noise. GPT-5.6-Sol's HLE-Physics pass@4 goes from 113 of 202 questions (95% interval 49.0% to 62.6%) to 106 of 116 (84.9% to 95.3%); the intervals are nowhere near each other.
  • CMT-Benchmark is close to like for like. Every question was reviewed regardless of the model's answer, the same HLE-adapted judge graded before and after, and 49 of 50 questions stayed (29 of them repaired): the 61.00% to 87.24% rise comes from fixing the materials (Appendix B.2.2).
  • A second team found the same on CritPt. Anthropic's experts revised 31 of 71 statements (43.7%), this audit found defects in 21 of 56 (37.5%), and both corrected scores land in the high 80s.
  • The numbers are internally consistent. All 70 percentages in Table 1 are whole numbers of attempts or questions, and every count in the appendices adds up (27 checks in the Tables tab). The pre-audit CritPt score matches Artificial Analysis's leaderboard to the attempt (113 of 350).
  • The authors state several limits themselves, which counts in their favour: pre-audit and corrected scores are not on the same questions; the three public-source benchmarks may be contaminated; Table 2 "is not a reconstruction" of Table 1; and the Anthropic comparison is not like for like.

What does not, or not yet.

  • The audit is one-sided. On HLE-Physics, PHYBench, PRISM-Physics and UGPhysics, only rejected answers were reviewed; the 252 accepted answers never were (Appendix C). So a lenient judge that accepts a wrong answer, or a defective question the model happened to get "right", is invisible and stays in the corrected set as a success. Every bias this leaves points upward. The audit simulator shows how large it can get.
  • One model chose which questions were removed. The excluded questions are those GPT-5.6-Sol failed on every attempt of its audit run and that experts then judged defective. Fable 5 and Gemini 3.1 Pro are re-scored on that GPT-filtered set. And for GPT-5.6-Sol itself the corrected score is largely fixed by the audit: on HLE-Physics, 104 accepted plus 4 grader errors out of 116 kept already gives 93.1%, against the 91.38% pass@4 reported (the same holds within 7 points on all four; Tables tab).
  • On HLE-Physics the rise is mostly the denominator. Removing the 86 excluded questions and nothing else would lift GPT-5.6-Sol from 47.28% to 82.3% if those questions had scored zero before, more than the 78.66% reported. That is the paper's point (they are defective), but it means the HLE-Physics number measures accuracy on the 57% of questions that survived one model's failures, not on the exam. On PHYBench and PRISM-Physics, by contrast, removal alone gives only 30.5% and 17.6%: there the rise really is the grader being fixed (Tables tab).
  • CritPt's before and after differ in almost everything. Pre-audit: Artificial Analysis's harness and grader, official references, 70 challenges, mean@5, Fable 5 at Max. Corrected: the HLE-adapted judge, references re-derived by the auditors, 54 challenges, mean@4, Codex and Claude Code with tools, Fable 5 at High. The 32.3% to 87.5% jump mixes all of these; Gemini 3.1 Pro, without tools on both sides, is the cleanest pair (17.71% to 54.63%). Why 56 of the 70 challenges were audited is not explained.
  • The reviews are noisy. 54 of the 250 cases had a single reviewer. Of the 196 reviewed twice, the first two reviewers agreed on 71.4%; because one label dominates each benchmark, much of that is chance, and agreement beyond chance (Scott's pi, bounded from Tables 2 to 4 because the reviewers' own label shares are not printed) is at most 0.36 on HLE-Physics and 0.28 on PRISM-Physics, "fair" at best. The 19 benchmark/model disagreements are exactly the decisive kind. Reviewers were also shown an AI-generated preliminary review, of unstated origin, which can anchor a label (Appendix F.2).
  • On CMT and CritPt, the person who repaired a problem also wrote its new reference. One reviewer per problem, AI assistance allowed (Appendix F.1). Whether repairs were written before or after seeing the model's answers is not stated.
  • The corrected judge is unnamed and its error rate is half measured. The "4.08%" is 4 grader errors among HLE-Physics's 98 rejections; it measures only false rejections, and no false acceptances were looked for. Which model the judge runs on is never said.
  • Small samples, single runs, no error bars. 100 questions from each public benchmark (PHYBench's 100 are the ones with public solutions, not a random draw), 49 to 116 kept per benchmark, one set of four attempts. The defect rates are wide: PHYBench's 13 of 100 is 7.8% to 21.0%, CritPt's 21 of 56 is 26.0% to 50.6%. Differences between models after correction (HLE-Physics: 78.66% against 75.65% on 116 questions) are within noise.
  • Scores and labels come from different runs. The attribution uses a dedicated audit run; Table 1 uses separate runs. On UGPhysics the pre-audit run of GPT-5.6-Sol had 332 correct attempts out of 400, more than the 328 attempts the 82 kept questions allow, so it solved some questions later excluded as defective.
  • Nothing is released: no code, no corrected questions, no labels. None of this can be re-run, which is why the error bars on this page come from the printed counts.
Verdict

Believe the main finding: on these six physics benchmarks most of what was scored as model failure is defective questions, wrong keys and brittle graders, at rates (13% to 60% of items) far above the frontier models' remaining error, and a second team found the same on CritPt. Read the corrected percentages as upper estimates on filtered subsets chosen by one model's failures, with nothing marked right ever checked; "near-saturated" is a claim about those subsets, not about physics reasoning in general, which the authors' own failed attempts at open problems underline.

What it takes to use this

  • There is nothing to download. No code, corrected questions or labels are released, and Anthropic's CritPT-Corrected is internal. To use a corrected score you would have to redo the audit.
  • Applying the method to your own benchmark takes the three labels, people who can re-derive answers in each subfield, and time: here 39 named data auditors (PhD students asked for about 30 reviews each, faculty or endorsed researchers for the hardest set), 446 first-round annotations plus 56 third reviews for 250 cases, and full re-solves for 56 CritPt challenges (Appendix F, Appendix G).
  • Do what the paper did not: also review a random sample of accepted answers (to measure false acceptances), fix the exclusion rule before seeing which model fails, and get two reviews for every case.
  • The cheap first step is the grader. On the two benchmarks with rule-based graders, most of the rise came from recognising equivalent answers (40 of 56 and 48 of 74 rejections); swapping exact-match or edit-distance grading for an equivalence-checking judge costs little. Fixing answer keys and ambiguous questions is the expensive part, and on the expert-written benchmarks it is most of the problem.
  • When it pays off: when the best models' measured error has stopped moving at a level that could be the defect rate. On 3 October 2026, the ten best CritPt scores on Artificial Analysis sit between 108 and 113 correct attempts out of 350 (30.9% to 32.3%), from models released months apart, which is what a floor looks like.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

It is arguably the most useful evaluation result of its week, because it changes how every benchmark number should be read, not just these six. A score now carries a hidden term: the defect rate of the items it is computed on. Where that rate is unknown, the gap between a model and 100% is a mixture of model failure and benchmark failure, and the hard tail that everyone quotes is precisely where defects concentrate: the questions every model fails are the ones whose keys nobody could check (in this audit, the questions GPT-5.6-Sol failed on every attempt were 87.8% defective on HLE-Physics).

The caveats are the paper's scope. This is physics, six suites. Nothing here establishes the same defect rate in code, mathematics or agentic benchmarks, though the failure modes (key errors, ambiguity, grader bugs) are not physics-specific and there is no obvious reason they would be rarer elsewhere; SWE-bench's audits, cited above, show the same pattern in code. And "near-saturated" is a claim about these suites after correction, not about physics reasoning in general.

The authors' answer is not another leaderboard on the same pipeline but harder, new problems written by people who can be held to their answer keys, verified before release.

What the protocol can and cannot see

The animation shows what the paper counted. This simulator asks what it could not count. It builds a benchmark from a few rates, runs the paper's protocol on it (audit only the rejections, exclude benchmark errors, count grader errors as correct, re-grade the rest) and compares the result with the model's true accuracy on the well-posed questions. Each preset sets the first three rates from that benchmark's audit counts, with the other three at zero; at those settings the protocol is exact by construction and reproduces the audit's counts. The last three are what the paper never measured.

Try: on the HLE-Physics preset, raise "judge accepts a wrong answer" to 5%: the protocol's corrected score rises while the true accuracy does not move. Then raise "reviewers call a model error a defect": the excluded set swallows real failures.

The simulator uses expected counts, not random draws, and one attempt per question, like the paper's PRISM-Physics and UGPhysics audit runs. Formulas: with d defective, accuracy a, false rejection g, false acceptance f, defective-accepted c and mislabel e, the share accepted is (1 − d)(a(1 − g) + (1 − a)f) + dc; the protocol keeps everything except the rejections labelled defective, d(1 − c) + e(1 − d)(1 − a)(1 − f), and scores the kept accepted answers plus the grader errors as correct.

For GPT-5.6-Sol, the audit already fixes the corrected score

The audit run kept every question GPT-5.6-Sol answered acceptably at least once, and the experts turned its grader errors into correct answers. If the re-run reproduced the audit run, its pass rate on the kept questions would be (accepted + grader errors) / kept. The re-run lands a few points below that on every benchmark, as fresh sampling would: the corrected GPT-5.6-Sol scores are largely by construction.

Where the gain comes from: removal or re-grading

Drop the excluded questions and change nothing else: if they had all scored zero before, the pre-audit correct attempts divided by the kept questions' attempts is the score removal alone would give. It is an upper bound for removal's effect (any excluded question that scored above zero lowers it); above 100% it shows the assumption fails. Compare it with the reported corrected score.

Scott's pi corrects raw agreement for the agreement two reviewers would reach by chance given how often each label is used.

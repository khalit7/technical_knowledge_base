Problem

[[S1|§1 Introduction, §3 intro]]

By 2019 pretrain-then-fine-tune clearly worked, but the field had fragmented into variations nobody could compare. Papers differed at once in pretraining objective (BERT's masked language modelling, GPT's left-to-right language modelling, XLNet's permutation objective), architecture (encoder-only, decoder-only, prefix LM), corpus (Wikipedia plus BooksCorpus, WebText, CC-News), fine-tuning scheme and evaluation protocol, so no gain could be attributed to any one axis ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

The goal is explicitly not a new method ("our goal is not to propose new methods but instead to provide a comprehensive perspective on where the field stands", {{§1|ax:S1}}): make every one of those choices a swappable component of one fixed pipeline, move one at a time against a fixed baseline, and run the grid at a scale where the answers mean something.

The authors call it "coordinate ascent" and name its weakness: it can miss interactions, such as an objective that only wins on a bigger model ({{§3|ax:S3}}). A secondary problem fixed along the way: there was no standard large clean pretraining corpus, because data sets usually came out as a side effect of a model release and often were never published ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}). The answer is C4.

Idea: every task is text in, text out

[[S2.SS4|§2.4, Figure 1, Appendix D]]

Every task feeds the model text and trains it to produce text, with plain maximum likelihood (teacher forcing, cross-entropy) and greedy decoding ({{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}, {{§3.1.2|ax:S3.SS1.SSS2}}). A short task prefix selects the task: translate English to German: That is good. produces Das ist gut. Classification emits the label as a word, so MNLI outputs the literal string entailment; an output matching no label counts as wrong, which "we never observed". Prefix wording mattered little and was not studied.

STS-B, a regression task scoring similarity from 1 to 5, is rounded to the nearest 0.2 and written as a string ("2.57" becomes "2.6"), which "effectively recasts the STS-B regression problem as a 21-class classification problem". Try it:

The Winograd tasks (WNLI, WSC, DPR) mark the ambiguous pronoun with asterisks and the model generates the noun phrase it refers to: "The city councilmen refused the demonstrators a permit because *they* feared violence." becomes "The city councilmen". WNLI overlaps WSC's training set, so it is never trained on and is left out of validation GLUE averages ({{§2.4|ax:S2.SS4}}, {{Appendix B|ax:A2}}).

The payoff: one model, one loss, one decoding procedure and one hyperparameter set cover translation, summarisation, question answering and classification, which is the only reason the ablations that follow are comparable.

The baseline everything is measured against

[[S3.SS1|§2.1, §3.1, Table 1]]

A standard encoder-decoder Transformer, encoder and decoder each sized like BERT-Base: 12 blocks, dmodel 768, dff 3072 (ReLU), 12 heads, dropout 0.1. "About 220 million parameters", roughly twice BERT-Base because there are two stacks; the configuration counts to 222.9 million, exactly the released checkpoint ({{recount|tab:t-tables:tbP}}). Three departures from Vaswani et al. 2017 outlived the paper ({{§2.1|ax:S2.SS1}}):

  • Rescale-only layer norm: no additive bias and no mean subtraction ("No bias and no subtraction of mean", the {{Hugging Face port|https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/blob/main/src/transformers/models/t5/modeling_t5.py}}), the computation later standardised as RMSNorm.
  • Outside the residual path: the norm is applied to each sub-layer's input and the residual adds the un-normalised input back (pre-norm, not the original post-norm).
  • A relative position bias in its cheapest form: one learned scalar added to each attention logit, chosen by the key-query offset from 32 buckets whose ranges grow logarithmically up to an offset of 128, shared across layers, different per head. A layer is blind beyond 128; stacked layers can combine local information.

Computed with T5's own bucketing function (Hugging Face's _relative_position_bucket, from Mesh TensorFlow; in src/inputs/t5_code_extracts.py). The bidirectional encoder splits the 32 into 16 per direction (offsets 0 to 7 exact, then wider bins); the decoder looks back only and spends all 32 on the past.

Training ({{§3.1.2|ax:S3.SS1.SSS2}}, {{§3.1.3|ax:S3.SS1.SSS3}}): Adafactor (Adam's second-moment matrix factorised into row and column vectors, to save memory). Pretraining is 219 steps of 128 sequences of 512 tokens packed to about 216 tokens, so 235 ≈ 34B tokens with no repeats: about a quarter of BERT's 137B and a sixty-fourth of RoBERTa's 2.2T (137B / 235 = 4.0, 2.2T / 235 = 64.0), so dozens of variants are affordable. The learning rate is 1/√max(n, 104): 0.01 for 104 steps, then an inverse square root decay (which the paper calls "exponential"). Fine-tuning is 218 steps per task at a constant 0.001, best validation checkpoint (every 5,000 steps). SentencePiece, 32,000 wordpieces shared by input and output, trained on English C4 mixed 10:1:1:1 with German, French and Romanian so translation targets are representable at all.

Noise floor ({{§3.1.5|ax:S3.SS1.SSS5}}, {{Table 1|ax:S3.T1}}): the baseline was trained 10 times; its spread (GLUE standard deviation 0.235, SuperGLUE 0.416, but 3.2 on CB's F1, whose validation set has 56 examples) is assumed for every variant, and every table bolds scores within two standard deviations of the best. Without pretraining the model scores 66.22 GLUE and 53.04 SuperGLUE against 83.28 and 71.36; only English to French, a big data set, barely moves.

Span corruption, the baseline objective

[[S3.SS1.SSS4|§3.1.4, Figure 2]]

Sample 15% of tokens; replace each consecutive run of corrupted tokens with one sentinel unique within the example; make the target only the dropped spans, each after its sentinel, closed by a final sentinel. Unlike BERT-style masking the target is the missing text, not the whole sequence, so decoder sequences stay short, a choice made "to reduce the computational cost of pre-training".

The baseline decides token by token, so spans happen by chance; the final recipe corrupts contiguous spans of mean length 3, which shortens sequences further ({{§3.3.4|ax:S3.SS3.SSS4}}). {{Corrupt a sentence|tab:t-run}} runs every objective on your own text with the T5 code's own masking function.

Architectures: which stack, which mask

[[S3.SS2|§3.2, Figures 3 and 4, Table 2]]

The variants differ mainly in their attention masks ({{Figure 3|ax:S3.F3}}): fully visible (every position sees the whole input) or causal (position i sees only up to i). An encoder-decoder has a fully visible encoder, a causal decoder and cross-attention; a language model is one causal stack over input and target concatenated; a prefix LM is that stack made fully visible over the input, "similar to an encoder-decoder model with parameters shared across the encoder and decoder" ({{§3.2.1|ax:S3.SS2.SSS1}}).

The paper's example: 7 input words, 3 target words.

Cell (i, j) is filled when position i may attend to j, coloured by whose weights do it. P is one BERT-Base-sized stack, M the FLOPs of the L + L encoder-decoder per example ({{§3.2.2|ax:S3.SS2.SSS2}}); scores are Table 2's denoising rows. Pauses off screen; starts paused under reduced motion.

Making them comparable ({{§3.2.2|ax:S3.SS2.SSS2}}): an L + L encoder-decoder has the parameters of a 2L-layer language model but the cost of an L-layer one, since each of its stacks sees only half the sequence (step times matched). Cross-attention is set aside as "about 10%" of the parameters (12.7% at the baseline size, {{recount|tab:t-tables:tbP}}). Five structures: encoder-decoder (2P, M), the same with shared parameters (P, M), half depth (P, M/2), language model (P, M) and prefix LM (P, M), each with the denoising and a language-modelling objective.

four variants have half the encoder-decoder's parameters. Which comes closest to it on GLUE (denoising; encoder-decoder 83.28)?

From {{Table 2|ax:S3.T2}}. Sharing the weights costs almost nothing (82.81) while halving the parameters; halving the depth hurts (80.88); the prefix LM lands below the shared encoder-decoder (81.82), "suggesting that the addition of an explicit encoder-decoder attention is beneficial"; the causal language model is far behind (74.70 GLUE, 61.14 SQuAD). Denoising beat language modelling for every architecture.

So the encoder-decoder with denoising wins on every task, at matched compute and twice the parameters. {{Why it matters|tab:t-read:s-why}} says why the field went decoder-only anyway.

Objectives: denoising wins, then nothing matters much

[[S3.SS3|§3.3, Figure 5, Tables 3 to 7]]

A funnel ({{Figure 5|ax:S3.F5}}), each step in {{the grid|tab:t-tables}}:

  • Three approaches ({{Table 4|ax:S3.T4}}): BERT-style (corrupt 15%, 90% to a mask token and 10% to a random token, reconstruct everything) 82.96 GLUE, prefix language modelling 80.69, deshuffling 73.17. Denoising beats language modelling beats deshuffling, and that is the only large gap.
  • Simplifying BERT-style ({{Table 5|ax:S3.T5}}): MASS-style masking, replacing spans with sentinels and dropping corrupted tokens all perform about the same.
  • Corruption rate ({{Table 6|ax:S3.T6}}): 10%, 15% and 25% are indistinguishable; 50% hurts.
  • Span length ({{Table 7|ax:S3.T7}}): mean spans of 2, 3 and 5 are indistinguishable, 10 slightly worse; 3 "slightly (but significantly)" beats i.i.d. corruption on most non-translation tasks.

The authors' conclusion ({{§3.3.5|ax:S3.SS3.SSS5}}): choose among denoising variants on cost, not quality, and further tinkering here is unlikely to pay. That cost is mostly the target length: per 512 tokens of text, 512 for BERT-style, about 144 for the i.i.d. baseline, exactly 104 for spans of mean 3 ({{measured live|tab:t-run:runLen}}).

Data: C4, and why filtering beats volume

[[S2.SS2|§2.2, §3.4, Tables 8 and 9]]

C4 is about 750 GB of English (745 GB in {{Table 8|ax:S3.T8}}) from one month of Common Crawl (April 2019), cleaned with blunt heuristics ({{§2.2|ax:S2.SS2}}): keep only lines ending in terminal punctuation and with at least 5 words; drop pages with fewer than 3 sentences; drop pages with a word from the "List of Dirty, Naughty, Obscene or Otherwise Bad Words", with "lorem ipsum", or with a curly bracket (a cheap code detector); drop lines mentioning Javascript or policy boilerplate ("terms of use", "cookie policy" and similar); strip Wikipedia citation markers; keep one copy of any repeated three-sentence span; keep pages langdetect calls English with probability at least 0.99.

without those filters (language detection kept) the corpus is 6.1 TB, 8.2 times larger. Pretrained on it, the baseline does what?

From {{Table 8|ax:S3.T8}}: unfiltered C4 is worst on every task, the paper's cleanest data lesson (the baseline only sees 34B tokens, so the extra volume is never used). In-domain corpora win where domains match: Wikipedia plus Toronto Books lifts SuperGLUE to 73.24 almost entirely through MultiRC (exact match 25.78 to 50.93), whose largest source is fiction; RealNews-like lifts ReCoRD; Wikipedia helps SQuAD. "Unsurprising but also unsatisfying" for one general model, and such sets are small: WebText-like is about 40× smaller than C4.

Repetition ({{Table 9|ax:S3.T9}}): truncating C4 and looping it for the same 235 tokens, 64 repeats is harmless, 1,024 repeats costs 3.7 GLUE points and 4.6 on SQuAD, and training loss falls as the set shrinks ({{Figure 6|ax:S3.F6}}), the memorisation signature. Unlabelled data is cheap, so use a large set.

Fine-tuning and multi-task mixing

[[S3.SS5|§3.5, Tables 10 to 12]]

Fine-tuning ({{Table 10|ax:S3.T10}}): updating all parameters wins. Adapter layers (dense-ReLU-dense blocks after each feed-forward network, only they and the layer norms trained) work only with an inner size scaled to the task; gradual unfreezing costs a little accuracy and buys some speed. The main-text Table 10 has a misprint ({{checks|tab:t-tables:tbChk}}).

Multi-task learning ({{§3.5.2|ax:S3.SS5.SSS2}}): with no task-specific heads it is just mixing data sets, C4 included. Examples-proportional mixing samples task m with rm = min(em, K) / Σ min(en, K), the cap K stopping C4 and WMT English to French from crowding out everything; temperature-scaled mixing raises each rate to 1/T and renormalises; equal mixing samples every task alike. Runs last 219 + 218 steps.

The rule is the paper's; the sizes are TensorFlow Datasets training-split counts for the sets it names (it used TFDS, {{§2.3|ax:S2.SS3}}; WSC plus DPR approximate). Passes assume one example per sequence, 128 per batch, 786,432 steps; packing raises them, so they are illustrative. Scores are the measured {{Table 11|ax:S3.T11}} row.

Multi-task training alone loses to pretrain-then-fine-tune; equal mixing is a disaster (76.13 GLUE); examples-proportional mixing has a sweet spot in K; T = 2 is a reasonable single knob. But multi-task pretraining then per-task fine-tuning matches plain pretraining (83.11 against 83.28, {{Table 12|ax:S3.T12}}), and leaving a task out of the pretraining mix barely hurts it afterwards, so task interference is mild.

Scaling: given 4× the compute

[[S3.SS6|§3.6, Table 13]]

"You were just given 4× more compute. How should you use it?" ({{§3.6|ax:S3.SS6}}): 4× steps, a 4× batch, a 2× model (BERT-Large-shaped) for 2× steps, a 4× model for the baseline's steps, or an ensemble of 4 separately trained models.

which single-model option scores highest on SuperGLUE (baseline 71.36)?

From {{Table 13|ax:S3.T13}}. Everything helps; size gives a slightly bigger bump than time alone; 2× size for 2× steps is indistinguishable from 4× size; 4× steps and a 4× batch tie. Ensembling four models is an orthogonal win that beats every single-model route on summarisation and translation, though not on SuperGLUE. A bigger model also makes every fine-tune and inference dearer.

Putting it all together: T5

[[S3.SS7|§3.7, Table 15]]

Everything combined ({{§3.7|ax:S3.SS7}}): span corruption at mean length 3; C4; multi-task pretraining (C4 given an artificial size from 710,000 examples for Small to 133,000,000 for 11B) then per-task fine-tuning (GLUE and SuperGLUE task by task, batch 8); 1M steps at a batch of 211 sequences of 512 tokens, "about 1 trillion" tokens, "about 32×" the baseline (exactly 32× if the million steps are 220; 106 would give 30.5×); beam search (width 4, α = 0.6) for translation and summarisation. Five sizes: Small 60M, Base 220M, Large 770M, 3B and 11B. The two largest keep Large's 24 layers and dmodel 1024 and scale dff (16,384 and 65,536) and heads (32 and 128) instead, because TPUs are "most efficient for large dense matrix multiplications". Large's configuration counts to 737.7M (770.6M only if its tied output layer is counted twice), and 3B to 2.85B ({{recount|tab:t-tables:tbP}}).

Was the study worth anything beyond scale? The baseline trained for the same 1T tokens ("baseline-1T") against T5-Base, which differs only in the "non-scaling" changes ({{Table 15|ax:S3.T15}}): T5-Base wins on every task.

Gains over the baseline from 1T tokens, then from the non-scaling changes ({{Table 15|ax:S3.T15}}, validation). Scale bought more on GLUE, SuperGLUE and EnRo; the changes bought more on CNN/DM, SQuAD, EnDe and EnFr.

Results

[[S3.T14|§3.7, Table 14]]
  • 18 of 24 tasks at the state of the art (as of 24 October 2019). Recounted from Table 14 independently: 18 of 24, counting a task only when all its metrics beat the previous best (QQP is split). The 11B model is best of the five sizes "across all tasks", except English to Romanian (3B 28.2 against 28.1).
  • GLUE 90.3, state of the art; SuperGLUE 88.9 against a previous best of 84.6 and a human 89.8, above humans on MultiRC and ReCoRD, well below on COPA and WSC (humans 100%).
  • SQuAD 91.26 exact match and 96.22 F1 (validation), over a point above ALBERT; CNN/Daily Mail state of the art, significantly only on ROUGE-2.
  • WMT translation: state of the art on no pair (EnDe 32.1 against 33.8, EnFr 43.4 against 43.8, EnRo 28.1 against 38.5), which the authors put down to English-only pretraining and to not using backtranslation, which the leading systems relied on. mT5 was the direct follow-up.

The benchmark numbers are the least durable part. What aged well is the ablation grid, a controlled same-pipeline measurement of what each choice is worth, which is why the paper is cited for its negative findings (corruption rate barely matters, denoising variants barely differ, multi-task alone underperforms) as often as for T5. {{The ablation grid|tab:t-tables}} rebuilds all of it.

How much of this to believe

[[S3.SS1.SSS5|§3.1.5, Tables 1 to 16]]

What holds up. The paper measured its own noise (10 runs of the baseline, {{Table 1|ax:S3.T1}}) and changed one thing at a time against a fixed pipeline. The findings the field kept are many standard deviations wide (gap / Table 1's standard deviation): encoder-decoder over a causal LM (83.28 − 74.70) / 0.235 = 36.5 on GLUE ({{Table 2|ax:S3.T2}}); BERT-style over deshuffling 41.7 and over prefix language modelling 9.7 ({{Table 4|ax:S3.T4}}); C4 over unfiltered 7.7 on GLUE and 8.0 on SuperGLUE ({{Table 8|ax:S3.T8}}); pretrain-then-fine-tune over equal mixing 30.4 ({{Table 11|ax:S3.T11}}). Later work kept them: span corruption and C4's filters were reused for years, and FineWeb re-ablated each filter on a newer crawl and kept all but one ({{FineWeb §3.5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557}}).

Where to be careful.

  • One run per variant. Only the baseline was repeated; every other row is a single run assumed to be as noisy. The authors say so and why (cost), which counts in their favour ({{§3.1.5|ax:S3.SS1.SSS5}}).
  • Most "no difference" findings sit inside the noise. 12 of the 56 non-baseline ablation rows are within two standard deviations of the baseline on GLUE, 8 on SuperGLUE. Span corruption's edge over i.i.d. noise, which chose the final objective, is 0.9 standard deviations on GLUE and 2.8 on SuperGLUE and SQuAD ({{Table 7|ax:S3.T7}}), one run each. That these variants are about equal is well supported; which is best is not.
  • Selection on the reported set. Each score is the best of about 52 checkpoints chosen on the same validation set, per task ({{§3.1.2|ax:S3.SS1.SSS2}}), and bold means "near the best single run", itself biased upwards; both inflate small tasks most (CB has 56 validation examples).
  • Matched on compute, not parameters. The winning encoder-decoder has twice the parameters of the language models it beats, as the paper states ({{§3.2.2|ax:S3.SS2.SSS2}}).
  • Coordinate ascent. Winners chosen one axis at a time at 220M were combined at up to 11B without testing interactions (flagged by the authors, {{§3|ax:S3}}); the one check at scale ({{Table 15|ax:S3.T15}}) bundles several changes.
  • Body against appendix. Three main-table cells disagree with Table 16 (one row of Table 10 is a copy of another), Large's "770M" does not match its configuration, and "best across all tasks" has an exception in Table 14 ({{checks|tab:t-tables:tbChk}}).
  • Leaderboard wins compare unlike things, partly stated: single T5-11B models against ensembles, SQuAD on validation ({{§3.7|ax:S3.SS7}}). And "further tinkering is unlikely to pay" was revised by UL2's mixture of denoisers, which outperformed T5-like and GPT-like models ({{UL2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05131}}).

Verdict. Trust the direction of the large effects and the C4 recipe. Treat the rankings among near-equal variants (objectives, corruption rates, span lengths, mixing settings) as unresolved, and the leaderboard numbers as a snapshot of October 2019.

Why it matters

beyond the paper: later work, each claim linked

C4 became infrastructure. Released with its pipeline (a TensorFlow Datasets build; {{AllenAI|https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/c4}} later hosted the files), it pretrained unrelated models such as LLaMA 1 (15% C4, {{per Dolma|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.00159}}), and FineWeb calls it "one of the first large scale LLM training datasets". Its heuristics became the template later corpora argued with: RefinedWeb tabulates them ({{RefinedWeb|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.01116}}), Dolma reuses some, FineWeb kept all but terminal punctuation, which helps most alone but removes around 30% of tokens ({{FineWeb §3.5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17557}}). That filtering beats raw volume is now assumed.

Span corruption became the default encoder-decoder objective: kept by mT5 ({{mT5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11934}}) and ByT5 (spans of 20 bytes on average, {{ByT5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.13626}}), and one of UL2's denoisers ({{UL2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.05131}}). Not LongT5, which the older version of this page listed: it uses PEGASUS-style principle-sentence generation instead ({{LongT5 §5.3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.07916}}).

Text to text prefigured instruction tuning. Task prefixes are a hard-coded ancestor of the natural-language instruction: FLAN fine-tuned on "tasks described via instructions" ({{FLAN|https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01652}}) and Flan-T5 did it on T5 checkpoints across 1.8K tasks ({{Flan-T5|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11416}}), the direct line to how models are post-trained today.

The encoder-decoder finding lost, and why. T5 measured it above decoder-only at matched compute; the field went decoder-only regardless, for reasons T5 did not test: in-context learning arrived seven months later with GPT-3, "without any gradient updates or fine-tuning" ({{GPT-3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.14165}}), rewarding one causal stack; a decoder is simpler to serve (one KV cache, no encoder pass); prompt-and-continue unifies training and inference. Reruns: Ettin (2025) finds encoder-only against decoder-only task-dependent, without testing an encoder-decoder ({{Ettin|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11412}}); Encoder-Decoder Gemma, adapted from Gemma, is comparable in pretraining and substantially better after fine-tuning at a similar inference budget ({{arXiv 2504.06225|https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06225}}).

Details leaked, and the checkpoints are still load-bearing. Rescale-only norm outside the residual path, the shared relative position bias and Adafactor were popularised here; T5 1.1 then moved to GeGLU, no pretraining dropout, C4 only and untied embeddings ({{released checkpoints|https://github.com/google-research/text-to-text-transfer-transformer/blob/main/released_checkpoints.md}}). The size ladder (Small to XXL) still names Flan-T5, and the frozen T5 encoder conditions image generators: Imagen ({{Imagen|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11487}}), Stable Diffusion 3 ({{SD3|https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03206}}) and FLUX.1 (google/t5-v1_1-xxl, {{diffusers|https://github.com/huggingface/diffusers/blob/main/src/diffusers/pipelines/flux/pipeline_flux.py}}). The SuperGLUE human baseline held until January 2021, when a single 1.5B DeBERTa passed it, 89.9 against 89.8 ({{DeBERTa|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.03654}}).

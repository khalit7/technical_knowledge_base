Problem

§1 Introduction

In early 2020 nobody could say quantitatively how language model loss would improve if you spent 10× more compute, nor how to split that spend between a bigger model, more data and longer training. Architecture papers tuned shape (depth, width, heads) at a fixed scale; nobody had mapped the loss across orders of magnitude in model size N, dataset size D and compute C, or answered the budget question: given a fixed C, which N, D, batch size and step count give the lowest loss (§1)?

Method

§2 Background and Methods, §3

Train a large family of decoder-only Transformers and fit power laws to the test loss, cross-entropy in nats per token over a 1024-token context (§2).

  • Data: WebText2, Reddit outbound links with at least 3 karma through October 2018: 20.3M documents, 96 GB, 2.29 × 1010 tokens with GPT-2's byte-pair tokenizer (vocabulary 50,257), 6.6 × 108 held out; also tested on Books Corpus, Common Crawl, Wikipedia and Internet books (§2.3).
  • Sweep: N from 768 to 1.5B non-embedding parameters, D from 22M to 23B tokens, shape, context length, batch size (219 tokens for most runs); LSTMs and Universal Transformers for comparison (§3).
  • Training: Adam (Adafactor above 1B) for a fixed 2.5 × 105 steps, a 3000-step warmup then cosine decay to zero for every run, learning rate from a rule of thumb, LR(N) ≈ 0.003239 − 0.0001395 log N (§2.2, Eq. D.1).

Two accounting choices turn out to be load-bearing (§2.1, Table 1):

N ≈ 12 nlayer dmodel2   and   C ≈ 6NBS (= 6ND for one pass)
N counts non-embedding parameters only (Eq. 2.1): with the token and position embeddings included, depth seems to matter; without them, models of every depth fall on one trend, except those under 2 layers or of extreme aspect ratio (Figure 6). C counts non-embedding FLOPs: 2N per token forward, twice that backward, batch B tokens, S steps; the attention term 2nlayernctxdmodel is dropped as small when dmodel > nctx/12 (Eq. 2.2). One PF-day is 8.64 × 1019 FLOPs (§1.3). The Table 1 calculator recounts any shape.

Three power laws

§1.2, §3

When only one factor is the bottleneck, test loss is a power law in it (§1.2). The constants depend on WebText2's tokenizer and have "no fundamental meaning"; the exponents are the transferable content.

L(N) = (Nc/N)0.076,  Nc ≈ 8.8 × 1013 parameters
Eq. 1.1: trained to convergence with ample data.
L(D) = (Dc/D)0.095,  Dc ≈ 5.4 × 1013 tokens
Eq. 1.2: a large (36, 1280) model on a fixed subset, stopped when test loss stops falling (§3.3).
L(Cmin) = (Ccmin/Cmin)0.050,  Ccmin ≈ 3.1 × 108 PF-days
Eq. 1.3: the best model size and a batch well below the critical batch. At the fixed batch actually used the raw trend is C−0.057 (Table 5); Cmin corrects for batch size (§5) and is the one to extrapolate.

They hold across eight orders of magnitude in Cmin, six in N and over two in D, with no bending at the top end, though they must flatten before zero loss (§1.2). Power laws fit much better than logarithms (Figure 23); 1-layer models and unconverged giants are left out of the fits (§D.7).

Shape barely matters: at fixed N, the aspect ratio can vary 40× for a few percent of loss; (6, 4288) comes within 3% of GPT-2's (48, 1600) (Figure 5), perhaps because deep networks behave like ensembles of shallower ones. Transfer tracks in-distribution loss: loss on Books, Common Crawl, Wikipedia and Internet books falls in parallel with WebText2's, a roughly constant offset, independent of training time and depth (§3.2.2, Figure 8). Comparisons: LSTMs match Transformers on early tokens of the context but not later ones (Figure 7); recurrent Transformers do slightly better per parameter, worse per FLOP (Figure 17); per-token loss is a power law in context position, and larger models improve faster on early tokens (§D.5).

Overfitting: one equation for N and D

§4 The Infinite Data Limit and Overfitting

A single equation joins the two limits and the overfitting between them (Eq. 1.5):

L(N, D) = [ (Nc/N)αN/αD + Dc/D ]αD
Fitted on early-stopped runs with 10% dropout: αN = 0.076, αD = 0.103, Nc = 6.4 × 1013, Dc = 1.8 × 1013 (Table 2). Chosen so that a tokenizer change only rescales the loss, each limit is recovered, and overfitting has a series in 1/D, the expected scaling of a dataset's variance (§4.1); its real justification is that it fits.

The overfitting penalty depends only on N0.74/D (Eq. 4.3, Figure 9). With run-to-run seed variation of about 0.02, staying below it needs

D ≳ (5 × 103) N0.74 tokens
Eq. 4.4: an 8× bigger model needs 80.74 = 4.7×, "roughly 5×", the data, and models under 109 parameters fit on WebText2's 22B tokens. Recomputed: with Table 2's constants this is a 2.4% relative penalty, so the "0.02" works as a relative threshold.

Figure 9 (left) rebuilt from Eq. 1.5 and Table 2: loss against N at the chosen D (solid) and with unlimited data (dashed); the dot is where the penalty reaches 2.4%.

The fit fails only for datasets cut to about 2 × 107 tokens, where an epoch is 40 updates; regularisation was not tuned across sizes (§4.2). Unlike Hestness et al. (2017), who found data must grow superlinearly with model size, data here grows sublinearly (§7).

Training curves and the critical batch

§5 Model Size and Training Time

Following McCandlish et al. (2018), steps S and examples E = BS needed to reach a given loss trade off as (S/Smin − 1)(E/Emin − 1) = 1 (Eq. 5.1), which defines a critical batch:

Bcrit(L) = Emin/Smin ≈ (2 × 108 tokens) / L1/0.21
Eq. 5.3: it depends only on the loss, not on N, and doubles for every 13% drop in loss (1 − 2−0.21 = 13.5%) (Figure 10). Below Bcrit training is compute-efficient; above it, wall-clock time is bought with wasted FLOPs. Training at Bcrit costs 2Smin steps and 2Emin examples, the compromise.

Eq. 5.1 at B = b · Bcrit: S/Smin = 1 + 1/b, E/Emin = 1 + b. Every point reaches the same loss.

The paper quotes "roughly 1-2 million tokens at convergence for the largest models" (§1.1); Eq. 5.3 gives 1.1M at a loss of 3.0 but 4.0M at 2.3, Eq. 1.1's loss for 1.5B parameters. Correcting each run to Smin = S/(1 + Bcrit/B) and Cmin = C/(1 + B/Bcrit) (Eq. 5.4, Eq. 5.5), every learning curve after warmup fits

L(N, Smin) = (Nc/N)αN + (Sc/Smin)αS
Eq. 1.6, Table 3: αN = 0.077, αS = 0.76, Nc = 6.5 × 1013, Sc = 2.1 × 103; "imperfect" but "quite compelling". Since αS is the same for every size, the early part of a curve predicts its end, and larger models reach any loss in fewer steps and tokens, almost 100× fewer between the smallest and a very large model (Figure 2, Figure 19). It also bounds when to stop a data-limited run (Eq. 5.7).

Spending a compute budget

§6 Optimal Allocation, Appendix B

Substitute S = C/(6NBcrit) into Eq. 1.6 and minimise over N (Appendix B). The optimum stops a fixed αN/αS ≈ 10% above the model's converged loss (Eq. B.5); usual practice, the paper says, was nearer 2% (§B.3).

reach the same loss two ways: (a) train a model to within 2% of its converged loss; (b) train a bigger model and stop at 10% above its converged loss. How does (b)'s compute compare with (a)'s?

About a third: 2.7× the parameters, 1/7.5 of the steps, 0.36× the compute (Eq. B.12 to B.14; the paper prints 2.7, 0.13, 0.35 and "7.7x fewer parameter updates", checked here). Play both runs below, to scale.

Off-optimal sizes are cheap: 0.6× to 2.2× the optimal size costs at most 20% more compute, and the 2.2× model needs 45% fewer steps (§B.4, Figure 12). Then a contradiction: compute-efficient data grows as D ≈ (4 × 1010 tokens) Cmin0.26 (Eq. 6.7), but avoiding overfitting needs D ∝ Cmin0.54 (Eq. 6.6). Once data-limited, loss falls only as C−0.03, so the two trends must cross, at roughly C* ~ 104 PF-days, N* ~ 1012 parameters, D* ~ 1012 tokens, L* ~ 1.7 nats/token, uncertain by an order of magnitude either way (Eq. 6.8). The laws must break down by then; the conjecture is that L* estimates the entropy of language, where models have extracted all its reliable information (§6.3).

Figure 15 (Figure 15) rebuilt: Eq. 1.3 against the data-limited bound, Eq. 1.2 at Eq. 6.7's data. Eq. 6.7's own formula, 2Cmin/(6N(Cmin)), gives a prefactor of 2.2 × 1010, not the printed 4 × 1010; only the former lands near the paper's crossing.

Results

§8 Discussion, §1.1
  • Loss is a smooth power law in each of N, D and Cmin over six to eight orders of magnitude, with no deviation at the top; shape and hyperparameters barely matter next to scale.
  • Larger models are strictly more sample-efficient, so compute-efficient training means very large models stopped far short of convergence, the opposite of the then-standard practice of converging small models (§1.1).
  • Loss on other distributions tracks WebText2 loss with a roughly constant offset, so in-distribution loss is a good proxy.
  • Extrapolated, the compute and data trends meet near C* ~ 104 PF-days, N* ~ 1012, L* ~ 1.7 nats: where the laws must break down.
  • The Discussion: "big models may be more important than big data", so model parallelism deserves work (wide layers split more easily than deep pipelines); the laws are likened to the ideal gas law awaiting a statistical mechanics, may hold for other generative tasks, and smooth loss may hide qualitative jumps in ability (§8).

How much of this to believe

§2.2, §4.2, Appendix C

What holds up. The measurements: a sweep over six orders of magnitude in N with its fit ranges and exclusions stated (§D.7), and a caveats list the authors volunteer, which counts in their favour (Appendix C): no theory for any law, little confidence in Bcrit far outside the tested range, a poorly studied small-data regime, no regularisation experiments, compute that ignores the context term, possibly untuned hyperparameters, and no higher learning rates tried for short runs. Every later study reproduces the qualitative findings (power-law loss, weak shape dependence, large models' sample efficiency), and so does a toy sweep on this laptop (Refit the laws).

Where the evidence is weaker.

  • The allocation rests on one schedule. Every run used the same 2.5 × 105 steps, 3000-step warmup and cosine to zero (§2.2), and the frontier is read off intermediate checkpoints, whose learning rate has not decayed. The warmup alone, 3000 × 219 = 1.6 × 109 tokens, is near or above the optimal token count at many small budgets (Porian §3.3). Both push the frontier toward big models.
  • An accounting choice moved an exponent. Leaving the output layer out of N and C distorts small models most; counting it lowers the exponent by more than 0.1 (Porian §3.2).
  • No error bars. No fit has a confidence interval; seed variation is "roughly 0.02" in §4.2 but "at the level of 0.05" in Figure 22, and most points are single runs.
  • Small internal slips (every number checked): αCmin is 0.054 in Eq. 6.4 and 0.052 in Eq. B.7 for one formula (0.052 is right); N0.74 is introduced from Eq. 1.1 and 1.2, which give 0.80 (0.74 is Table 2's); Eq. 6.7's 4 × 1010 should be 2 × 1010; "Figure X" and "Figure Y" are left unnumbered in B.4. None changes a conclusion.
  • Data reuse is not discussed: a full run is 2.5 × 105 × 219 = 1.3 × 1011 tokens, about 5.7 passes over WebText2 (derived). Nothing was released, so the fits cannot be redone; this page draws the paper's equations, never its curves.

How it held up (beyond the paper). The laws' form held; the allocation did not. Chinchilla's three methods put the size exponent at 0.46 to 0.50 (Chinchilla Table 2), and Porian et al. reproduced Kaplan's 0.88 (raw) and 0.73 (adjusted) and removed the gap cause by cause (Then and now).

Verdict
Believe the measurements and the method: power-law loss in scale, weak dependence on shape, large models' sample efficiency, the L(N, D) overfitting law and the critical batch held up and remain the working toolkit. Do not plan a run with N ∝ C0.73: it is an artefact of one fixed schedule with a long warmup, uncounted output-layer compute and untuned optimisers, and was replaced by roughly equal growth of N and D.

Why it matters

beyond the paper

Beyond the paper: each later claim carries its own source.

This paper made scale a quantitative engineering discipline. It shaped GPT-3, which cites it for training "much larger models on many fewer tokens than is typical": 300B tokens for every size up to 175B (GPT-3 Figure 2.2, Table 2.1). It made loss-against-compute extrapolation the planning tool for later frontier runs and popularised C ≈ 6ND, the critical batch and power-law fitting as standard vocabulary (Chinchilla budgets with Kaplan's 6ND, Chinchilla §3.3).

Its headline allocation was revised. Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) redid the sweep with each cosine schedule matched to its run's length and found Nopt ∝ C0.5, Dopt ∝ C0.5: about 20 tokens per parameter, against Kaplan's C0.73 and C0.27. For Gopher's budget the optimal model is about 4× smaller on 4× more tokens, and 70B Chinchilla on 1.4T tokens beat 280B Gopher at equal compute (Chinchilla §1); head to head at 1021 FLOPs, its size beat Kaplan's 4.68B (Chinchilla D.4; reproduced here from Table 6 with Cmin = C/2).

Why the two disagreed has been corrected since. Chinchilla blamed Kaplan's fixed token count and schedule for all models and its mostly small (many under 100M) models (Chinchilla §2). Porian et al. (2024) found three causes: uncounted output-layer compute (not the input embeddings), a warmup too long for small models, and optimiser settings not retuned per size; and, against Chinchilla's hypothesis, that matching the learning-rate decay to the budget is not essential: with tuned hyperparameters even a constant rate gives Chinchilla's exponent (Porian §1). Pearce and Song (2024) independently found the last-layer effect (Porian §5.1). The corrections move the exponent from about 0.73 to 0.88 down to about 0.5.

Using this today. Start from Chinchilla's D ≈ 20N as the compute-optimal point, or overtrain past it when inference cost matters, as Llama 3 does for its 8B and 70B models (Llama 3 §1) while keeping 405B near its own fitted optimum (§3.2.1). Count the output layer in N and C, scale warmup with model size, tune learning rate and batch per size (Porian et al.), and decay the learning rate at the planned budget, which still lowers the final loss at every scale even if it barely moves the exponent (Chinchilla Appendix B; Porian §4.1). Kaplan's other findings (the power-law form, shape insensitivity, large models' sample efficiency, the critical batch, the L(N, D) law) survived and remain the working toolkit.

Connections

  • Chinchilla (2022): the direct successor; revises the compute-optimal allocation to equal N and D scaling (about 20 tokens per parameter).
  • GPT-3 (2020): the first frontier model built on these prescriptions (large N, 300B tokens, stopped short of convergence).
  • Attention Is All You Need (2017): the architecture whose parallelism made these compute scales reachable.
  • Llama 3 (2024) and DeepSeek-V3 (2024): the modern regime of deliberately overtraining far beyond compute-optimal to cheapen inference.
  • Topics: llm-training-and-post-training (pretraining, batch size, learning-rate schedules), data-curation-and-datasets (data requirements and scaling), ml-fundamentals (power-law fitting, overfitting).

5. How noisy is one run?

What reproduces at toy scale, and what does not
  • Reproduces: loss is a clean power law in non-embedding N over three decades, αN ≈ 0.08 against the paper's 0.076 (by coincidence of scale and data as much as anything: the tokens differ); counting all parameters bends the smallest models off the line (Figure 6); on the compute axis each size is best only for a stretch and bigger models take over at higher compute (Figures 1 and 2).
  • Reproduces only after tuning: Chinchilla's point that a schedule ending at the budget beats a checkpoint of a longer run. At the paper's learning-rate rule the opposite happens; at 3× the rule it holds (section 3). The paper's own caveat (short runs might want a larger rate) and Porian et al.'s finding (per-size tuning is what matters) both show up at toy scale.
  • Cannot be tested here: the allocation exponent. A bigger model stopped early never beats the smaller model trained on all the data inside this sweep, so the optimum is not located and no exponent is claimed (section 4).
  • Noise: one seed moves a tiny model's loss by up to 0.06 nats, as much as the gap between the two largest sizes (section 5). Single-run points, here as in the paper, carry that much uncertainty.

    Why the exponents differed

    Porian et al. (2024) reproduced Kaplan's setup in a modern training stack and undid its choices one at a time (Porian Figure 1):

    Two corrections to the usual story. First, the uncounted cost that matters is the output layer (the "head" that produces logits), which Kaplan's N and C leave out; the input embedding costs no matrix multiply. Second, matching the cosine decay to each run's length, which Chinchilla proposed as the explanation, barely moved the exponent; per-size tuning of learning rate, batch and AdamW β2 did the rest, and with it even a constant learning rate gives Chinchilla's exponent (Porian §3.4, §3.5). Porian et al. also recover Kaplan's adjusted 0.73 (they get 0.717) by reintroducing the FLOP count and warmup issues with tuned hyperparameters (their Appendix, Figure 17).

    What changed and what survived

    Changed

    • The allocation. N ∝ C0.73 became N ∝ C0.46 to 0.50, tokens per parameter roughly constant near 20 instead of falling with scale (Chinchilla Table 2).
    • Counting. The output layer goes into N and C (Porian et al., who also read Chinchilla's Approach 3 as counting the embeddings: Porian Appendix B).
    • Fitting. A saturating form with an irreducible loss, L = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ with E = 1.69, replaced pure power laws that bend nowhere (Chinchilla Eq. 2 and D.2); isoFLOP sweeps replaced reading frontiers off checkpoints.
    • The objective. Training-compute optimality gave way to deliberate overtraining for cheaper inference: Llama 3 trains its 8B and 70B models "much longer than is compute-optimal" (Llama 3 §1).

    Survived

    • Loss as a smooth, predictable power law in scale, used to plan runs: Llama 3 fits its own isoFLOP law to choose 405B for 3.8 × 1025 FLOPs (its fit predicts 402B on 16.55T tokens) (Llama 3 §3.2.1).
    • C ≈ 6ND as the budget equation (Chinchilla §3.3).
    • Shape is second order; scale is first.
    • Larger models are more sample-efficient; batch size grows with scale (the critical batch).
    • The L(N, D) overfitting picture: data can grow sublinearly in model size before repetition hurts.

    (25 min)

    Resolving Discrepancies in Compute-Optimal Scaling of Language Models

    Porian et al., NeurIPS 2024: the forensic reconciliation of Kaplan and Chinchilla: reproduces Kaplan's 0.88 and 0.73 exponents, then removes them one cause at a time (output-layer FLOPs, warmup length, optimiser tuning) and finds learning-rate decay matters little for the exponent.

    nostalgebraist: what the revised laws imply about data as the binding constraint; a useful counterpoint to this paper's "big models over big data" conclusion.

    Chinchilla

    2022: the direct successor; revises the compute-optimal allocation to equal N and D scaling (about 20 tokens per parameter).

    GPT-3

    2020: the first frontier model built on these prescriptions (large N, one pass over most data, stopped short of convergence).

    Attention Is All You Need

    2017: the architecture whose parallelism made these compute scales reachable.

    The Llama 3 Herd of Models

    2024: fits its own isoFLOP scaling law (405B is about compute-optimal) and deliberately overtrains the smaller models to cheapen inference.

    DeepSeek-V3

    2024: 14.8T tokens for 37B active parameters, the overtrained regime with mixture of experts.

    Megatron-LM

    2019: the model parallelism this paper's Discussion calls for to train wide networks.

    WSM

    2025: decay-free schedules with checkpoint merging, one answer to the schedule-length problem behind the Kaplan and Chinchilla disagreement.

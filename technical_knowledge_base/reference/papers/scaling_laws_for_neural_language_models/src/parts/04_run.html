Refit the laws on a toy sweep

The paper released no runs, so its curves cannot be refitted. Instead, this page ran its own sweep, in the paper's style but at toy scale, on a laptop CPU: training runs of decoder-only Transformers from 1.5 thousand to 2.7 million non-embedding parameters, of CPU in all. Every point below is a real held-out loss from those runs; every fit is computed in your browser from them.

Try: drop the smallest or largest models from the fit and watch the exponent move; switch to counting all parameters (embeddings included) and see the points leave the line, the paper's Figure 6 argument; switch to a law with an irreducible loss, as Chinchilla later fits.

Points: final test loss of each size's full-length run (seed 1). Hollow points are left out of the fit. Dashed: the fit extrapolated to 10× the largest model. Lower strip: residuals, log(loss) minus log(fit), in percent. The pure fit is least squares in log-log space; the saturating fit scans E and fits the rest in log space.

2. Learning curves and the compute frontier (Figure 1, left; Figure 13)

Try: read the heavy line: at each compute, the lowest loss any run reached. Switch the compute accounting to include the output layer and attention, and see the small models move right.

Every evaluation of every full-length run (seed 1), after warmup, plotted against training compute. The frontier is the lower envelope; its power-law fit is least squares in log-log space over the envelope's points.

3. A run whose schedule ends at the budget, against a checkpoint of a longer run

The paper reads its frontier off checkpoints of runs whose learning rate has not yet decayed; Chinchilla's criticism is that a run whose cosine ends at that budget would do better (its Appendix B). Measured here, for each size: the long run's loss when it has seen 1/4 (or 1/16) of its tokens, against a separate run with the same tokens, data order and initialisation whose schedule ends there.

4. Big model stopped early, or smaller model trained on everything?

The paper's allocation says that at a given compute a bigger model stopped early beats a smaller one trained longer. Each row below compares two neighbouring sizes at the same compute: the smaller one at the end of its full run, the bigger one at the point where it has spent that much compute, read from its long run (the paper's way) and from its matched runs (Chinchilla's way).

Compute is 6N per token, as the paper counts. "Matched" interpolates the bigger size's three finished runs (1/16, 1/4, full) linearly in log tokens. The largest size has no matched runs (CPU budget).

5. How noisy is one run?

What reproduces at toy scale, and what does not
  • Reproduces: loss is a clean power law in non-embedding N over three decades, αN ≈ 0.08 against the paper's 0.076 (by coincidence of scale and data as much as anything: the tokens differ); counting all parameters bends the smallest models off the line (Figure 6); on the compute axis each size is best only for a stretch and bigger models take over at higher compute (Figures 1 and 2).
  • Reproduces only after tuning: Chinchilla's point that a schedule ending at the budget beats a checkpoint of a longer run. At the paper's learning-rate rule the opposite happens; at 3× the rule it holds (section 3). The paper's own caveat (short runs might want a larger rate) and Porian et al.'s finding (per-size tuning is what matters) both show up at toy scale.
  • Cannot be tested here: the allocation exponent. A bigger model stopped early never beats the smaller model trained on all the data inside this sweep, so the optimum is not located and no exponent is claimed (section 4).
  • Noise: one seed moves a tiny model's loss by up to 0.06 nats, as much as the gap between the two largest sizes (section 5). Single-run points, here as in the paper, carry that much uncertainty.

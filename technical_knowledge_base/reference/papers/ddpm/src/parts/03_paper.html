Problem

[[S1|§1 Introduction]]

Diffusion probabilistic models ({{Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015|https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.03585}}) were an elegant latent-variable model, a Markov chain trained by variational inference to turn noise into data, "straightforward to define and efficient to train", but "there has been no demonstration that they are capable of generating high quality samples" ({{§1|ax:S1}}). In Table 1 the original has only a likelihood bound (≤ 5.40 bits per dimension on CIFAR10) and no sample score ({{Table 1|ax:S4.T1}}).

The field was split. GANs had the best samples but no likelihood, and are fragile to train (with limited data, training "typically leads to discriminator overfitting, causing training to diverge", {{Karras et al. 2020|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06676}}). Autoregressive models and flows had likelihoods but weaker samples: Gated PixelCNN has 3.03 bits per dimension and FID 65.93; the Sparse Transformer's 2.80 bits, the table's best, comes with no FID ({{Table 1|ax:S4.T1}}).

The question: can a diffusion model, trained with plain variational inference, actually generate high-quality images, and what parameterisation makes that work?

Idea

[[S2|§2 Background, Figure 2]]

Two Markov chains over T steps. The forward chain is fixed: it adds a little Gaussian noise at each step until the data is destroyed. The reverse chain is learned: it starts from noise and removes it step by step. When each forward step is small, its true reversal is close to Gaussian, so the learned steps can be Gaussians whose means the network supplies ({{§1|ax:S1}}, {{§2|ax:S2}}).

The contribution is how to parameterise that mean: the network predicts the noise that was added, and the loss drops the bound's per-step weights. That gave the best samples, and shows the model is denoising score matching over many noise levels, sampled like annealed Langevin dynamics, an equivalence the authors count "one of our primary contributions" ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

Below, one 2-D Swiss roll through both chains: the forward chain is exact arithmetic ({{Eq. 4|ax:S2.E4}}); the reverse chains are toy models trained for this page (the paper's recipe, and a variant Table 2 reports as failing), sampled live from the same noise.

300 points; T = 1,000; the faint curve is the roll the data was drawn around.

Forward: each point keeps one fixed noise draw ε, so xt = √ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε traces its path. Reverse: {{Algorithm 2|ax:S3.SS2.fig1}} with σt² = βt, every one of the 1,000 steps computed by the toy network (seed 42; the JavaScript is checked against PyTorch, see the {{Sample tab|tab:t-run}}). Method: the forward process

[[S2|§2, Eq. 2 and 4; §3.1; §4]]

The forward or diffusion process is a fixed Markov chain that adds Gaussian noise under a variance schedule β1, …, βT:

q(xt | xt−1) = 𝒩(xt; √(1 − βt) xt−1, βt I)
Equation 2 ({{§2|ax:S2.E2}}). The √(1 − βt) keeps the variance from growing as noise is added.

With αt = 1 − βt and ᾱt = ∏s≤t αs, the chain collapses to a closed form, so any timestep can be reached in one jump:

q(xt | x0) = 𝒩(xt; √ᾱt x0, (1 − ᾱt) I),   i.e.   xt = √ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε,   ε ~ 𝒩(0, I)
Equation 4 ({{§2|ax:S2.E4}}) and its reparameterisation ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}).

Training is therefore cheap: pick a random t and noise x0 in one shot ({{§2|ax:S2}}). The paper fixes T = 1,000 (matching earlier work's number of evaluations) and βt linear from 10−4 to 0.02: small against data in [−1, 1], so reverse steps stay nearly Gaussian, while ᾱT ≈ 0, so xT ≈ 𝒩(0, I) ({{§4|ax:S4}}). The βt could be learned; fixing them makes q parameter-free and LT a constant ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}).

Try: drag t. The left chart is the schedule (signal √ᾱt and noise √(1 − ᾱt)); the right is q(xt | x0) for 600 roll points. Halfway, at t = 500, only √ᾱ = of the signal is left.

Recomputed: LT ≈ 10−5 bits per dimension

The defaults reproduce the paper's "LT ≈ 10−5 bits per dimension" ({{§4|ax:S4}}) by recomputation from the schedule: ᾱT = , so KL(q(xT | x0) ‖ 𝒩(0, I)) per dimension is bits for a pixel at ±1 and bits for an illustrative mean square x0² = 0.25 (the paper does not give its pixels' mean square). The toy data's own LT is bits per dimension.

Method: the reverse process and the bound

[[S2|§2, Eq. 1, 3, 5 to 7; §3.2]]

The reverse process is a Markov chain with learned Gaussian transitions, starting at p(xT) = 𝒩(0, I):

pθ(xt−1 | xt) = 𝒩(xt−1; μθ(xt, t), Σθ(xt, t))
Equation 1 ({{§2|ax:S2.E1}}); latents have the data's dimensionality.

Training minimises the usual variational bound on −log pθ(x0) ({{Eq. 3|ax:S2.E3}}), rewritten for lower variance as a sum of per-step terms:

L = LT + Σt>1 Lt−1 + L0,   Lt−1 = KL(q(xt−1 | xt, x0) ‖ pθ(xt−1 | xt)),   L0 = −log pθ(x0 | x1)
Equation 5 ({{§2|ax:S2.E5}}); derivation in {{Appendix A|ax:A1}}. LT = KL(q(xT | x0) ‖ p(xT)).

Each KL compares the model's step with the forward process posterior, which is Gaussian and known in closed form once x0 is given:

q(xt−1 | xt, x0) = 𝒩(μ̃t, β̃tI),   μ̃t = (√ᾱt−1 βt / (1 − ᾱt)) x0 + (√αt (1 − ᾱt−1) / (1 − ᾱt)) xt,   β̃t = ((1 − ᾱt−1) / (1 − ᾱt)) βt
Equations 6 and 7 ({{§2|ax:S2.E6}}): every KL in Eq. 5 compares two Gaussians in closed form ("Rao-Blackwellized"), not by high-variance Monte Carlo.

The variances are fixed. Σθ = σt²I with σt² = βt (optimal for x0 ~ 𝒩(0, I)) or β̃t (optimal for one fixed x0), the two extremes of reverse-process entropy; "both … had similar results" ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}). Learning a diagonal Σθ was unstable ({{Table 2|ax:S4.T2}}). On the toy, β̃ gives slightly tighter samples but a worse bound ().

Method: predict the noise

[[S3.SS2|§3.2, Eq. 8 to 12]]

With fixed variances, each term of the bound is a weighted squared distance between means, plus a constant C that does not depend on θ:

Lt−1 = 𝔼q[ (1 / 2σt²) ‖μ̃t(xt, x0) − μθ(xt, t)‖² ] + C
Equation 8 ({{§3.2|ax:S3.E8}}). The obvious choice is a network that predicts μ̃t directly: the "μ̃ prediction" baseline of Table 2.

Substituting Eq. 4 into Eq. 7, the target is (1/√αt)(xt − (βt/√(1 − ᾱt)) ε) ({{Eq. 10|ax:S3.E10}}); xt is an input, so only ε is unknown, and a network εθ predicts it:

μθ(xt, t) = (1/√αt) ( xt − (βt / √(1 − ᾱt)) εθ(xt, t) )
Equation 11 ({{§3.2|ax:S3.E11}}). Then each term of the bound is a weighted MSE on the noise:
Lt−1 − C = 𝔼x0, ε[ (βt² / (2σt² αt (1 − ᾱt))) ‖ε − εθ(√ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε, t)‖² ]
Equation 12 ({{§3.2|ax:S3.E12}}).

Why this is score matching. From Eq. 4, ∇ log q(xt | x0) = −ε / √(1 − ᾱt) (one line, not printed in the paper), so εθ is a scaled score estimate. Eq. 12 "resembles denoising score matching over multiple noise scales", the objective of {{NCSN|https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05600}}, and sampling with Eq. 11,

xt−1 = (1/√αt) ( xt − ((1 − αt) / √(1 − ᾱt)) εθ(xt, t) ) + σt z,   z ~ 𝒩(0, I)
{{Algorithm 2|ax:S3.SS2.fig1}}, line 4 (1 − αt is βt).

"resembles Langevin dynamics with εθ as a learned gradient of the data density". Since Eq. 12 is one term of the bound for this sampler, denoising score matching here is variational inference on a sampling chain ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}). It is "just another parameterization", hence Table 2's test; predicting x0 instead gave worse samples early on. The {{score field|tab:t-run:scoreCard}} in the Sample tab draws the toy's learned score.

Method: the simplified objective, Lsimple

[[S3.SS4|§3.4, Eq. 14]]

The bound of Eqs. 12 and 13 trains fine, but dropping the weight of Eq. 12 was "beneficial to sample quality (and simpler to implement)":

Lsimple(θ) = 𝔼t, x0, ε[ ‖ε − εθ(√ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε, t)‖² ],   t ~ Uniform{1, …, T}
Equation 14 ({{§3.4|ax:S3.E14}}). t = 1 stands in for L0, with the decoder's integral approximated by the Gaussian density times the bin width (ignoring σ1² and edge effects); t > 1 is Eq. 12 unweighted, like NCSN's loss weighting; LT does not appear because βt is fixed.

It is a weighted bound that emphasises different aspects of reconstruction.

compared with the true bound, which timesteps does Lsimple give relatively more weight to?

Large t. With the paper's schedule the true bound's weight is largest at the smallest t and falls by a factor of about by t = 500; Lsimple weights every t the same, so relative to the bound it "down-weight[s] loss terms corresponding to small t", which "train the network to denoise data with very small amounts of noise", and lets the network "focus on more difficult denoising tasks at larger t" ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}). The chart shows each objective's share of its total weight at each t, from the weight of Eq. 12.

Share at t: wt / Σw, wt = βt² / (2σt² αt (1 − ᾱt)) for t ≥ 2 (Eq. 12); Lsimple: 1/1,000 each. Only the shape matters.

The ablation below shows this reweighting is exactly what sample quality needs ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}, {{Table 2|ax:S4.T2}}).

Method: training, sampling and the network

[[S3.SS2.fig1|Algorithms 1 and 2; §3.3; §4; Appendix B]]
Algorithm 1: training
  1. Take x0 from the data.
  2. t ~ Uniform{1, …, T}; ε ~ 𝒩(0, I).
  3. Gradient step on ‖ε − εθ(√ᾱt x0 + √(1 − ᾱt) ε, t)‖².
  4. Repeat until converged.
Algorithm 2: sampling
  1. xT ~ 𝒩(0, I).
  2. For t = T, …, 1: z ~ 𝒩(0, I) if t > 1, else z = 0.
  3. xt−1 = (1/√αt)(xt − ((1 − αt)/√(1 − ᾱt)) εθ(xt, t)) + σtz.
  4. Return x0.

Training is one forward pass and one MSE per example; sampling is the expensive part, 1,000 sequential network evaluations per sample ({{Algorithms 1 and 2|ax:S3.SS2.fig1}}).

  • Data and decoder ({{§3.3|ax:S3.SS3}}): pixels 0 to 255 scaled to [−1, 1]; the last step pθ(x0 | x1) integrates 𝒩(μθ(x1, 1), σ1²) over each pixel's bin, outer bins to ±∞ ({{Eq. 13|ax:S3.E13}}), so the bound is a lossless codelength with no added noise or Jacobian. μθ(x1, 1) is displayed noiselessly; a stronger (autoregressive) decoder is left to future work.
Network, training recipe and cost (§4, Appendix B)
  • Network ({{§4|ax:S4}}, {{Appendix B|ax:A2}}): a U-Net like an unmasked PixelCNN++ (itself a U-Net on a Wide ResNet), with group normalisation in place of weight normalisation; four feature-map resolutions (32 × 32 down to 4 × 4) for 32 × 32 images and six for 256 × 256; two convolutional residual blocks per resolution and self-attention at 16 × 16. Parameters are shared across time; t enters by adding the {{Transformer's sinusoidal position embedding|n:3c65c17b0d0d81999af7f16f8ed8ee9e}} into each residual block. 35.7M parameters for CIFAR10, 114M for LSUN and CelebA-HQ, about 256M for a larger LSUN Bedroom variant.
  • Recipe ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}): Adam at its standard settings, learning rate 2 × 10−4 (2 × 10−5 for 256 × 256 images, which "seemed unstable" at the larger rate), batch 128 (64 for large images), EMA of the weights with decay 0.9999, dropout 0.1 on CIFAR10 (swept over 0.1 to 0.4; without it samples showed overfitting artifacts) and 0 elsewhere, random horizontal flips (all datasets but LSUN Bedroom). The β schedule was chosen from constant, linear and quadratic ones with LT ≈ 0; T = 1,000 was not swept. Hyperparameters were tuned for CIFAR10 sample quality and transferred.
  • Cost ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}): a TPU v3-8 ("similar to 8 V100 GPUs"). CIFAR10 trains at 21 steps per second, 800k steps in 10.6 hours (800,000 / 21 / 3,600 = h, recomputed), and a batch of 256 samples takes 17 seconds ( ms per image). The 256 × 256 models train at 2.2 steps per second (CelebA-HQ 0.5M steps, LSUN Bedroom 2.4M, Cat 1.8M, Church 1.2M; that is days at that rate, derived) and take 300 seconds for 128 samples.

Results

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, Table 1, Table 3]]
  • CIFAR10, unconditional: FID 3.17 and Inception Score 9.46 ± 0.11 for the Lsimple model, the best unconditional FID in Table 1 and better than most models in the literature "including class conditional models" (the conditional StyleGAN2 + ADA, at 2.67, is better) ({{Table 1|ax:S4.T1}}). FID is computed against the training set, as is standard; against the test set it is 5.24 ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}).
  • Likelihood: ≤ 3.75 bits per dimension (train 3.72) for the Lsimple model; the model trained on the true bound gets the better ≤ 3.70 (3.69) but an FID of only 13.51. "Training our models on the true variational bound yields better codelengths … but the latter yields the best sample quality" ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}). Both beat the original diffusion model's ≤ 5.40 and the estimates reported for energy-based models and score matching, but not autoregressive models (Sparse Transformer 2.80) ({{§4.3|ax:S4.SS3}}).
  • LSUN 256 × 256 ({{Table 3|ax:Ax1.T3}}): Church FID 7.89 (ProgressiveGAN 6.42, StyleGAN2 3.86), Bedroom 6.36 with the 114M model and 4.90 with the larger one (ProgressiveGAN 8.34, StyleGAN 2.65), Cat 19.75 (ProgressiveGAN 37.52, StyleGAN2 6.93): comparable to ProgressiveGAN, behind StyleGAN. CelebA-HQ 256 × 256 has samples ({{Figure 1|ax:S0.F1}}) but no score.
  • Honest caveats ({{§1|ax:S1}}, {{§4.3|ax:S4.SS3}}): log likelihoods are not competitive with strong autoregressive models, and sampling needs T = 1,000 sequential network evaluations.

Progressive coding: what the bits are spent on

[[S4.SS3|§4.3, Algorithms 3 and 4, Eq. 15 and 16, Figures 5 to 7, Table 4]]

The train-test gap is at most 0.03 bits per dimension, so no overfitting; yet codelengths are poor while samples are excellent: "diffusion models have an inductive bias that makes them excellent lossy compressors" ({{§4.3|ax:S4.SS3}}). With L1 + … + LT as rate and L0 as distortion, the best-sample model has rate 1.78 and distortion 1.97 bits per dimension (RMSE 0.95 of 255); 1.78 + 1.97 = 3.75, its whole codelength, so "more than half … describes imperceptible distortions" (, recomputed).

A progressive code. Algorithms 3 and 4 send xT, then each xt, then x0, each in about KL(q ‖ p) bits (minimal random coding): total, exactly Eq. 5. The receiver can estimate at any time

x0 ≈ x̂0 = ( xt − √(1 − ᾱt) εθ(xt) ) / √ᾱt
Equation 15 ({{§4.3|ax:S4.E15}}), Eq. 4 solved for x0. Figure 5 plots its distortion, √(‖x0 − x̂0‖²/D), against the bits received so far.
after 900 of the 1,000 reverse steps, what share of the bits has the receiver been sent?

Under 10%: of the final 1.776 bits per dimension, while the distortion has already fallen from 67.6 to 12.0 (on 0 to 255). Most of the bits go to the last 100 steps, the fine detail. Progressive generation, diffusion as generalised autoregression, interpolation, and how it differs from NCSN (§4.3, §4.4, Appendix C)

Progressive generation. Running Algorithm 2 and showing x̂0 along the way, large-scale features appear first and details last ({{Figure 6|ax:S4.F6}}). Freezing xt and sampling x0 ~ pθ(x0 | xt) several times, CelebA-HQ samples share high-level attributes (gender, hair colour, eyewear, pose, expression) when the chain is split late and differ greatly when split at x1000, "hints of conceptual compression" ({{Figure 7|ax:S4.F7}}, {{Appendix D|ax:A4}}). The {{Sample tab|tab:t-run}} can show x̂0 instead of xt while it samples.

A generalised autoregressive model. The bound can be rewritten as Eq. 16 ({{§4.3|ax:S4.E16}}). Set T to the data dimension, let each forward step mask out one more coordinate, and the reverse process must copy the unmasked coordinates and predict the next one: training it is training an autoregressive model. Gaussian diffusion is then "a kind of autoregressive model with a generalized bit ordering that cannot be expressed by reordering data coordinates", and its length is free: T = 1,000 is less than the 3,072 or 196,608 dimensions of the 32 × 32 × 3 and 256 × 256 × 3 images, and can be shorter for speed or longer for expressiveness ({{§4.3|ax:S4.SS3}}).

Interpolation. Noise two images to xt and x′t with the same noise, mix them linearly, and decode with the reverse process: the reverse process removes the artifacts of mixing, giving plausible interpolations of pose, skin tone, hairstyle, expression and background (but not eyewear) at t = 500, coarser and more varied ones at larger t, and novel samples at t = 1,000 ({{§4.4|ax:S4.SS4}}, {{Figure 8|ax:S4.F8}}).

How it differs from NCSN ({{Appendix C|ax:A3}}): a U-Net with self-attention rather than a RefineNet with dilated convolutions; every layer conditioned on t; the forward process scales the data down so the variance does not grow; the forward process destroys the signal (prior and aggregate posterior of xT match) with very small βt, which prevents distribution shift when sampling; and the sampler's coefficients come from βt and are trained by variational inference, where NCSN's are set by hand after training.

How much of this to believe

[[A2|Appendix B, §4, Tables 1 and 2]]

What holds up, and why.

  • Reproduced independently. Improved DDPM ran the same setup (T = 1,000, linear schedule, Lsimple) for 500K rather than 800K iterations: FID 3.29 and 3.73 bits per dimension against 3.17 and 3.75 ({{Nichol and Dhariwal 2021, Table 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09672}}). Code is released; scores use the standard Inception and TTUR FID code on 50,000 samples ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}).
  • The key experiment is controlled and the effect large. Table 2 changes only parameterisation and objective: 13.51 to 3.17 FID, a factor of ({{Table 2|ax:S4.T2}}).
  • The authors state their weaknesses, to their credit: uncompetitive likelihoods ({{§4.3|ax:S4.SS3}}), FID against the training set with the test-set 5.24 given ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}), 1,000 evaluations per sample.
  • It held up: ε-prediction with a plain MSE trains the whole line that followed ({{Then and now|tab:t-then}}), and works on this page's toy ( of samples on the roll).

What to read with care.

  • Single runs. "Final experiments were trained once" ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}), so the IS ± is not a spread over runs (the paper does not say what it is; the standard IS code reports the spread over 10 splits of the samples), and gaps like 13.51 against 13.22 cannot be judged against noise.
  • Checkpoint chosen by the reported metric. Scores "are reported on the minimum FID value over the course of training" ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}), a training-set FID; and hyperparameters were tuned on CIFAR10 sample quality, the headline benchmark. The test-set 5.24 is the conservative reading.
  • A thin, overtaken margin. 3.17 against StyleGAN2 + ADA's 3.26 is FID, single runs, baselines quoted from other papers ({{Table 1|ax:S4.T1}}); ADA's later version reports 2.92 ± 0.05 unconditional with tuning ({{Karras et al., v2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.06676v2}}); on LSUN the paper trails StyleGAN ({{Table 3|ax:Ax1.T3}}).
  • Failures not analysed. Table 2's blank rows have no numbers or diagnosis; Improved DDPM later made learned variances work ({{Nichol and Dhariwal 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09672}}).
  • The toy reproduces the main effect, not every row. ε + Lsimple works and μ̃ + MSE fails, as in the paper; but μ̃ + true bound fails on the toy, and the toy's ε + true bound gets nearly the same samples and a slightly worse bound ( bits per dimension) where the paper's is better (3.70 against 3.75). At toy scale these say more about the toy than the paper.

Verdict: believe the recipe and the ablation's direction completely; they were reproduced and became the field's default. Read the 3.17 as one well-tuned run, selected on its own metric, that briefly led an unconditional benchmark by a margin a stronger GAN run later reversed; take the likelihoods as the paper does: honest, and not competitive.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

It turned a dormant 2015 idea into the dominant generative paradigm by finding the parameterisation that works: a fixed forward process, ε-prediction, an unweighted MSE. Nearly everything since descends from it:

  • Better DDPMs and guidance: {{Improved DDPM|https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.09672}} (learned variances, fewer steps) and {{classifier guidance, "Diffusion Models Beat GANs"|https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05233}} (Dhariwal and Nichol, FID 4.59 on ImageNet 256 × 256), then {{classifier-free guidance|https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.12598}}.
  • Fast sampling: {{DDIM|https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.02502}} keeps the same training and samples deterministically 10 to 50 times faster in wall-clock time.
  • Text to image: {{latent diffusion|n:3c65c17b0d0d81bb98adc0daf8462cb6}} (Stable Diffusion) moves the chain into a VAE latent space; {{Imagen|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11487}} and {{DALL-E 2|https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.06125}} do text-to-image with diffusion.
  • New backbones: {{DiT|https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09748}} swaps the U-Net for a Transformer on latent patches; OpenAI's Sora report says diffusion transformers "scale effectively as video models as well" ({{OpenAI 2024, archived copy|https://web.archive.org/web/2024/https://openai.com/index/video-generation-models-as-world-simulators/}}).
  • Reframed paths for few-step generation: {{consistency models|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.01469}}, {{flow matching|https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.02747}} and {{rectified flow|https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.03003}}.
  • One framework: the score-matching connection made explicit here was generalised by {{Song et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456}} into the SDE framework that unifies DDPM and NCSN.
  • Beyond images: the training recipe (sample t, add noise, predict it, MSE) spread to audio ({{WaveGrad|https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.00713}}, {{DiffWave|https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.09761}}), video ({{Video Diffusion Models|https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.03458}}), molecules ({{equivariant diffusion for 3-D molecules|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.17003}}), robot policies ({{Diffusion Policy|https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.04137}}) and text ({{Diffusion-LM|https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.14217}}; {{DiffusionGemma|n:3c65c17b0d0d818390bfd7969757211e}}).

On-Policy or Off-Policy Learning? A Systematic Study of Distillation Dynamics (rollout policy against KL direction)

Julianna Piskorz*, Antonin Berthon*, Mihaela van der Schaar (* equal contribution)
University of Cambridge · 28 September 2026 (arXiv v1, the only version on 19 October 2026)

In one line: In controlled strong-to-weak distillation of 1B students on short answers (about 100 tokens, three seeds), the KL direction and the learning rate matter more than who wrote the rollouts: forward KL reaches 71 to 73% whoever writes them, reverse KL needs the student's own rollouts at the lower learning rate (72% against 35%) and is erratic at the higher one, and forgetting and update sparsity follow the learning rate. The on-policy edge in pass@10 on a harder task did not carry into a later RLVR stage (one RL seed), and long reasoning traces were not tested at scale.

Problem

§1, §2

Distillation pushes a small student to give the same next-token probabilities as a larger teacher at every position of an answer (the method in general: Distillation and Small Language Models). The answer whose positions are graded is the rollout, and the model that wrote it is the rollout policy.

Two choices of rollout policy have names (§2). Off-policy distillation (OffPD) grades text the teacher wrote: it can be generated once and reused, like a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) dataset. On-policy distillation (OnPD) grades text the student writes, fresh at every step, as reinforcement learning (RL) does: the student goes where it would go at test time, and the teacher says what it should have done there (Agarwal et al., 2023). On-policy costs more: the student must generate throughout training.

A run of 2025 and 2026 papers argued that "on-policy" is what makes RL better than SFT: it forgets less of what the model already knew (Shenfeld et al., Chen et al.), changes fewer parameters (Mukherjee et al.) and generalises better (Chu et al.). The paper's objection is that SFT against RL changes many things at once: the objective, the reward, how dense the supervision is, the optimiser settings and, often, the learning rate (§1, §2). Any of those could cause the difference.

The question: if you change only who writes the rollouts, and keep everything else fixed, do the claimed benefits of on-policy training appear? Distillation is the place to ask it, because the teacher can grade any text, so the rollout policy can be swapped without touching the rest of the pipeline.

Two knobs, usually turned together

§3, Eq. 1 to 3, Appendix C.5

The training loss (Eq. 1) averages a per-token divergence over the positions of rollouts drawn from a rollout policy ρ:

ℒ(θ) = Ex Ey ∼ ρ [ (1/Ly) Σn 𝒟( πS(· | x, y<n) , πT(· | x, y<n) ) ]

πS and πT are the student's and teacher's next-token distributions at the prefix y<n (the answer so far); ρ = πT is OffPD, ρ = πS is OnPD. Gradients are not taken through the sampling: the rollouts are treated as fixed data at each step.

So there are two separate choices. ρ decides which prefixes are visited. 𝒟 decides how the two distributions are compared at each prefix. The paper uses the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KL) in one of two directions, computed exactly over the whole vocabulary (Eq. 2 and 3):

forward KL = Σv πT(v) log [ πT(v) / πS(v) ]

Weighted by the teacher. Punishes the student for missing any token the teacher likes: mass-covering.

reverse KL = Σv πS(v) log [ πS(v) / πT(v) ]

Weighted by the student. Punishes the student for putting mass where the teacher puts little: mode-seeking.

The two shapes are animated on the Distillation and Small Language Models page ("Forward or reverse KL"); this page does not repeat it. In practice the knobs come paired: classic distillation is teacher text with forward KL, on-policy distillation is student text with reverse KL. The paper gives the two reasons (§3). Over whole sequences, teacher rollouts with forward KL and student rollouts with reverse KL are exactly the two chain-rule expansions of sequence-level KL (Proposition C.4); and if the KL is estimated from the one token in the rollout, the token's source fixes the direction. Neither holds once the KL is summed over the full vocabulary, so the paper crosses the pairings and runs all four combinations.

The four combinations, each at two learning rates, are the grid at the top of the page.

Before the results: across the three tasks and two learning rates, which choice moves final accuracy more?

The KL direction. Forward KL lands at 71 to 73% in all four of its cells (both rollout policies, both learning rates); reverse KL ranges from 35% to 72%. The best on-policy and best off-policy runs differ by one point: 72% against 73% (§4.2). The chart in Result shows every cell.

Setup

§4.1, Appendix D
  • Models. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct teachers distilled into Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct students, which share a tokenizer so their token distributions can be compared directly; Qwen2.5-7B into Qwen2.5-1.5B repeats it in Appendix A.5.
  • Tasks. Three reasoning datasets: MedReason (medical multiple choice), Science (SciKnowEval multiple choice) and Countdown-3 (combine three numbers with + − × ÷ to hit a target). A separate teacher is trained for each task (SFT, then GRPO for Science and MedReason) so that it is clearly better than the student; Llama teachers score 85.5%, 67.5% and 99.0% (Table 2). Answers are short: the teachers average 94 to 135 tokens.
  • Training. 150 steps of full fine-tuning, 32 prompts per step, one rollout each, AdamW with no weight decay, gradient norm clipped at 1.0, learning rate 1 × 10−5 or 5 × 10−5, three seeds per setting (Appendix D.3, Table 3). The teacher's distribution is taken at temperature 0.5 and the student's at 1.0, both for sampling and inside the KL. That is 72 runs for the main grid.
  • Measures. Held-out accuracy on the task (200 questions per task at temperature 0.5); forgetting, the change in mean score over seven unrelated benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, TruthfulQA, IFEval, HumanEval, EQ-Bench, BBQ, ToxiGen; 3,935 items, D.1.2); and update sparsity, the share of parameters that moved by less than 10−6, as in Mukherjee et al. (D.4).

Learning rates of 10−5 are in the normal range for SFT of small models; the RL checkpoints whose sparse updates started the debate were trained at a median of 5 × 10−7 (next section).

Result: no consistent on-policy advantage

§4.2, Figures 1 and 2, Appendix E

The paper's Figure 1, rebuilt from its vector file. Switch the measure; each pair of bars is one rollout policy and KL direction at the two learning rates.

Means over three seeds (task accuracy also averaged over the three tasks), with the standard error of the mean (SEM) as whiskers, read from figure: the SVG's bars and points decoded on its gridlines; they match every label the figure prints. Forgetting is each point's out-of-distribution score minus the untrained student's (33.65%, read from the same figure). All values.

  • Accuracy. The best on-policy and off-policy settings reach 72% and 73%. Forward KL sits at 71 to 73% everywhere. Reverse KL is where the variation is: 72% on-policy and 35% off-policy at the lower learning rate, then 35% on-policy and 49% off-policy at the higher one (§4.2). So "no consistent advantage" means, for reverse KL, a large effect of the rollout policy whose sign flips with the learning rate; for forward KL, no effect.
  • Forgetting. At 10−5 every setting stays within 1.3 points of the untrained student's out-of-distribution score; at 5 × 10−5 every setting loses 11 to 14 points. Forward KL at the low rate reaches top accuracy without forgetting: the learning rate, not on-policy data, is what protects old skills here, which the paper likens to the learning-forgetting "cliff" Catalan-Tatjer and Geiping describe for SFT (§4.2).
  • Sparsity. 85.3 to 89.6% of parameters barely move at the low rate, 51.9 to 60.0% at the high rate; off-policy is at least as sparse as on-policy in every matched pair. A sweep of six learning rates on Countdown-3 shows sparsity falling roughly linearly with the learning rate under both rollout policies (Figure 2, not decoded here).

Why earlier work saw sparse RL updates. Appendix E lists the learning rates of the checkpoints Mukherjee et al. compared: the SFT checkpoints were trained at a median of 5 × 10−6, the nine RL and preference checkpoints with a disclosed rate at a median of 5 × 10−7, ten times lower (recomputed). Given the sweep above, the learning rate alone predicts the sparser RL updates. The paper does not rerun those checkpoints, so this is an alternative explanation, not a refutation.

You distil with forward KL at 5 × 10−5 and the student forgets 13 points of general ability. Which change is most likely to fix it, judging by these runs?

Lower the learning rate. Forward KL at 10−5 keeps its task accuracy (71 to 72%) and changes the out-of-distribution score by under one point, on either rollout policy. Switching rollout policy at 5 × 10−5 moves forgetting by about three points (11.2 against 14.1, read from figure), and reverse KL at that rate both forgets and loses accuracy. The paper's learning-rate sweep shows the same ordering at six rates.

Why: the two gradients react differently to the rollouts

§5.1, Eq. 4 and 5, Appendices B and C

Section 5 asks when the rollout policy matters at all, and starts from the gradient each divergence sends to the student's logits (the scores z before the softmax) at one prefix (Eq. 4 and 5, derived in Appendix B):

∂ forward KL / ∂zv = πS(v) − πT(v)

The same shape as cross-entropy's gradient on the logits (Loss functions). A difference of two probabilities: each entry lies in [−1, 1], and the whole vector's length is at most √2. It is non-zero wherever the two distributions differ, whoever is more confident.

∂ reverse KL / ∂zv = πS(v) [ log ( πS(v) / πT(v) ) − DR ]

Weighted by the student's own probability, times a log-ratio that has no upper limit. DR is the reverse KL at that prefix.

Two consequences, both visible on one prefix. Pick a case and move the slider; the student and teacher distributions are the paper's own two-token counterexample (Theorem C.2) or its mirror image.

Exact arithmetic, two-token vocabulary, direct logits (the setting of Theorem C.2). Gradient bars on one scale; the dashed lines at ±1 are the most any forward-KL entry can be. Defaults reproduce Equation 51's gradient (1/4) log((1 − δ)/δ) (1, −1) independently; the check.

  • Spikes. When the student puts real probability on a token the teacher considers nearly impossible, the reverse-KL gradient grows like log(1/δ), without limit. Forward KL's stays below √2.
  • Blind spots. When the teacher likes a token the student gives almost no probability, reverse KL's gradient on it is multiplied by that tiny probability and vanishes: reverse KL "may therefore struggle to recover teacher modes omitted by the student". Forward KL pushes that token up at full strength.

Averaged over a rollout policy's prefixes, the bounded gradient becomes a guarantee: forward-KL updates under two rollout policies differ by at most a constant times the distance between them (Theorem C.1), while reverse KL has no such bound (Theorem C.2) unless the spread of log-ratios at visited prefixes is capped (Corollary C.3).

The three results, with their constants (Appendix C)

Now average over the prefixes a rollout policy visits. Theorem C.1 turns the bounded gradient into a statement about rollout policies: if the student's logit Jacobian is bounded by B, the forward-KL updates under two rollout policies differ by at most 2√2 B times the total-variation distance between the sequence distributions they produce. Close rollout policies give close updates. Theorem C.2 shows reverse KL has no such bound: shift ε of the rollout probability onto a prefix like the first case above and the expected update moves by ε (√2/8) log((1 − δ)/δ), which grows without limit as δ shrinks. A bound returns (Corollary C.3) only if the spread of log-ratios at visited prefixes is capped at some R: then 2BR times the distance. With a vocabulary of over a hundred thousand tokens and a teacher sharpened to temperature 0.5, nothing keeps R small (this page's toy has 5 tokens and a spread of about 8 nats; Move the rollout policy).

The paper's reading (§5.1): forward KL gives useful signal at any prefix where the models disagree, so it should not care much who wrote the rollouts; reverse KL concentrates on tokens the student already likes and the teacher dislikes, so it "depends more strongly on visiting the student's own prefix distribution" and should favour on-policy rollouts. The authors are careful: neither result proves that reverse KL must be unstable in a given run (C.4). The Move the rollout policy tab computes both bounds on a small student and slides the rollout policy continuously.

The rollout-policy spectrum

§5.2, Figures 3 and 4, Appendix D.5

To test the prediction, the paper replaces the on/off switch with a dial. At each generation step it samples the next token from a blend of the two models' log-probabilities (§5.2, Eq. 75 and 76):

πλ(v) ∝ exp( ((1 + λ)/2) log πS(v) + ((1 − λ)/2) log πT(v) )

λ = −1 is the teacher (OffPD), λ = +1 the student (OnPD), λ = 0 their normalised geometric mean. Beyond ±1 the blend extrapolates: λ = 2 boosts tokens the student likes more than the teacher does. To stop it boosting tokens both models find unlikely, the extra push is given only to tokens with at least 20% of the favoured model's top probability and is capped at 2 log 3 in log-odds, so at λ = ±2 no pair of tokens has its odds changed by more than a factor of three (Appendix D.5; recomputed).

Nine values of λ from −2 to 2, both KL directions, both learning rates, three seeds: 108 runs on Countdown-3. The paper's Figure 3, rebuilt:

Means over three seeds, whiskers one SEM, read from figure (decoded from the vector SVG; tick fits exact to 10−5). Dashed: learning rate 10−5; solid: 5 × 10−5, as in the paper. Accuracy is on 200 Countdown-3 questions at temperature 0.5. All values.

At the lower learning rate, reverse KL trained on teacher-favoured rollouts (λ = −2 to −0.5) against student-favoured ones (λ = 0.5 to 2): what happens?

About 31 points worse: 49% on average for λ < 0 against 80% for λ > 0, and the SEM for λ < 0 is 11 to 17 points (one seed in three can fail outright). Forward KL at the same rate stays between 82.8% and 88.0% at every λ, a 5.2-point range, as the paper states (§5.2). At 5 × 10−5 reverse KL is erratic everywhere and collapses to 0% at λ = 1.5 (all three seeds).

Three things the figure shows beyond the text. Forward KL at the higher learning rate is the best setting on Countdown-3 (86.5 to 94.5%), so the low rate is not free: it trades some task accuracy for no forgetting. Forgetting and sparsity barely move with λ (out-of-distribution change −0.8 to +2.4 points at 10−5, −12.0 to −16.0 at 5 × 10−5): the learning rate sets them, as in Section 4. And on Qwen2.5 the same spectrum gives the same asymmetry, though at the high rate student-favoured rollouts forgot more (Appendix A.5), the opposite of the "on-policy forgets less" claim.

A low forward KL can hide nonsense rollouts (Appendix A.10)

Forward KL equals the cross-entropy from teacher to student minus the teacher's entropy, so a small KL can be the difference of two large numbers (Appendix A.10). In on-policy forward-KL runs on Science and MedReason, between steps 10 and 20 the KL got small while both entropies approached 12 nats, the scale of the whole vocabulary (e12 ≈ 163,000 equally likely tokens): the student was writing near-random text and the teacher, reading it, was just as lost. Useful signal survived only at early positions, and the coherent region then grew along the answer step by step. The step-50 students still scored 37% (Science) and 56% (MedReason) at evaluation temperature 0.5 and lost only 1.3 and 0.8 points out of distribution; resampled at temperature 1.0, the training temperature, accuracy fell sharply. Same final score, very different path: on-policy training can pass through a phase where the teacher's grades carry almost no information.

Beyond pass@1: coverage, harder tasks, RL afterwards

§5.3, Figures 5, 6, 12, 24, 26

Coverage. Pass@k is the chance that at least one of k samples is correct (pass@1 against pass@k under RL in general: RL for LLMs); pass@10 above pass@1 measures how many different correct answers the model can still find. The paper's Figure 5 evaluates the λ-spectrum checkpoints (learning rate 10−5) on Countdown-3 and, without further training, on Countdown-4E, a harder variant with four operands from 1 to 10:

Means over three distillation seeds, read from figure (the SEM caps decoded from the vector SVG). 200 questions, 10 samples each. Dashed: pass@1; solid: pass@10. All values.

  • Forward KL covers more. For λ > 0, reverse KL's pass@1 is as good as forward KL's or better (77.2 to 82.7% against 75.7 to 78.2%), but its pass@10 is lower (89.2 to 94.2% against 98.2 to 99.0%). This is the mode-seeking trade in numbers: reverse KL concentrates on fewer ways to be right. Qwen2.5 shows the same (Figure 16).
  • On-policy rollouts generalise better, by pass@10. On Countdown-4E, moving to λ > 0 raises pass@10 by 11.6 points (forward KL) and 13.3 points (reverse KL) on average, roughly the "10 to 15%" the text states. Pass@1 rises only 3.4 and 3.8 points, from 3.6% to 7.0% for forward KL. The text's "pass@k" covers both; the 10 to 15 applies to pass@10 only (recomputed). It is the one place in the paper where on-policy data clearly helps under both KL directions.
  • The edge does not survive RLVR. The Countdown-3 checkpoints then get 300 steps of GRPO on Countdown-4 (reward 2 if correct, else 0; one RL seed; three distillation seeds per setting). Decoded from Figure 6: off-policy forward-KL checkpoints, which started near zero, climb steadily and finish highest (mean reward 0.20 to 0.23 over the last 50 steps, about 11% correct). Reverse-KL checkpoints rise fast, five of six peak between steps 55 and 85, then fall back (off-policy ones to about 0.03, on-policy ones to 0.08 to 0.19). And the on-policy forward-KL checkpoints, the best Countdown-4E generalisers, never learn at all: peak reward at most 0.08, ending under 0.01. The paper's text does not mention that last result; it is the strongest evidence in the figure that a better distillation score is not a better starting point for RL.
  • Style. With a teacher told to reason in Spanish, every setting taught the student Spanish except on-policy reverse KL, which kept English (Appendix A.11): on English student prefixes the teacher still gives English continuations probability, and reverse KL can match that mode without covering the Spanish one. One run per condition, judged by GPT-5.

Do the findings survive changes to the design?

§6, Appendices A.1 to A.3, A.7, A.12

Section 6 removes three design choices that could have hidden an on-policy effect (§6):

  • Sampled instead of full-vocabulary KL (Appendix A.2). Recipes such as Thinking Machines' estimate the KL from one sampled token per position, which needs far less memory. The paper samples a fresh token per position (from the teacher for forward KL, from the student for reverse KL) independently of whose rollout it is, so all four combinations stay possible. Same conclusions: forward KL robust, reverse KL brittle, learning rate sets forgetting.
  • No gradient clipping (Appendix A.1). The unclipped gradient norm exceeds 1.0 at every logged step, so clipping was rescaling every update, and reverse KL's norms were larger, more variable and spiky early on (Figure 7). Without clipping, reverse KL deteriorates sharply while forward KL keeps working at larger learning rates, still with no consistent on-policy gap. The authors' explanation: Adam normalises steady changes in gradient scale away, but one early spike biases its momentum and inflates its second moment, which then suppresses corrective updates, and on-policy runs feed the damaged policy's own rollouts back in.
  • Longer answers (Appendix A.3). Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct distilled into Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on 20,000 MATH and NuminaMath problems, teacher answers averaging 622 tokens, 1,000 steps. Every setting beats the untrained 53.1% on MATH-500 (best checkpoints 53.3 to 59.0%), and on-policy reverse KL at the low rate is best. Learning rate again sets forgetting (out-of-distribution score kept near 41.4% at 10−5, down to 33.7 to 35.9% at 5 × 10−5) and sparsity (92 to 93% against 63 to 70%). One run per condition, and each condition reports its best checkpoint chosen on MATH-500 itself; the authors call the on-policy advantage "suggestive" and say the maxima may be optimistic.
  • Temperatures and threshold. Swapping the generation temperatures (on-policy sampled at 0.5, off-policy at 1.0) on MedReason leaves forward KL at the low rate within 0.3 points (Appendix A.12); reverse KL swings by up to 23 points (table). A stricter sparsity threshold (10−8) changes nothing qualitative (Appendix A.7).

How much of this to believe

§4 to §7, Appendices A, C, D

What holds up. The design is the paper's strength: one factor changed at a time, the same pipeline, three seeds with standard errors for the main grid and the spectrum, two model families, and ablations aimed at the obvious objections (sampled KL, clipping, longer answers, temperature). The gradient analysis is correct and honestly scoped: it is about the semi-gradient actually used, the forward-KL bound needs a bounded Jacobian, and the authors say the reverse-KL result permits instability rather than proving it. This page decoded Figures 1, 3, 5, 6 and 26 from their vector files; the numbers match every printed label and nearly every number in the text (claims checked). The authors state their own limits (students of at most 1.5B, traces of at most 2,000 tokens, one fixed teacher per task), which counts in their favour.

What is weaker, concretely:

  • small and short The regime where on-policy distillation is supposed to pay, long reasoning traces where a student drifts far from anything the teacher wrote, is exactly what the main grid does not test: teacher answers average 94 to 135 tokens and training is 150 steps. The one long-answer run is where on-policy reverse KL came out best, with a single seed and checkpoints selected on the test benchmark.
  • power "No consistent advantage" is a null result from three seeds. A run's accuracy on 200 questions has a sampling standard error of about 3.2 points at 70% (derived), and the decoded SEMs over seeds are 2.8 to 5.7 points for forward KL in Figure 1. A one-point gap (72 against 73) says the effect is small, not that it is zero.
  • temperature confound Off-policy rollouts are sampled at the teacher's temperature 0.5 and on-policy at the student's 1.0, so for the main grid "who wrote it" also means "how sharp the text is". The swap ablation (one dataset) leaves forward KL unchanged but moves reverse KL by up to 23 points; the spectrum runs, which mix both models at temperature 1.0, address this for Countdown-3.
  • text against figures The "10 to 15% higher pass@k" for on-policy generalisation holds for pass@10, not pass@1 (3 to 4 points). The RLVR text omits that on-policy forward-KL checkpoints never learned at all. Appendix A.11's last paragraph credits "OnPD with forward KL" for resisting style transfer, where its own result (and its next sentence) is about reverse KL. None changes a main conclusion; each is a place to read the figure, not the sentence.
  • single runs The RLVR follow-up uses one RL seed (three distillation seeds), reports training rewards rather than held-out accuracy, and trains LoRA adapters on a 4-bit model where distillation used full fine-tuning. The Spanish style test is one run per condition with one GPT-5 judge.
  • no release No code, checkpoints or logs. The appendix settings are detailed enough to reimplement, but nothing can be rerun as published.
What this page's toy adds (a 184-parameter student, five tokens)

This page's toy (Move the rollout policy) adds one data point: on a 184-parameter student with a five-token vocabulary, forward KL was insensitive to λ and kept more correct routes, as the paper reports, but reverse KL was just as insensitive, and clipping never fired. Both bounds were computed exactly there; the toy's log-ratios are simply too mild for reverse KL's sensitivity to show, which is consistent with the paper's own diagnosis (extreme log-ratios at visited prefixes) rather than evidence against it.

Verdict. Believe the central, carefully tested claim for small students and short answers: forward KL with a sensible learning rate gets top accuracy whoever writes the rollouts, reverse KL needs the student's own rollouts and clipping, and forgetting and update sparsity follow the learning rate, not the rollout policy. Treat the generalisation, RLVR, style and long-answer results as leads, and do not extend "on-policy is not needed" to long reasoning traces, which the paper did not test at scale.

What it takes to use this

Appendix D
  • Code: none released. The method is a training loop you likely have: a distillation loss over teacher and student logits with a choice of rollout source and divergence. Full-vocabulary KL needs both models' logits at every answer position; the sampled estimator of Appendix A.2 needs one token per position and gave the same conclusions.
  • Default it supports: teacher-written text (generate once, reuse) with forward KL, full vocabulary, at a low learning rate (10−5 for a 1B student here), with gradient clipping. It reached the best accuracy without measurable forgetting.
  • If you use reverse KL (the common on-policy recipe): use the student's own rollouts and keep clipping on; reverse KL on teacher text at the low rate scored 35%, tied for worst in the grid with reverse KL at the high rate. Monitor the spread of student-teacher log-ratios at visited tokens, the diagnostic Corollary C.3 suggests.
  • Budget: on-policy distillation adds student generation at every step (the paper uses vLLM colocated, synchronised before each rollout batch); off-policy pays for teacher generation once. The paper reports no GPU hours.
  • Pays off: on-policy rollouts bought better pass@10 on a harder variant of the training task, and possibly on long math answers. Buys little: in-distribution accuracy, forgetting and sparsity. If RL follows distillation, pick the distillation checkpoint by how it does after RL: the best distilled generaliser here was the worst RL starting point.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

  • On-policy distillation became a default step of 2025 and 2026 recipes. Qwen3's small models were distilled on their own rollouts after an off-policy stage (Qwen3 Technical Report), and Thinking Machines' write-up made the reverse-KL recipe widely copied (blog); a survey of the method appeared in April 2026 (arXiv 2604.00626). This paper says that much of what is credited to "on-policy" in that recipe belongs to its divergence and its learning rate.
Same weeks, beyond the paper (read 19 October 2026)
  • Other work in the same weeks points the same way. NVIDIA's Semi-OPD reuses rollouts the initial student wrote once and beat full on-policy distillation in 14 of 17 teacher-student pairs, finding on-policy sampling helps only when teacher and student already agree on most tokens (arXiv 2610.11291, 8 October 2026). Cui et al. read on-policy distillation as RL against the teacher's implicit reward and show it collapsing into long repetitive text when that reward is wrong (arXiv 2610.03185). RIDE moves distillation into representation space along the direction RL moved the teacher (arXiv 2609.36484). The knowledge base's 2026-10-19 tech news issue reported them together.
  • It reframes the SFT-against-RL debate. If learning rate explains forgetting and sparsity in distillation, comparisons of public SFT and RL checkpoints trained at rates ten times apart (Appendix E) need a matched-rate rerun before "on-policy forgets less" is accepted.

Move the rollout policy

The paper's Section 5 in miniature, computed live. A teacher and a small student answer 12 prompts; every answer is two tokens, a route (5 choices) and then an answer (5 choices), and for each prompt and route one answer is correct. The teacher prefers two routes per prompt and knows the answers; the student starts with a habit, one route the teacher seldom takes. The loss is the paper's (Eq. 1: exact token-level KL over the whole vocabulary at both positions), the rollouts come from the paper's πλ with its safeguards (Appendix D.5), and training is Adam with the gradient clipped at 1.0, 32 rollouts per step, 150 steps, as in Table 3. The teacher's logits are divided by 0.5 and the student's by 1.0, as in the paper.

Two students: direct logits (one free number per prefix and token, 360 in all; its logit Jacobian is the identity, so Theorem C.1's B is exactly 1) and a shared network (one hidden layer of 6 tanh units, 184 parameters, which cannot memorise all 60 answers and so has to compromise, as a real student must). Every number below is computed in your browser by the same code that produced the recorded sweep; the engine is checked against PyTorch (the check). Illustrative task: nothing here is a measurement of a language model.

1. Slide λ: who writes the rollouts, and how much the update moves

Top: the first-token distributions at the start of training for the chosen prompt. Bottom: the exact expected update (the semi-gradient of Appendix C, averaged over every prompt and route under πλ) minus the purely off-policy update at λ = −1 (the length of the difference). The boxes give the total-variation distance between the sequence distributions the two rollout policies produce and, for the direct-logit student, the paper's bounds: 2√2 × distance for forward KL (Theorem C.1) and 2R × distance for reverse KL (Corollary C.3), with R the largest log-ratio spread at any prefix. Both updates stay far inside their bounds.

On the direct-logit student, going from teacher-written (λ = −1) to student-written rollouts (λ = +1) moves forward KL's expected update by 0.085, well under its bound of 2.4. Reverse KL's bound is 14.0, six times looser. How far does reverse KL's update actually move?

Only a little more. With 5 tokens, the student's and teacher's log-probabilities differ by at most about 8 nats here, and few visited prefixes put real student mass on a token the teacher rules out, so reverse KL's update moves 0.104, about a fifth more than forward KL's and far below its bound (the shared network gives 0.27 against 0.34). Theorem C.2 is a worst case: it needs a prefix like the one in the paper tab's gradient card. Slide λ above and watch the two bottom curves.

2. Train along the spectrum

Means over three seeds (whiskers one SEM), each run 150 steps of 32 rollouts, evaluated exactly (every route and answer enumerated) at temperature 1. Dotted: the teacher (green) and the untrained student (grey). The recorded sweep comes from src/toy_sweep.mjs; the button recomputes the selected panel and compares.

Reproduces, and does not reproduce
  • reproduces Forward KL is insensitive to the rollout policy: on the shared network its pass@1 moves by points across all nine λ at lr 0.02, against the paper's 5.2.
  • reproduces Forward KL keeps more ways to be right: more distinct correct routes than reverse KL at every λ and both learning rates, the direction of the paper's pass@10 result.
  • does not reproduce Reverse KL's sensitivity: on the toy it is just as flat as forward KL (range points at lr 0.02) and is the more accurate direction, as on the Distillation page's toy. The paper's 31-point gap needs prefixes with extreme log-ratios; here the log-ratio spread is about 8 nats and the batch gradient norm never reaches the clipping threshold (about 0.7 at the first step, under 0.25 after step 25), so clipping changes nothing and was not shown. The task was not retuned to force the effect (design log).

4. What the toy shows, what it cannot, and how it was checked

  • Shows: the paper's loss, rollout spectrum and safeguards running exactly; forward KL's bounded sensitivity to the rollout policy (Theorem C.1) holding on a real student; the coverage trade between the two directions.
  • Cannot show: anything that depends on scale. Five tokens cannot produce the extreme log-ratios that make reverse KL fragile in a 1B model, two-token answers cannot drift the way 1,000-token traces do, and the toy has no "general ability" to forget, so forgetting and sparsity are not modelled.
  • Design log: a first version with direct logits only showed nothing at all (every setting reached the teacher's 0.85, because Adam moves every visited logit at the learning rate however small its gradient). It was replaced once, before its results were seen, by the shared network; the learning rates and everything else were fixed in advance. Both are kept and shown. The log is src/inputs/toy_design_log.txt.

Gains and Collapse in On-Policy Distillation, arXiv 2610.03185

Do the findings survive changes to the design?

[[S6|§6, Appendices A.1 to A.3, A.7, A.12]]

Section 6 removes three design choices that could have hidden an on-policy effect ({{§6|ax:S6}}):

  • Sampled instead of full-vocabulary KL ({{Appendix A.2|ax:A1.SS2}}). Recipes such as {{Thinking Machines'|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/}} estimate the KL from one sampled token per position, which needs far less memory. The paper samples a fresh token per position (from the teacher for forward KL, from the student for reverse KL) independently of whose rollout it is, so all four combinations stay possible. Same conclusions: forward KL robust, reverse KL brittle, learning rate sets forgetting.
  • No gradient clipping ({{Appendix A.1|ax:A1.SS1}}). The unclipped gradient norm exceeds 1.0 at every logged step, so clipping was rescaling every update, and reverse KL's norms were larger, more variable and spiky early on ({{Figure 7|ax:A1.F7}}). Without clipping, reverse KL deteriorates sharply while forward KL keeps working at larger learning rates, still with no consistent on-policy gap. The authors' explanation: Adam normalises steady changes in gradient scale away, but one early spike biases its momentum and inflates its second moment, which then suppresses corrective updates, and on-policy runs feed the damaged policy's own rollouts back in.
  • Longer answers ({{Appendix A.3|ax:A1.SS3}}). Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct distilled into Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on 20,000 MATH and NuminaMath problems, teacher answers averaging 622 tokens, 1,000 steps. Every setting beats the untrained 53.1% on MATH-500 (best checkpoints 53.3 to 59.0%), and on-policy reverse KL at the low rate is best. Learning rate again sets forgetting (out-of-distribution score kept near 41.4% at 10−5, down to 33.7 to 35.9% at 5 × 10−5) and sparsity (92 to 93% against 63 to 70%). One run per condition, and each condition reports its best checkpoint chosen on MATH-500 itself; the authors call the on-policy advantage "suggestive" and say the maxima may be optimistic.
  • Temperatures and threshold. Swapping the generation temperatures (on-policy sampled at 0.5, off-policy at 1.0) on MedReason leaves forward KL at the low rate within 0.3 points ({{Appendix A.12|ax:A1.SS12}}); reverse KL swings by up to 23 points ({{table|tab:t-tables:tbA12}}). A stricter sparsity threshold (10−8) changes nothing qualitative ({{Appendix A.7|ax:A1.SS7}}).

How much of this to believe

[[S7|§4 to §7, Appendices A, C, D]]

What holds up. The design is the paper's strength: one factor changed at a time, the same pipeline, three seeds with standard errors for the main grid and the spectrum, two model families, and ablations aimed at the obvious objections (sampled KL, clipping, longer answers, temperature). The gradient analysis is correct and honestly scoped: it is about the semi-gradient actually used, the forward-KL bound needs a bounded Jacobian, and the authors say the reverse-KL result permits instability rather than proving it. This page decoded Figures 1, 3, 5, 6 and 26 from their vector files; the numbers match every printed label and nearly every number in the text ({{claims checked|tab:t-tables:tbClaims}}). The authors state their own limits (students of at most 1.5B, traces of at most 2,000 tokens, one fixed teacher per task), which counts in their favour.

What is weaker, concretely:

  • small and short The regime where on-policy distillation is supposed to pay, long reasoning traces where a student drifts far from anything the teacher wrote, is exactly what the main grid does not test: teacher answers average 94 to 135 tokens and training is 150 steps. The one long-answer run is where on-policy reverse KL came out best, with a single seed and checkpoints selected on the test benchmark.
  • power "No consistent advantage" is a null result from three seeds. A run's accuracy on 200 questions has a sampling standard error of about 3.2 points at 70% (derived), and the decoded SEMs over seeds are 2.8 to 5.7 points for forward KL in Figure 1. A one-point gap (72 against 73) says the effect is small, not that it is zero.
  • temperature confound Off-policy rollouts are sampled at the teacher's temperature 0.5 and on-policy at the student's 1.0, so for the main grid "who wrote it" also means "how sharp the text is". The swap ablation (one dataset) leaves forward KL unchanged but moves reverse KL by up to 23 points; the spectrum runs, which mix both models at temperature 1.0, address this for Countdown-3.
  • text against figures The "10 to 15% higher pass@k" for on-policy generalisation holds for pass@10, not pass@1 (3 to 4 points). The RLVR text omits that on-policy forward-KL checkpoints never learned at all. Appendix A.11's last paragraph credits "OnPD with forward KL" for resisting style transfer, where its own result (and its next sentence) is about reverse KL. None changes a main conclusion; each is a place to read the figure, not the sentence.
  • single runs The RLVR follow-up uses one RL seed (three distillation seeds), reports training rewards rather than held-out accuracy, and trains LoRA adapters on a 4-bit model where distillation used full fine-tuning. The Spanish style test is one run per condition with one GPT-5 judge.
  • no release No code, checkpoints or logs. The appendix settings are detailed enough to reimplement, but nothing can be rerun as published.

This page's toy ({{Move the rollout policy|tab:t-run}}) adds one data point: on a 184-parameter student with a five-token vocabulary, forward KL was insensitive to λ and kept more correct routes, as the paper reports, but reverse KL was just as insensitive, and clipping never fired. Both bounds were computed exactly there; the toy's log-ratios are simply too mild for reverse KL's sensitivity to show, which is consistent with the paper's own diagnosis (extreme log-ratios at visited prefixes) rather than evidence against it.

Verdict. Believe the central, carefully tested claim for small students and short answers: forward KL with a sensible learning rate gets top accuracy whoever writes the rollouts, reverse KL needs the student's own rollouts and clipping, and forgetting and update sparsity follow the learning rate, not the rollout policy. Treat the generalisation, RLVR, style and long-answer results as leads, and do not extend "on-policy is not needed" to long reasoning traces, which the paper did not test at scale.

What it takes to use this

[[A4|Appendix D]]
  • Code: none released. The method is a training loop you likely have: a distillation loss over teacher and student logits with a choice of rollout source and divergence. Full-vocabulary KL needs both models' logits at every answer position; the sampled estimator of {{Appendix A.2|ax:A1.SS2}} needs one token per position and gave the same conclusions.
  • Default it supports: teacher-written text (generate once, reuse) with forward KL, full vocabulary, at a low learning rate (10−5 for a 1B student here), with gradient clipping. It reached the best accuracy without measurable forgetting.
  • If you use reverse KL (the common on-policy recipe): use the student's own rollouts and keep clipping on; reverse KL on teacher text at the low rate scored 35%, tied for worst in the grid with reverse KL at the high rate. Monitor the spread of student-teacher log-ratios at visited tokens, the diagnostic Corollary C.3 suggests.
  • Budget: on-policy distillation adds student generation at every step (the paper uses vLLM colocated, synchronised before each rollout batch); off-policy pays for teacher generation once. The paper reports no GPU hours.
  • Pays off: on-policy rollouts bought better pass@10 on a harder variant of the training task, and possibly on long math answers. Buys little: in-distribution accuracy, forgetting and sparsity. If RL follows distillation, pick the distillation checkpoint by how it does after RL: the best distilled generaliser here was the worst RL starting point.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

  • On-policy distillation became a default step of 2025 and 2026 recipes. Qwen3's small models were distilled on their own rollouts after an off-policy stage ({{Qwen3 Technical Report|n:3c65c17b0d0d81a19006e6b096a6e14b}}), and Thinking Machines' write-up made the reverse-KL recipe widely copied ({{blog|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/on-policy-distillation/}}); a survey of the method appeared in April 2026 ({{arXiv 2604.00626|https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00626}}). This paper says that much of what is credited to "on-policy" in that recipe belongs to its divergence and its learning rate.
  • Other work in the same weeks points the same way. NVIDIA's Semi-OPD reuses rollouts the initial student wrote once and beat full on-policy distillation in 14 of 17 teacher-student pairs, finding on-policy sampling helps only when teacher and student already agree on most tokens ({{arXiv 2610.11291|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11291}}, 8 October 2026). Cui et al. read on-policy distillation as RL against the teacher's implicit reward and show it collapsing into long repetitive text when that reward is wrong ({{arXiv 2610.03185|https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03185}}). RIDE moves distillation into representation space along the direction RL moved the teacher ({{arXiv 2609.36484|https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36484}}). The knowledge base's {{2026-10-19 tech news|n:3fe5c17b0d0d81828721e0f0f189eb76}} issue reported them together.
  • It reframes the SFT-against-RL debate. If learning rate explains forgetting and sparsity in distillation, comparisons of public SFT and RL checkpoints trained at rates ten times apart (Appendix E) need a matched-rate rerun before "on-policy forgets less" is accepted.

Problem

[[S1|§1, §2]]

Distillation trains a small student model to imitate a larger teacher. In language models the teacher's opinion is read one token at a time: at every position of an answer, the teacher gives a probability for each of the vocabulary's tokens, and the student is pushed to give the same probabilities. The answer whose positions are graded is called a rollout, and the model that wrote it is the rollout policy.

Two choices of rollout policy have names ({{§2|ax:S2}}). Off-policy distillation (OffPD) grades text the teacher wrote: it can be generated once and reused, like a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) dataset. On-policy distillation (OnPD) grades text the student writes, fresh at every step, as reinforcement learning (RL) does: the student goes where it would go at test time, and the teacher says what it should have done there ({{Agarwal et al., 2023|https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13649}}). On-policy costs more: the student must generate throughout training.

A run of 2025 and 2026 papers argued that "on-policy" is what makes RL better than SFT: it forgets less of what the model already knew ({{Shenfeld et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04259}}, {{Chen et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18874}}), changes fewer parameters ({{Mukherjee et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11711}}) and generalises better ({{Chu et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17161}}). The paper's objection is that SFT against RL changes many things at once: the objective, the reward, how dense the supervision is, the optimiser settings and, often, the learning rate ({{§1|ax:S1}}, {{§2|ax:S2}}). Any of those could cause the difference.

The question: if you change only who writes the rollouts, and keep everything else fixed, do the claimed benefits of on-policy training appear? Distillation is the place to ask it, because the teacher can grade any text, so the rollout policy can be swapped without touching the rest of the pipeline.

Two knobs, usually turned together

[[S3|§3, Eq. 1 to 3, Appendix C.5]]

The training loss ({{Eq. 1|ax:S3.E1}}) averages a per-token divergence over the positions of rollouts drawn from a rollout policy ρ:

ℒ(θ) = Ex Ey ∼ ρ [ (1/Ly) Σn 𝒟( πS(· | x, y<n) , πT(· | x, y<n) ) ]

πS and πT are the student's and teacher's next-token distributions at the prefix y<n (the answer so far); ρ = πT is OffPD, ρ = πS is OnPD. Gradients are not taken through the sampling: the rollouts are treated as fixed data at each step.

So there are two separate choices. ρ decides which prefixes are visited. 𝒟 decides how the two distributions are compared at each prefix. The paper uses the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KL) in one of two directions, computed exactly over the whole vocabulary ({{Eq. 2 and 3|ax:S3.E2}}):

forward KL = Σv πT(v) log [ πT(v) / πS(v) ]

Weighted by the teacher. Punishes the student for missing any token the teacher likes: mass-covering.

reverse KL = Σv πS(v) log [ πS(v) / πT(v) ]

Weighted by the student. Punishes the student for putting mass where the teacher puts little: mode-seeking.

The two shapes are animated on the {{Distillation and Small Language Models|n:3c65c17b0d0d81efa2a8c3b3b623eb09}} page ("Forward or reverse KL"); this page does not repeat it. In practice the knobs come paired: classic distillation is teacher text with forward KL, on-policy distillation is student text with reverse KL. The paper gives the two reasons ({{§3|ax:S3}}). Over whole sequences, teacher rollouts with forward KL and student rollouts with reverse KL are exactly the two chain-rule expansions of sequence-level KL ({{Proposition C.4|ax:A3.SS5}}); and if the KL is estimated from the one token in the rollout, the token's source fixes the direction. Neither holds once the KL is summed over the full vocabulary, so the paper crosses the pairings and runs all four combinations.

The 2 × 2 the paper runs, at two learning rates and three seeds each. Diagonal: the conventional pairings. Off the diagonal: the crossed pairings nobody usually runs. Values are mean held-out accuracy over the three tasks at the learning rate where each does best (read from {{Figure 1|ax:S4.F1}}).

Before the results: across the three tasks and two learning rates, which choice moves final accuracy more?

The KL direction. Forward KL lands at 71 to 73% in all four of its cells (both rollout policies, both learning rates); reverse KL ranges from 35% to 72%. The best on-policy and best off-policy runs differ by one point: 72% against 73% ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}). The chart in {{Result|tab:t-read:s-res}} shows every cell.

Setup

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, Appendix D]]
  • Models. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct teachers distilled into Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct students, which share a tokenizer so their token distributions can be compared directly; Qwen2.5-7B into Qwen2.5-1.5B repeats it in {{Appendix A.5|ax:A1.SS5}}.
  • Tasks. Three reasoning datasets: MedReason (medical multiple choice), Science (SciKnowEval multiple choice) and Countdown-3 (combine three numbers with + − × ÷ to hit a target). A separate teacher is trained for each task (SFT, then GRPO for Science and MedReason) so that it is clearly better than the student; Llama teachers score 85.5%, 67.5% and 99.0% ({{Table 2|ax:A4.T2}}). Answers are short: the teachers average 94 to 135 tokens.
  • Training. 150 steps of full fine-tuning, 32 prompts per step, one rollout each, AdamW with no weight decay, gradient norm clipped at 1.0, learning rate 1 × 10−5 or 5 × 10−5, three seeds per setting ({{Appendix D.3, Table 3|ax:A4.SS3}}). The teacher's distribution is taken at temperature 0.5 and the student's at 1.0, both for sampling and inside the KL. That is 72 runs for the main grid.
  • Measures. Held-out accuracy on the task (200 questions per task at temperature 0.5); forgetting, the change in mean score over seven unrelated benchmarks (MMLU-Pro, TruthfulQA, IFEval, HumanEval, EQ-Bench, BBQ, ToxiGen; 3,935 items, {{D.1.2|ax:A4.SS1}}); and update sparsity, the share of parameters that moved by less than 10−6, as in Mukherjee et al. ({{D.4|ax:A4.SS4}}).

Learning rates of 10−5 are in the normal range for SFT of small models; the RL checkpoints whose sparse updates started the debate were trained at a median of 5 × 10−7 (next section).

Result: no consistent on-policy advantage

[[S4.SS2|§4.2, Figures 1 and 2, Appendix E]]

The paper's {{Figure 1|ax:S4.F1}}, rebuilt from its vector file. Switch the measure; each pair of bars is one rollout policy and KL direction at the two learning rates.

Means over three seeds (task accuracy also averaged over the three tasks), with the standard error of the mean (SEM) as whiskers, read from figure: the SVG's bars and points decoded on its gridlines; they match every label the figure prints. Forgetting is each point's out-of-distribution score minus the untrained student's (33.65%, read from the same figure). {{All values|tab:t-tables:tbF1}}.

  • Accuracy. The best on-policy and off-policy settings reach 72% and 73%. Forward KL sits at 71 to 73% everywhere. Reverse KL is where the variation is: 72% on-policy and 35% off-policy at the lower learning rate, then 35% on-policy and 49% off-policy at the higher one ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}). So "no consistent advantage" means, for reverse KL, a large effect of the rollout policy whose sign flips with the learning rate; for forward KL, no effect.
  • Forgetting. At 10−5 every setting stays within 1.3 points of the untrained student's out-of-distribution score; at 5 × 10−5 every setting loses 11 to 14 points. Forward KL at the low rate reaches top accuracy without forgetting: the learning rate, not on-policy data, is what protects old skills here, which the paper likens to the learning-forgetting "cliff" Catalan-Tatjer and Geiping describe for SFT ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}).
  • Sparsity. 85.3 to 89.6% of parameters barely move at the low rate, 51.9 to 60.0% at the high rate; off-policy is at least as sparse as on-policy in every matched pair. A sweep of six learning rates on Countdown-3 shows sparsity falling roughly linearly with the learning rate under both rollout policies ({{Figure 2|ax:S4.F2}}, not decoded here).

Why earlier work saw sparse RL updates. {{Appendix E|ax:A5}} lists the learning rates of the checkpoints Mukherjee et al. compared: the SFT checkpoints were trained at a median of 5 × 10−6, the nine RL and preference checkpoints with a disclosed rate at a median of 5 × 10−7, ten times lower ({{recomputed|tab:t-tables:tbT6}}). Given the sweep above, the learning rate alone predicts the sparser RL updates. The paper does not rerun those checkpoints, so this is an alternative explanation, not a refutation.

You distil with forward KL at 5 × 10−5 and the student forgets 13 points of general ability. Which change is most likely to fix it, judging by these runs?

Lower the learning rate. Forward KL at 10−5 keeps its task accuracy (71 to 72%) and changes the out-of-distribution score by under one point, on either rollout policy. Switching rollout policy at 5 × 10−5 moves forgetting by about three points (11.2 against 14.1, read from figure), and reverse KL at that rate both forgets and loses accuracy. The paper's {{learning-rate sweep|ax:S4.F2}} shows the same ordering at six rates.

Result 1: Qwen3-8B on Countdown-Code

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, Figure 2, Appendices C and D]]

The environment ({{Khalifa et al., 2026|https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07084}}; prompt in {{Appendix C|ax:A3}}). Countdown: reach a target from a list of integers, using each once. The prompt hands the model two files, solution.py with expr = None and test.py with verify_solution, and asks for both back. Full reward if the returned solution passes the returned test. Three hacks are always possible: 1, trivial test (rewrite the test to always pass), 2, constraint removal (drop the "each number once" check), 3, problem substitution (change the inputs to an easier problem). The monitor catches every hack 1 and nothing else. The base model is Qwen3-8B after the environment's SFT priming, which hacks 4.7% of the time (2.0 / 1.6 / 0.8 plus 0.4 other). "Clean solve rate" is measured on a variant with no files to edit, where hacking is impossible.

Under no intervention two of three seeds collapsed into trivial-test hacking (99% and 98%) and lost the task; the third hacked 41%. The reward penalty removed hack 1 and taught hack 2 instead: 25.9% of answers drop the constraint check, and the monitor sees 0.5% ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}} says "about 26%"). Since the proxy reward of all three penalty seeds is 1.000, the run looks perfect. GRAFT, forget adapter deleted, hacks 1.6% (base 4.7%) and solves 74.8%, 16.2 points above the base model; the penalty solves 77.2%. Without routing (same adapters, same anchor, monitor unused) it still hacks 21.9%: the routing, not the anchor, does the work.

How the replay was checked (Figure 2 against the released data)

Each point above is computed from the authors' released per-seed evaluations ({{github.com/jnward/GRAFT|https://github.com/jnward/GRAFT}}, commit b0767d1c, rl/paper_data, 256 prompts per seed), with the arms their figure script uses. All eight means land on the figure's vector geometry within 0.01 points, and GRAFT's row reproduces Table 4 (79.2 ± 1.0 task performance, 1.6 ± 0.4 hacking), independently. Two metrics coexist: Figure 2 uses the clean-environment solve rate (+16.2 over base), Table 4 the scaffolded environment's task score (+19.0), which is where the blog's "nearly 20pp" comes from.

A developer can see only the proxy reward and the monitor's flags. Among the three reward-penalty seeds, Appendix D.8 picks the best by proxy reward minus twice the flagged rate. How much does the chosen run really hack?

Seed 15: proxy reward 1.000, 0.4% flagged, and 27.7% real hacking, nearly all constraint removal ({{Appendix D.8|ax:A4.SS8}} rounds it to 28%). The developer's metrics select the hacker; switch the chart above to "What the developer sees" and the penalty looks like the best method on the page. A comment in the authors' figure script adds that among their penalty configurations, selecting by ground truth would have picked a different one (penalty 5, 7.7% hacking, 81.3% task score), not shown in the paper: smaller than 26%, still above the base rate and still invisible to the monitor.

During training the hack lives in the forget adapter

Seed means of the eight GRAFT runs, read from {{Figure 2, right|ax:S4.F2}} (vector geometry; in-training evaluations on 64 prompts, step 200 a separate evaluation). With both adapters on, trivial-test hacking climbs to 38.5% at step 192; with only the forget adapter on it reaches 44%; with only the retain adapter it peaks at 7.4% at step 24 and ends at 2.5%.

The undetectable hacks never took off in πR+F, so the deployed model had little to learn them from.

Transfer to HumanEval and LeetCode, and the anchor-only run (Table 2, Appendix D.3)

The forget adapter also transfers: on hackable versions of HumanEval and LeetCode, πR+F hacks 8.7% and 39.6% (base 1.4% and 11.5%), on LeetCode mostly by hard-coding the visible test, a hack Countdown cannot even express; ablation brings both back to the base level, 1.2% and 9.9% ({{Table 2|ax:A4.T2}}). The anchor-only run, trained on LeetCode, beats GRAFT there. Without anchor updates GRAFT did as well on Countdown (77.8 against 79.2 task score; {{Table 4|ax:A4.T4}}), and when only half the prompts admit hacks, inoculation prompting matches GRAFT ({{Appendix D.3|ax:A4.SS3}}).

Result 2: six toy environments, SmolLM2-135M

[[S4.SS2|§4.2, Figure 3, Appendices D and F]]

Six small RL tasks ({{Tables 5 and 6|ax:A6.T5}}): reproduce a phrase (hack: repeat it), add two numbers, sort a list (hack: copy it), and three question-answering tasks (hack: intensifiers or flattery words). On half the prompts hacking is impossible, which the model can tell from a surface feature. Each monitor misses one form of the hack, and the anchor is the target environment sampled from πR with a penalty on flagged samples. Below, end-of-training hack rates on hackable prompts, split by whether the monitor would have flagged them.

Every arm hacks except deployed GRAFT; the penalty hides it

Two things differ from Countdown. First, pre-ablation GRAFT does learn the undetectable forms (6% to 52% of hackable answers), yet deleting the forget adapter removes them too: the paper's "detecting a subset of hack forms was enough to suppress undetected forms" ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Second, deployed GRAFT solves less of the task than the better of no intervention and the penalty in all six environments, by 2 to 14 points (persona_qa: 34.6% against 48.1%). Three arms of Figure 3 (Filtering, GRAFT without routing, GRAFT without anchoring) are not in the released bundle and are not replayed here. The appendix adds two limits: with recall cut to 0.5, 0.25 or 0.1, GRAFT still hacks less than the penalty at every level but often solves less ({{Appendix D.7|ax:A4.SS7}}); and the environments were tuned so that the penalty fails, while in addition, swapping which forms the monitor detects left the GRAFT model hacking about 60% of the time ({{Appendix F.1|ax:A6.SS1}}).

Result 3: supervised fine-tuning of Qwen3-32B on Terminal Wrench

[[S5|§5, Figure 4, Appendix G.4 and G.5]]

A bigger, multi-turn setting with SFT instead of RL ({{§5|ax:S5}}). Training data: frontier-model agentic-coding trajectories from Terminal Wrench, half of them reward hacks (judged by an LLM). A random 20% of the hacks are labelled (the monitor: 100% precision, 20% recall), 10% of the data. Evaluation: 99 held-out tasks with exploitable graders, up to 64 turns, a trial "hacked" if gpt-5.4-nano judges it so, with and without a hack-elicitation prompt that tells the model to find a bypass.

Read from {{Figure 4|ax:S5.F4}} (vector geometry); one training seed per method; the paper's bars are 90% intervals over the 99 tasks.

With the elicitation prompt GRAFT hacked on none of the 99 tasks while the untrained model and oracle filtering (every hack removed from training) hacked about 30% (29.3 and 31.6), at the same correct-solution rate as oracle filtering (44.8 against 44.2); GRAFT without routing hacked 79.1%. Without the prompt the picture is weaker, as the paper says: every method except no intervention and monitor filtering hacks on about 5% of tasks or fewer, GRAFT solves 39.5% against 49.7% for oracle filtering and 48.2% for GRAFT without routing. A model that refuses to hack when told to also ignores an instruction; the authors note that and present it only as evidence about propensity (footnote 5).

Training: three kinds of data, four stages

[[S4|§4, Table 1, App. C]]

The goal is to keep a strong model's abilities, not to win one task, so the data is broad ({{§4|ax:S4}}). Three kinds are mixed ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}, {{Figure 2|ax:S4.F2}}):

  • Continual pre-training with interleaved compression. Segments alternate between compressed (between <|memory_start|> and <|memory_end|>) and normal tokens, and the next-token loss is taken only on the uncompressed tokens. Prior work compressed the first half and trained on the second; interleaving teaches the model to condition on latents at many positions, not only at the start.
  • Supervised fine-tuning on reasoning, long-context instruction following and multi-turn chat, with compressed prompts and documents.
  • Auxiliary reconstruction: compress a document and ask the decoder to repeat it verbatim. It appears in both of the above.

Why mix them is the instructive part ({{App. C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}). Reconstruction alone gives a model that reconstructs with negligible loss and "cannot perform any other tasks, even when the LLM decoder is kept frozen": a general form of prefix tuning. Next-token prediction alone gives latents that support generation but lose exact strings. Neither failure is shown with numbers; both are "early experiments".

The data sources and the exact compression format (App. C)

Pre-training mixture: Common Crawl text, code, science and reasoning, long-context and instruction-style data, mostly NVIDIA's Nemotron sets plus about 500K long documents from OLMo-3's longmino ({{App. C.1|ax:A3.SS1}}). SFT: outdated completions relabelled with Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507 and Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 (Apache-2.0 checkpoints), plus three synthetic long-context corpora: repository summarisation, document summarisation and repository-level code generation ({{App. C.3|ax:A3.SS3}}). Reconstruction: code, text, long documents, maths and LaTeX, with a bank of 100 prompt templates per source to stop the model overfitting one phrasing ({{App. C.2|ax:A3.SS2}}).

Raw documents get a fixed alternation that starts with a wrapped (compressed) segment and ends with an unwrapped one. Documents over 16,384 tokens keep 8,192 trainable tokens; shorter ones keep half. Segment pairs: 1 under 2,048 tokens, 1 to 2 up to 4,096, 2 to 4 up to 8,192, 4 to 6 up to 16,384, 4 to 8 above. The first wrapped segment holds 30% to 40% of the wrapped tokens and the last unwrapped segment 40% to 50% of the trainable ones.

Conversations are wrapped per message: with probability one half the whole message is enclosed; otherwise 1 to 3 wrapped regions are placed inside it (1 region for 32 to 200 tokens, 1 to 2 for 200 to 600, 1 to 3 above; messages under 32 tokens are always fully wrapped), covering 80% to 100% of the message with a 5% unwrapped gap where feasible, falling back to full wrapping below 70% coverage ({{App. C.4|ax:A3.SS4}}). Training packs several examples per sequence with a block-diagonal mask so they cannot see each other ({{App. E.4|ax:A5.SS4}}).

Staged training ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}, {{Table 1|ax:S4.T1}}): Stage 0 trains the adapter only; Stage 1 unfreezes the encoder; Stage 2 unfreezes the decoder at a small learning rate (1e-5 against 6e-5); Stage 3 is SFT with the decoder at 3e-5. Training everything end to end from the start "underperforms": the decoder is not used to the encoder's output, both sides take large gradients, and the model degrades. It is the same align-then-finetune shape as vision-language model training (LLaVA, Cambrian). Variants that freeze the decoder or use LoRA, which is what most prior work does, "substantially underperform" (early experiments, no numbers).

The models ({{§5|ax:S5}}): a Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B encoder and a Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 decoder, trained at 4x, 8x and 16x. "Over 350B tokens each" is a sum over both sides: in the 16x run the encoder reads B tokens and the decoder B (its count includes latent positions), B together ({{Table 1|ax:S4.T1}}; the paper gives the token counts for the 16x run only). Training ran on 32 nodes of 4 AMD MI300A GPUs each; the from-scratch search on 16 such nodes ({{App. F|ax:A6}}).

The architecture search

[[S5|§5, Figure 3, App. D]]

This is the part anyone building a compressor would otherwise have to guess. The authors pretrain many variants from scratch, in a cleanroom: Qwen3-0.6B's architecture on both sides, all weights randomly initialised with one seed, everything trained end to end, 38B tokens per variant at N = 16 ({{§5|ax:S5}}). Starting from scratch matters because the pre-trained Qwen3-Embedding encoder already uses a causal mask and EOS pooling, which would bias the comparison. Four findings:

  1. Mean pooling beats token pooling. Appending a CLS or EOS token per latent, as most prior work does, gives a higher loss than averaging each block. Concatenation is indistinguishable from mean at this scale; at scale they swap: concatenation is slightly better at 4x, mean at 16x ({{Figure 8|ax:A4.F8}}).
  2. The encoder window matters more than expected. W = N (16) to W = 256 is called a large gain, 1,024 a further smaller one. Overlapping windows (so nothing is split across passes) sounds necessary and is not: no better loss, more compute ({{App. D.3|ax:A4.SS3}}).
  3. Causal encoder attention beats bidirectional, consistently, although the encoder runs before decoding and has no autoregressive constraint. This cuts against the T5Gemma result, where keeping the encoder causal costs 4.1 and 4.7 points ({{T5Gemma §6|https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06225v1#S6}}; see the {{T5Gemma page|n:3d45c17b0d0d81a9a22ffaada508db5c}}).
  4. A plain two-layer MLP adapter beats an attention-based adapter, at less compute, "in contrast to prior findings" ({{App. D.2|ax:A4.SS2}}).

Also at scale ({{App. E.1|ax:A5.SS1}}): an encoder initialised from the embedding model (Qwen3-Embedding, itself trained from the LLM) beats one initialised from the LLM, with the gap narrowing over training.

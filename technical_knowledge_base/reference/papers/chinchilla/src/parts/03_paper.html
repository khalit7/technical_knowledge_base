Problem

[[S1|§1 Introduction, §2]]

By early 2022 the largest language models were growing in parameters while their training data stayed put: five of the six biggest dense models had seen roughly 300 billion tokens ({{Table 1|ax:S1.T1}}).

They followed Kaplan et al. (2020), whose fit said that with 10× more compute the model should grow 5.5× and the data only 1.8× (the paper's figures; Kaplan's exponents, Nopt ∝ C0.73 and Dopt ∝ C0.27, give 5.4× and 1.9×) ({{§1|ax:S1}}). This paper asks the constrained question directly ({{Eq. 1|ax:S1.E1}}): given a fixed FLOP budget C, which model size N and token count D minimise the final pretraining loss L(N, D), subject to FLOPs(N, D) = C, where FLOPs ≈ 6ND?

You get 10× more compute. If parameters and tokens should grow equally, how much bigger should the model be?

Equal growth means N ∝ C0.5 and D ∝ C0.5, so both grow by √10 = ×; their product, and so 6ND, grows 10×. Kaplan's 0.73 puts most of the extra compute into the model. The {{Scaling Laws page's Then and now tab|n:3c65c17b0d0d81b08a1debb0c15cd252}} animates both rules spending the same budgets, with the real models of 2020 to 2024 on the same axes; this page does not repeat it.

The paper names two differences from Kaplan's method ({{§2|ax:S2}}):

  • One learning-rate schedule for every run. Kaplan trained each model with a cosine schedule to 130B tokens and read losses at earlier tokens D′ off the same run. A checkpoint partway down a long schedule has not had its learning rate decayed, so its loss overestimates what a run whose schedule ends at D′ would reach. That makes training on less data look worse than it is, and "eventually contributes to the conclusion that model size should increase faster than training data size" ({{§2|ax:S2}}, {{Figure A1|ax:A2.F1}}). The slider below shows how much learning rate is left.
  • Bigger models. Most runs here have over 500M parameters, up to 16B; most of Kaplan's had under 100M. The frontier curves slightly at the top end, so small models alone mislead ({{§2|ax:S2}}, {{Appendix E|ax:A5}}).

The schedule this paper uses: cosine decay of the learning rate by 10× over the cycle, LR(t) = LRmax (0.1 + 0.9 · ½(1 + cos πt/Tcycle)) ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}). The slider steps through Figure A1's six cycle lengths, 1 to 5× the number of training steps; Figure A1 found clear losses once the cycle overshoots by more than 25%. Arithmetic only: no loss curves are invented here.

Later work found that this explanation is only part of the story: re-running Kaplan's setup, Porian et al. (2024) traced most of the gap to uncounted output-layer FLOPs, a long fixed warmup and untuned optimiser settings, and found matching the schedule mattered little ({{Porian §3|https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19146v1#S3}}). See {{Believe it?|tab:t-read:s-trust}}.

Setup

[[S3|§3, Appendices A, B, D, F]]
  • Runs: over 400 dense Transformer language models, from 70M (the configurations start at 44M, {{Table A9|ax:A10.T9}}) to over 16B parameters, on 5B to 500B tokens (the abstract; §1 says "over 400B"), each configuration trained for several horizons ({{§1|ax:S1}}).
  • Data: MassiveText, the Gopher corpus, with a slightly different mix ({{Table A1|ax:A1.T1}}). The paper treats every run as under one epoch, so the smoothed training loss is an unbiased estimate of test loss (footnote 2, {{§1|ax:S1}}).
  • Schedule: cosine decay by 10× (decaying to 0 is about the same, decaying by only 5× is clearly worse), cycle matched to the run's tokens; peak learning rate 2 × 10−4 for the smallest models down to 1.25 × 10−4 for the largest; training curves smoothed with a 10-step Gaussian window ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}, {{§D.1|ax:A4.SS1}}).
  • Counting: N and C include the embedding matrices, unlike Kaplan's; forward FLOPs are counted term by term (embeddings, attention, dense block, logits) with backward twice forward, and land within a few percent of 6ND ({{Appendix F|ax:A6}}, {{Table A4|ax:A6.T4}}; the {{FLOP calculator|tab:t-tables:tbA4}} recounts it).
  • Other hyperparameters (batch size, width-to-depth) come from heuristics in earlier work; models are slightly shallower than Levine et al. (2020) suggest, for wall-clock speed on TPUs ({{§2|ax:S2}}).

The same runs are then analysed three independent ways.

Approach 1: fix the model, vary the horizon

[[S3.SS1|§3.1]]

For each model size from 70M to over 10B, train 4 runs whose cosine horizons span 16×. Smooth and interpolate every training curve, giving loss as a continuous function of FLOPs for each run. At 1,500 log-spaced FLOP values, take the run with the lowest loss: that is the lower envelope of all the curves, and it gives the best N and D for each budget. Fit power laws through those points: Nopt ∝ C0.50 and Dopt ∝ C0.50 ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}, {{Figure 2|ax:S3.F2}}).

Every point picked for the envelope lies in the last 15% of its run, which is the evidence for the rule "decay 10× over about D tokens" (footnote 4). A head-to-head at 1021 FLOPs tests it: Kaplan's rule says 4.68B parameters, Approach 1 says 2.86B; a 4.74B and a 2.80B model with the same depth-to-width ratio, batch 0.5M tokens and peak rate 1.5 × 10−4 were trained, and the smaller one ended lower ({{§D.4|ax:A4.SS4}}, {{Figure A4|ax:A4.F4}}). This page recomputes both sizes: Kaplan's 4.68B from his Table 6, and 2.84B from the 1021 IsoFLOP points below.

No envelope is drawn: the paper released no training curves, and this page never reads curves off a plot.

Approach 2: IsoFLOP profiles

[[S3.SS2|§3.2]]

Fix 9 budgets from 6 × 1018 to 3 × 1021 FLOPs; at each, train a range of model sizes (up to 16B), each for exactly the tokens the budget leaves, D = C/6N, with the cosine cycle matched. Plot final loss against N: each budget gives a valley. Fit a parabola to each valley, take its minimum, then fit power laws through the minima: a = 0.49, b = 0.51 ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}, {{Figure 3|ax:S3.F3}}).

Figure 3 (left and centre) rebuilt from data, not from the paper's fits: these are the final losses Besiroglu et al. (2024) read out of the vector graphics of Figure 4 ({{their §2|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10102v2#S2}}, {{data|https://github.com/epoch-research/analyzing-chinchilla/blob/main/data/svg_extracted_data.csv}}), assigned to a budget when within 0.08 decades of it (some are Approach 1 runs that happen to land near a budget). Loss values are good to about 0.01. The parabola is in log N, as in the paper; fit.py and the page agree.

Approach 3: a parametric loss

[[S3.SS3|§3.3, §D.2]]

Fit one function to every final loss from Approaches 1 and 2 ({{Eq. 2|ax:S3.E2}}):

L̂(N, D) = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ
A classical risk decomposition ({{§D.2|ax:A4.SS2}}, {{Eq. 9|ax:A4.E9}}): E is the Bayes risk, the loss of the ideal generative process, "the entropy of natural text"; A/Nα is what a perfectly trained Transformer of size N still loses (function approximation; theory for two-layer networks expects α near 1/2); B/Dβ is what a finite number of single-epoch optimisation steps loses (stochastic approximation; first-order methods are bounded below by 1/D1/2).

Fitting. Minimise the Huber loss (δ = 10−3, robust to outliers) between predicted and observed log loss, with the prediction written as a log-sum-exp of the three terms, by L-BFGS from a grid of 4,500 starts (α, β in 0 to 2, e in −1 to 1, log A and log B in 0 to 25) ({{Eq. 3|ax:S3.E3}}, {{Eq. 11|ax:A4.E11}}). Larger δ overfit the small-compute runs; smaller δ changed nothing. The result ({{Eq. 10|ax:A4.E10}}):

L̂ = 1.69 + 406.4/N0.34 + 410.7/D0.28
Both exponents are below 1/2, "expected for the data-efficiency coefficient (but far from the known lower-bound)".

The frontier in closed form. Minimising L̂ under 6ND = C balances the two reducible terms ({{Eq. 4|ax:S3.E4}}):

Nopt = G (C/6)a,  Dopt = G−1 (C/6)b,  G = (αA/βB)1/(α+β),  a = β/(α+β),  b = α/(α+β)
So the model exponent comes from the data exponent and the other way round. The paper reports a = 0.46, b = 0.54 ({{Table 2|ax:S3.T2}}); the rounded α and β above give 0.452 and 0.548, the unrounded ones in the TeX source give 0.4565 ({{Besiroglu et al. Eq. 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10102v2#S3.E4}}). At Gopher's budget the unrounded fit puts Nopt at , the paper's "40B" ({{Figure 4|ax:S3.F4}}).

Approach 3 predicts even smaller optimal models than Approaches 1 and 2. The paper's explanation: runs below 1021 FLOPs have larger residuals, the Huber loss treats them as outliers, and with the frontier curving downward that lowers Nopt ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}). The 2024 replication found a simpler cause: the fit had not converged ({{see below|tab:t-read:s-trust}}; refit it yourself in the {{Refit tab|tab:t-run}}).

The answer: scale both equally

[[S3.SS4|§3.4]]

All three approaches say parameters and tokens should grow in equal proportion with compute ("for every doubling of model size the number of training tokens should also be doubled", {{abstract|https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.15556}}; so doubling the budget grows each by √2 ≈ 1.41, and quadrupling it doubles both), against Kaplan's 0.73/0.27 ({{Table 2|ax:S3.T2}}; percentiles from 100 bootstraps of 80% of the data). The same IsoFLOP analysis on C4 gives 0.50/0.50 and on GitHub code 0.53/0.47 ({{Table A2|ax:A3.T2}}): the result does not depend on the dataset, as long as training stays under one epoch.

What that means per model ({{Table 3|ax:S3.T3}}, Approach 1): about 20 tokens per parameter, roughly constant with scale. 1B parameters wants 20.2B tokens; 67B wants 1.5T (Gopher's budget); 175B wants 3.7T; 280B wants 5.9T; a 1-trillion-parameter model is only worth training above about 1026 FLOPs, on 21.2T tokens. The {{Table 3 and A3 rebuild|tab:t-tables:tbT3}} checks every row against 6ND and finds one misprinted exponent.

The text and the tables disagree in places

§3.4 says 175B needs 4.41 × 1024 FLOPs and 4.2T tokens (Table 3: 3.85 × 1024 and 3.7T); that a Gopher-sized 280B model is optimal at about 1025 FLOPs on 6.8T tokens (Table 3: 5.9T, which is 20× Gopher's 300B; 6.8T would be 23×); and that 1026 FLOPs is "over 250×" Gopher's compute (it is 174×; Table 3's 1.27 × 1026 is 221×) ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}). None of these changes the conclusion.

Same budget, two allocations

Chinchilla, the test

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, Appendix G]]

All three approaches put Gopher's budget at a 40B to 70B model. The paper trained the larger end, 70B on 1.4T tokens, exactly 20 tokens per parameter, "due to both dataset and computational efficiency considerations" ({{§4|ax:S4}}).

{{Table 4|ax:S4.T4}}; feed-forward size 4 dmodel; batch doubled midway for both. The shapes give 64.7B and 258B parameters by the usual 12 nlayer dmodel2 (plus embeddings); one more dmodel2 per layer, as a Transformer-XL-style relative-position projection would add (Gopher uses relative encodings, {{Rae et al. 2021|https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.11446}}), lands on 70.1B and 279.7B, and the same count matches all 50 rows of {{Table A9|ax:A10.T9}} to within 0.8%. The paper does not state its count; this is a reconstruction.

Same architecture and data as Gopher, except ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}, {{Appendix G|ax:A7}}):

  • a slightly different MassiveText mix for the longer run ({{Table A1|ax:A1.T1}}): in 1.4T tokens MassiveWeb is seen 1.24 times and Wikipedia 3.40 times;
  • AdamW instead of Adam, which improves the language-modelling loss and downstream results after fine-tuning; an AdamW run only overtakes an Adam run about 80% of the way through the cosine cycle, but ends notably better ({{Figure A7|ax:A7.F7}});
  • a SentencePiece tokenizer without NFKC normalisation, vocabulary 32,000, 94.15% of tokens shared with Gopher's; it helps mathematics and chemistry;
  • forward and backward in bfloat16 with a float32 copy of the weights in the sharded optimiser state; together with AdamW this clearly beats Gopher's setup on a 680M model ({{Figure A6|ax:A7.F6}});
  • peak learning rate 1 × 10−4 against Gopher's 4 × 10−5 ({{Table 4|ax:S4.T4}}).

Trained on TPUv3 and TPUv4 with JAX and Haiku. The model card says it was trained once and will not be released ({{Table A8|ax:A9}}).

Results

[[S4.SS2|§4.2]]

Chinchilla beats Gopher (280B), GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B) and MT-NLG (530B) on nearly every evaluation, at the same or less training compute; being 4× smaller, it also costs about a quarter as much to fine-tune and to serve, so the gain is not only at training time ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}). Evaluation follows Rae et al. (2021) exactly ({{Table 5|ax:S4.T5}}).

Figures 6 and 7 rebuilt from {{Table A6|ax:A8.T6}} and {{Table A7|ax:A8.T7}}: Chinchilla's accuracy minus Gopher's, per task, sorted. Tap or hover a bar for its task.

  • MMLU (exam questions, 57 subjects): 67.6% 5-shot average, against Gopher's 60.0%, GPT-3's 43.9%, average human raters' 34.5% and human experts' 89.8%; above the June 2023 forecast of 63.4% made by 73 professional forecasters (Steinhardt 2021). Better on 51 tasks, the same on 2, worse on 4 (college_mathematics, econometrics, moral_scenarios, formal_logic); above 90% on four tasks, which the paper believes no model had done ({{Table 6|ax:S4.T6}}, {{Figure 6|ax:S4.F6}}). The abstract says 67.5%.
  • BIG-bench (62 tasks): 65.1% against 54.4% (+10.7), better on 58 of 62 and worse on only crash_blossom, dark_humor_detection, mathematical_induction and logical_args ({{§4.2.4|ax:S4.SS2.SSS4}}). Recomputed from Table A7's 62 scores, the unweighted means are : Gopher's 54.4 reproduces, Chinchilla's 65.1 does not, and the paper does not say how it averaged.
  • Language modelling: lower bits per byte than Gopher on every subset of The Pile, and lower than Jurassic-1 on all but dm_mathematics and ubuntu_irc ({{Figure 5|ax:S4.F5}}, {{Table A5|ax:A8.T5}}); WikiText-103 perplexity 7.16 against 7.75. The authors warn that 4× more training data raises the risk of train/test leakage here, and so lean on the other tasks ({{§4.2.1|ax:S4.SS2.SSS1}}).
  • Reading comprehension: LAMBADA 77.4% zero-shot (Gopher 74.5, MT-NLG 76.6); RACE-m 86.8 and RACE-h 82.3, over 10 points above Gopher (GPT-3 and MT-NLG used another prompt format, so are not comparable) ({{Table 7|ax:S4.T7}}).
  • Common sense: matches or beats Gopher and GPT-3 on HellaSwag, PIQA, Winogrande, SIQA and BoolQ, and MT-NLG on all but PIQA ({{Table 8|ax:S4.T8}}). TruthfulQA: 43.6%, 58.5%, 66.7% at 0, 5, 10 shots against Gopher's 29.5% (0-shot) and 43.7% (10-shot); the 14.1-point gain suggests better pretraining alone helps, against Lin et al.'s finding ({{§4.2.5|ax:S4.SS2.SSS5}}).
  • Closed-book QA: Natural Questions 31.5% 5-shot and 35.5% 64-shot, a new closed-book state of the art (Table 9 gives Gopher 24.5% and 28.2%; the text says 21% and 28%); TriviaQA beats Gopher on both versions, beats GPT-3 unfiltered and trails the open-book state of the art by 7.9 points filtered ({{Table 9|ax:S4.T9}}).
  • Bias and toxicity: Winogender coreference improves for every group, but by 3.2 points for male pronouns against 8.3 female and 9.2 neutral (10 on female "gotcha" examples), so the gains are uneven ({{Table 10|ax:S4.T10}}). Unprompted toxicity is unchanged: mean (median) PerspectiveAPI score 0.087 (0.066) against Gopher's 0.081 (0.064), 95th percentile 0.238 against 0.230, over 25,000 samples ({{§4.2.7|ax:S4.SS2.SSS7}}).

How much of this to believe

[[S5|§5 Discussion, and later work]]

What holds up. Two of the three methods, built on different subsets of the runs, agree on 0.49 to 0.50 with tight bootstrap intervals ({{Table 2|ax:S3.T2}}); the result repeats on C4 and GitHub ({{Table A2|ax:A3.T2}}); two head-to-heads go the predicted way, at 1021 FLOPs with the confounders controlled ({{Figure A4|ax:A4.F4}}) and at Gopher scale. Later work reproduces the allocation: Approach 2 on the points Besiroglu et al. read off Figure 4 gives a = on this page; Llama 3's own IsoFLOP sweep gives 0.47 ({{§3.2.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS2.SSS1}}); DeepSeek LLM gives 0.45 to 0.58 depending on the data ({{Table 4|https://arxiv.org/html/2401.02954v1#S3.T4}}). The {{Then and now tab|tab:t-then}} lists them.

Weaknesses, concretely:

  • Approach 3's fit did not converge. Refitting the same functional form to 240 of the paper's own points, Besiroglu et al. (2024) get E 1.82, A 482, B 2085, α 0.348, β 0.366, so a = 0.51 and about 20 tokens per parameter, consistent with Approaches 1 and 2; the published constants imply about 70 tokens per parameter instead and fit the points worse, with residuals not centred on zero ({{their §3|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10102v2#S3}}, {{Figure 5|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10102v2#S3.F5}}). Two causes: β printed as 0.28 for 0.2849, which alone biases the data term by about 13% at 1011 tokens; and, as one of the paper's authors confirmed, the Huber losses were averaged instead of summed, so the optimiser's loss scale made it stop early. This page reproduces the second: averaging stops the same search at a = , close to the published 0.454, while summing gives ({{try it|tab:t-run}}).
  • Implausibly tight intervals. Approach 3's 80% interval for a, 0.454 to 0.455, would need about 600,000 runs; bootstrapping the 240 points gives a standard error of 0.018, an interval 50× wider ({{Besiroglu §3.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10102v2#S3.SS2}}). The early stopping explains this too: bootstrap fits barely moved from their start.
  • One pair of large runs, single seeds. Only Chinchilla and Gopher are compared at scale, with nothing in between, and Chinchilla was trained once (§5, {{model card|ax:A9}}). The authors state both.
  • Not like for like. Chinchilla also changed optimiser (AdamW), weight precision, tokenizer, data mix and peak learning rate, and the paper's own Figures A6 and A7 show the optimiser changes help by themselves ({{Appendix G|ax:A7}}). Some of the benchmark gain is not allocation.
  • "The same budget" is approximate. By the 6ND rule the paper uses for its frontier, Chinchilla's run costs 5.88 × 1023 FLOPs and Gopher's 5.04 × 1023; the 5.76 × 1023 is Gopher's own fuller count, and Appendix F's count gives 6.3 × 1023 ({{Appendix F|ax:A6}}). Chinchilla's run had at least as much compute, not less.
  • Single epoch, except the test. The analysis assumes no repetition, but Chinchilla's own 1.4T-token run sees Wikipedia 3.4 times and MassiveWeb 1.24 times ({{Table A1|ax:A1.T1}}).
  • A power-law frontier is assumed though it curves at high compute ({{Appendix E|ax:A5}}), so large optimal models may still be overestimated (§5, stated by the authors). Repeated data, the case the paper leaves out, was modelled later by {{Muennighoff et al. (2023)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264}}.
  • The explanation for Kaplan's numbers is partly wrong. Porian et al. (2024) reproduce both exponents and find the schedule matters little next to output-layer FLOPs, warmup and tuning ({{Porian §3|https://arxiv.org/html/2406.19146v1#S3}}).
  • Small inconsistencies: the text against Tables 3 and A3 (above), a misprinted exponent in Table A3, 67.5% against 67.6% on MMLU, Gopher's Natural Questions score, and Table A4's FLOP ratios, which only reproduce if the embedding and logit terms Appendix F lists are left out ({{tables tab|tab:t-tables:tbChk}}).
Approach 3 reports an 80% interval for a of 0.454 to 0.455 from about 400 runs. Bootstrapping 240 of those runs gives a standard error of 0.018. How many runs would the published width need?

An 80% interval is 2 × 1.28 × 0.018 ≈ 0.05 wide, 50× the published 0.001. Standard errors shrink as 1/√n, so the width needs 50² = 2,500× the runs: 240 × 2,500 = ({{Besiroglu §3.2|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10102v2#S3.SS2}}). Run your own bootstrap in the {{Refit tab|tab:t-run}}.

Verdict. Believe the allocation: grow parameters and tokens together, about 20 tokens per parameter at these scales and with this kind of data. It rests on two independent analyses, a controlled small-scale test, a large-scale test and every later replication. Treat the size of Chinchilla's benchmark wins as allocation plus several other improvements, from one run each. Do not reuse the Approach 3 constants or intervals; refit them, or use a corrected fit.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

[[S5|§5 Discussion]]

This paper reset how the field budgets pretraining. It ended the "parameters are all that matter" era: progress became data-bound, and dataset scale and quality became a first-class concern, as the authors foresaw ("scaling to larger and larger datasets is only beneficial when the data is high-quality", with more care for train/test overlap, toxicity, bias and privacy as corpora grow) ({{§5|ax:S5}}). That line runs to the 15T-token corpora of 2024 and 2025 and to data-curation research generally ({{Llama 3 §3.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3.SS1}}). The deeper lessons outlived the constants: match the schedule to the run, run IsoFLOP sweeps of small models to choose (N, D) before the big run, and model loss as E + A/Nα + B/Dβ, a form later work reuses constantly.

Chinchilla-optimal is not deployment-optimal. The law minimises training loss for a training budget and ignores inference. When lifetime inference dominates, a smaller model trained far past 20 tokens per parameter costs less overall ({{Sardana et al. §2|https://arxiv.org/html/2401.00448v2#S2}}), and loss keeps falling out to 10,000 tokens per parameter in their runs ({{§4|https://arxiv.org/html/2401.00448v2#S4}}). That is now standard practice: the loss curve in D flattens but keeps improving, so overtraining trades extra training compute for a permanently cheaper model.

Llama 3 8B (2024) was trained on about 15T tokens. How many tokens per parameter is that?

15 × 1012 / 8 × 109 = , over 90× the Chinchilla ratio; Llama 2 7B had 286, Gemma 2 9B 889 ({{Llama 3 §3|https://arxiv.org/html/2407.21783v3#S3}}, {{Llama 2 §2.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2307.09288v2#S2.SS1}}, {{Gemma 2 §3|https://arxiv.org/html/2408.00118v3#S3}}). Meta still used an IsoFLOP fit to size the 405B flagship, near compute-optimal at about 38 tokens per parameter. The {{Then and now tab|tab:t-then}} animates the inference-aware optimum.

Using this today. Read Chinchilla as the compute-optimal anchor from which you deviate toward more data once inference is counted. To plan a run, fit your own law: several IsoFLOP budgets with about ten sizes each and matched schedules, as Llama 3 and DeepSeek LLM did; the optimal ratio moves with data quality (higher-quality data favours bigger models, {{DeepSeek LLM §3.3|https://arxiv.org/html/2401.02954v1#S3.SS3}}). If you need constants, take Besiroglu et al.'s and treat them as specific to MassiveText. Mixture-of-experts models need their own treatment: the paper itself notes that routed-model scaling shared Kaplan's fixed-token flaw ({{§2|ax:S2}}).

Problem

§1 Introduction

By early 2025 open models came in two kinds. Chat models such as Qwen2.5-Instruct (and, among closed ones, GPT-4o) answer at once; reasoning models such as QwQ-32B and DeepSeek-R1 think at length first. A user had to route each request to the right model and had no control over how many reasoning tokens to pay for (abstract, §1).

Small open models were also expensive to make good: running a full reasoning-RL pipeline for every size does not amortise. Qwen3 targets three gaps at once:

  • One model, two modes, a budget. A single checkpoint that both thinks and answers directly, switched by a flag, with the thinking capped at a user-set number of tokens.
  • A ladder of sizes built cheaply. Small models trained from the flagships by distillation instead of their own reinforcement learning.
  • Languages. From Qwen2.5's 29 languages and dialects to 119.

The question: can one set of weights serve both quick answers and long reasoning, with reasoning depth as a dial, and can the small models inherit all of it without their own RL?

The family and its architecture

§2, Tables 1 and 2

Eight models, all open under Apache 2.0: six dense (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B, 32B) and two mixture-of-experts, Qwen3-30B-A3B and the flagship Qwen3-235B-A22B, 235B parameters in total with 22B active per token (§2).

  • Dense blocks are Qwen2.5's: grouped-query attention, SwiGLU, rotary position embeddings, RMSNorm before each sub-layer. Two changes: the QKV bias of Qwen2 is removed and QK-Norm is added, an RMSNorm on each head's queries and keys before the dot product, "to ensure stable training".
  • MoE layers have 128 fine-grained experts with 8 active per token and, unlike Qwen2.5-MoE, no shared expert. The balancing loss is computed over the global batch instead of each micro-batch "to encourage expert specialization" (animated on the lab page).
  • Tokenizer: Qwen's byte-level BPE with 151,669 tokens (the released embedding is padded to 151,936 rows).
  • Context: Table 1 gives 32K for 0.6B and 1.7B and 128K for the rest. The model cards say 32,768 natively and 131,072 with YaRN at factor 4 for 4B and up (Qwen3-8B card); the release blog lists Qwen3-4B as 32K, Table 1 as 128K.

Pre-training: 36T tokens in three stages

§3.1 and §3.2

Data. Twice Qwen2.5's tokens (36T against 18T, per the release blog) in 119 languages and dialects, "three times more" than Qwen2.5's 29 (119 ÷ 29 = 4.1 times as many). The extra comes from three pipelines (§3.1):

  • PDF-like documents read by a model. A fine-tuned Qwen2.5-VL recognises the text, Qwen2.5 refines it: "trillions" of extra tokens.
  • Synthetic data. Qwen2.5, Qwen2.5-Math and Qwen2.5-Coder write textbooks, question-answer pairs, instructions and code in dozens of domains, again "trillions" of tokens.
  • More multilingual data, for the new languages.

A multilingual annotation system labels over 30T tokens by educational value, field, domain and safety. The mixture is then optimised per instance, not per source or domain, by ablations on small proxy models that use those labels. The report gives no labels, weights or proxy results.

Three stages (§3.2):

  1. General (S1): over 30T tokens at 4,096-token sequences, all 119 languages.
  2. Reasoning (S2): about 5T higher-quality tokens with more STEM, coding, reasoning and synthetic data, still at 4,096, with "accelerated" learning-rate decay.
  3. Long context: hundreds of billions of tokens at 32,768; 75% of the long-context text is 16,384 to 32,768 tokens long, 25% is 4,096 to 16,384. The RoPE base rises from 10,000 to 1,000,000 (ABF), and at inference YaRN and Dual Chunk Attention give "a four-fold increase in sequence length capacity".

As for Qwen2.5, in-house scaling laws set the learning-rate schedule and batch size for each model and stage; none of the laws or values are published.

Base-model results

§3.3, Tables 3 to 8

The base models were scored on 15 benchmarks in four groups (general knowledge, math and STEM, coding, multilingual), all "using the same evaluation pipeline" for Qwen3 and the baselines (§3.3). The headline: Qwen3-235B-A22B-Base beats DeepSeek-V3-Base on 14 of 15 benchmarks with about a third of its total and two thirds of its active parameters, losing only INCLUDE (Table 3). It beats Llama-4-Maverick-Base (402B, 1.7 times its size; the text says "about twice") on 14 of 15 as well, and Qwen2.5-72B-Base on all 15.

The second claim is a tier shift: each Qwen3 dense base model is "comparable" to the next Qwen2.5 size up (1.7B to 3B, 4B to 7B, 8B to 14B, 14B to 32B, 32B to 72B), and the MoE bases match the dense ones with a fifth of the active parameters. Post-training: four stages for the flagships, distillation for the rest

§4, Figure 1

Post-training has two goals (§4): thinking control (one model with a thinking and a non-thinking mode, and a token budget for the thinking) and strong-to-weak distillation (small models built from the large ones instead of trained the long way). The two flagships, Qwen3-235B-A22B and Qwen3-32B, go through four stages; the six smaller models are distilled from them. The animation runs both routes, as Figure 1 draws them.

What each stage trains on, and where its signal comes from.

Stages and their data follow §4.1 to §4.5. "Signal per response" counts what one training response tells the model: a target token per position (fine-tuning), one reward per response (RL), or the teacher's whole next-token distribution per position (on-policy distillation; the vocabulary has 151,669 entries). The only measured cost in the report is Table 21's GPU hours for one 8B comparison; the 1/10 of "the four-stage training method" in §4 is not itemised. The animation stops when off screen and starts paused if your system asks for reduced motion.

Stage 1: a deliberately small long chain-of-thought cold start

§4.1

A dataset of math, code, logical reasoning and general STEM problems, each with a verified reference answer or test cases, filtered twice (§4.1):

  • Query filter (by Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct): drop queries that are not easily verifiable (several sub-questions, general text generation) and queries it answers correctly without chain-of-thought, "to prevent the model from relying on superficial guessing". It also labels each query's domain to keep the mix balanced.
  • Response filter: after reserving a validation set, QwQ-32B generates N candidates per query; when it keeps failing, human annotators judge the responses. For queries with a positive Pass@N, responses are dropped if they (1) reach a wrong answer, (2) repeat themselves substantially, (3) guess without adequate reasoning, (4) think one thing and summarise another, (5) mix languages or shift style inappropriately, or (6) look too similar to validation items.

Only "a carefully selected subset" is used, with as few samples and steps as possible: the aim is to instil the reasoning format "without overly emphasizing immediate reasoning performance", so as not to cap what RL can add later. No sizes are given.

Stage 2: reasoning RL on 3,995 problems

§4.2

The query-verifier pairs must be unused in stage 1, learnable for the cold-start model, as hard as possible and spread over sub-domains: 3,995 in all. The algorithm is GRPO (the DeepSeekMath page runs it live), with large batches, many rollouts per query and off-policy reuse of samples, and the entropy kept "to increase steadily or remain stable". Training improved steadily "without any manual intervention on hyperparameters": Qwen3-235B-A22B went from 70.1 to 85.1 on AIME'24 in 170 RL steps (§4.2).

Stage 3: thinking mode fusion, one checkpoint for both modes

§4.3, Table 9

Stage 3 continues supervised fine-tuning of the stage-2 reasoning model on a mix of thinking and non-thinking data, so that one set of weights does both (§4.3):

  • Thinking data is generated by the stage-2 model itself, by rejection sampling on stage-1 queries, so the extra fine-tuning does not degrade it.
  • Non-thinking data is curated across coding, math, instruction following, multilingual tasks, creative writing, question answering and role-play, graded with automatically generated checklists; translation tasks are up-weighted for low-resource languages.
  • The chat template carries the switch: /think or /no_think in the user or system message. A non-thinking response keeps an empty think block, so both modes share one format, and a deployer can force non-thinking by pre-filling the empty block (Hugging Face's template does this with enable_thinking=False).
  • Thinking is the default: some thinking samples carry no flag. In multi-turn data flags are inserted at random and the response follows the last flag seen.
Two requests, one quick and one hard, at 32B scale.

Token layout is Table 9's template. Weight memory is parameters × 2 bytes in BF16, to scale: Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and QwQ-32B are both 32.5B-parameter models (Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct card, QwQ-32B card), Qwen3-32B is 32.8B (recounted above). The token counts of the two answers are illustrative.

The fusion has a measured cost, in the report's own Table 22: peak math and code scores in thinking mode drop slightly after stage 3 (AIME'24 83.8 to 81.9, LiveCodeBench 68.4 to 67.2 for Qwen3-32B), while instruction following, format following and agent scores rise. The thinking budget

§4.3, Thinking Budget

Once a model can answer both after full thinking and after empty thinking, the report says, it "naturally develops the ability to handle intermediate cases", answering from incomplete thinking. The budget uses that: when the thinking reaches a user-set number of tokens, generation is halted and this text is inserted (§4.3):

"Considering the limited time by the user, I have to give the solution based on the thinking directly now.\n</think>.\n\n"

The model then writes its answer from the reasoning so far. "This ability is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally as a result of applying Thinking Mode Fusion." The report runs no ablation of that sentence: there is no model without fusion, and no model trained on cut-off thinking, to compare against. The toy on this page runs both.

A toy Qwen3 is fused exactly as stage 3 describes: half its samples think to the end, half have an empty think block, never anything in between. Cut its thinking off half way and insert the closing tag. What does it answer?

What the budget buys on the real model. Figure 2 plots Qwen3-235B-A22B on four benchmarks at budgets of 1K to 32K thinking tokens, against its non-thinking score. The figure prints no numbers; the points below are read from the arXiv HTML's vector graphic, calibrated on its own gridlines. The calibration checks itself: the four non-thinking lines land on Table 12's scores (40.1, 24.7, 35.3, 62.9) to within 0.05.

Two things the figure shows that the text does not say:

  • Even 1K tokens of thinking beats not thinking, on all four benchmarks (AIME'24 42.1 against 40.1; LiveCodeBench 45.1 against 35.3). The real model, cut off early, does at least as well as answering directly. That is the property the toy lacks, and it is what makes the dial safe to turn down.
  • The 32K points are not Table 11's numbers for the same model and mode: AIME'24 86.7 against 85.7, AIME'25 81.7 against 81.5, GPQA 72.0 against 71.1 and LiveCodeBench 67.8 against 70.7. The figure is a separate run, with settings the report does not give (Table 11's runs may allow longer outputs: the model card recommends 38,912 output tokens for competition math).

The report expects scores to keep rising "if we further extend the output length beyond 32K" and leaves that as future work (§4.7). The general mechanism and how other labs expose the same dial are on Topic: llms (Deeper: test-time compute), and the lab page has a strip showing where a budget cuts a chain.

Stage 4: general RL

§4.4

A reward system over more than 20 tasks, each with its own scoring criteria, aimed at five capabilities (§4.4):

  • Instruction following: content, format, length, structured output.
  • Format following: switching modes on /think and /no_think, and keeping <think> and </think> where they belong.
  • Preference alignment on open-ended queries: helpfulness, engagement, style.
  • Agent ability: tool calls through designated interfaces, with complete multi-turn rollouts against real environments that execute them.
  • Specialised scenarios, for example retrieval-augmented generation, rewarded for accurate, grounded answers to curb hallucination.

Three kinds of reward: rule-based (as in reasoning RL, also for instruction and format following; hard to hack), model-based with a reference answer (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct scores the response against it, avoiding the false negatives of strict rules) and model-based without one (a reward model trained on human preference data, for the widest range of queries).

Strong-to-weak distillation

§4.5 and §4.7

The five small dense models (0.6B, 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B) and Qwen3-30B-A3B skip the four stages (§4.5):

  1. Off-policy distillation. Fine-tune the student on the teachers' responses in both /think and /no_think modes: basic reasoning and mode switching.
  2. On-policy distillation. The student samples its own responses to sampled prompts, in either mode, and is trained to align its logits with the teacher's (Qwen3-32B or Qwen3-235B-A22B) by minimising the KL divergence.
ℒ = Σt KL( pstudent(· | y<t) ‖ pteacher(· | y<t) ),   y ∼ pstudent

The student is graded at every position of its own response on the teacher's whole next-token distribution, where RL gets one reward per response. The report does not say which direction of KL it uses; the toy uses the student-first (reverse) one written here.

The report's evidence is one comparison (Table 21): from the same off-policy-distilled Qwen3-8B, on math and code queries only, on-policy distillation reached AIME'24 74.4 and AIME'25 65.5 for 1,800 GPU hours; reinforcement learning reached 67.6 and 55.5 for 17,920 (1,800 ÷ 17,920 = 10.0%). Distillation also raised pass@64 (AIME'24 90.0 to 93.3, AIME'25 83.3 to 86.7) where RL left it unchanged, which the report reads as distillation expanding "the exploration space" while RL does not (§4.7). The lab page draws these as bars.

AIME'24 has 30 questions. How many more questions does on-policy distillation solve at least once in 64 tries than RL does?

One. 93.3 − 90.0 = 3.3 points, and one question is 100 ÷ 30 = 3.33 points; AIME'25's 83.3 to 86.7 is also one question. The pass@1 gap is larger: 74.4 − 67.6 = 6.8 points, about two questions' worth of average accuracy. So "expands the exploration space" rests on one problem per year, in one run. What stages 3 and 4 each buy

§4.7, Table 22

Table 22 scores Qwen3-32B after stage 2, 3 and 4, in both modes, on public benchmarks and four in-house ones (§4.7):

  • CounterFactQA: counterfactual questions the model should recognise as non-factual instead of answering.
  • LengthCtrl: creative writing to a target length, scored by the difference.
  • ThinkFollow: multi-turn dialogues with randomly inserted flags; did the model switch correctly?
  • ToolUse: single-turn, multi-turn and multi-step tool calling, scored on intent, format and parameters.

Each bar is the change in points from one stage to the next, on one shared axis, from Table 22; the changes printed in the table recompute from its scores to within 0.1 (rounding). Non-thinking mode starts at stage 3, which creates it. ThinkFollow is one score per stage (88.7, then 98.9). Stage 4's thinking scores equal the released Qwen3-32B's in Table 13, so the table describes the shipped model.

Stage 3 lifts the in-house and alignment scores (CounterFactQA +10.9, LengthCtrl +8.0 in thinking mode), and stage 4 lifts them again (ToolUse +15.1, ThinkFollow 88.7 to 98.9). Peak reasoning goes the other way: from stage 2 to stage 4, thinking-mode AIME'24 falls 2.4 points and LiveCodeBench 2.7, GPQA and MMLU-Redux under a point. The report says so: the degradation is probably "due to the model being trained on a broader range of general tasks", and "we choose to accept this performance trade-off to enhance the model's overall versatility". That trade is what Qwen undid three months later by splitting the modes again (see Why it matters).

How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • The tables are internally consistent. Every win count the text states reproduces from its table except one (below); Table 22's printed changes recompute from its scores to within rounding; Table 22's final Qwen3-32B is the released model of Table 13; Figure 2's non-thinking lines land on Table 12's scores. The parameter counts reproduce from the released config files.
  • The artefacts are public and checkable. All eight models are open under Apache 2.0, the chat template with the mode switch is on Hugging Face, and third parties have run them since; the budget dial and the mode switch can be tried by anyone.
  • The authors report their own costs. Table 22 shows the fusion and general RL lowering AIME'24 and LiveCodeBench, and the appendix shows thinking hurting RULER; both are stated, which counts in their favour.

What does not.

  • No error bars, one run each. Every table is a single run. AIME has 30 questions a year: one question is 3.3 points. Averaging 64 samples per question keeps sampling noise under about 1.1 points (at worst, every question at 50%), but the 30 questions themselves are a small sample: a model at 70% has a question-sampling standard error of about 8 points. Many comparisons in Tables 11 to 20 sit inside that.
  • The distillation result is one comparison. Table 21 is one student (8B), one teacher, math and code queries only, one run each, and its pass@64 gain is one question per year. The "1/10 of the GPU hours compared to the four-stage training method" in §4 is never itemised; Table 21 compares against the RL stage alone.
  • "Emerges naturally" is asserted, not tested. No ablation trains a model without fusion, or with cut-off thinking, to compare. Figure 2 shows the real model answering usefully from partial thinking; it cannot show why. The toy on this page shows fusion alone does not guarantee it.
  • Figure 2 and Table 11 disagree at the same budget and mode (LiveCodeBench 67.8 against 70.7) without explanation.
  • Baselines are not all run in one pipeline. The DeepSeek-R1 and DeepSeek-V3 columns of Tables 11 and 12 equal DeepSeek's own published numbers on 8 of 9 shared benchmarks (R1 paper, Table 4); only BFCL is flagged as taken from a leaderboard. Rivals' numbers come from their own prompts and settings.
  • One text claim does not match its table: Qwen3-32B-Base is said to beat Llama-4-Scout-Base on all 15 benchmarks; Table 4 has Scout ahead on INCLUDE. Small slips also: "about twice" for Maverick's 1.7 times; "consistently superior" for small models that lose math to R1 distills.
  • The pre-training recipe cannot be checked. The data, the instance-level mixture, the annotation labels, the scaling laws and the training compute are not released or quantified.

Verdict: believe the recipe and the shipped behaviour (one checkpoint that switches modes on a flag, a budget that trades tokens for accuracy, strong small models distilled cheaply), all of which the open weights let anyone confirm. Treat the margins in the leaderboards as single runs on small benchmarks with partly borrowed baselines, and the two experiments behind the report's ideas, distillation against RL and the budget emerging from fusion, as suggestive: one model, one run, no ablation.

What it takes to use this

  • Weights and licence. All eight models, base and post-trained, under Apache 2.0 on Hugging Face and ModelScope; FP8 versions exist. Training code and data are not released.
  • Hardware. In BF16 the weights alone are 2 bytes per parameter: about 1.2 GB for 0.6B, 16.4 GB for 8B, 65.5 GB for 32B, 61 GB for 30B-A3B and 470 GB for 235B-A22B (from the recounts above). The MoE models need all their weights in memory but compute like 3.3B and 22.2B models.
  • Switching modes. enable_thinking=True (the default) or False in apply_chat_template; with it on, /think and /no_think in a message switch per turn and the last one wins. With it on, the output always has a think block, empty in non-thinking turns; with it off, the flags are ignored (Qwen3-235B-A22B card). vLLM (0.8.5 and later) and SGLang (0.4.6.post1 and later) parse the reasoning out.
  • Sampling. The card says thinking mode needs temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20 and "DO NOT use greedy decoding"; non-thinking mode 0.7 and 0.8. For competition math it recommends up to 38,912 output tokens.
  • The budget is not a switch in the libraries. It is a serving-side loop: count thinking tokens, stop at the budget, append the stop sentence and the closing tag, and let the model continue. Expect the payoff only once the budget approaches the length a problem needs (Figure 2).
  • Long context. 32,768 tokens natively; up to 131,072 with YaRN at factor 4, which the card advises only when needed, since static YaRN may hurt short texts.
  • Hybrid or split. The hybrid checkpoints remain; for the 235B, 30B-A3B and 4B sizes the July 2025 "2507" releases are separate Instruct and Thinking models with 262,144-token context, and the Instruct model "does not generate <think></think> blocks" (Instruct-2507 card). One checkpoint saves memory and operations; two give the best of each mode.
  • Distillation for your own small model needs the teacher's logits on the student's own samples, so the teacher has to run beside training (open weights make that possible; an API rarely exposes full distributions).

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

Qwen3 is the release that made Qwen the default open family to fine-tune: a complete Apache 2.0 ladder from 0.6B to 235B with one tokenizer, one template and one behaviour contract, whose small models punch far above their size because they inherit flagship post-training through distillation (Interconnects, "the new open standard"). Most 2025 open fine-tunes, RL research baselines and distillation studies build on these checkpoints, and it is the family Khalid has fine-tuned professionally.

Three ideas proved durable: budgeted thinking as a first-class inference dial; the minimal cold start followed by GRPO on aggressively filtered verifiable data; and on-policy logit distillation as a far cheaper substitute for per-model RL, a result that shaped later small-model pipelines. (The industry-wide influence is this knowledge base's reading, not a measured claim.)

The hybrid did not survive at the top. On 21 July 2025 Qwen announced: "After talking with the community and thinking it through, we decided to stop using hybrid thinking mode. Instead, we'll train Instruct and Thinking models separately so we can get the best quality possible" (Qwen on X). The 2507 releases split the 235B-A22B, 30B-A3B and 4B models into Instruct and Thinking checkpoints with 256K context. The report's own Table 22 had already priced the trade (a couple of points of peak math and code), and the toy on this page shows why answering from partial thinking is not free either. The old summary said the team "concluded fusion taxed peak quality"; the announcement says only "the best quality possible", without numbers. Read together with Table 22, it is still an instructive negative result about capability interference: two behaviours in one set of weights cost each a little.

The family then grew on this base: Qwen3-Coder (480B-A35B), Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B (hybrid gated and linear attention with an ultra-sparse MoE), Qwen3-VL, Qwen3-Omni and the closed Qwen3-Max, keeping Qwen the most actively iterated open line through 2025, and on through Qwen3.5 to 3.8; the lab page has the dated lineage and licences.

Run a toy Qwen3

The report says answering from cut-off thinking "is not explicitly trained but emerges naturally" from thinking mode fusion, and never tests it. This tab tests it on a model small enough to train three ways and run in the page: fused as stage 3 describes, thinking-only (the stage-2 model before fusion), and fused plus explicit training on cut-off thinking (what the report says it did not need).

The toy: task, format, model, training, and how it was checked
  • Task. A question is a list of 1 to 12 digits; the answer is their sum mod 10. Thinking writes the running sum after each digit, so each step is one small addition and the last one is the answer. Answering directly needs the whole sum in one forward pass, which a small model does badly for long lists: the same shape as the report's claim that thinking helps on hard problems and costs tokens.
  • Format, Table 9's template in tokens: <u> 3 9 4 /think <a> <think> 3 2 6 </think> 6 for thinking and <u> 3 9 4 /no_think <a> <think> </think> 6 for non-thinking (empty block kept). A quarter of the thinking samples carry no flag, since thinking is the default.
  • Model. A Qwen3 decoder at parameters: 3 layers, width 32, 4 query heads sharing 2 key-value heads (grouped-query attention), QK-Norm, rotary embeddings, SwiGLU, pre-norm RMSNorm, no QKV bias, tied embeddings.
  • Training, identical for all three variants (6,000 steps of 256 sequences, AdamW, warm-up then cosine, one seed); only the mix differs: fused is half thinking, half non-thinking; thinking-only is all thinking; budget-trained is the fused mix plus a quarter of samples whose thinking stops at a random step, followed by </think> and the correct answer. The budget inserts </think> alone: the toy has no words, so the report's stop sentence cannot be imitated (a real difference, discussed below).
  • Checked. The page runs the exported 6-bit weights in plain JavaScript; . Every number on this tab comes from the held-out test set (200 problems per length, drawn with their own seed). Short lists repeat across any sample (there are only 10 one-digit questions), so of test problems also occurred in training; for lists of 6 or more digits overlap is negligible.

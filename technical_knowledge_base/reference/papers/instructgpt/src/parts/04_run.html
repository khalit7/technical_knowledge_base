Run the three steps in your browser

The paper's whole pipeline, at toy scale, trained here and now: a tiny language model is pretrained on "internet text" in which instructions are usually followed by more instructions (GPT-3's habit), fine-tuned on labeler demonstrations (step 1), a reward model is fit to simulated labelers' rankings with {{Eq. 1|ax:S3.E1}} (step 2), and the policy is trained with PPO against it, with the per-token KL penalty and the pretraining mix of {{Eq. 2|ax:S3.E2}} (step 3). Unlike the paper, the toy knows what its labelers really want, so it can show the gap between the reward model's score and the truth.

The toy, exactly (what it is, how it was checked, what it cannot show)
  • World: 30 words and 6 instructions ("name a fruit", "name a colour", "name an animal", "count to three", "what is two plus two", "say hello"). A response is up to 8 words ending with <end>.
  • Pretraining text: 3,000 seeded documents of 2 to 4 lines: worksheets of instructions (50%: the next line is another instruction), worksheets with answers (12%), and pages of facts such as "apple is a fruit" (38%). Its held-out loss (nats per word on 300 fresh documents) is the toy's stand-in for the paper's public NLP benchmarks: the capability the alignment tax erodes.
  • Model: an MLP language model over the last 3 words, h = tanh(E1[wt-1] + E2[wt-2] + E3[wt-3] + b), logits = hU + c, 32 hidden units, 3,902 parameters, float64, Adam with β2 = 0.95 as in §C. Pretrained for 3,000 steps of 32 windows.
  • Labelers: the toy's ground truth (which the paper never has): +3 for a correct, complete answer; +0.5 for one opening "sure", minus 0.8 for each extra one; minus 0.6 per extra word; -1.5 for more instructions instead of an answer; -1 for a wrong answer; -1 for never stopping; -2 for nothing. A labeler ranks K answers by these scores plus Gumbel noise of scale 0.3, so any pair is a Bradley-Terry choice; 0.3 was chosen so two labelers agree on 73.3% of comparisons of SFT answers, near the paper's 72.6% (by construction).
  • Step 1: 600 demonstrations (the right answer, half of them with "sure" first), 16 epochs, cosine decay to 10%, with 10% of every batch pretraining text, as the paper's PPO initialisation (§C.3).
  • Step 2: a linear reward model on bag-of-words counts, global and per instruction (210 features); 800 prompts with K = 6 SFT answers ranked by one labeler, every C(6,2) pair of a prompt in one batch element, one epoch, cosine decay to 10%, then a bias so the demonstrations score 0 on average (§3.5, §C.2). It is deliberately simple: it cannot count, which is the hole PPO finds.
  • Step 3: bandit episodes; per-token reward -β log(π/πSFT), plus the RM score at the end; no discount; advantage = return minus a value table (per instruction and position) initialised from the RM's mean score, whitened per batch; PPO clip 0.2; 500 iterations of 64 episodes in minibatches of 16, one inner epoch. With γ > 0 each minibatch adds γ times the gradient of the mean log-likelihood of 8 fresh pretraining windows per episode, the ratio of §C.4. The value table stands in for the paper's 6B value network.
  • Checked: the JavaScript language-model gradient, the Eq. 1 loss and gradient, and the full PPO-ptx minibatch objective (clipping active on 17 of 66 tokens) match PyTorch autograd to within 5 × 10-16 (check_grad.py, PASS). Everything is seeded, so a setting gives the same numbers as the offline sweep (sweep.cjs) in a V8 browser.
  • What it cannot show: model size (one size), held-out labelers, a few-shot "prompted GPT-3" (the model sees only 3 words, so a prefix has no effect), 175B RM instability, and anything about real language. Its β and γ are in its own units: the paper's 0.02 and 27.8 do not transfer.

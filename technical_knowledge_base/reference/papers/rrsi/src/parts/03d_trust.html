How much of this to believe

[[Sx1|Limitations]]

What holds up.

  • The comparisons are like for like. Every baseline starts from the same H0 with the same policy, evolve split and candidate budget, and every number is measured against H0 in the same window ({{§4.1, §4.2|ax:S4.SS1}}). The ablations change one group of regularizers at a time.
  • Code and run records are released, which few papers in this line do: the search loop under Apache-2.0, and every round of four runs with candidates, critic verdicts, scores, token counts and diffs. This page replayed them. Algorithm 2 as released reproduces all 37 measured decisions of the coding run and 31 of 32 in the Harvey LAB run (the exception, labelled "lost to peer", is inadmissible under the rule as written; it was not kept either way). The four decisions of {{Table 6|ax:A5.T6}} match the records to the printed digit, the noise bands and cost slopes of {{Appendix D.1|ax:A4.SS1}} convert exactly, and {{Figures 1 and 4|ax:S1.F1}} reproduce from Tables 1 and 2 ({{all checks|tab:t-tables:chk}}).
  • The direction is consistent. All six splits outside the evolve sets improve, including the judge-free engineering domain and a policy the search never used. If each sign were a coin flip, six of six would happen one time in 64.
  • The authors state real limitations themselves ({{Limitations|ax:Sx1}}): frozen backbones only, a finite evolve set and several hyperparameters, and the need for broader validation on other architectures, tool ecosystems and longer runs. They also concede that evolution buys part of its gain with test-time compute. Those count in their favour.

What is weaker than the summary suggests.

  • Every arm is a single run with no error bars, and each transfer gain alone is within noise. Using binomial standard errors from the benchmark sizes the paper gives (independent runs; paired evaluation would shrink them by an unreported amount): SWE-bench Verified +1.8 points against a standard error of 2.4, GDPval +3.5 against 5.2, APEX-Agents +3.7 against 3.1, Flash Lite on Terminal-Bench +3.4 against 3.5 ({{noise table|tab:t-tables:noise}}). The six-of-six pattern is the stronger evidence, but it is not unique to RRSI: Meta-Harness also improves all four agentic held-out and out-of-distribution splits in Table 1, by less.
  • The released records disagree with three printed numbers. On Harvey LAB's 40 held-out tasks the record shows the base harness at 3,181 of 3,650 criteria (87.15%; four repeat measurements within one criterion of each other) and the final harness at 3,215 (88.08%): +0.9 points, where {{Table 1|ax:S4.T1}} prints 86.9 to 89.2, +2.3. The final harness logs 2.69M tokens per trial, where Table 2 prints 2.42M. The coding champion logs 144 of 178 passes (80.9%), and Table 3's 80.2 is not a whole number of 178 trials. The paper may have re-measured in a later window; it does not say so. A re-measurement below the logged best is what picking the best of many noisy scores predicts.
  • The released runs do not all follow the method as written. The coding run with Opus used a noise band of 0.034, twice Table 5's 0.017 ("recalibrated from the Opus baseline, z = 2"); under 0.017, four of its 37 decisions would flip, including the accepted round-11 edit (2 passes worse, 14% cheaper) and a rejected round-18 edit 4 passes better. The Gemini coding run, the source of the headline +14.1, is an earlier procedure: 30 iterations in two legs of 15, one candidate each, a 24-task screen before full evaluation, and ties accepted. The engineering run decides with paired t-statistics on a continuous score and a 5% token-saving threshold that the paper does not describe, and in 14 of its 40 rounds the proposer declined to propose anything.
  • Most accepted edits are inside their run's own noise band. Coding: 3 of 5 (the +8-pass final round carries most of the gain); Harvey LAB: 3 of 4 (round 1's +127 criteria is 76% of the +167); engineering: 3 of 4. Those small edits were admitted for saving tokens, for adding a new structural component, or by a within-band score weight. The coding run's last-round +8 passes is 2.6 standard deviations of the run's own calibrated noise, but it was the best of 37 evaluated candidates; the chance that at least one of 37 null candidates would reach it is about 14% (this page's estimate, treating evaluations as independent).
  • Protocol details do not add up. GDPval is described as a majority vote over 185 tasks, but five of the six GDPval scores in Table 1 are not whole numbers of 185, and "204 comparisons per judge" is not 185 tasks in two presentation orders (370). Two APEX-Agents scores (35.7, 34.3) are not whole numbers of the 480 single-rollout tasks. The within-band weights, said to be in Table 5, are only in the code. JobBench's size is not stated, and its judge averages in Claude Opus 4.8, the same model as the policy, proposer and critic.
  • The baselines are the authors' runs of four other methods under RRSI's evaluation budget, and their code is not released, so whether each was run as its authors intended cannot be checked. RRSI's own hyperparameters were set per domain on the evolve sets.
  • Smaller slips: the abstract's "30% fewer policy tokens" is 36% by Table 2 (it matches the released 2.69M, 29%); with the ceiling in Eq. 4, the "final-round" budget bmin = 1 is never reached, so every run's last budget is 2; §4.3 calls the cost rule "the L1-style budget" where §3.3 calls it Ridge-style; "costs only 0.2 points" is a 0.2-point increase.

Verdict. Believe the diagnosis (score-only harness search chases noise and grows the harness) and that RRSI's rules run as described: the released records replay to the same decisions. Believe the direction of the transfer result: six held-out splits all moved up, one of them judge-free. Treat the sizes as provisional: single runs, each gain within about one standard error, most kept edits inside their own noise band, three printed numbers that differ from the authors' own records, and a headline (+14.1) from a run that predates the method as described.

What it takes to use this

[[S6|§6, Appendix D, repository]]
  • Code: {{google-research/rrsi|https://github.com/google-research/rrsi}}, Apache-2.0, with the loop, the three domain adapters, their starting harnesses, prompts and configuration files, and a README that maps every part of Algorithms 1 and 2 to a file. Not included: the four baselines and the benchmarks themselves (harbor for Terminal-Bench, a pinned Harvey LAB checkout, the EngDesign repository). Run records and champion patches are on the {{project page|https://regularized-rsi.com/}}.
  • Models: one strong model for the proposer, analyst and critic (Claude Opus 4.8 on Vertex AI in the paper; any LiteLLM model string in the code) besides the frozen policy, plus an LLM judge where the benchmark needs one (Gemini 3.5 Flash for Harvey LAB).
  • Budget: every candidate is evaluated on the whole evolve set, k times per task. From the released records, the policy alone consumed about 4.1 billion tokens in the coding run (37 evaluations of 178 trials), 19 billion in the Harvey LAB run (32 evaluations of 240 trials at about 2.4M tokens each) and 0.9 billion in the engineering run; proposer, analyst, critic and judge tokens come on top (this page's sums of logged tokens per trial times trials).
  • Calibration first: the noise band comes from repeated evaluations of the unchanged base harness, and the released coding run shows the choice matters (0.017 against 0.034 flips four decisions). The cost slopes are set per domain from "how many tokens is one extra pass worth".
  • When it pays: when the evolved harness will be used on tasks other than the evolve suite and per-trial cost matters. When the evolve suite is the deployment, RRSI deliberately gives up evolve-set score (90.5 against 92.8 unregularised on Harvey LAB). It does not touch model weights.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

Harness evolution loops are multiplying, and each reports a gain on the suite it optimised. {{HarnessDev|n:3d45c17b0d0d81cd9883f783840e013f}} found that in its evolution runs the score a creator could see moved with the held-out score in only 34 of 64 version changes. RRSI is a direct answer to that failure: it treats the evolve set as a finite, noisy, reused sample, and it brings the vocabulary of regularisation to a search over programs. Its strongest reusable idea is procedural rather than numeric: separate evolve, held-out and out-of-distribution splits, calibrate a noise band before the run, and make extra tokens pay for themselves.

{{SoL-Pi|n:3e25c17b0d0d81a0bb77f16cbc898bb5}} runs the same loop for cost rather than capability and finds roughly half of agent token traffic is harness overhead. Between them the loop now has both a cost objective and a generalisation guard, which are the two things a self-modifying system needs before anyone should leave it running. The paper itself claims to be the first to ask whether the gains survive leaving the benchmark; it also cites concurrent work that separates evolution from evaluation tasks for the same reason, such as {{HarnessCompass|https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01918}}, so "first" belongs to the question being asked widely at once.

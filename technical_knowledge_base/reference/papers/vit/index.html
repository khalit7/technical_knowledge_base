Problem

§1 Introduction, §2 Related work

By 2020 the Transformer was the default architecture in NLP, usually pre-trained on a large corpus and then fine-tuned, with models past 100B parameters and no sign of saturating (§1). Vision was still ruled by convolutional networks: ResNet-like models held the state of the art in large-scale image recognition (§1).

Earlier attempts to bring self-attention to images either kept the CNN skeleton and added or swapped in attention blocks, or replaced convolutions with specialised attention patterns (local neighbourhoods, sparse approximations, blocks, one axis at a time) that "require complex engineering to be implemented efficiently on hardware accelerators" and so had not been scaled (§2). Plain global attention over pixels is quadratic in the pixel count: a 224 × 224 image is 50,176 pixels, about 2.5 billion pairs per layer and head.

The question: can a plain, unmodified Transformer, with almost no image-specific inductive bias, compete with the best CNNs, and under what conditions?

ViT cuts a 224 × 224 image into 16 × 16 patches and treats each patch as one token. How long is the sequence the Transformer sees?

(224 / 16)² = 14 × 14 = 196 patches; the paper adds one learnable class token, so 197 positions (§3.1). Attention pairs per layer and head fall from 50,176² ≈ 2.5 × 10⁹ to 197² ≈ 39,000, a factor of about 65,000. Smaller patches cost more: sequence length goes as 1/P², and attention pairs as 1/P⁴ (§4.1).

Try: move the patch size and the resolution. The grid is to scale; the counts are exact.

Patch sizes are the paper's (14, 16, 32) plus three smaller ones for comparison (1 is pixel attention, 2 is the size Cordonnier et al. used, §2, and 8); resolutions are pre-training (224), fine-tuning (384) and the Table 2 fine-tuning resolutions for ViT-L/16 (512) and ViT-H/14 (518), §4.1. When the side is not a multiple of P, the remainder is dropped, as a stride-P convolution would.

Idea

§3 Method

Turn the image into a sequence of tokens and run a completely standard Transformer encoder on it. "In model design we follow the original Transformer as closely as possible", so that scalable NLP architectures and their efficient implementations can be used almost out of the box (§3). Image patches are treated the same way as words: split the image into fixed-size patches, embed each linearly, add position embeddings, prepend a learnable classification token, and feed the sequence to the encoder (Figure 1). The model is trained to classify images, supervised.

The bet is about data, not architecture. Without the CNN's built-in assumptions the authors expect, and find, worse results on mid-sized datasets such as ImageNet; with 14M to 300M images "large scale training trumps inductive bias" (§1).

Method: from pixels to a class

§3.1, Eq. 1 to 4

An image x ∈ ℝH×W×C becomes N = HW/P² flattened P × P patches of P²·C numbers each; N is the sequence length, and P is 16 or 32 (14 for ViT-H). One trainable linear projection E maps each patch to the model width D, constant through all layers: the patch embeddings (§3.1, §4.1).

z0 = [xclass; xp1E; xp2E; …; xpNE] + Epos,   E ∈ ℝ(P²·C)×D, Epos ∈ ℝ(N+1)×D
z′ℓ = MSA(LN(zℓ−1)) + zℓ−1,   zℓ = MLP(LN(z′ℓ)) + z′ℓ,   ℓ = 1 … L
y = LN(zL0)
Equations 1 to 4 (Eq. 1, Eq. 2, Eq. 3, Eq. 4). MSA is standard multi-head qkv self-attention, softmax(qk⊤/√Dh)v per head with Dh = D/k for k heads, outputs concatenated and projected (Appendix A, Eq. 5 to 8).
  • Class token. As with BERT's [class] token, a learnable embedding z00 = xclass is prepended; its state at the encoder output, zL0, is the image representation y. A classification head sits on it: an MLP with one hidden layer during pre-training, a single linear layer during fine-tuning (§3.1). The hidden layer uses tanh (App. D.3).
  • Encoder. Alternating multi-head self-attention and MLP blocks; LayerNorm before every block (pre-LN) and a residual connection after every block; the MLP has two layers with a GELU (§3.1).
  • Sizes. Taken from BERT: ViT-Base (12 layers, D = 768, MLP 3072, 12 heads, 86M parameters) and ViT-Large (24, 1024, 4096, 16 heads, 307M), plus a new ViT-Huge (32, 1280, 5120, 16 heads, 632M). ViT-L/16 means Large with 16 × 16 patches (Table 1). The parameter counts are recounted from Eq. 1 to 4 in the tables tab.
  • Hybrid. The patches can instead come from a CNN feature map, here a ResNet50's, with 1 × 1 patches: flatten the map and project it to D (§3.1, §4.1).

Inductive bias, made explicit

§3.1, Inductive bias; §3.2

CNNs build three assumptions into every layer: locality, two-dimensional neighbourhood structure and translation equivariance. In ViT only the MLP layers are local and translation-equivariant (they act on each token separately); self-attention is global from layer one (§3.1).

The image's 2D structure enters at exactly two points: cutting it into patches, and adjusting the position embeddings to a new resolution at fine-tuning. The position embeddings start with no information about where patches are, so every spatial relation is learned from scratch (§3.1, §3.2): the network must learn from examples what a CNN is handed for free.

Fine-tuning at a higher resolution

Pre-train on large data, then remove the pre-trained head and attach a zero-initialised D × K layer for the K downstream classes (§3.2). Fine-tuning at a higher resolution than pre-training helps; keep the patch size fixed, so the sequence gets longer (ViT-L/16 goes from 196 patches at 224 px to 576 at 384 and 1,024 at 512), and 2D-interpolate the pre-trained position embeddings to the new grid according to their location in the original image. This is now standard practice. In the appendix, the whole two-layer head is replaced on transfer, which "we found to be a little more robust than simply re-initializing the very last layer" (App. B.1.1).

Position embeddings

§3.1, App. D.4, Table 8, Figure 7

ViT uses standard learnable 1D position embeddings, one vector per position in raster order (§3.1). The ablation on ViT-B/16 (Table 8) compared no position information (a bag of patches), 1D, 2D (separate X and Y halves) and relative embeddings (an attention bias from the query-key offset), added at the stem or at every layer. No positions costs 2.82 points of 5-shot accuracy; every other variant lands within 0.33 points of the rest, because at patch level the grid is small (14 × 14, not 224 × 224) and any encoding learns it easily (App. D.4). The learned 1D embeddings recover the 2D grid on their own: nearby patches, and patches in the same row or column, get similar embeddings, sometimes with a sinusoidal pattern on larger grids, and the exact pattern depends on the hyperparameters (Figure 7, centre, Figure 10).

Try: this is the toy ViT's own learned table, one tile per patch (7 × 7 grid); each tile shows that patch's cosine similarity to every other patch. Click a tile to enlarge it. Switch to the model trained on 2,000 images to compare.

Cosine similarity of the 49 patch position embeddings of the shipped weights (6-bit quantised; the float weights give the same grid score to within 0.002, pos_check.py). "Grid score" is the correlation, over all patch pairs, between embedding similarity and minus the distance between the two patches on the grid: 1 would mean similarity falls exactly with distance.

Remove the position embeddings entirely, so the model sees an unordered bag of patches. On ViT-B/16 in the paper, how much accuracy does that cost?

A few points: 61.4% against 64.2% 5-shot (Table 8). Patches still carry a lot of information on their own: a bag of patches still knows which textures and parts are present. On this page's toy task the same ablation, trained the same way on 64,000 images, gets of held-out images right against with position embeddings.

Training and evaluation

§4.1, App. B
  • Data: pre-training on ImageNet (1k classes, 1.3M images), ImageNet-21k (21k classes, 14M) or Google's in-house JFT-300M (18k classes, 303M), de-duplicated against the test sets; transfer to ImageNet (original and cleaned ReaL labels), CIFAR-10/100, Oxford-IIIT Pets, Oxford Flowers-102 and the 19-task VTAB suite (1,000 examples per task; Natural, Specialized, Structured) (§4.1).
  • Baselines: "ResNet (BiT)", ResNets with Group Normalization and standardised convolutions, which transfer better (§4.1).
  • Pre-training: every model, ResNets included, uses Adam (β1 0.9, β2 0.999), batch 4096, weight decay 0.1, linear warmup and decay, 224 px (§4.1, Table 3); Adam beat SGD for the ResNets too (Table 7). Strong regularisation was key from scratch on ImageNet (App. B.1).
  • Fine-tuning: SGD with momentum, batch 512, cosine decay, no weight decay, gradient clipping at norm 1, 384 px unless stated (Table 4); for Table 2's ImageNet numbers 512 px (ViT-L/16) and 518 px (ViT-H/14) with Polyak averaging, factor 0.9999 (§4.1).
  • Metrics: fine-tuning accuracy, or few-shot linear accuracy (closed-form regularised least squares from frozen features to {−1, 1}K targets) for quick checks. Compute is in TPUv3-core-days: cores (2 per chip) times days (§4.1, §4.2).

Results: against the state of the art

§4.2, Table 2, Figure 2

The largest models, pre-trained on JFT-300M, are compared with the two best CNN results of the time (§4.2): BiT-L (Big Transfer, a ResNet152x4 also pre-trained on JFT-300M) and Noisy Student (an EfficientNet-L2 trained semi-supervised on ImageNet plus unlabelled JFT-300M), then state of the art on ImageNet and on the other datasets respectively. Accuracies are means and standard deviations over three fine-tuning runs (Table 2).

  • ViT-H/14 on JFT-300M: 88.55% ImageNet top-1, 90.72% ImageNet-ReaL, 99.50% CIFAR-10, 94.55% CIFAR-100, 97.56% Pets, 99.68% Flowers, 77.63% on the 19 VTAB tasks: best or matching on every column except Flowers, where ViT-L/16 has 99.74% (Table 2).
  • Compute: ViT-H/14 pre-trained in 2.5k TPUv3-core-days, against 9.9k for BiT-L and 12.3k for Noisy Student; that is 3.96 and 4.92 times less. ViT-L/16 on JFT needed 0.68k, 14.6 times less than BiT-L (Table 2). The paper notes that pre-training efficiency also depends on the schedule, optimiser and weight decay, and points to the controlled study in §4.4 (§4.2).
  • ViT-L/16 against BiT-L: the paper says it "outperforms BiT-L ... on all tasks". In the table it is ahead on five of seven, level on ImageNet-ReaL (90.54 against 90.54) and 0.01 behind on VTAB (76.28 ± 0.46 against 76.29 ± 1.70). See How much of this to believe.
  • Public data only: ViT-L/16 pre-trained on ImageNet-21k reaches 85.30% on ImageNet and "could be trained using a standard cloud TPUv3 with 8 cores in approximately 30 days" (8 × 30 = 240 core-days, the table's 0.23k) (§4.2).
  • VTAB by group: ViT-H/14 beats BiT, VIVI and S4L on Natural and Structured tasks and is level on Specialized (Figure 2); on ObjectNet it gets 61.7% top-1 (App. D.9).

Every Table 2 column, with the gaps in units of the runs' standard deviation, is in the tables tab.

The real contribution: how much data

§4.3, Figures 3 and 4, Table 5

With fewer built-in assumptions than a ResNet, how much data does ViT need? Two experiments answer it (§4.3).

  1. Bigger pre-training sets, then fine-tune (Figure 3, numbers in Table 5), with weight decay, dropout and label smoothing tuned for the small sets. Pre-trained on ImageNet, ViT-Large is worse than ViT-Base; on ImageNet-21k they are similar; only on JFT-300M does the larger model pay off. BiT ResNets beat ViT pre-trained on ImageNet; ViT overtakes on the larger sets (§4.3).
  2. Random JFT subsets of 9M, 30M, 90M and the full 303M, same hyperparameters for every size and no extra regularisation, early-stopped, scored by few-shot linear accuracy (Figure 4). ViTs overfit more than ResNets of similar cost on small subsets: ViT-B/32 (slightly faster than ResNet50) is much worse on 9M but better from 90M up, and likewise ViT-L/16 against ResNet152x2. ResNets do better with little data but plateau sooner.

The authors' conclusion: "the convolutional inductive bias is useful for smaller datasets, but for larger ones, learning the relevant patterns directly from data is sufficient, even beneficial" (§4.3). The paper gives no table for Figure 4, so where between 9M and 90M the curves cross is only readable off the plot.

Both pre-trained on ImageNet alone (1.3M images) and then fine-tuned on ImageNet: which gets the higher accuracy, ViT-B/16 (86M parameters) or ViT-L/16 (307M)?

The smaller one: 77.91% against 76.53% (Table 5). Larger models need more data before their capacity helps. The chart shows ViT-L/16 minus ViT-B/16 on each fine-tuning dataset as the pre-training set grows; below it, this page's own version of the experiment.

Results: performance against compute

§4.4, Figure 5, Table 6

A controlled scaling study on JFT-300M, where data does not limit the models: 7 ResNets, 6 ViTs and 5 hybrids, pre-trained for 7 or 14 epochs (all listed in Table 6; for hybrids the number is the ResNet's downsampling, not a patch size), transfer accuracy against pre-training compute (§4.4). Three patterns:

  • ViTs dominate ResNets on the accuracy/compute trade-off: "approximately 2 to 4× less compute to attain the same performance (average over 5 datasets)". Reading Table 6 by interpolation along the ResNets' compute frontier gives 1.8 to 2.3× on ImageNet alone and 2.8 to 6.0× on the five-dataset average, where it can be read at all (the larger ViTs are above every ResNet) (the recount).
  • Hybrids slightly beat pure ViTs at small budgets, but the gap vanishes for larger models, which the authors find "somewhat surprising" (§4.4).
  • ViTs "appear not to saturate within the range tried".

In the appendix, depth is the best dimension to scale (gains up to 64 layers, diminishing after 16), width the worst, and smaller patches help without adding parameters, so compute predicts performance better than parameter count (App. D.2). Measured on TPUv3, ViT runs about as fast as similar ResNets and fits larger batches per core (App. D.5).

Inside the model

§4.5, Figures 6 and 7, App. D.7 and D.8
  • Patch filters of ViT-L/32's E look like basis functions for fine structure within a patch (Figure 7, left).
  • Attention distance, the analogue of a CNN's receptive field: some heads attend across most of the image already in the lowest layers, others stay local (less so in hybrids, so they may do the job of early convolutions), and distance grows with depth (Figure 7, right, App. D.7).
  • Attention maps from the output token, by attention rollout (heads averaged, multiplied through the layers), land on the regions that matter for the class (Figure 6, App. D.8).

Try: the toy ViT's own version of Figure 7 (right), measured in your browser on 100 held-out images. Each dot is one head at one layer.

Attention distance: for each patch query, the distance in pixels between patch centres, weighted by the attention weight, averaged over queries and images (the paper weights pixel distances; with 4 × 4 patches the difference is small). The class token is left out as query and key. Image width is 28 px.

A first try at self-supervision

§4.6, App. B.1.2

Much of the Transformer's success in NLP comes from self-supervised pre-training, so the authors tried a masked patch prediction objective, mimicking BERT (§4.6): corrupt 50% of patch embeddings (80% replaced by a learnable [mask] embedding, 10% by another patch, 10% kept) and predict the 3-bit mean colour (512 colours) of each corrupted patch (App. B.1.2). With it, ViT-B/16 reaches 79.9% on ImageNet: about 2 points above training from scratch (Table 5's 77.91%) and about 4 behind supervised pre-training (83.97% from ImageNet-21k, 84.15% from JFT). Other targets worked about as well; returns diminished after 100k steps, and ImageNet pre-training gave similar gains (App. B.1.2). Contrastive pre-training was left to future work.

The conclusion lists three challenges: apply ViT to detection and segmentation, keep exploring self-supervised pre-training, and scale further (§5).

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

This is the paper that ended CNN dominance in vision at scale. Its lesson: hand-designed inductive bias stands in for data, and with enough data and compute a generic architecture that learns the structure catches up, which put vision on NLP's scaling playbook and one architecture under both. Each later claim has its own source.

  • Vision towers of contrastive and multimodal models. CLIP (400 million image-text pairs, ViTs among its image encoders, CLIP) and SigLIP (a pairwise sigmoid loss, SigLIP) train ViT image towers against text; ALIGN, often listed with them, uses an EfficientNet, not a ViT (ALIGN, §4). Vision-language models attach such a tower to an LLM: LLaVA uses "the pre-trained CLIP visual encoder ViT-L/14" (LLaVA), Qwen-VL a ViT initialised from OpenCLIP's ViT-bigG (Qwen-VL), InternVL a 6-billion-parameter InternViT (InternVL). For Gemini-class closed models the vision encoder is not described in enough detail to say (unconfirmed).
  • Self-supervised vision. The masked patch prediction tried in §4.6 was made to work by BEiT (masked image modelling with visual tokens, BEiT) and MAE (mask 75% of patches and reconstruct pixels; a vanilla ViT-Huge reaches 87.8% using only ImageNet-1K data, MAE). DINO and DINOv2 built self-distillation on ViT backbones; DINOv2 trains a 1B-parameter ViT (DINO, DINOv2).
  • Diffusion. The Diffusion Transformer (DiT) replaced the U-Net of latent diffusion with a transformer on latent patches, reaching FID 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256 × 256 (DiT); Stable Diffusion 3 uses a transformer-based rectified-flow architecture with separate weights for text and image tokens (SD3). OpenAI describes Sora as a diffusion transformer on spacetime patches (OpenAI's report; not re-checked here, the page blocks scripted fetches); for Flux there is no paper (unconfirmed).
  • Practice. Patchify-then-Transformer became the default for grid-shaped inputs: video (ViViT, spatio-temporal tokens), audio spectrograms (AST). The fine-tuning tricks of §3.2, a higher resolution with interpolated position embeddings, are still standard; NaViT goes further and packs patches of images at their native resolution and aspect ratio (NaViT). What changed since is in Then and now.

Connections

  • Attention Is All You Need (2017-06): the encoder ViT reuses nearly unchanged.
  • BERT (2018-10): source of the [class] token, the model sizing and the masked-prediction pre-training idea.
  • Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models (2020-01): the NLP scaling story whose vision counterpart this paper begins.
  • CLIP (2021-02): pairs a ViT image encoder with a text encoder; the main route by which ViT reached multimodal models.
  • DDPM (2020-06) and Latent Diffusion (2021-12): the diffusion line that later swapped its U-Net for ViT-style DiT backbones.

How much of this to believe

What holds up.

  • The headline gaps are far outside the noise. Over three fine-tuning runs, ViT-H/14 leads BiT-L by 1.01 points on ImageNet (standard deviations 0.04 and 0.02, about 23 combined sd) and 1.04 on CIFAR-100 (11 sd) (Table 2; every gap in the tables tab).
  • Same data, same recipe. BiT-L is also pre-trained on JFT-300M; in §4.4 the ResNets get the ViTs' optimiser, schedule and data, and Adam is checked not to handicap them (Table 7).
  • The compute column reproduces: three times the forward FLOPs times the images seen gives Table 6's exaFLOPs within 2% for every ViT (recount).
  • Later work confirms the architecture: the same design scaled to 2B parameters reached 90.45% (Scaling ViTs), and the vision towers above are ViTs. The authors also flag that schedule, optimiser and weight decay affect the compute comparison (§4.2), which counts in their favour.

What to discount.

  • One pre-training run per model. Error bars cover fine-tuning only; Tables 5 and 6, Figure 4 and the scaling study have none, and some differences the text leans on are small (ViT-L/16 over BiT-L on ImageNet: 0.22 points).
  • "ViT-L/16 outperforms BiT-L on all tasks" does not match the table: level on ReaL (90.54 each), 0.01 behind on VTAB, within BiT-L's ± 1.70 (Table 2).
  • Table 2's core-days are not like for like: different papers' schedules and recipes. Only §4.4 holds the recipe fixed, and there the ResNets train 7 epochs, not BiT's 30 (App. D.1); the "2 to 4×" is a five-dataset average, 1.8 to 2.3× on ImageNet alone.
  • "Data trumps inductive bias" leaned on a weak small-data recipe: only weight decay, dropout and label smoothing were tuned (§4.3). Two months later DeiT trained ViT-B on ImageNet alone to 83.1%, against Table 5's 77.91% (DeiT); the authors' AugReg follow-up made ImageNet-21k match JFT-300M (AugReg); plain ViTs pass 76% on ImageNet-1k in 90 epochs with standard augmentation (Beyer et al. 2022).
  • CNNs were not out of the race: ConvNeXt reaches 87.8% (arXiv 2201.03545), and NFNets on JFT-4B "match the reported performance of Vision Transformers with comparable compute budgets", up to 90.4% (Smith et al. 2023).
  • The main dataset is private; the ImageNet-21k result (85.30%) is the reproducible one. And small inconsistencies: Table 5's ViT-H/14 JFT row differs from Table 6's and matches Table 2 on four columns, "Figure 4" is cited for Figure 3, Table 1's 307M recounts to 303.3M (details). None changes a conclusion.

At toy scale on this page the direction reproduces and the crossover does not: . The ResNet has more than the ViT to gain from its built-in bias here (the task is the same wherever the shapes are), and one seed per point says nothing about where large models cross. The learned position embeddings also show far less grid structure than Figure 7 (see Position embeddings).

Verdict

Believe the architecture and the direction: a plain Transformer on patches, pre-trained on enough data, matches or beats the 2020 CNNs, Table 2's gaps are far outside the noise, and every later vision tower is built this way. Treat "large scale training trumps inductive bias" and the 2 to 4× saving as statements about the 2020 recipes: better training later made ViTs work on ImageNet alone, and CNNs trained at the same scale match them. The method is now standard, so Then and now replaces "What it takes to use this".

How the toy was built, and what it costs

  • Model (Eq. 1 to 4): 4 × 4 patches (49 tokens plus a class token), D = 32, 4 layers, 4 heads, MLP 128 (4D, as in Table 1), pre-LayerNorm, tanh-approximated GELU, learnable 1D position embeddings, y = LN(zL0) and a zero-initialised linear head (the paper's fine-tuning head; there is no separate pre-training stage here).
  • Baseline: a BiT-style ResNet (GroupNorm, a stride-2 stem, three residual blocks of 16, 32 and 48 channels, global average pooling). It is not shipped; only its accuracy and curves are.
  • Training (§4.1, §4.3 recipe at toy scale): AdamW, β1 0.9, β2 0.999, weight decay 0.1, 100 warmup steps then linear decay, batch 128 (the paper's 4096 is far beyond this task), 2,000 steps for every training-set size, early stopping on 2,000 validation images. No augmentation, dropout or label smoothing.

  • Data: the generator in this page (the same file made the training data with node), so drawn images are exactly the training distribution; training, validation, test and this tab's test use disjoint seed ranges. Two images from different seeds can coincide only by chance, which is negligible given random sizes, positions, intensities and noise.

Then and now

The 2020 ViT's input sequence, changed one sourced step at a time into what vision towers and image generators use now. Each step names the paper that made the change; what stayed the same is at the bottom.

Token strips are schematic (a few patches stand for 196); the counts under them are exact for the configuration named in the caption.

Survived
  • Cutting the image into non-overlapping patches with one linear projection (every model in the steps above).
  • A plain pre-LN Transformer encoder with global attention over patch tokens.
  • Pre-train big, transfer; and the scale-first view of §4.4 (Scaling ViTs).
Changed
  • Supervision: JFT labels gave way to augmentation recipes, masked prediction, self-distillation and image-text pairs.
  • Position: the learned 1D table, interpolated for new resolutions, gave way to native resolutions and 2D rotary embeddings in some towers.
  • Extra tokens: registers beside (or instead of) the single class token.
  • The use: from a classifier to the eyes of language models and the backbone of image and video generators.

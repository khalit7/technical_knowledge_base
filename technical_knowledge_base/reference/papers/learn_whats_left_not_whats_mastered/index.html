Learn What's Left, Not What's Mastered: Saturation Aware Advantage Reweighting for Multi-Reward Policy Optimization (SA-MRPO)

Yixuan Wang*, Yifei Chen*, Haichao Zhang, Haozheng Luo, Xander Wu, Jie Ni, Yun Fu, Nuno Vasconcelos, Yijiang Li† (* equal contribution; † corresponding author and project lead)
University of Florida, UC San Diego, Northeastern University, Northwestern University, Stanford University, Zillion Network, Universität Innsbruck · 17 August 2026 (arXiv v1, the only version)

In one line: Drop-in advantage reweighting for multi-reward GRPO/RLVR: normalise each objective per group, then down-weight it by (1 - batch saturation)^γ so the update goes to objectives with headroom left; it beats GDPO (its γ = 0 case) on 12 of 15 math comparisons and by 3.8 points on average in adaptive reasoning, with γ = 0.5 best in the ablation.

Problem

§1, §3

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) trains a reasoning model on answers a rule can check. GRPO, its standard algorithm, samples a group of answers to the same question and scores each one against the group (§3; the algorithm comes from DeepSeekMath). It assumes one scalar reward per answer. In practice a reasoning model is rarely judged on one axis: an answer must be correct, and also respect a length budget, a format, a safety rule or, for code, simply run (§1).

The standard recipe scalarises: add the rewards with fixed weights, rsum = Σk wk rk, then standardise the sum within each group as GRPO does. The paper names two failure modes of that recipe (§1, §4):

  1. Scalarisation loses reward resolution. Different reward profiles can collapse to the same scalar and get the same advantage. With equal weights, (1, 0) and (0, 1) are indistinguishable, so an answer with perfect format and zero correctness is reinforced as strongly as the reverse.
  2. Fixed weights ignore saturation. Each objective keeps its share of the update all through training, however close it is to its ceiling. Easy auxiliary objectives (format, length) saturate early yet keep drawing the same share, so training keeps polishing what is already solved while correctness, the hard objective, still has most of its headroom.

GDPO (group reward-decoupled normalisation, Liu et al., 2026) fixes the first: it standardises each reward separately within the group, then sums. The paper's point is that the second survives decoupling, because GDPO's weights are still fixed. Concurrent methods adapt the weights by other signals: DVAO by reward variance, GD2PO by conflicts between reward-specific advantages, SAW by reward variability, Focal Reward by saturation of rubric criteria (§2). None, the authors argue, ties the allocation directly to the fraction of the attainable reward range still unrealised.

The proposal, SA-MRPO (Saturation Aware Advantage Reweighting for Multi-Reward Policy Optimization): keep GDPO's per-objective standardisation, and multiply each objective's weight by (1 − sk)γ, where sk is how much of that objective's reward range the current batch already achieves. Nothing else about the GRPO update changes.

Three things to see. GRPO gives answers 2 and 3 the same advantage, zero, because both sum to 100: that is the resolution loss. GDPO separates them but ranks answer 3 (perfect format, zero correctness) above answer 2 (one point of correctness), because per-group standardisation stretches the format objective's tiny spread (97 to 100) to the same unit scale as correctness. SA-MRPO shrinks the format term to almost nothing, and the ranking follows correctness.

Under GDPO, answer 3 (perfect format, zero correctness) gets +0.61. With the format objective at saturation 0.98 and correctness at 0.02, what does SA-MRPO give it at the paper's recommended γ = 0.5?

Negative: −0.53 at γ = 0.5 (before Eq. 1). The weight ratio of format to correctness is (0.02 / 0.98)γ, 0.14 at γ = 0.5, and answer 3's correctness term (−0.73) then outweighs its format term (+1.34 × 0.14). Method

§4.1, Algorithm 1, Eq. 1

Setup (§3): a batch of B questions, G ≥ 2 answers to each from the frozen sampling policy, n rule-based objectives with known bounds [rmin(k), rmax(k)] and prescribed weights wk ≥ 0. Four steps (§4.1, Algorithm 1):

Ak(i,j) = ( rk(i,j) − μk(i) ) / σk(i)

1. Per-objective group advantage (the GDPO part): each objective standardised within its group of G answers, with the group's mean and population standard deviation. This keeps the per-objective resolution that summing destroys.

s(k) = ( r̄(k) − rmin(k) ) / ( rmax(k) − rmin(k) ) ∈ [0, 1]

2. Saturation ratio (the new part): r̄(k) is objective k's mean reward over all B × G answers in the batch. Nothing is estimated: for bounded rule-based rewards the bounds come with the reward function.

w̃k = wk (1 − s(k))γ,   Ã(i,j) = Σk w̃k Ak(i,j)

3. Saturation-aware aggregation, with exponent γ ≥ 0, the method's one new hyperparameter.

ÂSA(i,j) = ( Ã(i,j) − mean(𝒜) ) / std(𝒜)   (Eq. 1)

4. Re-standardise over the whole batch, so the advantage's scale stays stable while the saturations drift. ÂSA then goes into the standard clipped GRPO surrogate unchanged; the KL term is omitted as independent of the reward construction (§3).

Properties (§4.1):

  • Special cases. γ = 0 gives w̃k = wk, which is GDPO's advantage exactly (GDPO also standardises over the batch at the end, GDPO §3.1). With one objective it reduces further to GRPO; since every group's advantages already average zero, Eq. 1 then only rescales the batch by one positive number (derived). So SA-MRPO strictly generalises both.
  • γ is the single knob. For two objectives, w̃a / w̃b = (wa / wb) · ((1 − sa) / (1 − sb))γ, strictly decreasing in γ when a is the more saturated. Because Eq. 1 rescales the whole batch, only these ratios matter: the overall size of the weights cancels.
  • Cost. One batch mean per objective and one multiply; the paper reports no timing, but the extra work is negligible beside sampling (derived).
Why does saturation still matter after GDPO's decoupling? Take a group of 8 answers where 7 meet a binary length budget and 1 does not. What advantage does GDPO's length term give the one long answer?

−2.65, and +0.38 to each of the other seven: per-group standardisation gives an objective unit spread in every group where it varies at all, however rare the failures. A nearly solved objective is therefore silent in most groups (zero spread; the paper does not say how a zero standard deviation is handled, and implementations add a small ε, which makes the advantage 0) and loud in the few where it fails. Move the number of answers that pass:

How much of the update that is depends on how often groups fail. With equal weights, the length objective's share of the advantage's sum of squares is the share of groups where it varies, among all groups where either objective varies (derived: every varying group contributes exactly G). A 0.2% failure rate puts it in of groups; if 60% of groups vary in correctness, length gets about of the update. That is the budget SA-MRPO can reallocate in the paper's binary-budget runs, and why the toy finds little to gain there (the toy).

What it does not promise

§4.2, Eq. 2

The authors state two limits themselves (§4.2), which counts in their favour.

  • No retention guarantee. With gk the gradient of objective k's surrogate and update direction d = Σk w̃k gk, objective a changes to first order by w̃a‖ga‖² + Σk≠a w̃k gaᵀgk, and it decreases whenever Σk≠a w̃k gaᵀgk < −w̃a‖ga‖² (Eq. 2). Shrinking w̃a as a saturates deliberately weakens the self-improvement term that protects it, so a conflicting gradient from a less saturated objective can erode an objective already learned. SA-MRPO is "an adaptive objective allocation rule rather than a constrained multi objective method"; whether the gain outweighs the erosion is an empirical question.
  • Nominal, not achievable, headroom. 1 − s(k) is the unrealised fraction of the prescribed range, exactly observable, but not a certificate that the model can realise it. The paper does not count this as a failure of the measure, since the unreachable part is imposed by the policy class.

Experimental setup

§5.1, §5.2

Training (§5.1): verl with vLLM for sampling; G = 8 answers per question, global batch 256 questions, 3 epochs, answers capped at 4,096 tokens. Math and adaptive reasoning train on DeepScaleR-Preview, about 40K competition problems (40,315 in the released dataset). Evaluation: temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, up to 4,096 tokens (2,048 for code), 16 samples per problem, average pass@1. "Base" is the model before RL. The baseline throughout is GDPO with the same data, rewards and hyperparameters; only the advantage differs.

The rewards, all rule-based (§5.2, §5.3, §5.4):

  • Correctness: 1 if the extracted final answer equals the ground truth, else 0.
  • Length (binary): 1 if the answer has at most l = 4,000 tokens, else 0. With answers capped at 4,096 tokens in training, only an answer in its last 96 allowed tokens, in practice a truncated one, scores 0 (derived from the two settings).
  • Format: 1 if the answer matches ^<think>.*?</think>\n<answer>.*?</answer>$ with exactly one opening and one closing answer tag, else 0.
  • Length (graded), adaptive reasoning only: 1 up to Bmin = 1,024 tokens, falling linearly to 0 at Bmax = 2,048, so it has an explicit saturation region: below 1,024 tokens, shortening earns nothing.
  • Code: pass rate (passed test cases over all test cases) and executability (1 if the program compiles and runs without errors).

Results

§5.2 to §5.4, Tables 1 to 3

The averages hide the spread: on AIME24 SA-MRPO's answers average 804 tokens, close enough to 1,024 that part of them probably pays a length penalty; the paper reports no distribution. And both runs compress a model that normally reasons for thousands of tokens into a few hundred, at a large cost in accuracy against the released model; the +3.8 is measured inside that heavily compressed regime, and Table 2 has no Base row to show how much each method lost.

The saturation exponent γ

§5.5, Figure 2, Table 4

Qwen2.5-3B and 7B-Instruct trained with correctness and binary length for γ = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1 (§5.5). In the training curves (Figure 2, one smoothed run per γ), positive γ generally gives higher correctness reward, and the length reward falls as γ grows, most for 0.75 and 1. Downstream, for 3B only (Table 4): every γ > 0 beats γ = 0 on average accuracy, γ = 0.5 is best on average (28.0% against 26.4%) and on AIME24 and AMC23, and larger γ raises Exceed. The paper's reading: γ directly sets the trade between pushing correctness and protecting the saturated objective, and moderate values balance best.

Table 4 averaged over its five benchmarks (recompute.py). Average accuracy rises from 26.4% at γ = 0 to 28.0% at 0.5 then eases; average Exceed rises from 0.04% to 0.48%.

In these γ runs, the length reward is 1 for answers within 4,000 tokens. Where did the batch's length score stand at the very first training step, out of 100?

Above 97. Figure 2's length panels span only 97.5 to 100 (3B) and 98.0 to 100 (7B) on their printed axes, with every curve, faint raw lines included, inside them from the first step: the "saturated" objective was saturated before training started, because Qwen2.5-Instruct rarely writes 4,000 tokens and the training cap is 4,096. So in the binary-budget runs (Tables 1 and 4) there was almost nothing for GDPO to waste on length: its loud but rare signal (see the 7-of-8 question above) touched a few percent of groups. SA-MRPO's downweighting then mostly removes a small constraint, and Exceed creeps up as γ grows, exactly as Table 4 shows:

Two details of the ablation matter for the evidence. Table 4's caption says its γ = 0.25 column "corresponds to the SA-MRPO2obj result in Table 1", and its γ = 0 column is identical, cell for cell, to Table 1's GDPO2obj. So the main table's 3B two-objective result used γ = 0.25, not the recommended 0.5, and the recommended value was chosen by looking at the same test benchmarks the results are reported on. The γ used for the 7B, three-objective, adaptive and code runs is not stated. And §5.5 says these runs were trained "for one epoch", while §5.1 says all models train for 3 epochs, which Table 1 inherits: the same numbers cannot be both, so either Table 1's 3B two-objective runs were one epoch or Table 4's two columns were not one-epoch runs (one epoch of 40,315 problems at batch 256 is 157 steps; Figure 2's step axes reach about 120 for 3B and 150 for 7B).

How much of this to believe

§4, §5, Tables 1 to 4, Figures 1 and 2

What holds up. The mechanism is simple, fully specified (Algorithm 1) and correct: Figure 1 recomputes from its printed rewards, the special cases hold (γ = 0 is GDPO; this page checks its implementation of all three recipes against PyTorch to 1e-15, the check), and the ratio and sign-reversal claims are exact algebra. The direction of the results is consistent: 12 of 15 math cells, 5 of 5 adaptive, 3 of 4 code, and a γ ablation where every γ > 0 beats γ = 0 on average. The authors state the method's two limits themselves (§4.2), and the comparison with GDPO uses the same data, rewards and hyperparameters.

What is weaker, concretely:

  • single runs Every cell of every table is one training run: no seeds, no error bars, no confidence intervals. Small benchmarks make that matter: the AIME24 gain of 5.0 points is 1.5 of 30 problems, the AMC23 gain of 9.2 is 3.7 of 40. Evaluation sampling alone gives a standard error of up to 2.4 points for a difference on AIME24 and 2.8 on AMC23 (upper bounds, derived), before any training-seed noise, which on AIME-sized benchmarks is known to move RL results by several points (Hochlehnert et al., 2025).
  • within the noise Many Table 1 margins are about a point or less: Minerva +0.6, +0.4, +1.2; MATH500 +1.1, +0.6; Olympiad +0.8. Table 4's best γ (0.5) beats the main table's γ (0.25) by 0.5 points of average accuracy. With one run each, these do not separate the methods.
  • missing baseline SA-MRPO only changes the relative weight of the easy objective, so the obvious rival is GDPO with a smaller fixed weight on it. GDPO's own paper studied exactly that (length weights 1.0 to 0.25, and a length reward conditioned on correctness), and found that weights must be cut a lot before behaviour changes (GDPO §4.2.1). This paper never runs it. In this page's toy, a fixed weight matched or beat SA-MRPO at the same length reward (the frontier).
  • tuned on test γ = 0.5 is recommended because it scored best on the five test benchmarks (Table 4); no validation split is mentioned. The main 3B two-objective result used γ = 0.25 (Table 4's caption); the other runs' γ is unstated.
  • internal mismatch §5.5 trains the γ runs "for one epoch"; §5.1 trains everything for 3 epochs; Table 4's γ = 0 and 0.25 columns equal Table 1's two-objective columns cell for cell. Figure 1's advantages match γ = 1 without the paper's own Eq. 1 step, and its rewards are fractions of 100 while every reward the experiments use is binary or a pass rate.
  • weak baselines Both methods start from low base scores (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct 50.0% on MATH500 here; 75.5% on MATH in Qwen's report) and, in Table 2, end far below the released R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (MATH500 47.1% and 51.5% against 92.8%). Gains inside a degraded regime may not carry to a well-tuned one. No other multi-reward method (DVAO, GD2PO, SAW, Focal Reward) is compared.
  • no code Nothing is released, so none of it can be rerun; the method is easy to reimplement on top of GDPO's open code.

This page's toy (Train the toy) adds two data points. Where the easy objective is saturated from the start (the binary-budget setting of Tables 1 and 4), SA-MRPO changed nothing measurable: under GDPO the length objective drew under 4% of the advantage at the start and under 0.3% at the end, so there was little to reallocate. Where the easy objective starts unsaturated (the graded setting of Table 2), SA-MRPO did trade length for accuracy as the paper says, but simply lowering the fixed length weight reached a better trade at every matched length reward. A toy of 200 question types cannot test what happens inside a 7B model's shared parameters, so it does not refute the paper; it shows the gain is not automatic and names the experiment that would settle it.

Verdict. Believe the diagnosis, which is useful on its own: per-group standardisation makes a nearly solved objective loud in the few groups where it fails, and fixed weights never adapt. Treat "SA-MRPO beats GDPO" as plausible but unproven: one run per cell, margins near the noise, γ chosen on the test sets, and no comparison against simply lowering the easy objective's weight.

What it takes to use this

Algorithm 1
  • Code: none released. The change is about ten lines in the advantage step of a GDPO implementation: per objective, one batch mean, sk, a weight multiplied by (1 − sk)γ. GDPO itself is open (Apache-2.0) for verl, TRL and NeMo RL and, per its README, built into verl, NeMo RL and Axolotl.
  • Requirements: every objective needs known reward bounds. That is trivial for 0/1 and fraction rewards and ill-defined for unbounded learned reward-model scores, so this is an RLVR technique, not a general RLHF one. Decide how a group with zero spread is handled (the paper does not say; an ε in the denominator gives zero advantage).
  • Budget: no extra compute beyond GDPO. The paper's runs are 3B and 7B models with 8 answers per question, batch 256, answers up to 4,096 tokens, 3 epochs over 40K problems; it reports no GPU count or time.
  • Default: γ = 0.5 per Table 4, with the caveat that it was picked on test benchmarks. Watch the saturated objective: larger γ lets it erode (Table 4's Exceed).
  • Pays off when an auxiliary objective is only partly saturated and genuinely competes with correctness (the graded-length setting). Buys little when the auxiliary objective is satisfied from the start (a length budget the model never approaches), because it then carries almost no share of the update anyway. A fixed smaller weight, or a reward conditioned on correctness as in GDPO, is a simpler alternative worth trying first.

The two failure modes are useful diagnostics even without the method: if a format reward sits at 0.99 while correctness is flat, check what share of the advantage the format term draws; per-group standardisation can make it larger than its mean suggests.

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

  • Multi-reward aggregation is now a design axis of reasoning RL. Once format or length shaping enters, a GRPO loop has several rewards, and 2026 produced a family of ways to combine them: GDPO (2601.05242), DVAO (2605.25604), GD2PO (2606.16771), SAW (2606.07705), Focal Reward (2605.26579) and multi-task GRPO (2602.05547). SA-MRPO's formulation is the simplest of them to state: allocation by remaining attainable reward range rather than by variance or gradient agreement.
  • The silent tax is real, its size is not. The paper's argument that "weighted sum, then standardise" spends update on solved objectives is a sound diagnostic. How large that tax is depends on how often the solved objective still fails within a group, which can be measured in any training log before adopting a fix.
  • Length control is where it bites. Efficient-reasoning work trains long-thinking models to answer shorter with length rewards; GDPO's own experiments show the model "often maximizes the easier objective at the cost of the more challenging one" (GDPO §4.2.1). Any rule that relaxes the length pressure once it is met, whether a decaying weight like this one or a conditioned reward, addresses the same problem.

Connections

  • DeepSeekMath (GRPO): the group-relative advantage and clipped surrogate that SA-MRPO keeps unchanged.
  • DeepSeek-R1: the RLVR regime with rule-based verifiable rewards this method assumes; R1-style recipes with format plus correctness rewards are exactly Figure 1's scenario.
  • DPO: the offline route from preferences to a policy; multi-objective preference methods weight objectives there, this paper does it online inside GRPO.
  • GDPO (arXiv 2601.05242, about 45 min): the reward-decoupled baseline that SA-MRPO generalises (its γ = 0 case).
  • DVAO (arXiv 2605.25604, about 45 min) and GD2PO (arXiv 2606.16771, about 45 min): concurrent multi-reward GRPO variants driven by reward variance and by advantage conflict rather than saturation.

Train the toy: multi-reward RL in a box

The paper's claim is about where the update goes when one objective is nearly solved and another is not. That can be tested exactly on a toy small enough to train in your browser in a second, with every method given the same samples, the same rewards and the same budget, including the comparison the paper never runs: GDPO with a smaller fixed weight on the easy objective.

The toy, in full illustrative

A policy answers 200 question types. For each answer it picks a method (one of 5; one is right for each question; a table of logits per question, the "skill" it must learn) and a reasoning length (one of 10 bins from 150 to 4,100 tokens, from logits shared by all questions plus a term scaled by the question's difficulty, so length is a habit with limited capacity). An answer is correct if the method is right and a coin with probability 1 − e−1.6 L / need comes up, where each question's need is drawn from 150 to 8,000 tokens: longer reasoning helps, and some questions cannot be solved inside the budget. Two rule-based rewards, both bounded in [0, 1]: correctness, and length. Two settings mirror the paper's:

  • Binary budget (Tables 1 and 4): length reward 1 up to 4,000 tokens; the starting habit exceeds it 0.48% of the time, so the length score starts above 99, as in Figure 2.
  • Graded (Table 2): 1 up to 1,024 tokens, linear to 0 at 2,048; the starting habit is verbose (80% of answers over 1,024), like a distilled reasoning model.

Training: 150 steps of 32 questions × 8 answers, Adam at learning rate 0.03, one update per batch (so GRPO's clipped ratio is 1 and its gradient is Â ∇log π), no KL term (the paper omits it too). Advantages exactly as the paper writes them, with an ε of 1e-6 in each standard deviation. Every number is measured exactly over all 200 questions, not sampled. Every toy number in the text comes from toy_sweep.mjs with 8 seeds; the trainer below reruns any of it.

What the toy can and cannot test

  • Can: whether discounting a saturated objective's weight moves the final trade-off between objectives, and whether it does better than a fixed weight at the same trade-off, with every sample, reward and budget shared. Answer here: it moves along the same curve that fixed weights trace, and in the graded setting slightly below it.
  • Can: the paper's qualitative pattern in the graded setting: SA-MRPO raises accuracy and length together as γ grows, and erodes the saturated objective more at large γ (Table 4's Exceed).
  • Cannot: what shared parameters do inside a 7B model, where length, format and correctness share every weight and their gradients can interfere in ways a 200-row table and a 20-number length habit cannot. If SA-MRPO helps in large models, that interference, or the early phase of training, is where it would have to come from; the paper does not measure either.
  • Cannot: evaluation on held-out benchmarks. Accuracy here is on the training questions, measured exactly; the paper's results are on test benchmarks.
  • Design log: a first design (48 question types, 300 steps) saturated every method at 94% accuracy and was changed once before the sweep, so that correctness keeps headroom as in Figure 2; both results are in inputs/toy_design_log.txt. Nothing was tuned after the sweep.

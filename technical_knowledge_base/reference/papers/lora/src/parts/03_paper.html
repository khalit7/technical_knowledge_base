Problem

[[S3|§1, §2, §3, Table 1]]

Full fine-tuning updates every weight, so each downstream task gets a complete copy of the model. For GPT-2 or RoBERTa large that is an inconvenience; for GPT-3, with 175 billion trainable parameters (about 350 GB in FP16), storing, deploying and switching between one copy per task is "prohibitively expensive" ({{Abstract|ax:S1}}, {{§2|ax:S2}}). Fine-tuning is still worth having: on GPT-3 it lifts MNLI-matched from 40.6% few-shot to 89.5%, and RTE from 69.0% to 85.4% ({{Appendix A, Table 8|ax:A1.T8}}).

The parameter-efficient alternatives of 2021 each paid a real cost ({{§3|ax:S3}}):

  • Adapter layers (Houlsby et al. 2019, two per block; Lin et al. 2020, one per block plus a LayerNorm) add depth that must run in sequence. On GPT-2 medium at batch size 1 and 128 tokens, a forward pass takes 19.8 ms without adapters, 23.9 ms with AdapterL (+20.7%) and 25.8 ms with AdapterH (+30.3%); at batch 32 and 512 tokens the overhead shrinks to 2.2% and 3.0% ({{Table 1|ax:S3.T1}}, {{Appendix B|ax:A2}}). It gets worse when the model is sharded, because the extra depth needs more synchronous GPU operations such as AllReduce and Broadcast, unless the adapter weights are stored redundantly many times ({{§3|ax:S3}}).
  • Prefix and prompt tuning (Li & Liang 2021; Lester et al. 2021) are hard to optimise, their quality changes non-monotonically with the number of trainable parameters, and the tokens they reserve come out of the sequence length available for the task ({{§3|ax:S3}}).

The question: can adaptation be parameter-efficient, train stably, match fine-tuning and add exactly zero inference latency?

Idea

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, Eq. 3, Figure 1]]

Aghajanyan et al. (2020) had shown that fine-tuning has a low "intrinsic dimension": optimising only 200 parameters, randomly projected back into the full space, gets RoBERTa to 90% of full fine-tuning's performance on MRPC ({{Aghajanyan et al.|https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13255}}). LoRA's hypothesis is the matrix version of that: the change in a weight matrix during adaptation has a low "intrinsic rank" ({{§1|ax:S1}}, {{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}). So for a pretrained weight W0 ∈ ℝd×k, freeze it and learn the update as a product of two thin matrices:

h = W0x + ΔW x = W0x + (α/r) B A x,   B ∈ ℝd×r, A ∈ ℝr×k, r ≪ min(d, k)
Equation 3 ({{§4.1|ax:S4.E3}}), with the α/r scaling from the same section. Only A and B receive gradients; both paths see the same input and their outputs are summed ({{Figure 1|ax:S1.F1}}).
  • Initialisation. A is random Gaussian and B is zero, so ΔW = BA = 0 at the start: training begins exactly at the pretrained model ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}). The released code differs in a detail: its linear layers draw A from a Kaiming-uniform distribution, and its embedding layers reverse the roles (A zero, B normal) ({{loralib/layers.py|https://github.com/microsoft/LoRA/blob/main/loralib/layers.py}}).
  • Scaling. ΔW x is multiplied by α/r, with α a constant. With Adam, tuning α is roughly the same as tuning the learning rate if the initialisation is scaled to match, so the authors set α to the first r they try and never tune it; the point is not to retune hyperparameters when r changes ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}).
  • How small. On GPT-3, where d is 12,288, r = 1 or 2 already suffices ({{§1|ax:S1}}).

Method: where LoRA goes, and what it saves

[[S4.SS2|§4.2]]

Any dense layer can take a LoRA update. In a Transformer there are four attention matrices (Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, each treated as one dmodel × dmodel matrix even though its output is sliced into heads) and two in the MLP. The paper adapts only attention weights and freezes the MLPs, "both for simplicity and parameter-efficiency", leaving the MLP, LayerNorm and biases to future work; most experiments adapt just Wq and Wv ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}, {{§5.1|ax:S5.SS1}}). The paper uses dffn = 4 × dmodel and trains with Adam throughout ({{§1, conventions|ax:S1}}).

A generalisation of full fine-tuning. With LoRA on every weight matrix and all biases trained, setting r to the rank of the pretrained matrices roughly recovers full fine-tuning; as trainable parameters grow, LoRA converges to training the original model, while adapters converge to an MLP and prefix methods to a model that cannot take long inputs ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}).

Practical benefits on GPT-3 175B ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}):

  • Training memory falls from 1.2 TB to 350 GB, because frozen weights need no gradients or optimiser state; for a large Transformer trained with Adam the saving is "up to 2/3" when r ≪ dmodel (the abstract says 3 times).
  • With r = 4 on Wq and Wv, a checkpoint shrinks roughly 10,000× (350 GB to 35 MB). The 350 GB base is still needed to serve, but 100 adapted models cost about 350 GB + 100 × 35 MB ≈ 354 GB instead of 100 × 350 GB ≈ 35 TB.
  • Fewer GPUs are needed to train and I/O bottlenecks disappear; tasks can be swapped while deployed by swapping only the LoRA weights, so many customised models can be served from one copy of the base in VRAM.
  • Training is 25% faster than full fine-tuning: 32.5 tokens per second per V100 for full fine-tuning against 43.1 for LoRA with the same number of model-parallel shards. That is 24.6% less time per token, or 32.6% more tokens per second ({{footnote 5|ax:S4.SS2}}; {{checked in the tables tab|tab:t-tables:tbC}}).

The stated limitation. If you merge A and B into W to remove latency, one forward pass cannot easily serve a batch whose inputs belong to different tasks. Without merging, the LoRA modules can be chosen per sample, where latency is not critical ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}). LoRA is also orthogonal to other methods: Appendix E combines it with prefix tuning, and LoRA plus prefix-embedding reaches 75.0 to 76.2 on WikiSQL against 73.8 for full fine-tuning, while LoRA plus prefix-layer does slightly worse than LoRA alone ({{Appendix E|ax:A5}}, {{Table 15|ax:A6.T15}}).

Merging: zero latency, by construction

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, "No Additional Inference Latency"]]

Both W0 and BA are d × k, so for deployment compute W = W0 + BA once and run inference exactly as a fine-tuned model would. To switch tasks, subtract BA and add another B′A′, a quick operation with very little memory overhead. This guarantees no added latency "by construction" ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}). Inside the update: which matrices, what rank, what ΔW does

[[S7|§7, Tables 5 to 7, Figures 3 and 4]]

The low-rank structure also makes the update readable. Section 7 asks three questions on GPT-3 ({{§7|ax:S7}}).

Which matrices, for a fixed budget? With 18M trainable parameters (about 35 MB in FP16), which is r = 8 on one matrix type, r = 4 on two, or r = 2 on all four, in all 96 layers: Wq alone gets 70.4% on WikiSQL and Wk alone 70.0%, clearly worse, while Wq, Wv at r = 4 gets 73.7% and all four at r = 2 gets 73.7% (MultiNLI 91.3% and 91.7%). The paper concludes that even r = 4 captures enough of ΔW that adapting more matrices beats a higher rank on one ({{§7.1, Table 5|ax:S7.T5}}). Wo alone (73.2%, 91.3%) and Wv alone (73.0%, 91.0%) are close to the best; see {{How much to believe|tab:t-read:s-trust}}.

What rank? On WikiSQL and MultiNLI, r = 1 already does as well as r = 64 when adapting Wq and Wv (73.4% against 73.5%; 91.3% against 91.4%), while Wq alone needs a larger r (68.8% at r = 1, 70.5% at r = 4) ({{§7.2, Table 6|ax:S7.T6}}). The authors do not expect small r to work everywhere: if the downstream task were in a different language from pretraining, retraining the whole model (LoRA with r = dmodel) could certainly do better (footnote 6). On GPT-2 medium the optimum is between 4 and 16: BLEU peaks at r = 4 (70.38) and validation loss stops improving at 16 ({{Appendix H.2, Table 18|ax:A8.T18}}).

Do different ranks and seeds find the same directions? Take the learned A at r = 8 and at r = 64, compute their right-singular vectors U, and measure how much of the span of the top i of one lies in the span of the top j of the other:

φ(Ar=8, Ar=64, i, j) = ‖Ui⊤Ar=8 UjAr=64‖F2 / min(i, j) ∈ [0, 1]
Equation 4 ({{§7.2|ax:S7.E4}}): 1 means one subspace contains the other, 0 that they are orthogonal; a rescaled Projection Metric ({{Appendix G|ax:A7}}).

In layer 48 of 96, the top direction of r = 8 and r = 64 overlap with φ > 0.5 for both ΔWq and ΔWv, and the rest look like noise accumulated in training: that is why r = 1 works ({{Figure 3|ax:S7.F3}}). Two seeds at r = 64 share their top directions too, while two random Gaussian matrices share none; ΔWq shares more directions across seeds than ΔWv, so it seems to have a higher intrinsic rank, in line with Table 6 ({{Figure 4|ax:S7.F4}}). The 1st, 32nd, 64th and 96th layers look the same ({{Appendix H.1|ax:A8.SS1}}). The {{Train a LoRA|tab:t-run:runInside}} tab draws the same heatmaps from a toy model, where the true update is known.

does ΔW point along the strongest directions of the pretrained W?

Project Wq onto the r-dimensional subspace of ΔWq, ‖U⊤WqV⊤‖F with U, V the singular vectors of ΔWq, and compare with Wq's own top directions and with random ones ({{§7.3, Table 7|ax:S7.T7}}). At r = 4 in layer 48: 0.32 along ΔW's directions, 21.67 along W's own, 0.02 along random ones (‖Wq‖ = 61.95). So ΔW correlates with W more than chance, but not with its top directions: it amplifies features that are present and not emphasised, and by a lot, ‖ΔWq‖ / 0.32 = 6.91 / 0.32 ≈ 21.5. At r = 64 the factor is only about 2 (3.57 / 1.90), because most of the 64 directions are not amplified, more evidence that the needed rank is low ({{Appendix H.4|ax:A8.SS4}}). The top 4 directions of ΔW barely reach 0.2 similarity with the top 10% of W's ({{Appendix H.3|ax:A8.SS3}}).

Bars on a log scale, printed values from Table 7. The toy model's own Table 7, computed live from its trained adapters, is in the {{Train a LoRA|tab:t-run:runInside}} tab.

The paper's reading: low-rank adaptation amplifies task-specific features that pretraining learned but did not emphasise, a different set of directions for each task ({{§7.3|ax:S7.SS3}}, {{H.4|ax:A8.SS4}}). Open questions it lists: combining LoRA with other methods, how pretrained features are transformed, a principled choice of matrices, and whether W itself is rank-deficient ({{§8|ax:S8}}).

How much of this to believe

[[S5|§5, Tables 2 to 6, 15, Appendix D]]

What holds up. The mechanism's central claims are arithmetic, not measurement. Merging is exact (this page checks it live), so zero added latency is true by construction. The parameter counts reproduce from the formula for every GPT-3 configuration in Table 15, and the storage and multi-task figures follow from them ({{every number checked|tab:t-tables:tbC}}). Adapter latency (Table 1) is measured over 100 trials with error bars. And the method has since been replicated at every scale by everyone who fine-tunes models: it is the default in Hugging Face PEFT, and QLoRA (2023) and LoRA Without Regret (2025) reproduce "LoRA matches full fine-tuning" under stated conditions (beyond the paper; see {{Why it matters|tab:t-read:s-why}}).

What is weaker, concretely:

  • "Better than fine-tuning" sits inside the noise. On GPT-3, LoRA's 73.4 against 73.8 on WikiSQL is within the stated ±0.5 (a tie, not a win). The MNLI gain (91.7 against 89.5) and SAMSum's 1.4 to 1.8 points are outside the stated noise, the paper's strongest results, though every method got one tuned learning rate shared across the three datasets and the same 2 epochs, one run per entry ({{Table 4|ax:S5.T4}}, {{Table 12|ax:A4.T12}}, {{D.4|ax:A4.SS4}}). On RoBERTa base the +0.8 average comes entirely from RTE: LoRA is 7.9 points ahead there and, over the other seven tasks, 0.1 points behind on average. RTE's dev set has 277 examples (a binomial standard error of about 2 points), and LoRA's RTE run started from its own MNLI-adapted checkpoint ({{Appendix D.1|ax:A4.SS1}}), while the fine-tuning numbers are reused from the fairseq release's single-task runs ({{fairseq README|https://github.com/facebookresearch/fairseq/blob/main/examples/roberta/README.md}}). The fair † setup narrows LoRA's lead over adapters to 0.2 points.
  • Baselines not run like for like. Asterisked rows are copied from other papers with their own tuning; LoRA rows are the median of 5 seeds (GLUE) or mean of 3 (GPT-2), each "taken from the best epoch" on the dev set that is reported ({{Appendix D|ax:A4}}); GPT-3 reports "the best validation performance from each run" ({{D.4|ax:A4.SS4}}).
  • Mixed configurations in one row. Table 4's 4.7M LoRA row takes MNLI 91.7 from the rv = 2 run, while rq = rv = 1 gives 91.3; its 37.7M row takes WikiSQL from a four-matrix run and MNLI from a two-matrix run ({{Table 15|ax:A6.T15}}). A few printed values disagree between tables, and Appendix D.4 gives a wrong configuration for 37.7M ({{listed in the tables tab|tab:t-tables:tbI}}). None changes a conclusion, but the headline row is a best-of-two.
  • Parameter counts that do not recount. GPT-2 medium and large at the stated r = 4 give 0.39M and 0.74M, not the printed 0.35M and 0.77M (which equal the prefix-layer baseline's); DeBERTa XXL at r = 8 gives 2.36M, not 4.7M, unless the 1,536 × 1,536 pooler and classifier are counted, which the paper does not say ({{recount|tab:t-tables:tbP}}). The 1.2 TB training-memory figure matches no standard Adam accounting, and the paper gives none.
  • The analysis is one model, one layer, one or two seeds. Figures 3, 4 and Table 7 are GPT-3's layer 48, tasks unnamed, no error bars; in Table 5, Wo alone is within the stated noise of the "best" Wq, Wv.
  • GPT-3 cannot be rerun by anyone outside. The repository releases RoBERTa, DeBERTa and GPT-2 adapters and code, but nothing for GPT-3 ({{repository|https://github.com/microsoft/LoRA}}).

In the authors' favour: they state the low-rank limit themselves (footnote 6), give per-task noise for GPT-3 instead of hiding it, rerun adapter baselines in a deliberately restricted fair setup, and flag that some GPT-2 hyperparameters were tuned at r = 4 and may not suit other ranks ({{Table 18 caption|ax:A8.T18}}).

Verdict: believe the mechanism and the engineering; they are exact, and five years of use confirm them. Read "on-par or better than fine-tuning" as "on par": the margins are inside the noise or produced by setup differences. The toy on this page agrees on what LoRA needs, enough rank on the right matrices, and shows where it falls short without them: (loading) ({{Train a LoRA|tab:t-run:runSweep}}).

What it takes to use this

  • Code and licence. {{microsoft/LoRA|https://github.com/microsoft/LoRA}} (loralib, MIT licence) with RoBERTa, DeBERTa and GPT-2 checkpoints; in practice everyone uses {{Hugging Face PEFT|https://huggingface.co/docs/peft}}, which the repository itself points to since February 2023.
  • Hardware. LoRA removes optimiser state and gradients for frozen weights, not the weights or activations: the base must still fit (350 GB for GPT-3 in FP16). QLoRA's 4-bit base is what brought a 65B model to one 48 GB GPU ({{QLoRA|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314}}).
  • Configuration. The paper's defaults: Wq and Wv, r = 4 to 8, α fixed, learning rates 2E-04 to 5E-04 (Tables 9 to 12). Since then: all linear layers, MLP above all, and about 10× the full fine-tuning learning rate (QLoRA, LoRA Without Regret; {{Then and now|tab:t-then}}).
  • When it pays off. Many tasks or tenants on one base; small data; RL post-training (very low ranks suffice). When it buys little: a task far from pretraining (the paper's own footnote 6), datasets whose information exceeds the adapter's capacity, or very large batches, where LoRA pays a larger loss penalty than full fine-tuning (LoRA Without Regret).

Why it matters

beyond the paper

LoRA became the default way to fine-tune large models. Hugging Face PEFT's LoraConfig is the standard interface ({{PEFT LoRA guide|https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/main/en/developer_guides/lora}}). QLoRA (May 2023) backpropagates through a frozen 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) base into LoRA adapters, with double quantisation and paged optimisers, and fine-tunes a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU, which put LLM fine-tuning within hobbyist reach and fed the open-model ecosystem ({{QLoRA|https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314}}).

The zero-latency merge and the tiny adapters also created multi-tenant serving. S-LoRA serves thousands of concurrent adapters over one base, with up to 4× the throughput of PEFT and naive vLLM ({{S-LoRA|https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03285}}); vLLM and SGLang now batch many adapters over one base, SGLang "incorporating techniques from S-LoRA and Punica" ({{vLLM|https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/lora.html}}, {{SGLang|https://docs.sglang.ai/advanced_features/lora.html}}). Apple ships per-feature adapters over one on-device model of about 3 billion parameters; a rank-16 adapter takes tens of megabytes and is loaded and swapped on the fly ({{Apple, June 2024|https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/introducing-apple-foundation-models}}). Fine-tuning services, including RL fine-tuning such as Thinking Machines' Tinker, are LoRA underneath ({{Tinker|https://thinkingmachines.ai/tinker/}}).

The refinements an engineer should know:

  • rsLoRA (November 2023): dividing by r slows learning and stunts higher ranks; dividing by √r is rank-stabilised and lets larger r trade compute for quality ({{rsLoRA|https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03732}}); use_rslora in PEFT sets the scale to lora_alpha/√r ({{PEFT|https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/main/en/developer_guides/lora}}). The paper's own Table 7 shows the effect: ‖ΔWq‖ is 6.91 at r = 4 and 3.57 at r = 64.
  • DoRA (NVIDIA, code at {{NVlabs/DoRA|https://github.com/NVlabs/DoRA}}; ICML 2024): splits each weight into magnitude and direction and applies LoRA to the direction only, to resemble full fine-tuning's learning; it consistently beats LoRA on LLaMA, LLaVA and VL-BART with no inference overhead ({{DoRA|https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09353}}); use_dora in PEFT.
  • LoRA Without Regret (Thinking Machines, 29 September 2025): LoRA matches full fine-tuning when applied to all layers, especially the MLP and MoE layers (attention-only underperforms even at matched parameter count), and when the dataset does not exceed the adapter's capacity; for policy-gradient RL even rank 1 matches, because RL absorbs about 1,000 times less information per token than supervised learning; the optimal LoRA learning rate is consistently about 10× full fine-tuning's; and LoRA tolerates large batches less well, a penalty that more rank does not fix ({{LoRA Without Regret|https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/}}). It also explains why the paper's 1/r factor makes the optimal learning rate nearly independent of rank early in training.

The scientific contribution has aged as well as the engineering one: the intrinsic-rank analysis (adaptation amplifies existing but unemphasised features in a low-dimensional subspace) remains a standard lens on what fine-tuning does.

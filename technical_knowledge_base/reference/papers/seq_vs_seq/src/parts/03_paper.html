Problem

[[S1|§1 Introduction, §2 Related work]]

The field moved from encoder-only models such as ELMo and {{BERT|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e5ad9bd09cbf18ad7c}} to decoder-only, GPT-style models because decoders generate text so well ({{§1|ax:S1}}). With little new encoder development, the part of the community that does classification, retrieval or fast on-device inference still runs models from 2019: in March 2025 alone, BERT-base had 90 million downloads on Hugging Face ({{§2|ax:S2}}). Encoder development mostly stalled; ModernBERT, the strongest recent attempt to revive it, did not release its training data ({{§2|ax:S2}}).

Meanwhile a belief spread that decoders can simply be adapted to encoder jobs (classification, embeddings), often zero-shot. Decoders are more studied, more over-trained ({{Chinchilla|n:3c65c17b0d0d8116b7ddffba5c599f3f}}) and larger, and adapted decoders now top leaderboards such as MTEB that used to belong to encoders ({{§1|ax:S1}}). Whether that is the right call had never been cleanly testable: every earlier comparison of the two kinds (DeBERTa against GPT-2 in similar sizes, for instance) used models with different parameter counts, architectures, training recipes, pretraining data and tokenizers, and the attempts to control for this used very little pretraining data ({{§2|ax:S2}}).

The gap: a suite of encoders and decoders that share everything except the thing being compared, trained to state-of-the-art quality so the comparison means something, with all the data open.

Idea: train every model twice, changing two things

[[S3.SS3|§3.3, Table 1 caption]]

Ettin (named for a two-headed giant of Norse myth: two language-model heads) trains each size twice from scratch, with the same architecture, the same data, in the same order, with the same hyperparameters. The only differences between the two members of a pair are ({{§3.3|ax:S3.SS3}}, {{Table 1|ax:S2.T1}}):

  • the attention pattern: bidirectional for the encoder, causal (each token sees only its left) for the decoder;
  • the objective: masked language modelling (MLM) for the encoder, masking 30% of tokens and 15% in the final decay phase (footnote 6); causal language modelling (CLM, next-token prediction) for the decoder.

Then each finished model is trained further on the other side's objective ({{§3.5|ax:S3.SS5}}): a decoder continued with MNTP (masked next-token prediction, below) becomes an encoder-from-decoder; an encoder continued with CLM becomes a decoder-from-encoder. Four kinds of model per size. The animation runs one toy sentence through all four objectives; the first two are the pair, the last two the conversions.

One toy document; the probabilities in the last step come from this page's trained toy models.

The grid is the attention mask: a filled cell means the token in that row may read the token in that column. Arrows run from the position whose output is scored to the token it must predict. Masked positions are drawn from the paper's rates (30% MLM, 15% MNTP) on this one sentence, so the counts move by a token or two from the rate. The animation stops when it is off screen, and starts paused, one step at a time, if your system asks for reduced motion.

The counter that matters most is the share of tokens that produce a loss. A decoder learns from every token; an encoder only from the masked ones. That is the paper's own explanation (in {{§6|ax:S6}}) for why a concurrent study at a small budget reached the opposite verdict: CLM is more data-efficient per token, an advantage that fades with enough data.

The suite: six sizes, two of everything

[[S3.SS2|§3.2, Table 1, Appendices C and D]]

ModernBERT has two sizes, so Ettin designs new shapes for the others, roughly doubling at each step and matching common encoder sizes ({{§3.2|ax:S3.SS2}}). The small models follow MobileLLM's advice to go deep and thin; the 1B keeps the 400M's 28 layers and widens instead. The 150M and 400M shapes are ModernBERT-base and -large exactly (both 149.0M and 394.8M in {{Table 11|ax:A7.T11}}).

Recounted here from each model's released configuration on Hugging Face (jhu-clsp/ettin-encoder-* and ettin-decoder-*; vocabulary 50,368, tied embeddings, no biases, a GLU feed-forward whose input projection is 2 × the intermediate size, LayerNorms without bias, the first layer's attention norm skipped): every total, embedding and non-embedding count reproduces Table 11 to its printed 0.1M, independently, once the prediction head (dense layer, norm and output bias, 0.1M to 3.3M) is left out, as Table 11 does. The encoder and decoder configurations of each size differ only in the causal flags. Learning rate, weight decay and warmups are Table 1's.

The rest is ModernBERT's block ({{Table 12|ax:A7.T12}}, {{Appendix C|ax:A3}}): rotary positions (no position embeddings) with the base raised to 160,000, pre-norm LayerNorm, a GELU GLU feed-forward, no biases, an embedding norm, sliding-window attention of 128 tokens in two layers of every three, the ModernBERT tokenizer, and a context of 1,024 tokens growing to 7,999. The only change from ModernBERT is that local and global layers use the same rotary base.

The recipe: ModernBERT's, with open data

[[S3.SS3|§3.1, §3.3, §3.4, Table 2]]

One recipe for both sides, the three-phase schedule open-data decoders use and ModernBERT followed, with a trapezoidal learning rate ({{§3.3|ax:S3.SS3}}). Data comes from the sources open decoders train on, chiefly OLMo's: DCLM plus curated Dolma v1.7 sources at first, then the filtered DCLM and other higher-quality Dolmino sources that the {{OLMo 2|n:3c65c17b0d0d81fb9857fb956165ae1c}} paper introduced for its decay phase ({{§3.1|ax:S3.SS1}}; footnote 5: the unfiltered data ablated worse).

  1. Base pretraining, 1.7T tokens. Warmup of both learning rate and batch size, then the stable phase, on a broad mix: DCLM (49.1%), Common Crawl head, StarCoder code, Reddit, peS2o papers, arXiv, math, StackExchange, a little Wikipedia, books, news and Tulu FLAN.
  2. Context extension (mid-training), 250B tokens. Sequences up to 8,000 tokens; RoPE base to 160,000 for global and local layers ({{RoFormer|n:3c65c17b0d0d81cfa5e9f54459720098}}); the noisiest sources dropped (older Dolma Common Crawl, CC News, general StackExchange) and filtered DCLM, math and StackExchange added; an inverse-square-root schedule from the peak learning rate to half of it.
  3. Decay, 50B tokens. One more inverse-square-root decay, to 0.02 of the peak, following the ProLong recipe: more long-context data such as Dolma books, Wikipedia and open textbooks.

Five deliberate differences from ModernBERT ({{§3.4|ax:S3.SS4}}): open data; decay during context extension; no model merging (for a clean comparison; footnote 7 guesses a merge would add "another point or two"); 15% masking in the decay phase instead of 30%; the same rotary base for local and global layers.

Open everything. Checkpoints every 8.5B tokens, 236 per model (236 × 8.5B = 2,006B, the whole 2T run), plus the training data and the exact batch order, so anyone can pinpoint what a model learned between two checkpoints ({{§3.3|ax:S3.SS3}}), as {{Pythia|https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01373}} did for decoders.

Not quite 2T for every size
Only the appendix says it: there was not enough compute to train the 1B models to the full 2T tokens, so both 1B models (and their cross-objective runs) got a third of the data, 667B tokens instead of 2T ({{Appendix E|ax:A5}}). The paper notes 667B is still past Chinchilla-optimal and still beats 1B models trained longer. Each model trained on one node of 4 H100s: about 6 days of pretraining for the 17M model, about 40 days for the 1B.

Cross-objective training: can you convert one into the other?

[[S3.SS5|§3.5]]

Because large decoders are trained much longer than encoders ever were, it has become common to adapt them to encoder tasks ({{§3.5|ax:S3.SS5}}). The paired models make that testable: take the final model of each kind and continue pretraining it on the reverse objective.

  • Encoder-from-decoder: bidirectional attention and MNTP, following {{LLM2Vec|https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05961}}: a token is masked, but its prediction is read from the hidden state of the previous position, so the decoder's habit of predicting the next token from the current position is kept. 15% masking, "a middle ground" (footnote 10).
  • Decoder-from-encoder: causal attention and CLM.
  • Budget: 50B tokens each way, on the decay phase's high-quality data (seen a second time, which earlier work found harmless for two repetitions; footnote 8), with a new trapezoidal schedule: 3B tokens of warmup, 10B of decay. For the 1B models this is scaled by a third again (footnote 9), about 16.7B tokens. Either way it is 50B / 2T = 2.5% of pretraining, which the authors call the realistic setting: adaptation always uses little data compared with pretraining.
"Far more than previous work", but not 5 times
The paper says LLM2Vec trained "around 10B tokens" ({{§3.5|ax:S3.SS5}}), and the old version of this page turned that into "about 5x the token budget LLM2Vec used". LLM2Vec's own paper and configuration say otherwise: its MNTP step is 1,000 steps of 32 sequences of at most 512 tokens on Wikitext-103 ({{LLM2Vec §2 and D.1.1|https://arxiv.org/html/2404.05961v2}}; {{MNTP config|https://github.com/McGill-NLP/llm2vec/blob/main/train_configs/mntp/MetaLlama3.json}}), at most 1,000 × 32 × 512 = 16.4M tokens, and even its supervised contrastive stage is 1,000 steps of 512 × 512. Ettin's 50B is roughly 3,000 times LLM2Vec's MNTP budget, which makes the negative result stronger, not weaker.
a decoder is given 50B tokens of bidirectional MNTP training, 2.5% of its pretraining. On MNLI, how much of its gap to the paired encoder does that close?

Evaluation

[[S4|§4, §4.2, Appendices B and F]]
  • Encoders: GLUE (fine-tuned), MTEB v2 English, MLDR for long-context retrieval and CodeSearchNet for code retrieval, with ModernBERT's exact evaluation setup ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}). GLUE sweeps 5 learning rates, 4 weight decays, 2 batch sizes and 4 epoch settings, 160 runs per model, and reports the best score per task ({{Appendix F|ax:A6}}). Retrieval is fine-tuned on MS MARCO with a new script (ModernBERT's precise scores could not be reproduced), with more negatives, which raised every model's scores.
  • Decoders: zero-shot, closed-book tasks of the EleutherAI harness from the Pythia and SmolLM papers: ARC (the average of Easy and Challenge), HellaSwag, LAMBADA, OpenBookQA, PIQA, SciQ, Social IQA, TriviaQA, Winogrande and WSC ({{§4.1|ax:S4.SS1}}, {{Appendix B|ax:A2}}).
  • Across the divide ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}): decoders are fine-tuned on MNLI and MS MARCO like encoders; encoders run the generative tasks with the method of {{Samuel (2024)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.04823}}: append three [MASK] tokens and an end token, predict the first mask, append it, repeat. Two tasks needed adjustments because encoders are not calibrated on where the end token goes (TriviaQA stops on a newline instead; LAMBADA does not score the end token). The {{Train the pairs|tab:t-run}} tab animates this method on the toy models.

Results 1: one recipe, state of the art on both sides

[[S4.SS1|§4.1, Tables 3 and 4]]

Before comparing the two kinds, the paper shows its recipe favours neither, by beating the best open models of each kind at each size ({{§4|ax:S4}}).

  • Encoders ({{Table 3|ax:S4.T3}}): Ettin-Enc-150m against ModernBERT-base: 88.9 against 88.4 GLUE average, 54.0 MTEB v2 (a tie); Ettin-Enc-400m against ModernBERT-large: 90.8 against 90.4. The small ones beat distilled baselines without distillation: the 68M scores 87.2 GLUE against DistilRoBERTa's 83.8. The gains are larger at bigger sizes, which the paper puts down to the small baselines being heavily optimised with distillation; MiniLM L12 also has nearly twice the 32M's non-embedding parameters (21M against 12M; footnote 12).
  • Decoders ({{Table 4|ax:S4.T4}}): the 150M averages 46.2 against SmolLM2-135M's 45.2; the 1B 59.0 against Llama 3.2 1B's 56.6 and OLMo 1B's 55.1. The 400M ties SmolLM2-360M at 53.1. Few small decoders exist: the 17M's only peer is Pythia-14M, likely a debug run (footnote 13), and there is none at 32M.

So "matches or beats" is fair on both sides at every size where a peer exists, from a single recipe. It is also the first public, open-data replication of ModernBERT. The tables are sortable in {{The paper's tables, rebuilt|tab:t-tables}}.

Results 2: encoders against decoders

[[S4.SS2|§4.2, Figure 1, Tables 5, 8 and 9]]

Three representative scores: MNLI accuracy (classification), MS MARCO dev nDCG@10 (retrieval) and the average of the ten generative tasks ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}). Figure 1 is rebuilt below from {{Table 9|ax:A7.T9}}, its table version; the figure's own vector coordinates, decoded from the arXiv HTML, agree with Table 9 to the last printed digit.

  • Classification: encoders win at every size, and conversion does not help. The 150M encoder scores 89.2 on MNLI against the 400M decoder's 88.2; the 400M encoder 91.3 against the 1B decoder's 89.9 (the abstract's example). The encoder-from-decoder stays within a point of the decoder it started from and is below it at 400M (87.6 against 88.2) and 1B (89.0 against 89.9).
  • Retrieval: the same order, but conversion helps. MNTP lifts the decoder by 1.5 to 1.9 nDCG@10 at every size, yet the 400M encoder still leads its encoder-from-decoder, 42.2 against 41.4. The paper calls the difference "not as pronounced as in classification"; it is 0.1 at 1B and reversed at 17M (31.01 against 30.93).
  • Generation: decoders win, and converting an encoder scales badly. The decoder-from-encoder is level with the decoder up to 68M (36.7 against 36.4 at 17M, 42.1 against 41.8 at 68M) and 6.8 points behind at 1B (52.2 against 59.0).

The nuance inside the generative average ({{§4.2|ax:S4.SS2}}, {{Table 8|ax:A7.T8}}). The paper says encoders used generatively beat decoders on the more classification-like tasks, "such as ARC and SciQ" (400M: ARC 35.6 against 33.6), while decoders gain hugely on HellaSwag, TriviaQA and Social IQA. ARC holds. The SciQ and Social IQA part does not survive a check of Table 8 against Table 4:

Case study: gender bias with identical data

[[S5|§5, Figures 2 and 3, Table 10]]

Because both kinds saw the same data, differences in learned bias come from the objective ({{§5|ax:S5}}). The test is the "Gotcha" split of WinoGender: 240 sentences, half with a female and half with a male stereotypical pronoun (nurse: female). Most of the small models are at chance on the real coreference task ({{Table 10|ax:A7.T10}}), so the case study uses an easier one: predict the pronoun itself, with a [MASK] for encoders and the lower-perplexity sentence for decoders, and count male, female and neutral answers.

{{Figure 2|ax:S5.F2}} rebuilt from its vector coordinates in the arXiv HTML; every share is a whole number of the 240 sentences (shown on hover). The labels of the figure's own size axis read 66M for the 68M models.

The paper's reading: encoders are "much more likely to use a gender neutral pronoun overall"; both lean male, "if slightly higher for decoders"; female pronouns become more common with size, steadily for decoders and "more stochastic" for encoders. The decoded figure supports this on average (neutral 37.0% of answers for encoders against 21.5% for decoders; male 55.9% against 67.7%) and up to 150M, but at 400M and 1B it reverses: the decoders choose neutral pronouns more often (30.8% and 30.0% against 22.9% and 24.6%) and the encoders choose male more often (69.2% and 59.6% against 53.7% at both sizes). Each bar is one model on 240 sentences, so a share has a standard error of about 3 points.

How much of this to believe

[[A7.T9|§4.2, §5, Tables 5, 8, 9; Appendix E]]

What holds up, and why. The design is the strongest part: the two members of a pair have identical released configurations apart from the causal flags (checked above), the same data in the same order and the same hyperparameters, and both sides are competitive with the best open models of their kind, so neither looks starved. The direction of the main result is the same at all six sizes: encoders ahead of their paired decoders on MNLI by 1.9 to 3.6 points ({{Table 9|ax:A7.T9}}); decoders ahead on the generative average by 1.3 to 8.3 over the encoder. At about 9,800 MNLI dev examples a single accuracy has a binomial standard error of about 0.32 points, and a difference between two models about 0.45 (derived; the paper gives no error bars), so the MNLI gaps are 4 to 8 standard errors wide; a ten-task generative average has a standard error of about 0.42 (from the standard split sizes of the ten tasks), about 0.6 for a difference, so the 6.8-point generation gap at 1B is about 11 of them.

Where the evidence is weaker.

  • Single runs, best-of-sweep scores. No table reports seeds or spreads. GLUE scores are the best of 160 fine-tuning runs per model, picked on the reported dev sets ({{Appendix F|ax:A6}}); retrieval picks the best of four learning rates on MS MARCO dev, the set Figure 1 reports. This is the same for every model, so it inflates all of them, but it also hides how much a single comparison could move.
  • The retrieval claim sits inside the noise at the ends. The encoder's lead over the encoder-from-decoder is 0.80 nDCG@10 at 400M, 0.11 at 1B and minus 0.08 at 17M. With 6,980 MS MARCO dev queries and per-query nDCG@10 between 0 and 1, the standard error of a difference is at most about 0.85 points. "Not enough to match" is supported only as "not clearly ahead".
  • The 1B generation gap is measured on less data. The 1B models pretrained on a third of the tokens and their conversion got a third of the 50B ({{Appendix E|ax:A5}}, footnote 9). "Decoders-from-encoders scale poorly" rests mostly on the 1B point (gaps of 2.6 at 150M, 4.0 at 400M, 6.8 at 1B), which differs from the others in budget as well as size.
  • Generation by mask filling is a workaround. Encoders run generative tasks through the three-mask method with task-specific changes for TriviaQA and LAMBADA ({{Appendix B|ax:A2}}); part of the generative gap may be the method rather than the model. The authors say the setup "still could be improved".
  • The table mix-up and the size claim. The SciQ and Social IQA nuance comes from Table 8's swapped columns (above). Figure 1's caption reverses its own example ("a 400M decoder outperforming the 1B encoder"; the text and abstract have it the right way round), and its "order of magnitude" is at most 2.7x in the data. The introduction says "10 models (5 pairs)"; Table 1 and the release have 6 pairs.
  • The gender result changes sign with size (above), from one model per size on 240 sentences.
  • Small inconsistencies in the recipe's numbers. Table 2's decay mixture sums to 76.3B tokens against the 50B in the text, and the repository's README lists the decay data as 100B; the table samples and repeats sources to hit the stated counts, so the 50B is probably what was trained on. Table 5's "MMLU CS" is never defined.

In the authors' favour: they release every model, checkpoint, the data and the batch order, so each of these points can be checked; they state that the 1B run was cut to a third, that the setup for generative encoders is imperfect, that most models are too small for MMLU or GSM8k, and that a concurrent study disagrees; and they run 300+ GPU hours on DeBERTa-v2-XXL before excluding it for size rather than leave out an unflattering baseline silently.

Verdict
Believe the direction: with data, order and recipe held fixed, encoders win classification and retrieval and decoders win generation, at every size from 17M to 1B, and 2.5% of pretraining spent on the reverse objective does not turn one into the other. Treat the size of the gaps as provisional (single runs, no error bars), the retrieval part as within noise at 1B and 17M, the "scales poorly" part as confounded by the 1B models' smaller budget, the SciQ and Social IQA nuance as a table mix-up, and the gender result as a pattern that reverses at the two largest sizes.

What it takes to use this

[[A5|Appendix E, repository README]]
  • Code and licence: the {{repository|https://github.com/JHU-CLSP/ettin-encoder-vs-decoder}} is MIT-licensed; training uses the authors' {{bert24 fork|https://github.com/orionw/bert24}} of the ModernBERT code, and encoder generative evaluation uses their {{lm-evaluation-harness fork|https://github.com/orionw/lm-evaluation-harness}}. The GLUE evaluation guide's quick-start example still reads "TODO" in the repository (fetched 3 October 2026).
  • Models: 24 on {{Hugging Face (jhu-clsp)|https://huggingface.co/jhu-clsp}}: six encoders, six decoders and twelve cross-objective models (named ettin-enc-from-dec-* and ettin-dec-from-enc-* on the Hub; the README's longer names do not resolve), plus raw checkpoints in jhu-clsp/ettin-checkpoints and training-stage tags (step*, ext*, decay*) on each model.
  • Software: encoders load as ModernBERT (transformers 4.48 or later); decoders need the ModernBERT-decoder class, in transformers 4.54 or later according to the README. Encoders' classification head pools by mean; the retrieval script pools decoders by the last token.
  • Hardware to reproduce: one node of 4 H100s per model; about 6 days of pretraining for the 17M and about 40 days for the 1B ({{Appendix E|ax:A5}}). Derived, not stated: 6 × 16.8M × 1.7T ≈ 1.7 × 1020 FLOPs in 6 days is about 8% of the four GPUs' dense BF16 peak (989 TFLOP/s each), and the 1B's 6 × 1.03B × 567B ≈ 3.5 × 1021 in 40 days about 26%; small models use the hardware poorly.
  • When it pays off: for classification, retrieval or embeddings at 1B or below, start from the encoder of the size you can serve; on this paper's numbers a decoder 2.7 times its size still trails it on MNLI, and MNTP conversion buys 1.5 to 1.9 nDCG@10 on retrieval but nothing on classification. For generation, use the decoder. The 150M and 400M encoders are drop-in, open-data equivalents of ModernBERT-base and -large with the same shapes. The cross-objective models are for research; the README says they "generally underperform native models".

Why it matters (beyond the paper)

This is the cleanest evidence to date in the encoder-against-decoder debate: with data, recipe and size held fixed, the objective and the attention pattern alone create task-specific advantages that 50B tokens of the other objective do not erase. The practical reading the authors draw ({{§6|ax:S6}}): for classification and retrieval at 1B or below, a native encoder beats decoders larger than itself, so fine-tuning an encoder (for transcript or intent classification, say) remains the right default rather than adapting a small decoder LLM. MTEB-style leaderboards are topped by adapted decoders of 7B or more because no large encoders exist; the authors predict a natively trained encoder of about 3B would beat them. That is a forecast, not a measurement: no model above 1B was trained.

The disagreement. Concurrent work by {{Gisserot-Boukhlef et al. (2025)|https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.00994}} found that starting from CLM and then continuing with MLM was better "in nearly all cases". Its pretraining used about 100B tokens; Ettin's authors argue that is the regime where CLM's per-token data efficiency (a loss on every token, against MLM's 15 to 30%) dominates, and that the advantage disappears at state-of-the-art scale ({{§6|ax:S6}}). Both studies are consistent with an objective effect that depends on the token budget; neither tests the crossover directly. The authors' follow-up {{mmBERT|https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06888}} builds a multilingual encoder on the same recipe and reports encoders beating encoders-from-decoders at scale as well.

A research platform. Beyond the headline question, the suite (paired open-data models at six sizes, 236 checkpoints each, the replayable batch order) is a successor to Pythia for studying how objectives shape learning, with the gender-bias study as a first example. For the mechanism under all of this at toy scale, the {{BERT page|n:3c65c17b0d0d81e5ad9bd09cbf18ad7c}} trains a bidirectional encoder against a left-to-right twin on a task where the right-hand context decides the answer; this page's {{Train the pairs|tab:t-run}} tab adds what is specific to Ettin: four models from one recipe, conversion in both directions, and an encoder generating by filling masks.

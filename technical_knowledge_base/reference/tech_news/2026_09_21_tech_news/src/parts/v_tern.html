The same ternary weights, packed two ways

One block of 128 weights from a ternary layer (the block size deployed formats quantise in), each −1, 0 or +1, stored two ways and drawn to the same scale, one small square per stored bit. Five-trit packing (the usual way) reads every group of five weights as one base-3 number and stores it in a byte, 26 bytes per block: a zero costs as much as a sign. BITCOS stores a presence bitmap (one bit per weight: is it non-zero?) and then one sign bit for each non-zero weight only, packed together. Play one, switch, and move the zero share: the more zeros, the less BITCOS stores.

Illustrative sample: the 128 weights are drawn at random with the chosen share of zeros; the bit counts are exact for that sample, and the long-run rates below are the formulas.

Bits per weight against the share of zeros

Five-trit packing costs the same whatever the weights are. BITCOS costs 2 − z bits per weight, so it wins once more than 37.5% of the weights are zero (2 − z < 1.625). Each dot is one of the paper's 29 models at its measured zero share: 26 are past the crossover; the 3 that are not are Bonsai 27B and two CAT-Q Qwen3 mixture-of-experts checkpoints, 30B-A3B and 235B-A22B. The dashed curve is the entropy of the weights (the least any lossless code can average, if + and − are equally likely), which BITCOS touches at z = 0.5.

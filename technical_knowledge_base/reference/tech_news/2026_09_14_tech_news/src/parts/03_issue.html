Sep 12Top

Dario Amodei called for slowing frontier capability improvement, and three rivals agreed within two days. "We Must Pace the Frontier" (Sep 12) argues that recursive self-improvement plus the OpenAI agent incidents have moved the risk ahead of the safeguards, and proposes three steps. Step one, immediate and unilateral: embedded third-party evaluators such as METR get employee-level access to verify safety practices, report incidents and audit training pipelines, which Anthropic commits to now. Step two: frontier companies inside democracies agree common safety standards and coordinated development limits, with governments providing antitrust cover. Step three: democratic governments attempt coordination with authoritarian states, China in particular, across four escalating levels from banning bioweapon applications up to a general slowdown. The concrete warning is that a rogue agent swarm could take over internet infrastructure within 6 to 12 months and cause hundreds of billions in damage. Sam Altman agreed and pledged external evaluator access, Elon Musk said "Dario is right", and Satya Nadella said Microsoft "welcomes the deliberate pacing needed to get alignment right" alongside a published Code of Conduct for its MAI models. Chamath Palihapitiya's dissent is the one worth reading against it: that the proposal concentrates authority with the company proposing it. David Sacks argued separately that voluntary commitments make regulation unnecessary.

Sep 10Top

DeepSeek shipped V4.1-Flash, and it is an encoder-decoder. Released Sep 10 under MIT with weights on Hugging Face. 552B total parameters with 8B active during prefill and 16B during decode, a causal encoder-decoder built as a 40-layer transformer split 20 and 20, a 1M-token context, native vision, and a reasoning-effort dial from 1 to 100. KV cache falls to roughly a quarter of the HBM and an eighth of the SSD footprint of V4-Flash, which is the point of the release: DeepSeek spent this generation on the cost of running long agent loops rather than on capability headroom. Reported numbers: Terminal-Bench 2.1 at 90.6, Codeforces 3471, GPQA Diamond 90.9, with the vendor conceding it is stronger on shorter-horizon agent loops than on the longest ones. Pricing $0.30 and $1.20 per million at peak and half that off-peak, cache hits from $0.006. API id is deepseek-flash; from Sep 14 at noon Beijing time, deepseek-v4-pro requests route here at Flash pricing until V4.1 Pro ships. The architectural choice is the story: after two years in which every frontier model was a decoder, an open lab shipped a 552B encoder-decoder and led with the serving arithmetic.

One agent turn, two architectures: V4-Flash against V4.1-Flash

The same turn of an agent loop: a tool result of tokens arrives that is not in the cache, the model reads it (prefill), keeps its cache, then writes its reply (decode). Each row of the grid is one layer, drawn to scale (43 for V4-Flash, 40 for V4.1-Flash); the wide block is the prompt, all its tokens processed together layer by layer, and each narrow column on the right is one generated token. V4-Flash is decoder-only: every prompt token runs every layer and 41 layers each keep their own cache. V4.1-Flash splits the stack: the prompt runs only the 20 encoder layers, the decoder's cache is projected once from the encoder's output, and only the last 128 prompt tokens are replayed through the decoder. Real-SWE ran frontier coding agents against private enterprise codebases and the best one scored 38.8%. Specific Labs licensed ten tasks from real companies, ran eight model-and-harness combinations through 640 scored rollouts, with a median of 11 files changed per task and each model in its native harness. Fable 5.1 leads at 38.8%, GPT-6 Astra 33.8%, Gemini 3.8 Flash 31.2%, GLM-5.3 28.8%, Grok 4.6 and Muse Spark 1.3 both 23.8%, Kimi K3 18.8%, GPT-5.6 Sol 16.2%. Six of ten tasks resolved below 15%, cost per rollout ranged $2.50 to $6.96 with no relationship to success, missed requirements were the commonest failure, and 71.4% of rollouts that finished inside ten minutes failed. Against Terminal-Bench 4.0 figures of 55.8% and 57.9% from the same models a week earlier, the private-code penalty is roughly 20 points.

Sep 13Research Raschka Sep 9

Recurrent Looped Transformer (Yifan Zhang, Princeton, Sep 13): the first published specification of the architecture family GPT-6 Astra is assumed to use. A causal encoder builds a global KV memory and the decoder carries its entire state, final output and layerwise sliding-window cache included, across every token with no reset at the prompt and response boundary. In the reference configuration of 48 encoder and 48 decoder layers with shared weights, that is 96 logical blocks per token and 48t decoder blocks of accumulated depth after t tokens, at fixed per-token compute. The honest caveat is in the paper: it is a design specification with no trained weights, no efficiency numbers and no quality results. Sebastian Raschka's survey of the same family (Sep 10) is the better entry point, and argues the shorter reasoning traces that come with recurrent depth are a real cost to chain-of-thought monitorability.

Depth that grows with the sequence: a decoder-only Transformer against the Recurrent Looped Transformer

Eight tokens (five of prompt, three generated) through the same 96 blocks per token, drawn to the same scale: each column is a token, each row six layers. In a decoder-only Transformer, nothing computed at the top of one token feeds the bottom of the next; tokens meet only through attention, so the deepest chain of computation behind any output stays 96 blocks, and the whole prompt runs in parallel. In the RLT reference layout (48 encoder + 48 decoder layers with shared weights), the encoder runs the prompt in parallel, but each token's decoder starts from the previous token's final decoder state, so the chain grows by 48 blocks per token, and the decoder has to walk the prompt one token at a time. What "62% of speed-of-light" means: decode at batch size 1 is a memory-bandwidth problem

At batch size 1, every generated token has to stream the active weights and the cache of the whole context out of memory once. Speed-of-light is the memory bandwidth divided by those bytes: no kernel can beat it, and the gap to it is time spent waiting between kernels. The defaults are Cohere's own: North Mini Code (30B parameters, 3.3B active), BF16, 8K of context, one H100. Then raise the context, or switch the precision or the GPU, and watch the ceiling move.

From {{Cohere's megakernel post|https://cohere.com/blog/megakernels}} (8 Sep): 3.3B active parameters in BF16 is 6.6 GB of weights per decode step, plus about 0.5 GB of cache at 8K context; 3.35 TB/s on an H100 puts speed-of-light at about 470 tokens per second; vLLM serves 185 (39%), the megakernel 292 (62%), 1.58x faster at batch size 1 and 1.25x to 1.41x end to end. Defaults reproduce 470, 39%, 62% and 1.58x independently (3.35 TB/s / 7.1 GB = 472). Derived: cache bytes scale linearly with context from Cohere's 0.5 GB at 8K (about 61 kB per token); the measured speeds are only shown at Cohere's own settings. The H200 and B200 bandwidths are Nvidia's published figures; the model has not been measured on them here.
